5

minor

“os que devoram o meu povo como quem come pão” qui dévorant plebem meam ut cibum panis

'ut cibum panis' is a noun phrase, 'as food of bread' (LXX brōsei artou): the people are devoured as bread-food. 'como quem come pão' adds an eater ('quem come') and turns the object comparison into a comparison of actions. Traditional and defensible, but the Latin is closer kept as a noun.

proposedos que devoram o meu povo como alimento de pão

kept as an option under qui dévorant plebem meam ut cibum panis — Ps 13 wording kept (13:4, rule 6: the Greek is one for both twins). The same remark was refused there (Ps 13 v2 gate): βρώσει is the eating, and “alimento de pão” is not Portuguese.

A faithful rendering: tenses, persons, voices and the Septuagintal readings (trepidaverunt timore, qui hominibus placent, dissipavit ossa, confusi sunt) are all kept against the Hebrew. The pointing marks match the Latin exactly (one mediant per verse, no flex). The only point worth weighing is 'ut cibum panis' in 52:5, a minor issue.

2

fails: sayable

“Corromperam-se, e tornaram-se abomináveis”

Two enclitic '-se' verbs in a row with a hiatus in '-se, e tor-'. The mouth trips on 'se-e' before it reaches the long word 'abomináveis'. The first colon is heavy for one breath.

proposedCorromperam-se e fizeram-se abomináveis nas iniquidades: * não há quem faça o bem.

not taken — Ps 13 wording kept: the colon is 13:1b’s word for word (identical Latin up to in iniquitátibus); a change must be made in both twins. “fazer-se” is the fíeri row’s word for Ps 117, but 13:1b and 13:3a (= 52:4) chose “tornar-se” for a state that comes upon one, and 52:4 repeats it — the Latin’s facti sunt twice. For Gustavo, with Ps 13.

5

fails: no rhyme

“como quem come pão”

'como … come' is a mild jingle (com-/com-) on the last beat. The phrase itself is native and concrete, and it keeps 'cibum panis' as an act of eating. Keep it unless a plainer sound is wanted.

proposedos que devoram o meu povo como se come o pão?

kept as an option under qui dévorant plebem meam ut cibum panis — Ps 13 wording kept (13:4); he himself advised keeping it.

6b

fails: native, plain

“foram envergonhados”

A stiff, bookish passive. In Brazilian Portuguese people say 'ficaram envergonhados' or 'cobriram-se de vergonha'. The auxiliary is negotiable, and 'ficaram' keeps the Latin's state without the clunk.

proposedficaram envergonhados, porque Deus os desprezou.

kept as an option under confúsi sunt — “ficar envergonhado” is a feeling; confúsi sunt is being put to shame, and D15’s passive stands so elsewhere (21:6, 24:3).

7

fails: plain, no rhyme, sayable

“quando Deus reverter o cativeiro”

'Reverter' sounds juridical and modern: you reverse a court decision or a trend, not a captivity. The ear hears a news report.

proposedquando Deus fizer voltar o cativeiro do seu povo

❧ taken — The glossary’s verb for convértere; draft 1’s fear that it would be heard as the captivity returning was not borne out by any reader.

“de Sião a salvação … Jacó exultará, e Israel se alegrará”

The -ão/-ão echo in the first colon and the -ará/-ará pair at the end make the final cadence sing-song. The paired futures come from the Latin (exsultabit/laetabitur), so this can only be softened, not removed. The second colon is also long for one breath.

proposedquando Deus fizer voltar o cativeiro do seu povo, Jacó exultará e Israel há de alegrar-se.

not taken — Ps 13 wording kept: the first colon is 13:7a word for word and the last clause is 13:7’s (decision exsultabit there); the paired futures are the Latin’s (exsultábit / lætábitur), as he says, and the Portuguese matches them in form.

A lean, sober psalm that sounds like prayer in Portuguese. Verses 1, 3, 4 and 6 are plain and rhythmic, and the repetitions (não há quem faça o bem; temor … temor) are kept to good effect. The weak spots are the clumsy passive 'foram envergonhados' and the technical 'reverter' in the closing verse, which lets down an ending that should rise.

5

minor

“como quem come pão” ut cibum panis

The Latin keeps a concrete noun phrase ('as food of bread', i.e. as a meal of bread); 'como quem come pão' recasts it as a comparison with an eater and adds a verb and subject the Latin does not have. The sense survives, but the rendering explains rather than translates.

proposedos que devoram o meu povo como alimento de pão?

kept as an option under qui dévorant plebem meam ut cibum panis — Ps 13 wording kept (13:4, rule 6: the Greek is one for both twins). The same remark was refused there (Ps 13 v2 gate): βρώσει is the eating, and “alimento de pão” is not Portuguese.

A faithful and close translation: tenses (future 'scient', future perfect 'converterit'), voice ('confusi sunt'), and the Septuagintal additions of 52:6 ('ubi non erat timor', 'qui hominibus placent') are all kept as the Latin has them. The pointing marks match the Latin in every verse. The only point worth a look is 'ut cibum panis' in 52:5, which is paraphrased slightly.

Verse by verse

1

Copied from 13:1a (the Latin is identical, word for word; Latin capital 'Non' as 13:1a's 'non' — no difference in the Portuguese). Ps 13's decision insipiens (→ insensato) governs; the glossary row insípiens names 52:1.

2

First colon copied from 13:1b ('Corromperam-se, e tornaram-se abomináveis' — decision corrupti in Ps 13; facti sunt → 'tornaram-se') up to the phrase that differs: in iniquitátibus for in stúdiis suis (decision iniquitatibus). Second colon: 52:2 has only 'non est qui fáciat bonum' — Ps 13's 'non est usque ad unum' is absent here, so the Portuguese stops where the Latin stops ('não há quem faça o bem', 13:1b's words).

3

Copied from 13:2 with Deus for Dóminus, the one word the Latin changes (Ps 13 decisions prospexit → 'olhou do céu para', requirens → 'procure'). The verse now has 'Deus' twice (Deus … requírens Deum), as the Latin does. The Latin has ':' before 'ut vídeat' where 13:2 has ','; the Portuguese keeps Ps 13's comma — punctuation is grammar and a colon before 'para ver' is not Portuguese usage.

4

Copied from 13:3a — identical Latin (= Rom 3:12). Ps 13 decisions declinaverunt, simul, usque govern.

5

Copied from 13:4. The Latin differs twice, each time where the Greek is one: scient for cognóscent (γνώσονται both), ut cibum for sicut escam (βρώσει ἄρτου both) — the glossary rows esca / cibus and Ps 13's decision escam already give 'como quem come pão' for both (Matos Soares 1932 has it in this very verse). 'saber' is here the plain word for scire itself (Ps 13 chose it for cognóscere because 'conhecer' needs an object). Ps 13 decisions cognoscent, escam govern. Readers v1: the Latinist asked “como alimento de pão” and the stylist offered “como se come o pão” — both refused to keep 13:4’s wording (decision cibum).

6

Second colon copied from 13:5b — identical Latin (glossary timor: 'de temor … temor', the noun twice). First colon: Deum for Dóminum (decision invocaverunt). The Latin has ':' where 13:5 has ','; Ps 13's comma kept, as 52:3.

6b

DO's second '52:6' line, id 52:6b as latin.py gives it. Not in Ps 13 (13:6 stands here), so decided here: dissipáre (decision dissipavit), placére (decision placent), confúndi (D15, decision confusi), spérnere → desprezar (glossary, as 21:25, 118:118). The Latin's two quóniam kept as two 'porque', as 13:6. This is the Septuagint's reading (ἀνθρωπαρέσκων); the Hebrew and the Diurnal-type versions have another verse — not followed (rule 1).

7

First colon copied from 13:7a (identical Latin). Second colon: 'quando Deus … do seu povo, Jacó exultará, e Israel se alegrará' copies 13:7 except where the Latin differs — Deus for Dóminus, convérterit for avérterit (decision converterit: draft 2 “fizer voltar o cativeiro”, the stylist’s and the glossary’s verb). The end (captivitátem plebis suæ, exsultábit Jacob, et lætábitur Israël) is Ps 13's decision exsultabit, the direct order.
