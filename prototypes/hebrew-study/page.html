Prototype A

The Hebrew page

The Leningrad Codex set right to left in Ezra SIL, justified, with the verse number on the right. The same reader, the same Text · Interlinear · Parallel line as the Greek.

  • Letters · Vowels · Chant takes the page from the bare consonants to the full cantillation the synagogue sings. Vowels is the default.
  • Prose runs in the manuscript’s paragraphs: an open paragraph (פ) starts a new line, a closed one (ס) leaves a gap in the line. Verses run on inside a paragraph, as in a printed Hebrew Bible.
  • Poetry is set in lines, cut where the accents divide the verse (atnach; oleh weyored in the Psalms), so the parallel halves stand one under the other.
  • Tap a word for its study; tap a verse number (or hold a word) for the verse’s half page.
  • The ° over a word marks one read otherwise than written (Psalm 100:3).

Prototype B

A word on the sheet

The same half-page sheet as the Greek, built for how Hebrew makes words: from pieces, on roots.

  • The pieces of the word as it stands, joined, each with its gloss (“in · beginning”). Tap a piece to study it: the prefix בְּ has its own entry.
  • The sound, syllable by syllable, the stressed one in bold; then the dictionary form, its sense and its grammar in plain words — the verb’s stem with what it does (“Hiphil, causative”).
  • The root, letter by letter, opens the Family: every word Brown–Driver–Briggs files under it, with how often each occurs. רֹעִי, “my shepherd” (Ps 23:1), sits with מַרְעִית, “pasture” (Ps 100:3).
  • Meaning · BDB · Strong’s · Occurrences as tabs, each occurrence with its Douay line.
  • God’s Name (any יהוה) reads Adonai, “the LORD”, with a note; its family is not shown.
  • The ° word in Psalm 100:3 shows what is read and what is written, and which one the Vulgate and the Douay translate.
