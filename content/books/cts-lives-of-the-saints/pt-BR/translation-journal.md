## Translation Decisions

- **Register split**: The book collects material from very different eras (a 1958 novena booklet, a 1945/1950s Australian CTS-style hagiography series, and reprinted 18th-century Butler's-style "Lives of the Saints" entries with period English). Rather than flattening everything into one voice, I matched the source register within the bounds of the skill's "not archaic, not casual" rule: modern devotional Portuguese for the 20th-century pieces (Anthony, Bernadette, Dymphna, Dominic Savio, Hofbauer, Edith Stein, Cataldus, Brigid), and long, formal, periodic-sentence Portuguese for the Butler-style entries (Augustine, Benedict, Bernard, Dominic, Clare, Cajetan, Camillus), preserving their characteristic subordinate-clause-heavy structure rather than breaking it into short modern sentences.
- **Quotation marks**: Source files use inconsistent/OCR'd single quotes for direct speech. Standardized all direct speech and quoted material to Portuguese double quotes ("…").
- **"Saint Anthony and You"** (booklet) contains an apparent OCR-era editorial artifact — a duplicated clause ("every sin offends the entirety of humanity" appears twice in one sentence) and an internally inconsistent commandment number ("fifth commandment" where context implies the sixth/purity commandment). Per the skill's fidelity rule, both were translated as-is rather than silently corrected, since they are peculiarities of this specific source printing, not something a translator should quietly fix.
- **Litany of St. Dymphna**: preserved the full call-and-response litany structure and markdown bold formatting for the response phrases (*rogai por nós*, etc.), translating each invocation individually rather than summarizing.
- **Place names**: kept in their standard Portuguese exonym where one is well established (Roma, Nápoles, Veneza, Milão, Colônia [for Köln], Espira [for Speyer], Reims, Genebra, etc.); left less common places in their original spelling (e.g. Fanjeaux, Prouille, Tagaste, Sublacum/Subiaco footnote, Bocchianico).
- **"Sedia Gestatoria"** (Dominic Savio) kept in Latin/Italian as it is a proper liturgical-object name still used as such in Portuguese.
- **Cardinal Salotti's explanatory footnote-style paragraph** in Saint Dominic Savio (about the 1908 Eucharistic Congress) was translated in full as part of the body text, matching the source, which presents it as inline text rather than a footnote.
- No editor/translator footnotes were added in this batch — all source content was translatable without needing a *Nota do tradutor* gloss.

## Translation Decisions

- **Dropped editor/footnote apparatus.** *Saint Gregory Nazianzen* and *Saint Ignatius of Loyola* are reprinted from an older hagiographical collection (Butler's-style) with heavy scholarly-apparatus footnotes (bracketed numbers `[1]`, `[2]`... referencing `Or.`, `Carm.`, `Ep.` citations of patristic/primary sources, plus a numbered endnote list). Per the skill's default policy, these editorial citation footnotes were dropped entirely (both the inline markers and the endnote list at the bottom of each source file) since they are scholarly apparatus, not part of the saint's own words or the narrative. No content was lost — only citation apparatus.
- **Convocation footnote in *Saint John Fisher*** — no footnote apparatus was present in that chapter (only one translator-modernization note in the source itself, which was about English quotations and didn't need reproducing).
- **Imperial measurements converted to metric** where the original gave concrete distances/heights for reader clarity (e.g. "thirteen miles" → "cerca de vinte e um quilômetros"; "five-foot four" → "um metro e sessenta e três"; "3500 feet" → "a mil e sessenta metros"; "twelve miles" → "vinte quilômetros"). Currency amounts (£, francs) were left as historical currency figures rather than converted, since they name a specific historical sum, not a generic measurement.
- **Latin liturgical/devotional phrases kept in Latin** where the source itself quotes them untranslated inline (e.g. "*Omnes sancti Innocentes, orate pro eo*" in St Francis de Sales; "*Les Controverses*" as the title of Francis de Sales' book, kept in French since it's a proper title). "Nihil Obstat", "Imprimi Potest", "Imprimatur" front-matter at chapter ends kept in Latin per standard convention (already established in shared journal).
- **Scripture references** converted to standard Portuguese Catholic abbreviation style (e.g. "Matt. xi, 29" → "Mt 11, 29"; "1 Cor. IX. 16" → "1 Cor 9, 16"; "Dan 12:3" → "Dn 12,3") rather than kept as literal English abbreviations, for consistency with how Portuguese Bibles cite references. Chapter/verse numbers themselves are unchanged.
- **Villeneuve-en-Berg** (St John Francis Régis chapter) — silently normalized to "Villeneuve-de-Berg," the actual/standard name of the town in Ardèche; the source's spelling appears to be a period variant/typo.
- **"Le Puy," "Montpellier," "Toulouse," "La Louvesc," "Sommières," "Fontcouverte," etc.** (Régis chapter) kept in their French form — no standard Portuguese exonym exists for these smaller French towns.
- **Established French/Italian/Spanish city exonyms used where they exist**: Lyon → Lyon (no common PT exonym, kept as-is — note: some PT sources do use "Lião" but this is archaic/uncommon today, so "Lyon" was kept), Genebra (Geneva), Turim (Turin), Bolonha (Bologna), Nápoles (Naples), Pádua (Padua), Veneza (Venice), Pisa/Roma as usual.
- **"comes rerum privatarum"** (St Gregory Nazianzen, Roman civil office) kept in Latin as the source does, since it's a specific historical Roman title without a natural Portuguese calque.
- **Basque proper names** (St Francis Xavier chapter) — "Xavier" kept as the family/place name (already the saint's own PT name per shared glossary); "Pamplona," "Castela," "Aragão" per standard PT historical spelling.
- **"amo" Latin-conjugation anecdote** (St Ignatius chapter, Barcelona grammar-school episode) — the Latin verb "amo" ("I love") happens to be identical in form to the Portuguese first-person "amo," which let the wordplay of the anecdote translate naturally without needing a translator's note.
- **Kept "S.J."** (Society of Jesus abbreviation) in the St Francis Xavier title per existing convention for post-nominal abbreviations (already noted in shared journal for other order abbreviations).

## Non-Obvious Translation Decisions

- **saint-john-the-baptist.md**: The source text is heavily OCR-corrupted (broken mid-word line breaks like "b asis", "s econd", "with out"). These were silently corrected in translation rather than reproduced as errors, since they are clearly OCR artifacts, not authorial style. Scripture quotations (Gospel of Luke, John, etc.) were rendered into natural modern liturgical Portuguese rather than a strict transliteration of the English Douay-Rheims wording, aiming for the register of a Catholic Bible translation (e.g. Ave Maria / CNBB style) while preserving the exact content and order of the source's paraphrase.
- **saint-joseph.md**: The two footnotes citing De La Broise and Orsini were kept (these are author/source citations integral to the text, not later editorial apparatus, so per the skill's default they were translated and retained) with their book titles kept in English/French as in the original (since these are bibliographic references, not quoted body text) — italicized per convention.
- **saint-joseph-cafasso.md**: Distances originally in miles were converted to their approximate metric (km) equivalents (e.g. "twenty miles" → "trinta e dois quilômetros"; "2,800 ft" → "850 metros") for a Brazilian readership, consistent with how other batches have likely handled measurements — noted here in case other translators want to align.
- **saint-jude-helper-in-great-need.md**: This chapter contains the full text of the Epistle of St. Jude and the Tridentine Mass propers (Introit, Collect, Epistle, Gradual, Gospel, Offertory, Secret, Preface, Communion, Postcommunion) plus a full litany. These liturgical texts were rendered into natural modern Portuguese liturgical register (not a literal transliteration of the 1962-Missal English), following standard Catholic Portuguese conventions (e.g. "Senhor, tende piedade de nós" for Kyrie eleison responses, "rogai por nós" for ora pro nobis). Devotional prayer language ("vinde em meu auxílio", "eu vo-lo suplico") uses the classic devotional register with enclitic pronouns, appropriate to this genre of prayer manual text, distinct from the narrative chapters' more modern register.
- **saint-louis-ix-king-of-france-confessor.md**: French proper names and titles were kept in their standard Portuguese historical renderings where established (e.g. "Filipe II Augusto", "Henrique III", "Carlos VII") but place names were left as-is when there is no common Portuguese exonym (e.g. "Taillebourg", "Saintonge", "Mansurá" for Mansourah — Arabic-origin place name has an established Portuguese form). "Oriflamme" → "Oriflama" (established term). The chapter's footnote about the Crown of Thorns relic was kept as a footnote-style aside at the end matching the source formatting (the source itself embeds it mid-paragraph via an asterisk; it was moved to the end of the document as a single note, consistent with how footnote markers function in the rest of this book's chapters).
- **saint-louise-de-marillac.md**: Long chapter with many direct quotations attributed to St. Vincent de Paul and St. Louise — quotation marks and capitalization of pronouns referring to God ("Ele", "Sua", "Vontade") preserved consistently. "Mademoiselle Le Gras" kept as her historical name-in-religion during her early widowhood, per the source's own usage.
- **saint-margaret-clitherow.md**: This chapter contains an internal date inconsistency in the source itself (the trial narrative jumps between "March 12", "March 14", and later "Tuesday, March 5th" before "the 15th" — almost certainly an OCR/editing error in the original CTS text, likely meant to be "March 15th"). This was translated faithfully as-is without silent correction, since it's a factual/editorial matter beyond a translator's remit to fix, not an OCR word-break issue.
- **saint-margaret-of-cortona.md**: "Val di Chiano" in the source (a typo for "Val di Chiana") was rendered as "Val di Chiana" (the correct, real place name), since this is a geographic proper noun rather than stylistic content, and the source itself uses "Chiana" correctly elsewhere in the same chapter ("valley of the Chiano" vs "Lake of Montepulciano" being on the actual Val di Chiana). ## Non-obvious Translation Decisions

- **Two St. Pius X chapters**: `saint-pius-x-460.md` is a fictionalized frame narrative (a priest telling the Morton family the saint's life story by the fireside) culminating in his Eucharistic reforms — titled "São Pio X, Papa da Eucaristia" (matching its own concluding line "Pope of the Eucharist"). `saint-pius-x.md` is a straight biographical account emphasizing his fight against Modernism — titled "São Pio X, Flagelo do Modernismo" (matching the source's own heading "Scourge of Modernism"). Kept distinct per instructions.
- **Padre Pio**: kept "Padre Pio" untouched throughout (per shared glossary), including the publisher's note about his 2002 canonization. Translated the note itself. Kept his book titles ("Per la Storia", "Fatti Nuova", "Fino alla Meta") in Italian, as they are actual Italian publication titles, not translatable content.
- **saint-peter-aloysius-mary-chanel.md**: the English source itself is inconsistent, titling the chapter "Saint Peter Aloysius Chanel" but using "Peter Aloysius Mary Chanel" throughout the body and in the final canonization decree. Followed the body/decree form and the shared glossary entry ("St. Peter Louis Mary Chanel" = Aloysius→Luís), rendering him consistently as "Pedro Luís Maria Chanel" / "São Pedro Luís Maria Chanel".
- **Poem translations**: Both the Chanel hymn ("To Mary's son who died...") and Southwell's own poems ("On the Loss of the Child", "Decease, Release", "Times Go by Turns", the "Not where I breathe..." verses) were translated as verse, prioritizing meaning and rhythm over strict rhyme, since these are minor devotional/period pieces rather than canonical liturgical texts.
- **St. Patrick's Breastplate**: translated as free verse (not attempting to match the traditional sung English/Irish hymn meter), consistent with treating it as the chapter author's own illustrative quotation rather than a liturgical text requiring a fixed existing PT translation.
- **Dialogue formatting**: Source OCR uses single quotes (' ') inconsistently for dialogue throughout (especially in saint-mary-mazzarello, saint-maximilian-kolbe, padre-pio, saint-pius-x-460). Rendered all dialogue with standard Portuguese double quotes ("...") for consistency and readability.
- **"Persuivants" (pursuivants/priest-hunters)** in poet-priest-martyr.md: translated as "batedores" (with a parenthetical gloss "batedores de perseguição" on first use) rather than a loanword, since no single standard PT term exists for this specific Elizabethan-era office.
- **Topcliffe's torture device ("the manacles")**: rendered as "as manoplas", a literal and clear PT equivalent.
- **Modernized/kept as-is**: "Mahometans"/"Mussulman" do not appear in this batch. "Communist"/"comunista" in Padre Pio and Saint Paschal Baylon kept as-is (period-accurate polemical usage from the original 1950s text, not requiring modernization).
- **Numbers/measurements**: Converted miles to approximate kilometers where the original used precise distances in a narrative (e.g., "four miles" → "quase três milhas" contexts in Chanel's chapter were left in narrative prose rather than hard-converted where the English itself was approximate; distances explicitly in miles elsewhere were converted to km for a modern PT-BR reader, e.g. Southwell "twenty miles" → "trinta e dois quilômetros").
- **"Nihil obstat" / "Imprimatur" / censor lines**: kept untranslated (standard ecclesiastical Latin/formula, appears in every chapter of this book per the existing shared journal's convention).
- **Archbishop titles** (e.g. "Archiepiscopus Melbournensis"): translated as "Arcebispo de Melbourne" for readability, matching how the imprimatur blocks were likely handled by other translators of this book (per shared-journal precedent of translating structural/front-matter text).
