## Translation Decisions (2026-09-08)

- **padre-pio.md**: The source text is a mid-20th-century first-person memoir (by an unnamed author, likely Fr. Alberto D'Apolito) with many OCR artifacts (missing opening quotation marks throughout, run-together words, stray periods). Translated the intended sense throughout without "fixing" the English beyond what was needed for coherent Portuguese. Kept Italian book/publication titles in italics as in source ("Per la Storia," "Fino alla Meta," "Fatti Nuova," "L'Italia Laica," "Casa Sollievo della Sofferenza"). Kept the Italian exclamation "Viva Gesù!" as-is (already Italian in source, intelligible to Portuguese readers). The "Nihil Obstat"/publisher's note preserved with original Latin/place-name spelling ("Paulopoli die 27a Julii 1955"). Kept "Padre Pio" untranslated throughout (never rendered as "Pai Pio") since this is how he is universally known in Portuguese as well.
- **eugene-de-mazenod.md**: Rendered "Eugene de Mazenod" as "Eugênio de Mazenod" (standard Portuguese form of the saint's first name), keeping surname "de Mazenod" unchanged. All other French proper names (place names, other clergy) kept unchanged per convention. "Midi" (the south of France) kept as "Midi" (widely recognized loanword) since there is no single natural Portuguese equivalent that isn't clunkier; used "sul da França" once for clarity at first mention region description elsewhere. "Vicar General," "Chief Justice," etc. translated functionally ("Vigário Geral," "Presidente da Alta Corte").
- **saint-clement-mary-hofbauer-c-ss-r.md**: This is a heavily rhetorical 20th-century hagiographic essay (by "John Dvorak," a pen name referencing the saint's Moravian surname). Preserved the essay's high rhetorical register and second-person direct addresses to the reader ("dear reader" → "caro leitor"). Kept "C.SS.R." (Congregatio Sanctissimi Redemptoris) untranslated as a standard postnominal abbreviation.
- **saint-margaret-mary-alacoque.md**: The twelve numbered "Promises of the Sacred Heart" at the end were translated as a numbered list, preserving numbering exactly as in source (this is standard catechetical content, translated using the conventional Portuguese wording found in Brazilian devotional literature where it coincides with a natural rendering of the English).
- **saint-john-bosco.md**: "Don Bosco" rendered as "Dom Bosco" throughout (standard Brazilian Portuguese usage — Salesian sources in Brazil always use "Dom Bosco", not "Padre João Bosco"). Kept "Cheerful Club" as "Clube da Alegria" (translating the sense, since it's a translated nickname in the English source itself, not an official Italian proper name). Converted imperial units to metric (miles → quilômetros, feet/inches for height → metros) since the source itself is inconsistent and a modern Portuguese reader expects metric; noted here as a minor, deliberate modernization for readability.
- **saint-mary-euphrasia-pelletier.md**: "Sister Marie" at the opening (the future saint as postulant) kept as "Irmã Marie" (not "Irmã Maria") since the source explicitly uses the French form of her name in that one early passage before switching to "Rose Virginia"; this preserves the source's own name-switching device.
- **saint-gregory-nazianzen-b-c.md**: This chapter is a Butler's-Lives-of-the-Saints-style entry with extensive scholarly citation apparatus (bracketed superscript numbers referencing Gregory's own orations, letters, and poems, collected in an endnote list at the end). Per the book's general policy (see original journal entry on OCR/no-editor-footnotes), these citation numbers are treated as part of the *author's* (not a later editor's) apparatus — akin to Scripture references — and were therefore kept as-is (e.g. "[1]", "[2]") with the endnote list translated/kept in the same abbreviated Latin bibliographic form (Or., Carm., Ep., etc.) rather than dropped, since dropping them would leave floating bracketed numbers with no referent. Note this differs from a normal CTS pamphlet chapter — this entry reads as reprinted directly from an older scholarly hagiography (e.g., Alban Butler), which the source itself signals with the opening bibliographic note.
- **saint-patrick.md**: This chapter is a sermon/lecture (addressed to a live audience — note "Beside this church*..."), not a straightforward biography; preserved its oratorical, second-person rhetorical style and rhetorical questions throughout. The footnote marker "*" beside "this church" (referring to a specific church where the lecture was delivered) was kept in place even though the explanatory footnote itself is not present in the source file — this is evidently a case where the original pamphlet's own footnote was lost to OCR; per policy we neither invented content to fill it nor removed the marker, since removing it would alter the visible text structure of the source.
- **saint-lucy-of-syracuse.md**: Source file title is "St. Lucy" (no "of Syracuse" in the H1), though the filename and content establish Syracuse; kept the title as simply "Santa Luzia" to mirror the source's own H1 exactly.
- **saint-philip-benizi-confessor.md**: "Conf." (Confessor, liturgical title) kept abbreviated in the title as in source: "Santo Filipe Benício, Conf." Servite proper names (Buonfiglio Monaldi, Bonaventura, etc.) given standard Portuguese saint-name forms where they carry the title "St./Bd." (e.g. "St Buonfiglio" → "São Buonfiglio" keeping the distinctive Italian name itself unchanged, as these are less commonly used in Portuguese devotional literature and there's no established Portuguese-form convention).

## Expressions & Idioms

- English "a dead letter" (saint-mary-euphrasia-pelletier.md, "names without facts are a dead letter to the average reader") → "letra morta", a natural existing Portuguese idiom with the same sense.
- "burning the candle at both ends" type imagery and other period English idioms were translated by sense rather than literal equivalents throughout, per general policy already in the main journal.
