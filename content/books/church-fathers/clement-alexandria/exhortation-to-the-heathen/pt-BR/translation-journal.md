# Translation Journal — Exhortation to the Heathen (pt-BR)

Source: en-US (William Wilson, Ante-Nicene Fathers Vol. 2, via New Advent). There is no Greek directory, so the English is the only source.
Target: pt-BR

## Key Terms

The same author's *Who is the Rich Man That Shall Be Saved?* already has a pt-BR translation. It sets the author name ("Clemente de Alexandria", no "São"), "Word" → "Verbo" and "Paraclete" → "Paráclito". The other conventions follow `athenagoras/plea-for-the-christians/pt-BR` and `tatian/address-to-the-greeks/pt-BR`, which are from the same ANF volume and the same apologetic genre: Scripture book names in full Portuguese with the source's chapter:verse, curly quotes “ ”, lowercase pronouns for God (ele, dele) with capitalized titles, and Greek deity names in their standard Portuguese form.

## Translation Decisions

- **Editor footnotes:** the New Advent import has none, so nothing was dropped. No translator notes were added.
- **Headings:** each file keeps its `# Chapter N.` H1, the bold-italic byline (`***POR CLEMENTE DE ALEXANDRIA***`, written in the same caps as the source) and the ANF `## Chapter N. …` title, which is translated in sentence case as "Capítulo N. …". The toc titles are "Capítulo N.", mirroring the source's trailing period.
- **Scripture** is translated from Wilson's wording, not from a Portuguese Bible. Inline references sit where the source places them, including mid-sentence and inside quotations. The source's citations are mirrored even where they are wrong (ch. 8, "by Hosea" followed by Amos 4:13).
- **Verse quotations** (Homer, Euripides, Orpheus, the Sibyl, etc.) are rendered line for line from the English, without meter or rhyme, keeping the source's line breaks and ellipsis lines.
- **Address:** `vós` for the heathen addressed as a body; `tu` wherever the source addresses one person (ch. 1 "supposest"; Homer, Plato, Croesus, Solon and Tiresias in apostrophe; "O man"; the singular imperatives of ch. 10–12).
- English `:—` before a quotation is rendered as `:`; stray spaces before punctuation ("Lord .") are not reproduced.
- Ch. 1 "not leaves of laurel fillets interwoven with wool and purple" → "não folhas de louro nem fitas entretecidas de lã e púrpura". This reads the laurel and the woollen fillets as two separate pagan purifications, as the Greek has them.
- Ch. 2 "Sagra" is mirrored as printed (the place is Agrae). "braize-fish" → "o pargo", the sea bream that "braize" names. The verse "See this lofty, this boundless ether" is rendered as an imperative ("Vê"), with no question mark, as in the source.
- Ch. 2 "orgies" and "mysteries" are put in quotation marks in the etymology sentence, where they are used as words. This adds one pair of curly quotes that the source does not have.
- Ch. 3 "Elusinium" → "Eleusínio" (the Athenian Eleusinion), and ch. 7 "Nyssa" → "Nisa", both in their standard forms. The English is left as printed.
- Ch. 4 "Philip of Pella, the son of Amyntor" is mirrored as printed ("filho de Amintor"), although history gives Amyntas.
- Ch. 7 "ate green figs to flesh" → "comeu figos verdes com carne".
- Ch. 11 "This, too, He has been promised to the Father" → "Isto também ele prometeu ao Pai". The text reads it as an active verb, which suits the Psalm quotation that follows.
- Ch. 11 "that, have been baptized by the Word" → "nós que fomos batizados pelo Verbo". The stray comma is not reproduced.

## Review log

### Round 1

Focus: completeness, paragraph by paragraph (12 chapters, 287 paragraph pairs), and Scripture book names and chapter:verse. Verdict: no paragraph, quotation, parenthetical, bracketed gloss, Greek word or reference is missing or added; every reference uses the Portuguese book name and the source's numbers. Five defects fixed:

- Ch. 1 "a temple by reason of the word": "templo por causa do verbo" → "templo por causa da palavra". The source lowercases "word" here, and Key Terms renders lowercase "word" as "palavra".
- Ch. 2 "in the regions of Cynosuris": "Cinosura" → "Cinosúride". Cynosura is a different name; "-is" names take "-ide", as with "Titânide" in the same chapter.
- Ch. 3 "Phoroneus or Merops": "Mérope" → "Mérops". "Mérope" is the female name (the Pleiad); Merops is a man.
- Ch. 9 "the first-born Church": "a Igreja dos primogênitos" → "a Igreja primogênita". The pt-BR followed Hebrews 12:23, not the source's wording.
- Ch. 11 "will you not allow the heavenly Word … to be bound on to you as an amulet, and … be delivered": "permitirás que o Verbo … te seja atado … e, confiando …, ser libertado" → "… e que, confiando …, sejas libertado". The infinitive after a `que` clause broke the construction.

Rejected findings:

- Ch. 1 "the mother of noble rule" → "a mãe de nobre linhagem". Wilson's "rule" renders ἀρχή (origin as well as rule); "linhagem" reads it as origin, a defensible choice for an obscure phrase.
- Ch. 2 "Cease, foolish men, from mortals" → "Cessai … de buscar os mortais". The added verb only fills the ellipsis.
- Ch. 2 "slipped into the bedroom" → "leito nupcial". The rite's παστός is the bridal chamber, so the referent is the same.
- Ch. 2 "Marpissa", "Cletor", "Argennus", "Heraclitopolites"; ch. 4 "Proclus", "Scyles" → "Marpessa", "Clítor", "Argino", "heracleopolitas", "Procles", "Scilis". These are the standard forms of the same persons and peoples. A wrong person (Merops/Mérope) is a defect; a variant spelling of the right one is not.
- Ch. 4 "But that one may suppose that I have passed over them through ignorance" → "para que ninguém suponha …". The English has evidently lost a "not". The pt-BR gives the intended sense. The source is left unedited because no printed reading was checked.
- Ch. 7 "No God pleases me" → "Nenhum deus". This is a pagan god in Menander, and lowercase follows the book's treatment of pagan gods.
- Ch. 10 "good fathers to their children; and good parents to their sons" → "bons pais para os seus filhos" twice. Portuguese has one word for each pair, so nothing is dropped.
- Ch. 10 "as the Iris is not a god" → "não é uma deusa". The gender agrees with "Íris".
- Ch. 11 "human learning" → "sabedoria humana". This is a near-synonym with the same sense here.

### Round 2

Focus: a clause-by-clause bilingual fidelity read of all 12 chapters, plus the mechanical audit (book.json; paragraph, quotation, heading and reference counts; Key Terms frequencies). Verdict: four defects, all fixed. The mechanical audit is clean. The Greek words in ch. 2 and 6 differ from the source only in codepoint (monotonic tonos for polytonic oxia), which are NFC-equivalent.

- Ch. 2, Ephesians 2:5, "when we were now dead in trespasses": "quando estávamos ainda mortos" → "quando já estávamos mortos". "now" means "already". "ainda" was the Almeida wording.
- Ch. 8, the Shema, "the Lord your God is one Lord": "é o único Senhor" → "é um só Senhor". The source states oneness, not exclusivity. The pt had followed the familiar Bible wording.
- Ch. 10, "and to whom alone He promises": "e a quem só ele promete" → "e só a ele promete". The pt put "only" on the promiser instead of the recipient.
- Ch. 10, "You yourself will spontaneously surmount": "Vós mesmos superareis" → "Tu mesmo superarás". "yourself" is singular, and the address rule above gives `tu` for one addressee.

Rejected findings:

- Ch. 4 "the gods called at Athens venerable" → "das deusas". The referents are the Semnai, who are goddesses, and English "gods" does not mark gender.
- Ch. 12 "pass by Pleasure, she beguiles" → "ele seduz". The pronoun agrees with the masculine noun "o Prazer".
- Ch. 4 "the peculiar people" → "o povo adquirido". Here "peculiar" has its KJV sense, "one's own possession", so the meaning is the same.
- Ch. 2 "Quench the flame, O Hierophant … let your mysteries be honoured" → "os vossos mistérios". The mysteries belong to the pagans at large, not to the hierophant alone.
- Ch. 9 "I testify in the Lord, that you walk no longer" → "Dou testemunho … de que não andeis". The subjunctive keeps the source's jussive sense.
- Ch. 2 "that Cyrenian … Theodorus" → "cirenaico". Theodorus was a Cyrenean and a Cyrenaic, so both readings fit.

Evidence (quoted pairs, audit output): `ember-translation-evidence/church-fathers__clement-alexandria__exhortation-to-the-heathen/review-2.md`.

### Round 3

Focus: a source-blind cold read of all 12 chapters, with each mark then checked against the source, followed by a mechanical sweep of spelling, diacritics, crase, hyphenation, punctuation and quote/dash pairing. The mechanical audit is also clean: book.json, and the paragraph, heading and blockquote counts. Verdict: three defects, all fixed.

- Ch. 2, "the goats of the Thmuitæ": "tmuitas" → "tmuítas". The Greek has a hiatus (Θμουΐται), so the tonic í takes an accent, as in *jesuíta*. Without it the word reads as "t-muitas".
- Ch. 3, "And Immarus, the son of Eumolpus and Daira, were they not buried": "não foram sepultados" → "não foi sepultado". The pt copied Wilson's plural onto a singular subject, which breaks concord. The Greek verb is singular.
- Ch. 11, "the goad that urges to salvation— He who expels destruction and pursues death— He who builds up": "persegue a morte —, aquele que edifica" → "persegue a morte, aquele que edifica". This removes a closing dash that had no opening one.

Rejected findings:

- Ch. 7, Euripides' *Ion*, "You, Poseidon, and you, Zeus, the ruler of heaven,—" → "soberano do céu —,". The dash is in the source and resumes the long "if" clause after its parenthesis. It is not a misplaced pair.
- Ch. 1, "the barren and the desolate is childless no more" → "a estéril e a desolada já não fica sem filhos". The singular verb follows the source, which treats the pair as one referent.
- Ch. 10, "your ancestral customs, with which your minds are preoccupied" → "de que as vossas mentes estão preocupadas". "preocupado de" is classical regency, not an error.

Evidence (cold-read marks beside the source, sweep output): `ember-translation-evidence/church-fathers__clement-alexandria__exhortation-to-the-heathen/review-3.md`.

### Round 4

Focus: function words, headings and the places where Portuguese must commit and the English is open. This covered the gender of referents, pronoun antecedents, connectors, and a turn-by-turn tu/vós trace through every apostrophe. A per-chapter Key Terms frequency sweep and the mechanical audit were also run. The mechanical audit is clean: book.json, and the line, heading, blockquote, bold and reference counts. Verdict: three defects, all fixed.

- Ch. 1, "we, who, because destined to be in Him, pre-existed in the eye of God": "que, por estarmos destinados a estar nele, preexistíamos antes aos olhos de Deus" → "que preexistíamos antes aos olhos de Deus, por estarmos destinados a estar nele". "Nele" came right after "mundo", so it read "destined to be in the world". It now follows "Deus".
- Key Terms, "Deity / deity": the row claimed "divindade" throughout, but ch. 2's Sabazian formula has "o deus" twice. The row now records that exception.
- Key Terms, "lust / lusts": the row claimed the noun is "luxúria", but ch. 10 has "não cobiçarás, pois só pela cobiça", where the noun follows the verb. The row now records the verb "cobiçar" and this noun.

Rejected findings:

- Ch. 1, "if one of those serpents even is willing to repent" → "se uma dessas serpentes quiser ao menos arrepender-se". English "even is willing" reads "is so much as willing", which is what "ao menos" says.
- Ch. 1, "then both become mothers through the word, the one of fruits, the other of believers" → "ambas … uma … a outra". The feminine personifies the barren woman and the desert as the "mães" of the predicate, and it is used consistently through the clause.
- Ch. 1, "By the fear which these inspired" → "estes". The masculine takes "signs and wonders" (sinais, prodígios) as the antecedent, a reading the English allows.
- Ch. 2, "the other two sons of Kronos; and of these, one in Crete, and the others again in Arcadia" → "o outro". There are only two sons, so the English plural is a slip. The singular gives the only possible sense.
- Ch. 2, "this deity, always changing sides … was a Spartan; Sophocles knew him" → "esta divindade … era espartano; … conhecia-o". This is agreement by sense with Mars (silepse de gênero), and it carries into "o".
- Ch. 2, "that Zeus of yours" (just after the apostrophe to Homer) → "aquele vosso Zeus". The clause leaves the apostrophe and speaks of the heathen's Zeus, as with "your Poseidon" in the same chapter.
- Ch. 2, "Explore not … Recount to us … if you will" → `tu`. These imperatives address one interlocutor ("Recount to us") before the address to the heathen at large in the next paragraph.
- Ch. 8, Isaiah, "if He open heaven, quaking will seize you" → "de ti". Inside the quotation the singular is a generic address, and the English does not mark number.
- Ch. 10, "But do you still continue in your sins? … 'Yours is the kingdom of heaven?' Yours … if you will" → `tu`, then "Vosso … se quiserdes". The Gospel's "Yours" (Luke 6:20) is plural, and the author's echo keeps it.
- Ch. 12, "a hymn of the King of the universe" → "um hino ao Rei do universo". The genitive is objective (a hymn addressed to the King), so "ao" is a defensible reading.

### Round 5

Focus: terms, journal and names. This round ran a per-paragraph term-frequency diff of every Key Terms row in both directions. It also grepped every claim in the journal against the chapter files and the en-US source, and checked each proper name against the standard Portuguese form and against sibling pt-BR works. The mechanical audit covered book.json, paragraph and heading counts, footnotes, quotes and the Scripture reference sequence. Verdict: clean. Every Key Terms row holds, every journal claim checks out, and no defect was found.

Rejected findings:

- Ch. 4 "I have been in the habit of walking on the earth" → "tenho o costume de andar"; ch. 6 "avoid the practice of soothing our crying children" → "evitamos o costume de acalmar". "costume" here is the everyday idiom "ter o costume de", not the key term. Every "custom" in the source is still "costume", so the row holds.
- Ch. 3 "for anger or covetousness" and ch. 4 "base greed of gain", "deeds of covetousness" → "cobiça". This is the ordinary word for covetousness. The lust row records "cobiça" only for ch. 10's noun, which follows the verb "cobiçar", and it does not claim that "cobiça" is used for nothing else.
- Ch. 4 "the other divinities", "squinting divinities", "a motley crowd of divinities"; ch. 5 "a divinity"; ch. 7 "Your godship" → "divindade(s)". These are near-synonyms of "deity" with the same referents, and nothing in them contradicts the Deity row.
- Ch. 2 and 4 "Pelops" → "Pélope", where Tatian's pt-BR has "Pélops". Both are standard Portuguese forms of the same hero, and this book uses "Pélope" consistently.
- Ch. 1 "nor that of Capito" → "Capitão". Latin names in -o take -ão in Portuguese, as "Varro" → "Varrão" does in ch. 4. It is the man's name, not "captain".
- Ch. 2 "from Odrysus" → "Odrisso". No standard Portuguese form exists for this obscure eponym, and the spelling names the right person.

### Round 6

Focus: completeness, paragraph by paragraph. All 12 chapters were aligned one to one (424 line pairs) and read clause by clause. The round also checked Bible book names and every chapter:verse (68 references), plus the mechanical audit: book.json, paragraph, blockquote and heading counts, footnotes, quote pairing and line truncation. Verdict: clean. No paragraph is dropped, added, merged or split, and no quotation, parenthetical, bracketed gloss or Greek word is missing. Every book name is in Portuguese and every reference matches the source.

Rejected findings:

- Ch. 2, "butchered victims, crowned with snakes, shrieking" → "vítimas degoladas, coroadas de serpentes, gritando". "coroadas" can refer to the bacantes or to the vítimas, just as the English word order allows. The Portuguese adds no new ambiguity.
- Ch. 6, Cleanthes, "That which is regular, just, holy, pious." → "piedoso,". The source has a period in the middle of the list. The comma keeps the list going and loses no content.
- Ch. 7, Orestes in Euripides, "Him do you regard as stained with guilt— put him to death" → "considerai … dai-lhe". Orestes is speaking to the onlookers, and the English allows a plural addressee.
- Ch. 10, "this blindness of eyes and dulness of hearing" → "dureza do ouvido". This is the Portuguese idiom for being hard of hearing ("duro de ouvido").
- Ch. 10, "Wealth … [as Plutus]" → "[como Pluto]". Pluto is the Portuguese name for Plutus, the god of wealth. Plutão is a different figure (Hades), the one ch. 2 names.
- Ch. 12, "Such are the reveries of my mysteries" → "êxtases". Wilson's "reveries" stands for the Bacchic revels, and "êxtases" has the same sense.
