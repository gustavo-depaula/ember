Let us therefore proceed to consider the properties of fire. The nature of fire is such that in different things it acts in different and often in contrary ways: it forthwith consumes wood, hay, or stubble: gold, silver, precious stones it purifies and makes more beautiful: iron, which naturally is black, cold, hard, and heavy, is transformed by fire, its qualities are changed, and it is gradually rendered clear, hot, soft, and light, so that it shines like a star, burns as fire, dissolves like water, and becomes so light that a blacksmith can very easily move and lift it.

All these things manifestly correspond with the working of God. And first, according to the Apostle in the former Epistle to the Corinthians, wood, hay, stubble signify evil deeds which cannot stand the fire of the divine judgement.!_ And we can hardly believe how sorely all sins displease God who is most pure fire, and with what zeal he consumes and destroys them, if so be they can be destroyed by patience, that is, if he who has sinned is in a condition to do penance: for all sins are remitted by penitence. But if the sinner is incapable of penitence, as are devils and men who have passed out of this life, the divine wrath of God is turned upon the wicked themselves, ‘For the ungodly and his ungodliness are both alike hateful to God,’ saith the Wise Man.?,_ And how real and severe this hatred is, the devil is a witness, who once sinned and when he was the most noble angel and, as Saint Gregory testifies, the chief of the first order of angels and the most glorious of the beings created by God, yet was cast out of heaven and despoiled of all supernatural grace and glory and changed into a hideous monster and enslaved to eternal destruction. There are now and there have been in the past, many preachers of the word of God in the Church. What then is the reason, that so few are converted by the exhortations and expostulations of so many men? In truth, in great cities daily during Lent twenty, thirty, or even forty sermons are preached, and yet when Lent is over there appears to be hardly any change in the life of the city: the same vices, the same sins, the same coldness, the same dissoluteness, as before is seen. I find no other cause than this: for the most part the discourses delivered are learned and flowery, but the soul is wanting, the life is wanting, the fire is wanting: in short that great charity is wanting which alone can animate the words of the speakers and kindle and inflame and change the hearts of the hearers. And I say it is not because a persuasive voice and ease of movement is lacking to many preachers: for a machine for attacking a fortress having no ammunition might make a great noise, but would have no effect. What is to be desired is that preachers should be animated by a great love for God and the salvation of souls, and this not feignedly but truly, not strained but as it were springing up naturally from the depth of the heart.

But we may be allowed to bring forward an example of much later time. William, Duke of Aquitaine, lived in the time of Saint Bernard.
