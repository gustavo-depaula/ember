Saint Gregory Nazianzus indeed writes in his second discourse on Easter, that God placed men, as it were, a great world within a less, which is true, if. we consider the angels apart from the world; for man, considered as part of the universe, is greater than the whole world of bodies, not in size but in power: but if the angels are included in the world, as here we understand it, then man 1s a little world placed within a greater world. Now in the great world which comprises the sum total of things, there are many most wonderful qualities, but notably these —greatness, multitude, variety, efficiency, and beauty. If we consider all these matters attentively in the light which God gives us, they will have great power to raise our soul so that we shall, as it were, faint in admiration of them, and then, returning to ourselves, we shall look upon everything outside God as naught,

and shall consider it worthless. The earth certainly is large, very large, as the wise man says, ‘Who can find out the height of heaven, and the breadth of the earth, and the deep?’! Andi its greatness may also be understood from the fact that in so many thousand years that have passed since the beginning of the world, the whole surface of the earth (and this is what the wise man calls the breadth) has not yet become known to us men, even to those who carefully explore it. And if I ask what is the size of the earth compared with the circuit of the highest heaven, astronomers answer, and not inaptly, ‘ No more than a point.’ For we see that the rays of the sun reach out to the stars at the opposite end of the y firmament as though no earth existed, though indeed it lies in the midst. And if it be, as is the general opinion of learned men, that every star in the firmament is larger than the whole globe of the earth, and yet these stars appear to be very small indeed on account of the enormous distance, who can conceive the extent of the sky in which so many thousand stars are shining? If then, in regard to the surface and the depth of the earth, the wise man said, ‘ The breadth of the earth and the depth of the abyss who can measure ?’ what would he say of the exterior surface of the sky and of the depth of the whole world from the highest part of the heavens to the lowest hell ? So very great is the bulk of this corporeal world, that no mind, no thought, can grasp it. ## Chapter 2: The number of created things and the infinite perfection of the Creator

Now who shall count the number of things created by one God the Maker of heaven and earth? ‘ Who can number the sand of the sea and the drops of rain ?’ saith the wise man.!

But setting aside these very small things, how many metals are there, gold and silver, brass and lead, precious stones, gems, and pearls, both in the earth and in the sea? On the earth how many genera, species, and kinds of herbs, fruits, and plants, and how many partsineach ? Finally, ask how many stars there are in the sky? Concerning the stars we read in the Scriptures, which we know to be true, ‘ Tell the stars if thou canst number them.’3 And elsewhere it compares the number of the stars with the sand of the sea. ‘ As the host of heaven cannot be numbered, neither the sand of the sea measured,’4 which is as much as to say they are countless. ## Chapter 3: The variety of created things and God the source of all good

But although the multitude of created things is wonderful, and a proof of the multiform perfection of the one God, still more wonderful is the variety which appears in that multiplication, and it leads us more easily to the knowledge of God; for it is not difficult with one seal to make many impressions exactly alike, . or to cast numberless letters in the same mould, but to vary shapes almost infinitely, which is what God has done in creation, this is in truth a divine work and most worthy of admiration. I pass by genera and species, which every one agrees are exceeding various and diverse: in the separate forms of herbs, plants, flowers, and fruits what a great variety there is ! Shapes, colours, scent, taste, do not these vary almost infinitely ? - But what shall I say of mankind; when in a large army, consisting of an immense number of men, scarcely two are found exactly alike ? It is the same with the stars and the angels; for ‘ one star differeth from another star in glory,’ as the Apostle testifies,2 and Saint Thomas affirms that although the

Raise now, my soul, the eye of thy mind towards God in whom are the ideas of all things, and from whom, as from an inexhaustible fountain, this wellnigh infinite variety springs: for God could not have impressed those numberless forms on created things unless in a most eminent and exalted mode he had kept their ideas or patterns in the depth of his own Being. Therefore well does the Apostle exclaim, ‘O the depth of the riches of the wisdom and knowledge of God !’2 For truly fathomless must be the depth of that well, in which lie hidden the treasures of riches, of wisdom, of knowledge, able to produce such an infinite variety of things.

Well also did Saint Francis, when alinerinered by the divine light, say unto the Lord, ‘ My God and my all,’ because the. various good things which are rarely found distributed among created things are found in altogether a higher and better way united in one God.

## Chapter 5: The created beauty of things and the uncreated beauty of God

Ir remains for us to consider the beauty of created things, of which the prophet saith, ‘ For thou, Lord, hast made me glad through thy works ’;2 and indeed as all things that God hath made are good, so also will they be found beautiful, if they be rightly regarded. But leaving other things aside, we will consider those things which the opinion and judgement of all men allow to be beautiful. In truth, great is the beauty of a green field, of a well-kept garden, of a pleasant wood, of a calm sea, of serene air, of springs, of rivers, of cities, of the glittering heavens bedecked with countless stars shining like jewels. Then how delightful the beauty of a tree clothed with flowers, or laden with fruit, the forms of various four-footed creatures, the flight of birds, the play of fishes ! What shall I say of the beauty of the stars and of the moon, and still more of that very great and notable splendour of the sun, which at its rising animates the whole earth ?

But nothing delights men more than the beauty of their own form, and it is to men that we are particularly speaking. We have often seen and grieved over, intelligent men, enamoured of the beauty of women, and on the other hand, grave and honourable women led to foolishness by reason of the beauty of men, so that they put home, honour, children, and parents, life itself, and I might even say their eternal salvation, second to their love of beauty in men. Now my soul, if such beauty has been bestowed upon creatures by God, their Creator, how great and how marvellous must be the beauty of the Creator himself, the All-beautiful ! For no one can give to another what he does not possess, and if men were so charmed with the form of the sun and stars, that they mistook those luminous bodies for gods, Wisdom saith, ‘ Let them know how much better the Lord of them is, for the first author of beauty hath created them.’* But the greatness of the beauty of God is learnt not only from the fact that the beauty of all creatures is found gathered together in a far higher degree in him, but also from this, that though he is invisible to us and only known by our belief in the Scriptures, and in the mirror of created things, so long as we are wandering apart from him, yet many of the saints have been so ardent in their love of him that some even hid themselves in desert places and gave themselves up to the contemplation of him alone: such were Saint Mary Magdalene, Paul the first hermit, Anthony the Great, and others without number, concerning whom Theodoret’s religious history may be consulted ; others, leaving wives and children and whatever possessions they had in the world, chose to live in monasteries, subject to the will of others, that they might enjoy the friendship of God; others again desired to spend their life freely in sharp pain, that they might be worthy to attain to the vision of his infinite beauty. If then the divine beauty, not yet seen, but only believed and hoped for, stirreth up such a great fire of longing, what will it do when the veil is removed, and it shall be seen as it is in itself? This will happen, that, drinking of his pleasures as out of a river,! we shall neither wish to, nor shall we be able to, turn our eyes away from it for one moment.
