# Meditation for Tuesday

On this day thou shalt think upon the miseries of human life, that from them thou mayest see how vain is the glory of the world, and how worthy of being held in contempt it is, seeing that it rests upon so weak a foundation as this poor human life. And, although the defects and miseries of this life are well-nigh innumerable, thou shalt now take but seven of them for thy especial consideration.

Consider, first, how short this life is ; for its longest span is but seventy or eighty years, and then there is but labour and sorrow in it. And if from this then is taken the period of infancy, when life is little more than merely animal existence ; or the time which is spent in sleep, when our senses and our reason are at rest, we shall find it even shorter than it seemed. If, besides all this, thou comparest it with the eternity of the life to come, it will appear to them to be but a pin’s point. See then from this how insane they are who, for the enjoyment of a breath of life so short, bring upon themselves the loss of that rest which is to be for ever.

Consider, secondly, how uncertain is this life—another defect in addition to those spoken of, seeing that not only is it so short, but this very shortness is not sure, but doubtful. For how many are there who come to those seventy or eighty years we have spoken of? To how many is the thread cut short in the very beginning of Mentat Prayer. 17 of its spinning? How many pass away in the flower of their youth? “Ye know not,” says our Saviour, “when” your Lord “cometh, whether at even, or at midnight, or at cockcrowing, or in the morning.”

For the better understanding of this, remind thyself of the many persons thou hast known in the world, especially amongst thy friends and acquaintances, and of persons illustrious and distinguished, to whom, at divers ages, death leapt down, and at one swoop dismissed in confusion all their plans and hopes.

Think, thirdly, how fragile and brittle is this life, and thou shalt find that there is no glass vase so delicate ; how a breath of wind, the sun, a cup of cold water ‘is enough to rob us of it, as is evident in the daily experience of many to whom some such occasion as I have spoken of is sufficient to carry them off in the very flower of their age.

And, lastly, consider what are called the changes of fortune which never abides for long, or in the same stage of prosperity, to gladden the affairs of this life, but is ever moving backwards and forwards. Beside all this consider how continuous must be the movements of this life, since neither day nor night abide with us—but are ever, and rightly so, losing themselves. Then, what is our life but a candle, which is ever wasting, and is wasting the more, the more it burns and is shining? As said patient Job, What is our life but a flower which in the morning opens, and at mid-day withereth, and in the evening is cut down.’ So, too, by reason of this continual changing, saith God by the mouth of Isaiah, “ All flesh is grass, and all the glory of it as the flower of the field.”? Fifthly, consider how deceitful life is, which is perhaps its worst feature. It deceives so many, and draws after it so many, and so blind, lovers of it; for it appears beautiful when it is ugly, and sweet when it is bitter, long when it is short to oneself, and happy when miserable. There is no danger or trouble to which men will not commit themselves for its sake, even though it should be with the loss of the life everlasting.

Consider, sixthly, that besides being so short, as we have said, this short span of life is subject to so many miseries, both of soul and body, that it is nothing else than a vale of tears, a sea of unending troubles. S. Jerome writes that Xerxes, that allpowerful king, who levelled the mountains, and overcame the seas, having ascended a hill, that from thence he might inspect the army of so many nations under his command, is said to have burst into tears. And when asked why he wept, he answered, “I weep because in so few years hence, not one will remain of all those whom I now see.” “Oh,” says S. Jerome upon this—“ Oh! that we might ascend some mighty tower, and see thence the whole earth below at our feet! Then wouldst thou see the falls and miseries of the whole world, nations destroyed by nations, kingdoms by kingdoms. Then wouldst thou see how some torment, and others slay ; how some are drowned in the sea, and others taken captive. Here thou wouldst see wedding festivites, there mourning; here some born, there some dying ; some abounding in wealth, others begging.
