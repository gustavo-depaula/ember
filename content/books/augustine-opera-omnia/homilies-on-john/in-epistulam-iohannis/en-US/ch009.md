# Tractate 8: No one has seen God

## (1 John 4:12–16).

> *Let love always abide in a humble heart. It seeks to make the wretched happy, the enemy a brother, the unlearned learned. Submit yourself to him who is above you and who sought you without cost.*

## He who praises God by his conduct can do so always.

**1.** Love is a sweet word, but a sweeter deed. We cannot always speak of it. For we do many things, and various activities distract us, so that our tongue is not free always to speak of love: yet our tongue could do nothing better. But that of which we are not permitted always to speak, we are permitted always to guard. Just as now, what we sing, Alleluia, do we always do this? It is scarcely for a small part, not the whole span, of one hour that we sing Alleluia; and we are free for something else. Now Alleluia, as you already know, means Praise God. He who praises God with his tongue cannot do so always: he who praises God by his conduct can do so always. The works of mercy, the affection of love, the holiness of piety, the incorruption of chastity, the modesty of sobriety—these must always be held: whether we are in public, or at home, whether before men, or in our chamber, whether speaking, or silent, whether doing something, or at leisure; these must always be held; for all these virtues that I have named are within.

But who is able to name them all? It is like the army of a commander who sits within in your mind. For just as a commander acts through his army to do whatever pleases him, so the Lord Jesus Christ, beginning to dwell in our inner man, that is, in the mind through faith[^1], uses these virtues as his ministers.

And through these virtues, which cannot be seen with the eyes, and yet, when they are named, are praised—but they would not be praised unless they were loved, they would not be loved unless they were seen; and if indeed they would not be loved unless they were seen, they are seen with another eye, that is, with the inner gaze of the heart—through these invisible virtues the members are moved visibly: the feet to walk; but where?

Wherever the good will moves them, the will that serves as a soldier under the good commander. The hands to work; but what? What love commands, the love that has been inspired within by the Holy Spirit. The members, then, are seen when they are moved; he who commands within is not seen. And who commands within is known almost to him alone who commands, and to that one within who is commanded.

## Let him be praised in you who works through you.

**2.** For, brethren, you heard just now, when the Gospel was being read; surely if you had there not only the ear of the body but also of the heart. What does he say? *Beware of doing your righteousness before men, in order to be seen by them*[^2].

Did he mean to say that whatever good we do, we should hide it from the eyes of men, and be afraid to be seen? If you fear onlookers, you will have no imitators: you ought, then, to be seen. But you ought not to do it for this end, that you may be seen. The end of your joy ought not to be there, the limit of your gladness ought not to be there, that you should think you have obtained the whole fruit of your good work when you have been seen and praised.

This is nothing. Despise yourself when you are praised: let him be praised in you who works through you. Do not, then, do the good you do for your own praise, but for the praise of him from whom you have it that you do good. From yourself you have to act badly, from God you have to act well. On the contrary, see how perverse and backward wicked men are.

What they do well, they want to attribute to themselves: if they do badly, they want to accuse God. Turn this twisted and backward thing—whatever it is—around, making it, as it were, head downward: what is above, making it below; what is below, making it above.

Do you want to make God below, and yourself above? You are cast down headlong, not lifted up: for he is always above. What then? You do well, and God does badly?

On the contrary, say this, if you want to speak more truly: I do badly, he does well; and what I do well, is done well by him: for whatever I do badly is from myself. This confession strengthens the heart, and makes the foundation of love.

For if we ought to hide our good works, lest they be seen by men, where is that saying of the Lord in the sermon he gave on the mountain? Where he said this, there too he said this a little before: *Let your good works shine before men.* And he did not stop there, he did not make an end there; but he added: *And let them glorify your Father who is in heaven*[^3].

And what does the Apostle say? *I was unknown by face to the churches of Judea that are in Christ: but they had only heard that he who once persecuted us now preaches the faith that he once ravaged; and in me they glorified God*[^4].

See how he too, because he became known in this way, set the end not in his own praise but in the praise of God. And as far as it concerns himself, a ravager of the Church, an envious persecutor, malicious, he himself confesses it, we do not revile him. Paul loves to have his sins told by us, so that he may be glorified who healed such a sickness. For the physician's hand cut the greatness of the wound, and healed it. That voice from heaven laid low the persecutor and raised up the preacher; it killed Saul and gave life to Paul[^5]. For Saul was the persecutor of a holy man[^6];

from this he had his name when he was persecuting Christians: afterward from Saul he became Paul[^7]. What is Paul? Little. So when Saul, proud, exalted: when Paul, humble, little. This is why we speak thus: I will see you a little after (paulo post), that is, after a little while. Hear that he became little: *For I am the least of the Apostles*[^8]; and: *To me, the least of all the saints*[^9]*,* he says in another place. So he was among the Apostles like the fringe of a garment: but the Church of the Gentiles touched him as the woman suffering a flow of blood did, and was healed[^10].

## Let love within not be interrupted.

**3.** So, brethren, this I have said, this I say, this I would not keep silent if I could: let those works be in you at one time, those at another, according to the season, according to the hours, according to the days.

Are we to speak always? Are we to be silent always? Are we always to refresh the body? Are we always to fast? Are we always to give bread to the needy? Are we always to clothe the naked? Are we always to visit the sick? Are we always to reconcile those at odds? Are we always to bury the dead? Now this, now that. These things are begun, and they cease: but that commander is neither begun nor ought to cease. Let love within not be interrupted: let the offices of love be displayed according to the season. *Let* love, then, as it is written, *brotherly love abide*[^11].

## He who reaches even to his enemies does not leap over his brethren.

**4.** But perhaps it has troubled some of you, ever since we began to expound this Epistle of blessed John to you, why he has commended above all nothing but brotherly love. *He who loves his brother*[^12], he says; and: *A commandment has been given to us, that we love one another*[^13]. He constantly named brotherly love: but the love of God, that is, by which we ought to love God, he did not name so constantly; yet he did not keep wholly silent about it. But of the love of an enemy he kept altogether silent through almost the whole of this Epistle.

Although he vehemently preaches and commends love to us, he does not tell us to love our enemies; but he tells us to love our brethren. But just now, when the Gospel was being read, we heard: *For if you love those who love you, what reward will you have? Do not even the tax collectors do this*?[^14]

What is it, then, that John the apostle commends to us as something great, a brotherly love directed toward a certain perfection; but the Lord says that it is not enough for us to love our brethren, but that we ought to extend this love so as to reach our enemies?

He who reaches even to his enemies does not leap over his brethren. It is necessary, like fire, that it first take hold of what is nearest, and so stretch itself out to what is farther off. Your brother is nearer to you than some unknown man. Again, the one you did not know, who nevertheless does not oppose you, clings to you more closely than an enemy who actually opposes you. Extend your love to those near you, and do not call that an extension. For you love almost yourself when you love those who cling to you. Extend it to the unknown, who have done you no harm. Pass beyond even these; reach the point of loving your enemies. This certainly the Lord commands. Why did this man keep silent about love of an enemy?

## You love a happy man more genuinely.

**5.** Every love—whether the love that is called carnal, which is usually called not "dilectio" but rather "amor" (for the word "dilectio" is more often used of better things, and taken in a better sense)—yet every love, dearest brethren, certainly has a kind of goodwill toward those who are loved.

For we ought not to love men in such a way, nor can we so love or cherish them; for the Lord used this word too when he said: *Peter, do you love me*?[^15] we ought not to love men in the way we hear gluttons say: I love thrushes. You ask why? In order to kill and consume them.

He says he loves them, and he loves them to this end, that they should not be, he loves them to this end, that he may destroy them. And whatever we love for food, we love it to this end, that it may be consumed and we refreshed. Are men to be loved in this way, as if to be consumed?

But there is a kind of friendship of goodwill, by which we sometimes do a service to those we love. What if there is nothing we can do for them? Goodwill alone suffices for the one who loves.

For we ought not to wish people to be wretched, so that we may be able to exercise the works of mercy. You give bread to the hungry: but it would be better if no one were hungry and you gave to no one. You clothe the naked: would that all were clothed, and that this necessity did not exist! You bury the dead: would that the time would come at last when no one dies!

You reconcile those who quarrel: would that there might at last be that eternal peace of Jerusalem, where no one is at odds! For all these are offices required by needs. Take away the wretched; the works of mercy will cease. The works of mercy will cease; will the ardor of love be extinguished?

You love a happy man more genuinely, one for whom you have nothing to do; that love will be purer and far more sincere. For if you do a service to a wretched man, perhaps you desire to exalt yourself over him, and you want him to be subject to you, since you are the author of his benefit.

He was in need, you bestowed something; you seem, as it were, greater because you gave the service than he to whom the service was given. Wish him your equal, so that both of you may be under the One to whom nothing can be given.

## Submit to him who is above you, and the things over which you are set will be beneath you.

**6.** For in this the proud soul went beyond measure, and in a way was avaricious; for *avarice is the root of all evils*[^16]. And again it was said: *Pride is the beginning of all sin*[^17]. And we sometimes ask how these two sayings agree with each other: *Avarice is the root of all evils*; and: *Pride is the beginning of all sin.* If pride is the beginning of all sin, then pride is the root of all evils. Certainly avarice is the root of all evils: we find that there is avarice in pride too; for the man went beyond measure.

What is it to be avaricious? To go further than what suffices. Adam fell through pride: *Pride is the beginning of all sin,* it says. Was it not avarice? What is more avaricious than the one for whom God could not suffice? So, brethren, we read how man was made to the image and likeness of God: and what did God say of him? *And let him have power over the fish of the sea, and the birds of the air, and all the cattle that creep upon the earth*[^18].

Did he say: Let him have power over men? *Let him have power,* he says: he gave a natural power. Over what should he have power? *Over the fish of the sea, the birds of the air, and all creeping things that creep upon the earth.* Why is this the natural power of man over these things? Because man has power from the source from which he was made to the image of God. But where was he made to the image of God?

In the understanding, in the mind, in the inner man; in that by which he understands truth, judges between justice and injustice, knows by whom he was made, can understand his creator, praise his creator. He has this understanding who has prudence.

This is why, when many were wearing away the image of God in themselves through evil desires, and were in a way extinguishing the very flame of understanding by the perversity of their conduct, Scripture cried out to them: *Do not become like the horse and the mule, which have no understanding*[^19].

This is to say: I have set you above the horse and the mule; I made you to my image, I gave you power over these things. Why? Because the beasts do not have a rational mind: but you grasp truth with a rational mind, you understand what is above you; submit to him who is above you, and the things over which you are set will be beneath you. But because through sin man forsook the one under whom he ought to have been, he is made subject to those above which he ought to have been.

## God did not deliver some men visibly, yet he delivered everyone spiritually.

**7.** Attend to what I say: God, man, beasts: for example, above you God; below you the beasts. Acknowledge him who is above you, so that those things which are below you may acknowledge you. And therefore, when Daniel had acknowledged God above himself, the lions acknowledged him above themselves[^20].

But if you do not acknowledge him who is above you, you despise your superior, and you are made subject to your inferior. This is why the pride of the Egyptians—by what was it tamed? By frogs and flies[^21]. God could have sent lions too, but some great person has to be terrified by a lion. The prouder they were, the more their evil neck was broken by contemptible and abject things. But the lions acknowledged Daniel, because he was subject to God.

What then? Were the martyrs who fought with beasts, and were torn by the bites of wild animals, not under God? Or were three men servants of God, and were the Maccabees not servants of God? The fire acknowledged the three men, servants of God, whom it did not burn, whose garments it did not even harm[^22]; and did it not acknowledge the Maccabees? It acknowledged the Maccabees; it acknowledged these too, brethren[^23].

But there was need of a certain scourge, by the permission of the Lord, who said in Scripture: *He scourges every son whom he receives*[^24]. For do you think, brethren, that the iron would have pierced the body of the Lord, unless he himself permitted it; or that he would have hung on the wood, unless he himself had willed it? Did his own creature not acknowledge him? Or did he set forth an example of patience for his faithful? Therefore God delivered some visibly, and did not deliver some visibly: yet he delivered all spiritually, he forsook no one spiritually.

Visibly he seemed to have forsaken some, he seemed to have rescued some. He rescued some so that you would not think he was unable to rescue. He gave testimony that he is able, so that where he does not do it, you may understand a more hidden will and not suspect a difficulty. But what then, brethren? When we have escaped all these snares of mortality, when the times of temptation have passed, when the river of this world has run its course and we have received that first robe, that immortality which we lost by sinning, when this corruptible thing has put on incorruption, that is, when this flesh has put on incorruption, and this mortal thing has put on immortality[^25]; then, as now perfect children of God, where there is no need to be tempted or scourged, all creation will acknowledge us: all things will be subject to us, if here we are subject to God.

## If you want always to have a learner, you will be an envious teacher.

**8.** So a Christian ought to be such that he does not glory over other men. For God gave you to be above the beasts, that is, to be better than the beasts. This you have by nature; you will always be better than a beast.

If you want to be better than another man, you will envy him when you see him equal to you. You ought to wish all men to be equal to you; and if you have surpassed someone in prudence, you ought to wish that he too may be prudent. As long as he is slow, he learns from you; as long as he is unlearned, he has need of you; and you seem the teacher, he the learner: you, then, are superior, because you are the teacher; he inferior, because he is the learner.

Unless you wish him your equal, you want always to have a learner. But if you want always to have a learner, you will be an envious teacher. If an envious teacher, how will you be a teacher? I beg you, do not teach him your own envy. Hear the Apostle speaking from the heart of love: *I would that all men were as I myself am*[^26]. How did he want all to be equal? He was superior to all for this reason, that out of love he wished all to be equal. So the man went beyond measure; he wanted to be more avaricious, so that he who was made above the beasts might be above men: and this is pride itself.

## See with what mind you act.

**9.** And see how many works pride does; set it in your heart how similar are the things it does, as it were equal to love. Love feeds the hungry, and so does pride—love, that God may be praised; pride, that it may itself be praised. Love clothes the naked, and so does pride; love fasts, and so does pride; love buries the dead, and so does pride. All the good works that love wishes to do and does, pride drives against it, and as it were drives its own horses. But love is interior: it takes the place of pride badly driven; not of one driving badly, but of one driven badly. Woe to the man whose charioteer is pride, for he must needs go headlong.

But that there be no pride driving the good deeds—who knows it? Who sees it? Where is this? We see the works: mercy feeds, and pride feeds; mercy receives the guest, and pride receives the guest; mercy intercedes for the poor, and so does pride. What is this? In the works we do not distinguish them. I dare to say something, but not I; Paul said it: love dies, that is, a man having love confesses the name of Christ, undergoes martyrdom; pride also confesses, and also undergoes martyrdom. The one has love, the other does not have love. But let the one who does not have love hear from the Apostle: *If I distribute all my goods to the poor, and if I hand over my body to be burned, but have not love, it profits me nothing*[^27].

So the divine Scripture calls us back within, from the display of this outward face; and from that surface which is displayed before men, it calls us back within. Return to your conscience, question it. Do not attend to what flowers outside, but to what the root is in the earth. Is cupidity rooted there? There can be the appearance of good deeds, but they cannot truly be good works. Is love rooted there? Be secure, nothing evil can come forth.

The proud man caresses, love rages. The one clothes, the other beats. For the one clothes in order to please men: the other beats in order to correct by discipline. The blow of love is received better than the alms of pride. Return, then, within, brethren; and in all things whatsoever you do, look to God as witness. See, if he sees, with what mind you act. If your heart does not accuse you, that you act for the sake of display; it is well, be secure.

But do not be afraid, when you do well, that another may see. Fear lest you do it for this end, that you may be praised: but let another see, so that God may be praised. For if you hide it from the eyes of man, you hide it from the imitation of man, you withdraw praise from God.

There are two to whom you give alms: two are hungry; one for bread, the other for righteousness. Between these two hungry ones, since it was said: *Blessed are those who hunger and thirst for righteousness, for they shall be satisfied*[^28]: between these two hungry ones, you have been set as a good worker;

if love works in him, it pities both, it wishes to help both. For the one seeks what he may eat, the other seeks what he may imitate. You feed the one, offer yourself to the other; you have given alms to both: the one you have made grateful for hunger destroyed; the other you have made an imitator by the example set before him.

## Love your enemy, that he may be your brother.

**10.** Have mercy, then, as those who are merciful; because in the very fact that you love your enemies, you love your brethren. Do not think that John has commanded nothing about love of an enemy; for he did not keep silent about brotherly love: you love your brethren.

How, you ask, do we love our brethren? I ask why you love your enemy: why do you love him? That he may be healthy in this life? What if it is not expedient for him? That he may be rich? What if he will be blinded by those very riches? That he may take a wife? What if he will suffer a bitter life from it? That he may have children? What if they will be wicked?

So these things that you seem to wish for your enemy, because you love him, are uncertain; they are uncertain. Wish for him that he may have eternal life with you; wish for him that he may be your brother. If, then, you wish this, in loving your enemy, that he may be your brother; when you love him, you love a brother. For you do not love in him what he is; but what you wish him to be.

I once said to your Charity, if I am not mistaken: A beam of wood is set before the eyes; the best craftsman saw the wood unhewn, cut from the forest, and conceived a love for it: he wants to make something of it. For he did not love it for this, that it should always remain so. In his art he saw what it is to be, not in his love what it is; and he loved what he is going to make of it, not that which it is.

So God too loved us sinners. We say that God loved sinners: for he says: *The healthy have no need of a physician, but those who are sick*[^29]. Did he love sinners for this, that we should remain sinners? Like wood from the forest, the craftsman saw us, and thought of the building he is going to make of it, not the forest that it was.

So you too look upon your enemy opposing, raging, biting with words, exasperating with insults, harassing with hatreds; you notice there that he is a man. You see all these hostile things done by the man; and you see in him that he was made by God. But the fact that he was made man, he was made by God.

But the fact that he hates you, he himself made; the fact that he envies, he himself made. And what do you say in your soul? Lord, be merciful to him, forgive him his sins; strike him with terror, change him. You do not love in him what he is, but what you wish him to be. So when you love an enemy, you love a brother.

Therefore perfect love is the love of an enemy: which perfect love exists in brotherly love. And let no one say that John the apostle warned us of something less, and the Lord Christ warned us of more: John warned us to love our brethren; Christ admonished us to love even our enemies[^30]. Attend to why Christ warned you to love your enemies. Was it that they should always remain enemies? If he warned you to this end, that they should remain enemies, you hate, you do not love.

Attend to how he himself loved, that is, because he did not want the persecutors to remain so; he says: *Father, forgive them, for they know not what they do*[^31]. Those whom he wished to be forgiven, he wished to be changed: those whom he wished to be changed, he deigned to make brethren out of enemies, and truly he did so.

He was killed, he was buried, he rose again, he ascended into heaven, he sent the Holy Spirit to the disciples; they began to preach his name with confidence, they were working miracles in the name of the crucified and slain one: those slayers of the Lord saw them; and they who in their raging shed his blood, by believing drank it.

## God takes care of you with respect to your enemy: wish that he too may be healed.

**11.** These things I have said, brethren, and at some length: yet because love itself had to be commended rather vehemently to your Charity, it had to be commended in this way. For if there is no love in you, we have said nothing. But if it is in you, we have, as it were, added oil to the flames; and in the one in whom it was not, perhaps it has been kindled by the words.

In one, what was there grew; in another, what was not there began to be. To this end, then, we have said these things, that you may not be sluggish to love your enemies. Does a man rage against you? He rages, you pray; he hates, you have mercy. It is the fever of his own soul that hates you: he will be healthy, and he will give you thanks.

How do physicians love the sick? Do they love them as sick? If they love them as sick, they want them always to be sick. They love the sick for this end, not that they should remain sick, but that from being sick they may become healthy.

And how much do they often suffer from delirious patients? What sort of verbal abuse? Often they are even struck. The physician pursues the fever, he pardons the man: and what shall I say, brethren? Does he love his enemy? On the contrary, he hates his enemy, the disease; for that is what he hates, and he loves the man by whom he is struck: he hates the fever. For by what is he struck? By the disease, by the sickness, by the fever. He removes that which is hostile to the man, so that there may remain the one over whom he may rejoice.

So you too: if your enemy hates you, and hates you unjustly; know that the cupidity of the world reigns in him, and that this is why he hates you. If you too have hated him, you in turn render evil for evil. What does rendering evil for evil accomplish? I was weeping for one sick man, who hated you; now I mourn two, if you too have hated.

But he attacks your property; he takes from you some things or other that you have on earth: so you have hated him, because he straitens you on earth. Do not suffer straits, migrate up to heaven: there you will have your heart where there is breadth, so that you suffer no straits in the hope of eternal life.

Notice what he takes from you: he would not even take these things from you, unless he permitted it who *scourges every son whom he receives*[^32]. In a way your enemy himself is the instrument of God by which you may be healed. If God knows it is useful for you to be stripped, he permits him; if he knows it is useful for you to be beaten, he permits him to beat you: through him he takes care of you; wish that he too may be healed.

## Have you begun to love? Then God has begun to dwell in you.

**12.** *No one has ever seen God.* See, beloved: *If we love one another, God will abide in us, and his love will be perfected in us.* Begin to love, and you will be perfected. Have you begun to love? Then God has begun to dwell in you; love him who has begun to dwell in you, so that by dwelling more perfectly he may make you perfect. *In this we know that we abide in him, and he in us, because he has given us of his Spirit*[^33]*.* Good, thanks be to God. We know that he dwells in us. And this very thing, how do we know it, that we have come to know that he dwells in us? Because John himself said this: *Because he has given us of his Spirit.* How do we know *that he has given us of his Spirit*? This very thing, that he has given you of his Spirit, how do you know it? Question your heart: if it is full of love, you have the Spirit of God. How do we know that from this you know the Spirit of God dwells in you? Question the apostle Paul: *Because the love of God has been poured out in our hearts through the Holy Spirit who has been given to us*[^34].

## Such a physician has come, and do you despair?

**13.** *And we have seen, and we are witnesses, that the Father sent his Son the Savior of the world.* Be secure, you who are sick: such a physician has come, and do you despair? Great were the diseases, incurable were the wounds, the sickness was desperate. Do you attend to the greatness of your evil, and not attend to the omnipotence of the physician?

You are in a desperate state; but he is omnipotent: of whom these are witnesses, who were first healed, and who announce the physician; and yet they themselves were healed more in hope than in fact. For so the Apostle says: *For in hope we have been saved*[^35]. We have begun, then, to be healed in faith: but our salvation will be perfected when this corruptible thing has put on incorruption, and this mortal thing has put on immortality[^36]. This is hope, not yet the fact. But the one who rejoices in hope will hold the fact too: but the one who does not have hope will not be able to reach the fact.

## He asks nothing from us, and he sought us.

**14.** *Whoever confesses that Jesus is the Son of God, God abides in him, and he in God.* Now let us not say to many: *Whoever confesses,* not in word, but in deed; not with the tongue, but with his life. For many confess with words, but deny with deeds. *And we have come to know, and have believed, what love God has in us.* And again, how have you come to know it? *God is love.*

He already said that above; see, he says it again. Love could not be commended to you more than by saying it is God. Perhaps you were going to despise the gift of God. And do you despise God? *God is love. And he who abides in love abides in God, and God abides in him*[^37]*.* They dwell in each other in turn, the one who contains and the one who is contained.

You dwell in God, but so as to be contained: God dwells in you, but so as to contain you, lest you fall. Do not perhaps suppose that you become the house of God in the way your house carries your flesh: if the house in which you are withdraws itself, you fall; but if you withdraw yourself, God does not fall.

He is whole when you forsake him; whole when you return to him. You are healed, you bestow nothing on him; you are cleansed, you are refreshed, you are corrected. He is a medicine to the unhealthy, a rule to the crooked, light to the darkened, a dwelling to the abandoned. All things, then, are conferred on you.

See that you do not suppose anything is conferred on God when you come to him; not even a servant. So will God not have servants, if you are unwilling, and if all are unwilling? God does not need servants, but servants need God: therefore the Psalm says: *I said to the Lord: You are my God.*

He is the true Lord. And what does it say? *For you have no need of my goods*[^38]. You have need of the good of your servant. The servant needs your good, that you may feed him: you too have need of the good of your servant, that he may help you. You cannot draw water for yourself, you cannot cook for yourself, you cannot run before your own horse, you cannot tend your own beast.

You see that you have need of the good of your servant, you have need of his service. So you are not a true lord, since you have need of an inferior. He is the true Lord who seeks nothing from us; and woe to us if we do not seek him. He seeks nothing from us; and he sought us, when we were not seeking him. One sheep had strayed; he found it, and rejoicing carried it back on his shoulders[^39]. And was the sheep necessary to the shepherd, rather than the shepherd necessary to the sheep?

The more gladly I speak of love, the less I want this Epistle to be finished. There is none more ardent in commending love. Nothing sweeter is preached to you, nothing more wholesome is drunk: provided that by living well you confirm in yourselves the gift of God. Do not be ungrateful for so great a grace of his, who, although he had an Only Son, did not want him to be one alone; but, that he might have brethren, he adopted to him those who would possess eternal life together with him.
