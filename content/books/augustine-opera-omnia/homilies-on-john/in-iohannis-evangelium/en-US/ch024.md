# Homily 24

## Jesus took the loaves, and when he had given thanks, he distributed them to those reclining (Jn 6:1-14).

Let us ask the miracles what they say to us about Christ; for if they are understood, they have their own tongue. For because Christ himself is the Word of God, even a deed of the Word is a word to us. This miracle, then—as we have heard how great it is, let us also ask how deep it is. It has something within, this thing that we marvel at without. Raised up to faith, and cleansed through faith.

**1.** The miracles which our Lord Jesus Christ did are indeed divine works, and from things visible they admonish the human mind to understand God. For because he is not such a substance as can be seen with the eyes, and his miracles by which he governs the whole world and administers the whole creation have grown cheap by their constancy—so that almost no one deigns to attend to the marvelous and astonishing works of God in any single grain of seed—according to his own mercy he reserved to himself certain things which he would do at an opportune time apart from the usual course and order of nature, so that those to whom daily things had grown cheap might be astonished at seeing things not greater, but unusual.

For the governance of the whole world is a greater miracle than the feeding of five thousand men from five loaves[^1]: and yet no one marvels at this; men marvel at that, not because it is greater, but because it is rare. For who even now feeds the whole world, but he who from a few grains creates the crops? He acted, then, as God. For from where he multiplies the crops from a few grains, from there he multiplied in his hands the five loaves. For there was power in the hands of Christ: and those five loaves were as it were seeds, not indeed committed to the earth, but multiplied by him who made the earth.

This, then, was brought to the senses, by which the mind might be raised, and exhibited to the eyes, where the understanding might be exercised, that we might marvel at the invisible God through visible works, and, raised up to faith and cleansed through faith, might desire to see invisibly even him whom from visible things we knew to be invisible.

**2.** Yet it is not enough to gaze at these things in the miracles of Christ. Let us ask the miracles themselves what they say to us about Christ: for if they are understood, they have their own tongue. For because Christ himself is the Word of God, even a deed of the Word is a word to us. This miracle, then—as we have heard how great it is, let us also ask how deep it is: let us not only be delighted by its surface, but also search out its depth. For it has something within, this thing that we marvel at without.

We have seen, we have beheld something great, something splendid, and altogether divine, which could not be done except by God: we have praised the maker on account of the deed. But just as, if we somewhere inspected beautiful letters, it would not be enough for us to praise the writer's hand, because he made them even, equal, and comely, unless we also read what he indicated to us through them: so he who only inspects this deed is delighted by the beauty of the deed so as to admire the craftsman; but he who understands, as it were reads. For a painting is seen in one way, letters are seen in another. When you have seen a painting, this is the whole of having seen, of having praised it: when you have seen letters, this is not the whole; because you are prompted to read as well.

For indeed you say, when you have seen letters, if perhaps you do not know how to read them: What do we think is written here? You ask what it is, when you already see something. He from whom you seek to recognize what you have seen is going to show you something else. He has different eyes, you have different ones. Do you not see the strokes alike? But you do not recognize the signs alike. You, then, see and praise; he sees, praises, reads, and understands. Because, then, we have seen, because we have praised, let us read and understand.

**3.** The Lord on a mountain: let us much rather understand that the Lord on the mountain is the Word on high. Accordingly, what was done on the mountain does not as it were lie low; nor is it to be passed over in passing, but to be looked up to.

He saw the crowds, recognized that they were hungry, mercifully fed them: not only out of goodness, but also out of power. For what would goodness alone profit, where there was no bread from which the hungry crowd might be fed? Unless power were present to goodness, that fasting and hungry crowd would have remained so. In short, the disciples too, who were with the Lord in the hunger, themselves also wished to feed the crowds, that they might not remain empty, but they had nothing from which to feed them. The Lord asked from where loaves might be bought to feed the crowds. And Scripture says: *This he said to test him*: namely the disciple Philip, whom he had asked. *For he himself knew what he was about to do*[^2].

For what good, then, was he testing him, except that he was showing the disciple's ignorance? And perhaps in showing the disciple's ignorance he signified something. It will appear, then, when the sacrament of the five loaves begins to speak to us, and to indicate what it signifies: for there we shall see why the Lord in this deed willed to show the disciple's ignorance by asking what he knew. For sometimes we ask what we do not know, wishing to hear that we may learn; sometimes we ask what we know, wishing to know whether the one we ask knows it too: both of these the Lord knew; both what he asked he knew, for he himself knew what he was about to do; and likewise he knew that Philip did not know this. Why, then, did he ask, except that he was showing his ignorance? And why he did this, as I said, we shall understand later.

**4.** Andrew says: *There is here a certain boy who has five barley loaves and two fish; but what are these among so many?*[^3] When Philip, on being asked, had said that loaves worth two hundred denarii would not be enough for so great a crowd to be refreshed, there was there a certain boy carrying five barley loaves and two fish. And Jesus said, *Make the men recline*. Now there was much grass there, and about five thousand men reclined. And the Lord Jesus took the loaves, gave thanks, gave the order, the loaves were broken, set before those reclining. No longer five loaves, but what he had added, who had created what was increased. And of the fish as much as was enough. It is a small thing that that crowd was satisfied; even fragments remained over: and these were ordered to be gathered, lest they perish; and they filled twelve baskets of fragments[^4].

The five loaves are the five books of Moses. 5. That we may run through it briefly, the five loaves are understood as the five books of Moses: rightly not of wheat, but of barley; because they belong to the Old Testament. Now you know that barley is so formed that its core is reached only with difficulty: for that same core is clothed with a covering of chaff, and the chaff itself is tenacious and clinging, so that it is stripped off only with labor. Such is the letter of the Old Testament, clothed with the coverings of carnal mysteries: but if its core is reached, it feeds and satisfies.

A certain boy, then, was carrying five loaves and two fish. If we ask who this boy was, perhaps it was the people of Israel: with a childish understanding it carried, but did not eat. For the things it carried, when closed up, burdened it; when opened, fed it.

But the two fish seem to us to signify those two lofty persons in the Old Testament, who were anointed for sanctifying and ruling the people, that of the priest and the king. And he himself came at last in mystery, who was signified through them: he came at last who was shown through the core of the barley, but hidden through the chaff of the barley. He came, one, bearing both persons in himself, that of priest and king: of priest through the victim, which he offered as himself to God for us; of king, because we are ruled by him: and the things that were carried closed up are opened.

Thanks to him; he fulfilled through himself what was promised through the Old Testament. And he ordered the loaves to be broken: by breaking they were multiplied. Nothing is truer. For those five books of Moses—how many books did they make, when they are expounded, as it were by breaking, that is, by discussing? But because in that barley the ignorance of the former people was covered, of which former people it was said, *As long as Moses is read, a veil is placed over their hearts*[^5] (for the veil had not yet been taken away, because Christ had not yet come; the veil of the temple had not yet been torn while he hung on the cross): because, then, the people's ignorance was in the Law, on that account that testing by the Lord was showing the disciple's ignorance.

The core of the barley must be reached. 6. Nothing, therefore, is idle, everything signifies something, but they require an understander: for this number too of the people fed signified the people set under the Law. For why were there five thousand, except that they were under the Law, which Law is unfolded in the five books of Moses? For which reason the sick too were laid out in those five porticoes, but were not healed[^6]. But he healed the sick man there, who here too fed the crowds from five loaves. For they reclined upon the grass too[^7]: therefore they thought carnally, and rested in carnal things. *For all flesh is grass*[^8].

But what are those fragments, except those which the people could not eat? There are understood, then, certain more secret things of understanding, which the multitude cannot grasp. What remains, then, except that the more secret things of understanding, which the multitude cannot grasp, be entrusted to those who are fit to teach others as well, as the Apostles were? For which reason twelve baskets were filled.

This was done both wonderfully, because it was a great deed; and usefully, because it was a spiritual deed. Those who saw it then were amazed: but we do not marvel when we hear of it. For it was done that they might see, but written that we might hear. What the eyes availed in them, faith avails in us. For we discern with the mind what we could not with the eyes: and we have been preferred to them, since it was said of us, *Blessed are those who do not see, and believe*[^9]. And I add that perhaps we have also understood what that crowd did not understand. And truly we have been fed, we who were able to reach the core of the barley.

## We have merited to have as our prophet the very Word of God.

**7.** In short, what did those men who saw this think? *Those men, when they had seen the sign that he had done, said: This is truly the prophet*[^10]. Perhaps they still thought Christ a prophet on this account, that they had reclined upon the grass. But he was the Lord of the Prophets, the fulfiller of the Prophets, the sanctifier of the Prophets, but a prophet too: for it was said to Moses, *I will raise up for them a prophet like you*[^11]. Like, according to the flesh, not according to majesty. And that this promise of the Lord is to be understood of Christ himself is plainly expounded and read in the Acts of the Apostles[^12]. And the Lord himself says of himself, *A prophet is not without honor, except in his own country*[^13]. The Lord is a prophet, and the Lord is the Word of God, and no prophet prophesies without the Word of God: the Word of God is with the Prophets, and the Word of God is a prophet.

The earlier times merited Prophets inspired and filled with the Word of God: we have merited to have as our prophet the very Word of God. But Christ is a prophet, the Lord of the Prophets, in the same way as Christ is an angel, the Lord of the Angels. For he too was called *the angel of great counsel*[^14]. Nevertheless, what does the prophet say elsewhere? That *not a legate nor an angel, but he himself, coming, will save them*[^15]: that is, to save them he will not send a legate, will not send an angel, but will come himself. Who will come? The angel himself. Surely not through an angel, except because he is an angel in such a way as to be also the Lord of the Angels. For "angels" in Latin are "messengers." If Christ announced nothing, he would not be called an angel: if Christ prophesied nothing, he would not be called a prophet. He exhorted us to faith, and to laying hold of eternal life: he announced something present, foretold something future: from the fact that he announced something present, he was an angel; from the fact that he foretold something future, he was a prophet: from the fact that the Word of God was made flesh, he was the Lord both of the Angels and of the Prophets.
