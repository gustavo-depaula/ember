# Tractate 90

## *They have seen and hated both me and my Father*[^1].

O wretched men, who, while they wish to be evil, are unwilling that there be a truth by which the evil are condemned! For they are unwilling that it should be what it is, when they ought rather to be unwilling that they themselves should be what they are, so that, while it remains, they might be changed, lest, while it judges, they be condemned.

**1.** You have heard the Lord saying: *He who hates me, hates my Father also*[^2] — he who had said above: *These things they will do to you, because they do not know him who sent me*[^3]. There arises, therefore, a question not to be passed over: how they can hate one whom they do not know. For if they suspect or believe God to be, not what he is, but some other thing, and hate this, then surely they do not hate him, but what they conceive by their lying suspicion or empty credulity; but if they think of him what he is, how are they said not to know him? And of men, indeed, it can come about that we often love those whom we have never seen; and through this it is also, by the contrary, not impossible that we hate those whom we have never seen. For from a report speaking of someone, whether well or ill, it comes about, not undeservedly, that we love or hate one unknown. But if the report is truthful, how is one of whom we have learned true things to be called unknown? Is it because we have not seen his face? Which, since he himself does not see it, can yet be known to no one better than to himself. It is not, then, in a man's bodily face that knowledge of him is conveyed to us. But then it lies open to our knowledge, when his character and life are not hidden. Otherwise no one could know even himself, who cannot see his own face. But surely each one knows himself the more certainly than he is known to others, the more certainly he can see by an inner gaze what he savors, see what he desires, see what he lives by: which, when they are opened to us also, then he truly becomes known to us. And so, since these things are for the most part brought to us about the absent or even the dead, whether by report or by writings, hence it comes that we often either hate or love men whom we have never seen in the face of the body (yet not men whom we do not know at all).

## We cannot search out the conscience of men.

**2.** But for the most part our credulity is deceived in them; for sometimes both history, and much more report, lies. But it pertains to us, lest we be deceived by a ruinous opinion, that, since we cannot search out the conscience of men, we hold a true and certain judgment about the things themselves. That is, that if we do not know whether this or that man is unchaste or chaste, we nevertheless hate unchastity and love chastity; and if we do not know whether this or that man is unjust or just, we nevertheless love justice and detest injustice — not such as we ourselves frame by erring, but such as we behold faithfully in the truth of God, this to be sought and that to be avoided: so that when, concerning the things themselves, we seek what is to be sought and avoid what is to be avoided, it may be pardoned us that concerning the hidden things of men we sometimes — nay, constantly — do not think true things. For this, I judge, belongs to human temptation, without which this life cannot be lived, so that the Apostle said: *Let no temptation take hold of you but such as is human*[^4]. For what is so human as not to be able to look into the human heart; and therefore not to search out its hidden places, but for the most part to suspect something other than what is being done there? Although even in these shadows of human things — that is, of others' thoughts — even if we cannot understand them, since we are men, and so can only suspect; yet we ought to refrain from judgments, that is, from definite and fixed sentences, and to judge nothing before the time, until the Lord comes and illumines the hidden things of darkness and makes manifest the thoughts of the heart; and then each one will have his praise from God[^5]. When, therefore, there is no error in the things — so that the disapproval of vices and the approval of virtues is right — then surely, if there is error concerning men, the human temptation is venial.

**3.** But through these shadows of human hearts, a thing much to be wondered at and much to be lamented comes about: that sometimes one whom we think unjust — and yet he is just — and, not knowing the justice in him, we nevertheless love it, we shun, we turn away from, we forbid from our company, we are unwilling to share a common life and food with him; and him too, if the necessity of imposing discipline compels — whether that he may not harm others, or that he himself may be made more corrected — we pursue with a salutary harshness; and we afflict a good man as though he were evil, whom, not knowing him, we love. This comes about if someone, for example, while he is chaste, is believed by us to be unchaste. For without doubt, if I love the chaste man, this is the very thing that I love; therefore I love him too, and do not know it. And if I hate the unchaste man, then I do not hate him; because he is not what I hate: and yet to my beloved, with whom my soul always dwells in the love of chastity, I do, unknowing, an injury — not erring in the distinction of virtues and vices, but in the shadows of human hearts. Accordingly, just as it can come about that a good man hates a good man unknowingly, or rather loves him unknowingly (for he loves him when he loves the good, because that which the one is, this the other loves); but hates unknowingly not him, but what he thinks him to be: so it can come about that even an unjust man hates a just man, and, while he thinks he loves an unjust man like himself, unknowingly loves a just man; and yet, while he believes him unjust, loves not him, but what he thinks him to be.

But just as a man, so also God. Finally, if the Jews were asked whether they loved God, what else would they answer but that they loved him — not lying from the heart, but rather, in erring, supposing it? For how would they love the Father of truth, who hated the truth? For they are unwilling that their deeds be condemned, and the truth is such that such deeds are condemned: they hate the truth, therefore, just so far as they hate their own punishments, which the truth inflicts on such men. But they do not know that to be the truth which condemns such as they are: they hate, then, what they do not know; and when they hate it, they assuredly cannot but hate also him from whom it was born. And through this, because they do not know that the truth, by whose judgment they are condemned, was born of God the Father, they certainly both do not know and hate him too. O wretched men, who, while they wish to be evil, are unwilling that there be a truth by which the evil are condemned! For they are unwilling that it should be what it is, when they ought rather to be unwilling that they themselves should be what they are; so that, while it remains, they might be changed, lest, while it judges, they be condemned.
