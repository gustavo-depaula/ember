# Tractate 43

## *Your father Abraham rejoiced to see my day; he saw it and was glad*[^1].

Abraham saw it and was glad. Who unfolds this gladness? If those rejoiced to whom the Lord opened the eyes of the flesh, what gladness was there for one seeing with the eyes of the heart the ineffable light, the abiding Word, the unfailing wisdom, God abiding with the Father, and one day to come in the flesh, yet not to depart from the bosom of the Father?

**1.** In this reading of the holy Gospel, which was recited today, from power we learn patience. For what are we as slaves toward the Lord, as sinners toward the just one, as creation toward the Creator? Yet, just as whatever evil we are, we are of ourselves, so whatever good we are, we are of him and through him. And man seeks nothing as he seeks power: the Lord Christ has great power: but let him first imitate his patience, that he may arrive at power. Which of us would patiently hear it, if it were said to anyone: You have a demon? Which was said to him who not only saved men, but even commanded demons.

**2.** For when the Jews had said this: *Do we not say rightly that you are a Samaritan, and have a demon?*[^2]—of these two things cast at him he denied one, the other he did not deny. For he answered and said: *I do not have a demon*[^3]. He did not say: I am not a Samaritan: and surely two things had been cast at him. Although he did not return curse for curse, although he did not refute insult with insult; yet it pertained to him to deny one thing, and not to deny the other. Not in vain, brothers. For "Samaritan" is interpreted "Guardian." He knew himself to be our guardian. For *he who guards Israel neither sleeps nor slumbers*[^4]: and: *Unless the Lord guards the city, in vain do they keep watch who guard it*[^5]. He, then, is our guardian who is our creator. For did it pertain to him that we should be redeemed, and not pertain that we should be kept safe?

Finally, that you may more fully know the mystery why he ought not to deny himself a Samaritan, attend to that most well-known parable, where a certain man was going down from Jerusalem to Jericho, and fell among robbers, who, gravely wounding him, left him half-alive on the road. A priest passed by, neglected him: a Levite passed by, he too went past: a certain Samaritan passed by—he is our guardian; he himself drew near to the wounded man, he himself spent mercy, and made himself a neighbor to him whom he did not reckon a stranger[^6]. To this only, then—that he did not have a demon, but not that he was not a Samaritan—did he reply.

**3.** Then, after such an insult, he said this only about his own glory: *But I honor,* he says, *my Father, and you have dishonored me*[^7]. This is: I do not honor myself, lest I seem arrogant to you, I have one whom I honor; but if you recognized me, as I honor the Father, so would you also honor me: I do what I ought, you do not do what you ought.

**4.** *But I,* he says, *do not seek my own glory; there is one who seeks it and judges*[^8]. Whom does he wish to be understood, except the Father? How, then, does he say in another place, *The Father judges no one, but has given all judgment to the Son*[^9]; and here he says: *I do not seek my own glory; there is one who seeks it and judges*? If, then, the Father judges, how does he judge no one, but has given all judgment to the Son?

## There are two kinds of testing.

**5.** That we may solve this question, attend; it can be solved by a similar mode of speech. You have it written: *God tempts no one*[^10]; and again you have it written: *The Lord your God tempts you, that he may know whether you love him*[^11]. It is a question, you see. For how does God tempt no one, and how does the Lord your God tempt you, that he may know whether you love him? Likewise it is written: *There is no fear in love, but perfect love casts out fear*[^12]; and in another place it is written: *The fear of the Lord is chaste, remaining forever and ever*[^13]. And that too is a question. For how does perfect love cast out fear, if the chaste fear of the Lord remains forever and ever?

**6.** We understand, then, that there are two kinds of testing; one that deceives, the other that proves: according to that which deceives, *God tempts no one*; according to that which proves, *the Lord your God tempts you, that he may know whether you love him.* But here again another question arises, how he tempts that he may know, when nothing can be hidden from him before he tempts. So God is not ignorant: but it was said, *that he may know*, which is, that he may make you know. Such modes of speech are both in our conversations and are found in the authors of eloquence. I will say something about our own speech. A ditch is called blind, not because it itself has lost its eyes, but because, by lying hidden, it makes those who do not see it. I will say something too about those authors. Someone calls lupines sad[^14], that is, bitter: not because they themselves are sad, but because, being tasted, they sadden, that is, they make sad. So there are in the Scriptures modes of speech of this kind. Those who labor at recognizing such questions do not labor at solving such questions. So *the Lord your God tempts you, that he may know*: what is, that he may know? That he may make you know whether you love him. Job was hidden from himself, but not from God; he admitted the tempter, and made him one who came to know him.

## Servile fear and chaste fear.

**7.** What of the two fears? There is a servile fear, and there is a chaste fear; there is the fear lest you suffer punishment, there is another fear lest you lose righteousness. That fear lest you suffer punishment is servile. What great thing is it to fear punishment? This even the most worthless slave does, this even the cruelest robber does. It is no great thing to fear punishment, but it is a great thing to love righteousness. Does he, then, who loves righteousness fear nothing? He fears plainly: not lest he fall into punishment, but lest he lose righteousness.

My brothers, believe, and infer from what you love. Some one of you loves money. Do you think I find anyone who does not love it? Yet from this very thing that he loves, let him understand what I say. He fears loss: why does he fear loss? Because he loves money. As much as he loves money, so much does he fear lest he lose money.

So some lover of righteousness is found, who fears loss in his heart more, who fears more to be despoiled of righteousness, than you to be despoiled of money. This is the chaste fear, this remains forever and ever: love does not take it away, nor cast it out, but rather embraces it, and holds it as a companion and possesses it at once. For we come to the Lord, that we may see face to face: there chaste fear keeps us; for that fear does not disturb, but confirms. The adulterous woman fears lest her husband come, the chaste woman fears too lest her husband depart.

**8.** So, just as according to one testing, *God tempts no one*; but according to another, *the Lord your God tempts you*: and according to one fear, *there is no fear in love, but perfect love casts out fear*; but according to another fear, *the chaste fear of the Lord remains forever and ever*: so in this place too, according to one judgment, *the Father judges no one, but has given all judgment to the Son*; but according to another judgment, *I,* he says, *do not seek my own glory; there is one who seeks it and judges.*

**9.** And let this question be solved from the very word. You have, mentioned in the Gospel, a penal judgment: *He who does not believe is already judged*[^15]; and in another place: *The hour will come when those who are in the tombs will hear his voice, and those who have done good will come forth into the resurrection of life; those who have done evil, into the resurrection of judgment*[^16]. See how he set judgment for condemnation and punishment. And yet, if judgment were always taken for condemnation, would we hear in the Psalm: *Judge me, O God?* There judgment is set according to affliction, here according to distinguishing. How according to distinguishing? As he himself expounds who says: *Judge me, O God.* For read and see what follows. What is: *Judge me, O God? And distinguish my cause,* he says, *from an unholy people*[^17]. What, then, was said: *Judge me, O God, and distinguish my cause from an unholy people*; according to this the Lord Christ now says: *I do not seek my own glory; there is one who seeks it and judges.* How is there one who seeks it and judges? There is the Father who distinguishes and separates my glory from your glory. For you boast according to this world: I do not boast according to this world, who say to the Father: *Father, glorify me with that glory which I had with you before the world was*[^18]. What is, that glory? Distinguished from human inflation. According to this the Father judges. What is, judges? He distinguishes. What does he distinguish? The glory of his Son from the glory of men; for therefore it was said: *God, your God, has anointed you with the oil of gladness above your fellows*[^19].

For not because he was made man is he now to be compared to us. We are men with sin, he without sin: we men drawing from Adam both death and trespass; he drawing from the Virgin mortal flesh, no iniquity. Finally, we are neither born because we will, nor live as long as we will, nor die as we will: he, before he was born, chose her of whom he would be born; being born, he brought it about that he was adored by the Magi; he grew as an infant, and by miracles he showed himself to be God, and by weakness he displayed himself a man. Last of all, he chose even the kind of death, that is, that he should hang on the cross, and should fix that very cross on the foreheads of the faithful: so that the Christian may say, *But far be it from me to boast, except in the cross of our Lord Jesus Christ*[^20]. On that very cross, when he willed, he dismissed his body, and departed: in that very tomb, as long as he willed, he lay; when he willed, he rose, as from a bed.

Therefore, brothers, according to the very form of a slave (for who speaks worthily of that other: *In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God*[^21]?)—according to that form of a slave, I say, there is a great difference between the glory of Christ and the glory of other men. Of that glory he was speaking, when he heard that he had a demon: *I do not seek my own glory; there is one who seeks it and judges*[^22].

**10.** But you, Lord, what do you say of yourself? *Amen, amen, I say to you; if anyone keeps my word, he will not see death forever*[^23]. You, he says, say: You have a demon; I call you to life: keep my word, and you will not die.

They heard: *He will not see death forever, who keeps my word*; and they were angry, because they were already dead with that death which was to be avoided. So the Jews said: *Now we know that you have a demon. Abraham is dead, and the prophets, and you say: If anyone keeps my word, he will not taste death forever*[^24]. See the mode of speech of the Scriptures: *He will not see death*, that is, taste it. He will see death, he will taste death. Who sees it? Who tastes it? What eyes does a man have, that he may see when he dies? When death, by coming, closes those very eyes so that they see nothing, how is it said, he will not see death? Likewise, with what palate, with what throat is death tasted, that what it has for flavor may be discerned? When it takes away all sensation, what will remain on the palate? But it was said *he will see* and *he will taste*, for that which is, he will experience.

## The first death and the second death.

**11.** The Lord—it is too little to say, to those about to die—was speaking, himself about to die: for *the going forth from death is the Lord's*[^25], as the Psalm says. Since, then, he was both speaking to those about to die, and speaking as one about to die, what does he mean by saying: *He who keeps my word will not see death forever*, except that the Lord saw another death, from which he had come to set us free—the second death, the eternal death, the death of the hells, the death of damnation with the devil and his angels? That is the true death: for this present one is a passing over. What is this death? The leaving of the body, the laying down of a heavy burden: but only if no other burden be carried, by which a man is hurled into the hells. Of that death, then, the Lord said: *He who keeps my word will not see death forever.*

**12.** Let us not dread this death, but fear that one. And, what is graver, many, by perversely fearing this one, have fallen into that one. It was said to some: Adore idols; but if you do not do it, you will be killed: or, as that Nebuchadnezzar said: *If you do not do it, you will be cast into a furnace of burning fire*[^26]. Many feared and adored; unwilling to die, they died: by fearing the death that is not escaped, they fell into the death they could happily escape, if they did not unhappily fear that one which is not escaped.

You were born a man, you are going to die. Where will you go, that you may not die? What will you do, that you may not die? That your Lord might console one who must die of necessity, he deigned to die by will. When you see Christ dead, do you disdain to die? You are going to die, then: there is no way for you to escape this. Be it today, be it tomorrow; it is going to happen, the debt must be paid. What, then, does a man accomplish by fearing, fleeing, hiding himself lest he be found by the enemy? Does he accomplish that he may not die? No, only that he may die a little later. He does not obtain release from the debt, but asks for a postponement. However long it is deferred, what is deferred will come.

Let us fear that death which the three men feared, when they said to the king: *God is able even to set us free from this flame; but even if not*[^27]. There was the fear of that death which the Lord now threatens, when they said: But even if he should be unwilling to set us free openly, he can crown us in secret. Hence the Lord himself, who was to make martyrs, and to be the head of the martyrs, says: *Do not fear those who kill the body, and afterward have nothing they can do.* How have they nothing they can do? What if, when they have killed, they cast out the body to be torn by beasts and ravaged by birds? Cruelty still seems to have something it can do. But to whom does it do it? To one who has passed over. The body is there, but there is no sensation: the dwelling lies there, the dweller has departed. So afterward they have nothing they can do: for to one who does not feel they do nothing. *But fear him who has power to kill both body and soul in the hell of fire*[^28]. Behold of what death he was speaking, when he said: *He who keeps my word will not see death forever.* Let us keep, then, brothers, his word in faith, going to arrive at the vision, when we have received the fullest freedom.

**13.** But these men, indignant, dead, and predestined to everlasting death, answered insultingly and said: *Now we know that you have a demon. Abraham is dead, and the prophets*[^29]. But with that death which the Lord wishes to be understood, neither Abraham is dead, nor the prophets. For they died, and they live: these men were living, and they were dead. For answering in a certain place the Sadducees raising a question about the resurrection, the Lord himself says this: *But concerning the resurrection of the dead, have you not read how the Lord says from the bush to Moses: I am the God of Abraham, and the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob? He is not the God of the dead, but of the living*[^30]. If, then, they live, let us labor so to live that we may be able to live with them when we are dead. *Whom do you make yourself,* they say, *that you say: He who keeps my word will not see death forever*[^31]; when you know that both Abraham is dead and the prophets?

**14.** Jesus answered: *If I glorify myself, my glory is nothing: it is my Father who glorifies me*[^32]. He says this because of what they said: *Whom do you make yourself?* For he refers his glory to the Father, from whom is that he is God. Sometimes the Arians slander our faith even from this word, and say: Behold, the Father is greater, because surely he glorifies the Son. Heretic, have you not read the Son himself also saying that he glorifies his Father[^33]? If both that one glorifies the Son, and the Son glorifies the Father: put away your stubbornness, acknowledge the equality, correct your perversity.

## The Manichees and the Marcionites.

**15.** *It is, then,* he says, *my Father who glorifies me, of whom you say: Because he is our God, and you have not known him*[^34]. See, my brothers, how he shows that the very God who was announced to the Jews is the Father of Christ. I say this because, again, certain heretics say that the God announced in the Old Testament is not the Father of Christ, but some prince of evil angels. It is the Manichees who say these things: it is the Marcionites who say these things. There are perhaps also other heretics, whom either there is no need to mention, or all of whom cannot be recalled by me at present: yet there have not been lacking those who would say this. So attend, that you may have something to say even against them.

The Lord Christ says that he is his Father whom they called their God, and did not know: for if they had known him, they would have received his Son. *But I,* he says, *know him.* To those judging according to the flesh he could here too seem arrogant, because he said: *I know him.* But see what follows: *If I were to say that I do not know him, I would be like you, a liar.* So arrogance is not to be guarded against in such a way that the truth is abandoned. *But I know him, and keep his word*[^35]. He was speaking the word of the Father, as the Son; and he himself was the Word of the Father, which was speaking to men.

## Abraham saw it and was glad.

**16.** *Your father Abraham rejoiced to see my day; and he saw it, and was glad*[^36]. He bears great witness to Abraham—the seed of Abraham, the creator of Abraham: *Abraham rejoiced,* he says, *to see my day.* He did not fear, but rejoiced to see it. For there was in him the love that casts out fear[^37]. He does not say: He rejoiced because he saw; but, he rejoiced to see. Believing, surely, he rejoiced by hoping, that he might see by understanding. *And he saw it*: and what more could the Lord Jesus Christ say, or what more ought he to have said? *And he saw it,* he says, *and was glad.* Who unfolds this gladness, my brothers? If those rejoiced to whom the Lord opened the eyes of the flesh, what gladness was there for one seeing with the eyes of the heart the ineffable light, the abiding Word, the splendor shining on devout minds, the unfailing wisdom, God abiding with the Father, and one day to come in the flesh, yet not to depart from the bosom of the Father? All this Abraham saw.

For as to what he says, *my day*, it can be uncertain in what sense he said it; whether the temporal day of the Lord, on which he was to come in the flesh, or the day of the Lord which knows no rising, knows no setting. But I do not doubt that father Abraham knew all of it. And where shall I find it? Or ought the testimony of our Lord Jesus Christ to suffice us? Let us suppose that we cannot find it, because perhaps it is difficult, how it may be manifest that Abraham rejoiced to see the day of Christ, and saw it, and was glad. And if we do not find it, could the truth lie? Let us believe the truth, and not in the least doubt the merits of Abraham.

Yet hear one passage, which comes to me for the moment. Father Abraham, when he sent his servant to seek a wife for his son Isaac, bound him with this oath, that he should faithfully fulfill what was commanded, and that he himself too should know what he was doing. For a great matter was being done, when a marriage was sought for the seed of Abraham. But that the servant might recognize what Abraham knew—that he was not carnally desiring grandchildren, nor thinking anything carnal about his lineage—he said to his servant whom he was sending: *Put your hand under my thigh, and swear by the God of heaven*[^38]. What does the God of heaven want with Abraham's thigh? Now you understand the mystery: by the thigh, the lineage. So what was that oath, except that it was signified that from the lineage of Abraham the God of heaven would come in the flesh?

Fools reproach Abraham, because he said: *Put your hand under my thigh.* Those who reproach the flesh of Christ reproach the deed of Abraham. But we, brothers, if we acknowledge the flesh of Christ as to be venerated, let us not despise that thigh, but receive it as said in prophecy. For indeed Abraham was a prophet. Of whom a prophet? Of his own seed and of his Lord. He signified his own seed by saying: *Put your hand under my thigh*: he signified his Lord by adding, *and swear by the God of heaven.*

**17.** The angry Jews answered: *You do not yet have fifty years, and you have seen Abraham!* And the Lord: *Amen, amen, I say to you; before Abraham came to be, I am*[^39]. Weigh the words, and recognize the mystery. *Before Abraham came to be.* Understand that "came to be" pertains to human making, but "I am" to the divine substance. *Came to be*, because Abraham is a creature. He did not say: Before Abraham was, I was; but: *Before Abraham came to be*—who would not come to be except through me—*I am.* Nor did he say this: Before Abraham came to be, I was made: for *in the beginning God made heaven and earth*[^40]; but *in the beginning was the Word*[^41]. *Before Abraham came to be, I am.* Recognize the Creator, distinguish the creature. He who was speaking had been made the seed of Abraham; and that Abraham might come to be, he himself was before Abraham.

**18.** At this, now, they were the more sharply stirred, as though by a most open insult to Abraham. For the Lord Christ seemed to them to have blasphemed, since he said: *Before Abraham came to be, I am.* So *they took up stones to throw at him.* Where would such hardness run, except to those like it? But Jesus, as a man, as one in the form of a slave, as lowly, as one about to suffer, as one about to die, as one about to redeem us by his blood: not as he who is, not as the Word in the beginning, and the Word with God. For when they took up stones to cast at him, what great thing would it have been for the earth at once to open and swallow them, and for them to find the underworld instead of stones? It was no great thing for God: but patience was rather to be commended than power to be exerted. So *he hid himself from them*[^42], lest he be stoned. As a man he fled from the stones: but woe to those from whose stony hearts God fled!
