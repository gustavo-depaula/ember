# Tractate 87

## *These things I command you, that you love one another*[^1].

## Each one loves his neighbor as himself if he loves God; for if he does not love God, he does not love himself. — Who is good, unless he is made so by loving?

**1.** In the Gospel passage that precedes this one, the Lord had said: *You did not choose me; but I chose you, and appointed you that you should go and bear fruit, and that your fruit should abide: that whatever you ask the Father in my name, he may give you*[^2]. On these words you remember that we have already discoursed, as the Lord granted. But here, in the following passage which you have just heard as it was being read, he says: *These things I command you, that you love one another*[^3]. And through this we ought to understand that this is our fruit, of which he says: *I chose you that you should go and bear fruit, and that your fruit should abide.* And as for what he added, *that whatever you ask the Father in my name, he may give you*: he will then give us, surely, if we love one another; since this very thing too he himself has given us, who chose us when we had no fruit, because we had not chosen him; and appointed us to bear fruit, that is, to love one another — which fruit we cannot have without him, just as the branches can do nothing without the vine.

Love, then, is our fruit, which the Apostle defines as *from a pure heart and a good conscience and faith unfeigned*[^4]. By this we love one another, by this we love God. For we would not love one another with true love unless we loved God. For each one loves his neighbor as himself if he loves God; for if he does not love God, he does not love himself. For *on these two commandments* of love *hang the whole Law and the Prophets*[^5]: this is our fruit. And so, commanding us concerning the fruit, he says: *These things I command you, that you love one another.*

Whence the apostle Paul too, when he wished to commend the fruit of the Spirit against the works of the flesh, set this at the head: *The fruit,* he says, *of the Spirit is love*; and then he wove in the rest as springing from and bound to this head, which are *joy, peace, long-suffering, kindness, goodness, faith, gentleness, self-control*[^6]. But who rightly rejoices who does not love the good from which he rejoices? Who can have true peace, except with him whom he truly loves? Who is long-suffering by abiding perseveringly in the good, unless he is fervent in loving? Who is kind, unless he loves the one he helps? Who is good, unless he is made so by loving? Who is healthfully faithful, except by that faith which works through love? Who is profitably gentle, if love does not moderate him? Who restrains himself from that by which he is disgraced, unless he loves that by which he is honored?

Rightly, then, does the good master so often commend love, as though it alone were to be enjoined — that love without which the other goods cannot profit, and which cannot be had without the other goods by which a man is made good.

## The whole world is the Church.

**2.** But for this love we ought also patiently to bear the hatreds of the world. For it must needs hate us, whom it sees unwilling to do what it loves. But the Lord greatly consoles us concerning himself, who, when he had said, *These things I command you, that you love one another,* added, saying: *If the world hates you, know that it hated me before you*[^7]. Why, then, does a member exalt itself above the head? You refuse to be in the body, if you are unwilling to bear the world's hatred together with the head. *If you were of the world,* he says, *the world would love what was its own*[^8]. This he certainly says to the whole Church, which he also often calls by the name of the world: as in this, *God was in Christ, reconciling the world to himself*[^9]; and likewise in this, *The Son of man did not come to judge the world, but that the world might be saved through him*[^10]. And in his epistle John says: *We have an advocate with the Father, Jesus Christ the righteous, and he is the propitiation for our sins; and not for ours only, but also for those of the whole world*[^11]. The whole world, then, is the Church, and the whole world hates the Church. The world, therefore, hates the world — the hostile world the reconciled, the condemned the saved, the defiled the cleansed.

**3.** But this world which God reconciles to himself in Christ, and which is saved through Christ, and to which every sin is forgiven through Christ, was chosen out of the world that is hostile, condemned, and defiled. For from that mass which wholly perished in Adam are made vessels of mercy, in which is the world that belongs to reconciliation — which world the world hates, the world that from the same mass belongs to the vessels of wrath, which are fitted for destruction. Finally, when he had said, *If you were of the world, the world would love what was its own,* he at once added: *But because you are not of the world, but I chose you out of the world, therefore the world hates you*[^12]. They too, then, were from that source; and that they might not be of it, they were chosen out of it — not by their own merits, none of whose good works had gone before; not by nature, which had been wholly vitiated at its very root through free choice; but freely, that is, by true grace. For he who chose the world out of the world made what he should choose, he did not find it: because *a remnant has been saved by the election of grace. But if by grace,* he says, *then no longer of works; otherwise grace is no longer grace*[^13].

**4.** But if it is asked how the world of perdition loves itself, which hates the world of redemption — it loves itself, certainly, with a false love, not a true one. Accordingly it loves itself falsely, and truly hates itself. *For he who loves iniquity hates his own soul*[^14]. But it is said to love itself, because it loves the iniquity by which it is iniquitous; and again it is said to hate itself, because it loves that which harms it. It hates, then, the nature in itself, and loves the vice; it hates what was made through the goodness of God, and loves what was made in it through free will. Whence we too are both forbidden to love it, if we understand rightly, and commanded to do so: forbidden, namely, where it is said to us, *Do not love the world*[^15]; but commanded, where it is said to us, *Love your enemies*[^16]. They are the world that hates us. We are therefore both forbidden to love in it what it loves in itself; and commanded to love in it what it hates in itself, namely the workmanship of God, and the various consolations of his goodness. For we are forbidden to love the vice in it, and commanded to love the nature, while it loves the vice in itself and hates the nature: so that we may both love it and hate it rightly, while it loves and hates itself perversely.
