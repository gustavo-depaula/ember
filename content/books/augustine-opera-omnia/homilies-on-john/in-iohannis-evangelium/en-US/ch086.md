# Tractate 86

## *You did not choose me; but I chose you*[^1].

## What are we to say on hearing, *You did not choose me,* but that we were evil and were chosen, that we might be good by the grace of the one who chose us?

**1.** It is rightly asked how we are to take what the Lord says: *But I have called you friends; because all things whatsoever I have heard from my Father I have made known to you*[^2]. For who would dare to affirm or believe that any of men knows all things whatsoever the only-begotten Son has heard from the Father — when no one even grasps how the Son hears any word from the Father, since he is himself the only Word of the Father? What of the fact that a little later, in this very discourse which he held to the disciples after supper before the passion, he says: *I have many things to say to you; but you cannot bear them now*[^3]? In what way, then, are we to understand that he made known to the disciples all things whatsoever he heard from the Father, when for this reason he does not say certain many things, because he knows they cannot bear them now? But doubtless what he is going to do, he says he has done — he who has done the things that are to be. For just as he says through the prophet, *They dug my hands and my feet*[^4] — and he does not say, they will dig; speaking as of things past, yet foretelling them as future — so also in this place he says that he has made known to the disciples all things which he knows he will make known in that fullness of knowledge, of which the Apostle says: *But when that which is perfect has come, that which is in part will be done away*[^5]. For there he says: *Now I know in part, but then I shall know as I also have been known; and now through a mirror in an enigma, but then face to face*[^6]. For the Apostle himself too says that we have been saved through the washing of regeneration; yet in another place he says: *In hope,* he says, *we have been saved. But hope that is seen is not hope; for what a man sees, why does he hope for it? But if we hope for what we do not see, we wait for it through patience*[^7]. Whence also his fellow apostle Peter says: *In whom, though now you do not see him, you believe; and when you shall see him, you will exult with joy unspeakable and full of honor, receiving the reward of your faith, the salvation of your souls*[^8]. If, then, now is the time of faith, but the salvation of souls is the reward of faith, who would doubt that the day is to be spent in faith, which works through love, and that at the end of the day the reward is to be received — not only the redemption of our body, of which the apostle Paul speaks, but also the salvation of our souls, of which the apostle Peter speaks? For the happiness of both is in this time and in this mortality held in hope rather than possessed in fact. But there is this difference: that our outer man, that is, the body, is still being corrupted; whereas the inner, that is, the soul, is already being renewed day by day. And so, just as we await the future immortality of the flesh and the salvation of souls, although we are already said to have been saved by receiving the pledge; so we ought to hope for the future knowledge of all things whatsoever the Only-begotten heard from the Father, although Christ has already said that he has done this.

## It is not grace if merits had preceded; but it is grace!

**2.** *You did not choose me,* he says, *but I chose you*[^9]. This is that ineffable grace. For what were we when we had not yet chosen Christ, and therefore did not love him? For he who did not choose him, how does he love him? Was there yet in us what is sung in the Psalm: *I have chosen to be cast off in the house of the Lord, rather than to dwell in the tents of sinners*[^10]? Certainly not. What, then, were we, but unrighteous and lost? For we had not yet believed in him, that he should choose us; for if he chose us already believing, then the chosen one chose. Why, then, would he say, *You did not choose me,* unless because his mercy went before us? Here certainly the empty reasoning of those is idle who defend the foreknowledge of God against the grace of God, and therefore say that we were chosen before the foundation of the world because God foreknew that we would be good, not that he himself would make us good. This is not said by him who says, *You did not choose me.* For if he had chosen us for this reason, because he had foreknown that we would be good, he would at the same time also have foreknown that we would first choose him. For we could not be good otherwise; unless perhaps he is to be called good who did not choose the good. What, then, did he choose in those who were not good? For they were not chosen because they were good, who would not be good unless they had been chosen. Otherwise grace is no longer grace, if we contend that merits preceded. For this is the election of grace, of which the Apostle says: *So then also at this present time a remnant has been saved by the election of grace.* Whence he adds: *But if by grace, then no longer of works; otherwise grace is no longer grace*[^11]. Hear, ungrateful one, hear: *You did not choose me, but I chose you.* You cannot say: I was chosen because I already believed. For if you believed in him, you had already chosen him. But hear: *You did not choose me.* You cannot say: Before I believed, I was already doing good works, and therefore I was chosen. For what good work is there before faith, since the Apostle says: *Everything that is not of faith is sin*[^12]? What, then, are we to say on hearing, *You did not choose me,* but that we were evil and were chosen, that we might be good by the grace of the one who chose us? For it is not grace if merits had preceded; but it is grace; this grace, therefore, did not find merits, but made them.

**3.** And see, dearly beloved, how he does not choose the good, but makes good those whom he has chosen. *I,* he says, *chose you, and appointed you that you should go and bear fruit, and that your fruit should abide*[^13]. Is this not the fruit of which he had already said, *Without me you can do nothing*[^14]? He chose us, then, and appointed us that we should go and bear fruit: we had, therefore, no fruit for which he should choose us. *That you should go,* he says, *and bear fruit.* We go in order to bear fruit, and he himself is the way by which we go, on which he set us that we should go. Accordingly, in all things his mercy goes before us. *And that your fruit,* he says, *should abide; that whatever you ask the Father in my name, he may give you*[^15]. Let love, then, abide; for it is itself our fruit. This love is now in desire, not yet in fullness; and by that very desire, whatever we ask in the name of the only-begotten Son, the Father gives us. But what it is not expedient for us, who are to be saved, to receive, let us not suppose that we ask in the name of the Savior; rather, we ask in the name of the Savior that which belongs to the account of salvation.
