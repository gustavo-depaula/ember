# Tractate 114

## *They did not enter the praetorium, so that they might not be defiled* (Jn 18:28-32). — 

## O wicked blindness! Would they be defiled by another's dwelling, and not be defiled by their own crime?

**1.** Let us now see what was done with our Lord Jesus Christ, or concerning him, before Pontius Pilate the governor, as far as John the evangelist indicates. For he returns to the point in his narrative where he had left it, in order to set forth Peter's denial. He had already said: *And Annas sent him bound to Caiaphas the high priest*[^1]; and then, returning from there to where he had left Peter warming himself at the fire in the courtyard, after he had finished the whole of his denial, which was done three times, *They led Jesus, therefore,* he says, *from Caiaphas to the praetorium*[^2]. For he had said that he was sent to Caiaphas by Annas, his colleague and father-in-law. But if to Caiaphas, why to the praetorium? This can only be understood to mean the place where the governor Pilate was dwelling. Either, then, for some pressing reason Caiaphas had gone from the house of Annas, where both had come together to hear Jesus, to the governor's praetorium, and had left Jesus to be heard by his father-in-law; or else Pilate had taken up the praetorium in the house of Caiaphas, and the house was so large that it accommodated its own master dwelling in one part and the judge in another.

**2.** *Now it was early, and they themselves,* that is, those who were leading Jesus, *did not enter the praetorium,* that is, that part of the house which Pilate held, if it was the house of Caiaphas. And explaining why they did not enter the praetorium, he gives the reason: *So that they might not be defiled,* he says, *but might eat the Passover*[^3]. For they had begun to keep the days of unleavened bread, on which days it was a defilement for them to enter a foreigner's dwelling. O wicked blindness! Would they, that is, be defiled by another's dwelling, and not be defiled by their own crime? They feared to be defiled by the praetorium of a foreign judge, and did not fear to be defiled by the blood of an innocent brother—to say only this much for the moment, where a guilty conscience of evils held them fast. For as to the fact that it was also the Lord who was being led to death by their impiety, and that the giver of life was being killed, let that be charged not to their conscience but to their ignorance.

**3.** *Pilate therefore went out to them and said, What accusation do you bring against this man? They answered and said to him, If this man were not an evildoer, we would not have handed him over to you*[^4]. Let those freed from unclean spirits be questioned and answer—the sick made well, the lepers cleansed, the deaf hearing, the dumb speaking, the blind seeing, the dead rising, and—what surpasses all—the foolish made wise: whether Jesus is an evildoer. But they kept saying these things, of whom he himself had already foretold through the prophet: *They repaid me evil for good*[^5].

## The Jews killed Christ not by themselves, yet it was they who killed him.

**4.** *Pilate therefore said to them, Take him yourselves and judge him according to your law. The Jews therefore said to him, It is not lawful for us to put anyone to death*[^6]. What is this that insane cruelty speaks? Were they not putting to death the one whom they were offering up to be put to death? Or does the cross perhaps not put to death? So do they lose their wits who do not follow but persecute wisdom. But what does this mean: *It is not lawful for us to put anyone to death*? If he is an evildoer, why is it not lawful? Did not the law command them not to spare evildoers, especially—such as they thought this man to be—seducers from their own God? But it must be understood that they said it was not lawful for them to put anyone to death because of the holiness of the feast day, which they had already begun to celebrate, on account of which they feared to be defiled even by entering the praetorium.

Have you so hardened yourselves, false Israelites? Have you by your excessive malice so lost all sense, that you believe yourselves unstained by the blood of the innocent merely because you handed him over to another to be put to death? Was Pilate, indeed, going to kill with his own hands the one whom you thrust upon his power to be killed? If you did not wish him to be killed, if you did not lie in wait for him, if you did not pay money to have him handed over to you, if you did not seize, bind, and lead him away, if you did not offer him with your hands to be killed and demand it with your voices—then do not boast that he was not put to death by you. But if, after all those preceding deeds of yours, you also cried out *Crucify, crucify*[^7], hear what the prophet also cries out against you: *O sons of men, their teeth are weapons and arrows, and their tongue a sharp sword*[^8]. Behold with what weapons, what arrows, what sword you killed the just one, when you said it was not lawful for you to put anyone to death. Hence it is that, to seize Jesus, although the chief priests had not come themselves but had sent others, Luke the evangelist nevertheless, in the same place in his narrative, says: *Jesus said,* he says, *to those who had come to him, the chief priests and the officers of the temple and the elders, Have you come out as against a robber*[^9], and the rest. Therefore, just as the chief priests came, not by themselves but through those whom they had sent to seize Jesus, what else did they do but come themselves in the power of their own command? So all those who with wicked voices cried out that Christ should be crucified killed him not, indeed, by themselves, yet it was they who killed him, through the one who was driven to this crime by their clamor.

**5.** But as for what John the evangelist adds—*that the word of Jesus might be fulfilled which he had spoken, signifying by what death he was to die*[^10]—if we wish to take this to mean the death of the cross, as though the Jews said *It is not lawful for us to put anyone to death* because to put to death is one thing and to crucify another, I do not see how this can be understood consistently, since they answered this in reply to Pilate's words, when he had said to them: *Take him yourselves and judge him according to your law.* Could they not, then, take him and crucify him themselves, if they wished to avoid the killing of anyone by means of such an equivalent kind of punishment? And who does not see how absurd it is that those for whom it was not lawful to put anyone to death should be permitted to crucify anyone? What of the fact that the Lord himself calls this same death of his, that is, the death of the cross, also a putting to death, as we read in Mark, where he says: *Behold, we are going up to Jerusalem, and the Son of man will be handed over to the chief priests and the scribes, and they will condemn him to death and hand him over to the Gentiles; and they will mock him, and spit upon him, and scourge him, and put him to death, and on the third day he will rise*[^11]? Surely, then, in saying these things, the Lord signified by what death he was to die—not that he wished the death of the cross to be understood here, but that the Jews were to hand him over to the Gentiles, that is, to the Romans. For Pilate was a Roman, and the Romans had sent him as governor into Judea. So that this word of Jesus might be fulfilled—that is, that the Gentiles should kill him once he had been handed over to them, which Jesus had foretold would happen—therefore Pilate, who was a Roman judge, when he wished to give him back to the Jews to judge him according to their law, they refused to take him, saying: *It is not lawful for us to put anyone to death.* And thus was fulfilled the word of Jesus, which he foretold concerning his death—that the Gentiles should kill him once handed over by the Jews, with less wickedness than the Jews, who in this way wished to make themselves out, as it were, strangers to his killing, not so that their innocence, but so that their madness, might be made manifest.
