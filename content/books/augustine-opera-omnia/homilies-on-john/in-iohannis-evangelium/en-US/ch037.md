# Tractate 37

## *No one seized him, because his hour had not yet come* (Jn 8:19-20).

His hour had not yet come, not the hour by which he would be compelled to die, but by which he would deign to be killed. The Lord, when he willed, went forth to men; as long as he willed, he lived among men; when he willed, he departed from the flesh; this is of power, not of necessity. This hour, then, he awaited—not a fated one, but an opportune and voluntary one.

**1.** What is said briefly in the holy Gospel ought not to be expounded briefly, that what is heard may be understood. For the words of the Lord are few, but great; to be valued not by number, but by weight: not to be despised because few; but to be sought because great. You who were present yesterday heard us discuss, as we could, what the Lord says: You judge according to the flesh; I judge no one. But even if I judge, my judgment is true; because I am not alone, but I and the Father who sent me. In your Law it is written that the witness of two men is true. I am one who bears witness of myself, and the Father who sent me bears witness of me[^1]. From these words, as I said, yesterday a discourse was rendered to your ears and minds.

When the Lord had said these things; those who heard, You judge according to the flesh, made manifest what they heard. For they answered the Lord speaking of God his Father, and said: *Where is your father?*[^2] They took the Father of Christ carnally, because they judged the words of Christ according to the flesh. But he who spoke was in the open flesh, in secret the Word: man manifest, God hidden. They saw the garment, and despised the one clothed: they despised, because they did not know; they did not know, because they did not see; they did not see, because they were blind; they were blind, because they did not believe.

**2.** Let us see, then, what the Lord answered to these things too. *Where*, they say, *is your father?* For we heard you say: I am not alone, but I and the Father who sent me: we see you alone, we do not see your father with you; how do you say you are not alone, but are with your father? or show us that your father is with you.

And the Lord: Do you see me, that I should show the Father to you? For this follows, this he himself answered with his own words, the exposition of which words we have set forth before. For see what he said: *Neither do you know me, nor my Father. If you knew me, perhaps you would know my Father also*[^3].

You say, then: Where is your father? as though you already knew me; as though I were all this that you see. Therefore, because you do not know me, for that reason I do not show you my Father. For you think me a man, therefore you seek my Father as a man, because you judge according to the flesh. But because according to what you see I am one thing, and according to what you do not see another; and I, hidden, speak of my Father, the hidden one: it is first that you should know me; then you will know my Father also.

**3.** For if you knew me, you would perhaps know my Father also[^4]. He who knows all things, when he says "perhaps," does not doubt, but reproaches. For notice how reproachfully that very "perhaps" is said, which seems to be a word of doubt. But it is a word of doubt when it is said by a man, doubting because not knowing: but when a word of doubt is said by God, since nothing at all is hidden from God, by that doubt the unbelief is reproved, not the divinity made to suppose.

For men sometimes doubt reproachfully about those things which they hold for certain, that is, they put a word of doubt, though in heart they do not doubt: as if you are indignant at your servant and say: You despise me; consider, perhaps I am your master. Hence the Apostle too, speaking to certain despisers of his, says: *But I think that I too have the Spirit of God*[^5]. He who says, I think, seems to doubt: but he was reproaching, not doubting.

And the Lord Christ himself, in another place, reproaching the future unbelief of the human race: *When the Son of man comes*, he says, *do you think he will find faith on the earth?*[^6]

**4.** Now, as far as I judge, you have understood how "perhaps" is used: lest some weigher of words and examiner of syllables, as though knowing how to speak Latin, reprehend the word that the Word of God spoke; and by reprehending the Word of God, remain not eloquent, but mute. For who speaks as the Word speaks, which was in the beginning with God? Do not consider these words, and from these accustomed words wish to measure that Word which is God. For you hear "Word" and despise; hear "God" and fear: In the beginning was the Word. You recall it to the use of your speech, and say within yourself: What is a word? what is great about a word? it sounds and passes; striking the air it hits the ear, afterward it will not be. Hear further: The Word was with God: it remained, it did not pass away by sounding. Still perhaps you despise: God was the Word[^7].

Within yourself, O man, when there is a word in your heart, it is something other than a sound: but the word that is with you, in order to pass over to me, seeks a sound as a vehicle. It takes up, then, a sound, sets itself in a way upon a vehicle, runs through the air, comes to me, and does not withdraw from you. But the sound, in order to come to me, withdrew from you, and did not remain with me. The word, then, that was in your heart—did it pass away with the passing sound?

What you were thinking you spoke; and that what was hidden with you might reach me, you sounded syllables: the sound of the syllables led your thought to my ear; through my ear your thought descended into my heart, the sound in between flew past: but that word which took up the sound, before you sounded, was with you; because you sounded, it is with me, and did not withdraw from you. Attend to this, whoever you are, examiner of sounds. You despise the Word of God, you who do not comprehend the word of man!

## He knows all things, through whom all things were made.

**5.** He knows all things, then, through whom all things were made, and yet he reproaches by doubting: If you knew me, perhaps you would know my Father also. He reproaches the unbelievers. For he said such a sentence to the disciples too: but there it is not a word of doubt, because there was no cause for reproaching unbelief: for what he just now said to the Jews: If you knew me, perhaps you would know my Father also: he said also to the disciples when Philip asked him, indeed demanded, and said: *Lord, show us the Father, and it is enough for us*[^8]: as if he said: We ourselves now know you, you have appeared to us, we have seen you, you have deigned to choose us, we have followed you, we have seen your marvels, we have heard the words of salvation, we have received the precepts, we hope for the promises; you yourself have deigned to confer much on us by your presence: but yet, since we know you, because we do not yet know the Father, we are inflamed with the desire of seeing him whom we do not yet know: and therefore, because we know you, but it is not enough for us until we know the Father also; show us the Father, and it is enough for us.

And the Lord, that they might know that they did not know what they thought they already knew: *Have I been so long a time with you, and you do not know me? Philip, he who has seen me has seen the Father also*[^9]. Does this sentence have a word of doubt? did he say: He who has seen me has perhaps seen the Father also?

Why?

Because a faithful man was hearing, not a persecutor of the faith: therefore the Lord was not a reproacher, but a teacher. He who has seen me has seen the Father also; and here: If you knew me, you would know my Father also: take away the word by which the unbelief of the hearers was marked, and the sentence itself is the same.

## Shipwreck is to be guarded against on both sides.

**6.** Already yesterday we commended to your Charity, and said, that the sentences of the evangelist John, by which he narrates to us what he learned from the Lord, would not have to be discussed, if it could be so, were it not that the fabrications of the heretics compel us.

Briefly, then, yesterday we intimated to your Charity that there are heretics who are called Patripassians, or, from their author, Sabellians: these say that he himself is the Father who is the Son; diverse names, but one person.

When he wills, he is the Father, they say; when he wills, the Son: yet he is one. Likewise there are other heretics who are called Arians. They indeed confess our Lord Jesus Christ to be the only Son of the Father, that one the Father of the Son, this one the Son of the Father; that he who is the Father is not the Son, that he who is the Son is not the Father: they confess the generation, but deny the equality. We, that is, the catholic faith, coming from the teaching of the Apostles, planted in us, received through the series of succession, to be transmitted sound to posterity, held the truth between both, that is, between each error.

In the error of the Sabellians, there is one alone, he himself is the Father who is the Son: in the error of the Arians, the Father is indeed another, the Son another; but the Son himself is not only another, but even another thing:

you in the middle, what? You have excluded the Sabellian, exclude the Arian too. The Father is the Father; the Son is the Son: another, not another thing; because *I and the Father*, he says, *are one*[^10], as yesterday too, as far as I could, I commended. When he hears, are, let the Sabellian withdraw confounded; when he hears, one, let the Arian withdraw confounded: let the catholic steer between both the vessel of his faith, since shipwreck is to be guarded against on both sides. Say, then, you, what the Gospel says: I and the Father are one. Therefore not diverse, because one; not one person, because are.

**7.** A little before he said: My judgment is true; because I am not alone, but I and the Father who sent me: as if he said: Therefore my judgment is true, because I am the Son of God, because I speak the truth, because I myself am the very truth. They, understanding carnally, said: Where is your father?[^11] Now hear, O Arian: Neither do you know me, nor my Father; for if you knew me, you would know my Father also. What is, if you knew me you would know my Father also, except, I and the Father are one?

When you see someone like someone—let your Charity attend, it is a daily manner of speaking; let not what you notice to be customary be hard for you: when, then, you see someone like someone, and you know whom he is like, marveling you say: How like this man is to that man! You would not say this unless they were two. Here that one who does not know him to whom you say this one is like: Is he then, he says, like? And you to him: Why, do you not know that one? But he: I do not know him, he says. Now you, in order to make known to him from the present one whom he beholds the one he does not know, answer and say: This one you have seen, that one you have seen.

Surely not because you said this did you assert them to be one, and deny that they were two: but on account of the likeness you gave such an answer: This one you know and that one you know; for he is very like, and differs in nothing at all. Hence the Lord too: If you knew me, he says, you would know my Father also, not because the Father is the Son, but because the Son is like the Father. Let the Arian blush. Thanks to the Lord that even the Arian himself has withdrawn from the Sabellian error, and is not a Patripassian: he does not say that the Father himself, clothed with flesh, came to men, that he himself suffered, that he himself rose, and in a way ascended to himself; he does not say this: he acknowledges with me that the Father is the Father; and the Son is the Son.

But, O brother, you have escaped that shipwreck, why do you make for the other? The Father is the Father; the Son is the Son: why do you say unlike? why another thing? why another substance? If he were unlike, would he say to his disciples: He who has seen me has seen the Father also? would he say to the Jews: If you knew me, you would know my Father also? Whence would this be true, unless that also were true: I and the Father are one?

## Some believe the Lord was under fate.

**8.** *These words Jesus spoke in the treasury, teaching in the temple*[^12]: with great confidence, without fear. For he would not suffer what he did not will, who would not even be born if he did not will. Finally, what follows? *And no one seized him, because his hour had not yet come.* This too, when some hear it, they believe the Lord Christ was under fate, and say: Behold, Christ had a fate. O if your heart were not foolish, you would not believe in fate! If fate, as some understood, is named from *fando*, that is, from speaking; how does the Word of God have a fate, when in that very Word are all things that have been founded? For God did not establish anything that he did not first know; in his Word was what was made. The world was made; and it was made, and it was there.

How was it both made, and there? Because the house that the builder builds was first in his art; and there it was better, without age, without ruin: yet that he may show his art, he fashions the house; and the house in a way proceeded from the house; and if the house falls, the art remains.

So with the Word of God were all things that have been founded; because *God made all things in wisdom, and made all things known*[^13]: for not because he made did he learn, but because he knew did he make. To us, because they were made, they are known; to him, unless they were known, they would not have been made.

The Word, then, went before. And what before the Word of God? Nothing at all. For if there were something before, it would not be said: In the beginning was the Word[^14]; but: In the beginning the Word was made. Finally, what does Moses say of the world? *In the beginning God made heaven and earth*[^15]. He made what was not: if, then, he made what was not, what was there before? In the beginning was the Word. And whence heaven and earth? *All things were made through him*[^16]. Do you, then, put Christ under fate? Where are the fates?

In heaven, you say, in the order and revolutions of the stars.

How then does he have a fate, through whom heaven and the stars were made; when your own will, if you are rightly wise, transcends even the stars? Or because you have known that the flesh of Christ was under heaven, do you therefore think that the power of Christ too is subject to heaven?

**9.** Hear, O fool: His hour had not yet come[^17], not the hour by which he would be compelled to die, but by which he would deign to be killed. For he himself knew when he ought to die: he attended to all things that were foretold of him, and awaited that all things should be finished that were foretold as to be before his Passion; that when they were fulfilled, then the Passion too would come, by the order of his arrangement, not by fatal necessity. Finally, hear, that you may prove it: among the other things that were prophesied of him, it is also written: *They gave me gall for my food, and in my thirst they gave me vinegar to drink*[^18]. How these were done, we know in the Gospel. First they gave gall; he received it, tasted it[^19], and spat it out: afterward, hanging on the cross, that all things foretold might be fulfilled, he said: *I thirst*: they took a sponge full of vinegar, bound it on a reed, and brought it to him hanging; he received it and said: It is finished. What is, It is finished? All things that had been prophesied before my Passion are fulfilled: therefore what do I yet do here? Finally, after he said: It is finished, bowing his head he gave up his spirit[^20].

Did those robbers fixed beside him expire when they willed? They were held by the bonds of the flesh, because they were not creators of the flesh: fixed with nails, they were long tortured, because they did not have dominion over their weakness. But the Lord, when he willed, took flesh in the virgin's womb: when he willed, he went forth to men; as long as he willed, he lived among men; when he willed, he departed from the flesh: this is of power, not of necessity.

This hour, then, he awaited—not a fated one, but an opportune and voluntary one: that first all things might be fulfilled which ought to be fulfilled before his Passion. For how was he set under the necessity of fate, who in another place said: *I have power to lay down my soul, and I have power to take it up again: no one takes it from me, but I myself lay it down of myself, and take it up again*[^21]? He showed this power, when the Jews sought him. *Whom do you seek?* he said. And they: Jesus. And he: I am he. At which voice heard, *they went backward and fell down*[^22].

## Because he was teaching patience, therefore he deferred power.

**10.** Someone says: If this power was in him, why, when the Jews insulted him hanging and said: *If he is the Son of God, let him come down from the cross*[^23], did he not come down, that he might show them his power by coming down?

Because he was teaching patience, therefore he deferred power. For if, as though moved by their words, he had come down, he would have been thought conquered by the pain of their reproaches. He did not at all come down, he remained fixed, about to depart when he willed. For what great thing was it for him to come down from the cross, who was able to rise from the tomb?

Let us, then, to whom this has been ministered, understand the power of our Lord Jesus Christ to have been then hidden, to be manifest in the future in the judgment: of which it is said: *God will come manifestly, our God, and he will not keep silence*[^24]. What is, he will come manifestly? Because he came hidden, our God will come manifest, that is, Christ. And he will not keep silence: what is, he will not keep silence? Because first he kept silence. Where did he keep silence? When he was judged; that this too might be fulfilled, which the prophet also foretold: *Like a sheep he was led to be sacrificed, and like a lamb before its shearer without voice, so he did not open his mouth*[^25].

If, then, he had been unwilling to suffer, he would not have suffered; if he had not suffered, that blood would not have been shed; if that blood had not been shed, the world would not have been redeemed. Let us, therefore, give thanks both to the power of his divinity, and to the compassion of his weakness: both for the hidden power which the Jews did not know, whence it was just now said to them: Neither do you know me, nor my Father; and for the flesh he assumed which the Jews knew, and whose homeland they knew; whence he said to them in another place: And you know me, and you know whence I am[^26]. Let us know both in Christ; both whence he is equal to the Father, and whence the Father is greater than he. The one is the Word, the other the flesh; the one is God, the other man: but Christ, God and man, is one.
