# Tractate 113

## *Why do you strike me?* (Jn 18:13-27).

## What could he not have done by power, through whom the world was made—had he not preferred to teach us the patience by which the world is overcome?

**1.** After the persecutors, with Judas betraying him, had seized and bound the Lord—who *loved us and gave himself up for us*[^1], and whom the Father did not spare but gave up for us all—so that Judas may be understood as not praiseworthy for the usefulness of this betrayal but damnable for the wickedness of his will, *they led him first to Annas,* as John the evangelist relates. Nor does he keep silent about the reason this was done: *for he was,* he says, *the father-in-law of Caiaphas, who was high priest that year. Now it was Caiaphas,* he says, *who had given counsel to the Jews that it was expedient that one man should die for the people*[^2]. With good reason Matthew too, when he wished to relate this more briefly, records that he was led to Caiaphas; for he was first led to Annas because Annas was Caiaphas's father-in-law, and here we are to understand that this same Caiaphas had wished it to be done.

## Peter denied him when he denied that he was his disciple.

**2.** *Now Simon Peter,* he says, *followed Jesus, and so did another disciple*[^3]. Who this disciple may be is not to be rashly affirmed, since it is not stated. But this same John is accustomed to indicate himself in this way and to add *whom Jesus loved*[^4]. Perhaps, then, this too is he; but whoever he may be, let us look at what follows. *That disciple,* he says, *was known to the high priest, and he entered with Jesus into the courtyard of the high priest, while Peter stood outside at the door. So the other disciple, who was known to the high priest, went out and spoke to the maid who kept the door, and brought Peter in. The maid who kept the door then said to Peter, Are not you also one of this man's disciples? He said, I am not*[^5]. Behold, the most steadfast pillar trembled all over at the breath of a single puff. Where is that boldness of the one who made promises and presumed so much upon himself? Where are those words, when he said: *Why cannot I follow you now? I will lay down my life for you*[^6]? Is this to follow his master—to deny that he is his disciple? Is one's life laid down for the Lord in such a way that, to keep this from happening, a maidservant's voice is dreaded? But what wonder is it if God foretold the truth, while a man presumed falsely?

Indeed, in this denial of the apostle Peter, now begun, we ought to observe that Christ is denied not only by one who says that he is not the Christ, but also by one who, being a Christian, denies that he is one. For the Lord did not say to Peter, You will deny that you are my disciple, but *you will deny me*[^7]. He denied him, then, when he denied that he was his disciple. And what else, in that manner, did he deny but that he was a Christian? For although the disciples of Christ were not yet called by this name—it was after his ascension that they first began to be called Christian disciples at Antioch—yet the reality itself was already there which would afterward be designated by that word; already there were disciples who were afterward called Christians; and this common name, like the common faith, they handed on even to those who came after. He, therefore, who denied that he was Christ's disciple denied the very reality whose name it is to be called Christian.

How many afterward—I do not say old men and old women, in whom satiety with this life could the more easily despise death for the confession of Christ; nor only the young of both sexes, of whom fortitude seems fittingly to be required; but even boys and girls—were able to do, and an innumerable company of holy martyrs entered the kingdom of heaven bravely and by force, what at that time this man could not do, who received the keys of that kingdom!

Behold the source of what was said, *Let these men go*[^8], when he gave himself up for us, who redeemed us by his own blood; *that the word might be fulfilled which he had spoken, Of those whom you gave me I lost not one*[^9]. For surely if Peter, having denied Christ, had departed this life then, what else would he have done but perish?

**3.** *Now the servants and the officers were standing by a charcoal fire, because it was cold, and they were warming themselves*[^10]. It was not winter, yet it was cold, as sometimes happens even at the spring equinox. *Peter also was with them, standing and warming himself. The high priest then questioned Jesus about his disciples and about his teaching. Jesus answered him, I have spoken openly to the world; I have always taught in the synagogue and in the temple, where all the Jews come together, and in secret I have said nothing. Why do you question me? Question those who heard what I said to them; behold, these know what I said*[^11].

A question arises that is not to be passed over: how the Lord Jesus could say *I have spoken openly to the world*, and especially what he says, *In secret I have said nothing.* Did he not, in this very recent discourse that he spoke to the disciples after supper, say to them: *I have spoken these things to you in figures; the hour is coming when I will no longer speak to you in figures, but will tell you plainly of my Father*[^12]? If, then, he was not speaking openly even to his own closer disciples, but was promising an hour when he would speak openly, how did he speak openly to the world? Furthermore, to these his own, as the authority of the other evangelists also attests, he was clearly speaking much more plainly, by comparison with those who were not his disciples, when he was alone with them, withdrawn from the crowds; for then he opened to them the parables which he set forth to others as things shut up.

What, then, is meant by *In secret I have said nothing*? It must be understood that he said *I have spoken openly to the world* as if he had said: Many heard me. And this very word *openly* was in one way open and in another way not open. It was open, because many heard; and again it was not open, because they did not understand. And as for what he spoke apart to his disciples, that surely was not spoken in secret. For who speaks in secret who speaks before so many people, when it is written: *By the mouth of two or three witnesses every word shall stand*[^13]—especially if he speaks to a few what he wishes through them to become known to many, as the Lord himself said to those whom he then had as few: *What I tell you in the dark, speak in the light; and what you hear whispered, proclaim upon the housetops*[^14]? Therefore even this very thing that seemed to be said by him in secret was in a certain way not said in secret, because it was not said so as to be kept silent by those to whom it had been said, but rather so as to be proclaimed everywhere. Thus something can be said both openly and at the same time not openly, or in secret and at the same time not in secret, just as it was said, *That seeing they may see, and not see*[^15]. For how do they *see*, except because it is openly, not in secret; and how do these same ones again *not see*, except because it is not openly, but in secret? Yet those very things which they had heard and not understood were of such a kind that they could not justly and truthfully bring a charge over them; and as often as they tried by questioning to find some ground for accusing him, he answered them in such a way that all their tricks were blunted and their slanders frustrated. Therefore he said: *Why do you question me? Question those who heard what I said to them; behold, these know what I said.*

**4.** *When he had said this, one of the officers standing by struck Jesus with the palm of his hand, saying, Is that how you answer the high priest? Jesus answered him, If I have spoken wrongly, bear witness to the wrong; but if rightly, why do you strike me?*[^16] What could be more truthful, more gentle, more just than this answer? For it is the answer of him about whom the prophetic word had gone before: *Aim, and go forward prosperously, and reign, for the sake of truth and gentleness and justice*[^17]. If we consider who it was that received the blow, would we not wish the one who struck him to be consumed by heavenly fire, or swallowed up by the earth opening beneath him, or seized by a demon and rolled about, or punished by some such penalty, or even a heavier one? For which of these could he not have commanded by power, through whom the world was made—had he not preferred to teach us the patience by which the world is overcome? Here someone will say: Why did he not do what he himself commanded? For he ought not to have answered the one who struck him in this way, but to have offered the other cheek. What of it, that he both answered truthfully, gently, and justly, and prepared not only the other cheek for one who would strike it again, but his whole body to be fastened to the wood? And hereby he rather showed what had to be shown: namely, that those great precepts of his concerning patience are to be carried out not by an outward display of the body but by the preparation of the heart. For it can happen that a man offers the other cheek visibly even while angry. How much better, then, both to answer truthfully when calm, and to be made ready with a tranquil mind to bear heavier things? For blessed is he who, in all that he suffers unjustly for the sake of justice, can truthfully say: *My heart is ready, O God, my heart is ready*[^18]; for from this follows what comes next: *I will sing and chant a psalm*[^19]—which Paul and Barnabas were able to do even in the harshest of chains.

**5.** But let us return to what follows in the gospel narrative. *And Annas sent him bound to Caiaphas the high priest*[^20]. To him, as Matthew says, he was being led from the beginning, since Caiaphas was the chief of the priests that year. For both pontiffs—that is, the chiefs of the priests who were at that time, Annas and Caiaphas, whom Luke the evangelist mentions, relating at what time the Lord's forerunner John began to preach the kingdom of heaven and to gather disciples—are to be understood as accustomed to hold office in alternate years. For Luke says thus: *In the high priesthood of Annas and Caiaphas, the word of the Lord came to John the son of Zechariah in the wilderness*[^21], and the rest. Accordingly these two pontiffs held their years in turn; and it was the year of Caiaphas when Christ suffered. Therefore, according to Matthew, when he had been seized he was led to him; but first, according to John, they came with him to Annas—not because Annas was his colleague, but because he was his father-in-law. And it is to be believed that this was done according to the will of Caiaphas, or else that their houses were so situated that Annas ought not to be passed by those going by.

**6.** But when the evangelist had said that Annas sent him bound to Caiaphas, he returned to the point in the narrative where he had left Peter, in order to set forth what had befallen, in the house of Annas, regarding Peter's threefold denial. *Now Simon Peter,* he says, *was standing and warming himself.* He recapitulates what he had already said before; then he joins to it what followed. *So they said to him, Are not you also one of his disciples? He denied it and said, I am not.* He had already denied once; behold, now a second time. Then, that the third denial may be completed: *One of the servants of the high priest, a kinsman of the man whose ear Peter had cut off, said, Did I not see you in the garden with him? Peter therefore denied it again, and at once the cock crowed*[^22]. Behold, the physician's prediction is fulfilled, the sick man's presumption refuted. For what this man had said did not come to pass—*I will lay down my life for you*[^23]; but what the other had foretold did come to pass—*You will deny me three times*[^24].

But now that Peter's threefold denial is completed, let this homily also be completed, so that hereafter we may consider, from a new beginning, what was done with the Lord before Pontius Pilate the governor.
