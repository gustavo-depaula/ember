# Tractate 110

## *You have loved them as you also loved me*[^1].

## In the Son the Father loves us, because in him he chose us before the foundation of the world. For he who loves the Only-begotten surely loves also his members, which he adopted into him and through him.

**1.** When the Lord Jesus had prayed for his disciples whom he then had with him, and had added his others, saying, *But not for these alone do I ask, but also for those who will believe in me through their word*; as though we should ask what or why he asked for them, he at once subjoined, saying: *That all may be one, as you, Father, in me, and I in you, that they too may be one in us*[^2]. And above, when he was still praying for the disciples alone whom he had with him: *Holy Father,* he says, *keep in your name those whom you gave me, that they may be one as we also are*[^3]. This, then, he now asked for us too, which he then asked for them, that all, that is, both we and they, may be one.

Here it is carefully to be noted that the Lord did not say, that all may be one as we; but, *That all may be one, as you, Father, in me, and I in you* (it is understood, we are one; which is more openly said afterward): because he had said before of the disciples who were with him, *That they may be one as we also are.* Wherefore the Father is so in the Son, and the Son in the Father, that they are one, because they are of one substance: but we can indeed be in them, yet we cannot be one with them; because we and they are not of one substance, insofar as the Son with the Father is God. For insofar as he is man, he is of the same substance of which we too are. But now he wished rather to commend that which he says elsewhere, *I and the Father are one*[^4]; where he signified that the nature of the Father and his own is the same. And through this, even when the Father and the Son, or also the Holy Spirit, are in us, we ought not to think them to be of one nature with us. So, then, they are in us, or we in them, that they are one in their nature, we one in ours. For they are in us, as God in his temple: but we are in them, as the creature in its Creator.

**2.** Then, when he had said, *That they too may be one in us,* he added: *That the world may believe that you sent me*[^5]. What is this? Will the world then believe, when we shall all be one in the Father and the Son? Is not that the perpetual peace, and rather the reward of faith than faith? For we shall be one, not that we may believe, but because we have believed. But even if in this life, on account of that common faith, all we who believe in one are one, according to that of the Apostle, *For you are all one in Christ Jesus*[^6]; even so we are one, not that we may believe, but because we believe.

What is it, then: *That all may be one, that the world may believe*? For those very *all* are the believing world. For they are not others who will be one, and the world another that is going to believe for the reason that they will be one; since without doubt he says of these, *That all may be one,* of whom he had said, *But not for these alone do I ask, but also for those who will believe in me through their word,* at once subjoining, *That all may be one.* But these *all* — what is it, but the world, not the hostile world surely, but the faithful? For behold, he who had said, *I do not ask for the world*[^7], asks for the world that it may believe. For there is a world of which it is written, *Lest we be condemned with this world*[^8]. For this world he does not ask: for he is not ignorant to what it is predestined. And there is a world of which it is written, *For the Son of man did not come to judge the world, but that the world might be saved through him*[^9]: whence the Apostle too says, *God was in Christ, reconciling the world to himself*[^10]. For this world he asks, saying, *That the world may believe that you sent me.* For through this faith the world is reconciled to God, when it believes in Christ who was sent by God. How, then, are we to understand what he says, *That they too may be one in us, that the world may believe that you sent me*: unless that he did not place the cause that the world should believe in this, that they are one, as though it would for that reason believe because it sees them to be one; since the world itself is all who, by believing, become one: but in praying he said, *That the world may believe*; as in praying he said, *That all may be one*; in praying he said, *That they too may be one in us*? For this is *that all may be one,* which is *that the world may believe*: since by believing they become one; perfectly one, who, though by nature they were one, by dissenting from the One were not one.

Finally, if the word which he says, *I ask,* we understand a third time, or rather, that it may be made fuller, place it everywhere; the explanation of this saying will be more manifest: I ask *that all may be one, as you, Father, in me, and I in you*: I ask *that they too may be one in us*: I ask *that the world may believe that you sent me.* For this reason, surely, he added what he said, *in us,* that we may know it is to be attributed to the grace of God, not to ourselves, that we are made one by a most faithful love: just as the Apostle, when he had said, *For you were once darkness, but now light,* he says; and lest they should attribute this to themselves, he added, *in the Lord*[^11].

**3.** Then our Savior, by asking the Father, showed himself a man: now showing that he himself too, since with the Father he is God, does what he asks: *And I,* he says, *have given them the glory which you gave me*[^12]. What glory, but immortality, which the human nature in him was to receive? For neither had he himself yet received it, but in his manner, on account of the immobility of predestination, he signifies future things by words of past time, because, now to be glorified, that is, raised up by the Father, he himself too is going to raise us to that glory at the end. This is like that which he says elsewhere: *As the Father raises the dead and gives them life, so the Son also gives life to whom he wills*[^13]. And to whom, but the same as the Father? *For whatever things* the Father *does,* not other things, but *these the Son also* does; nor otherwise, *but in like manner he does*[^14]. And through this he raised even himself too. For hence is that: *Destroy this temple,* he says, *and in three days I will raise it up*[^15].

Accordingly, the glory of immortality, which he says was given him by the Father, he is to be understood to have given to himself too, even though he does not say so. For this reason, surely, he more often says that the Father alone does what he himself too does with the Father, that he may attribute whatever he is to him from whom he is. But also sometimes, the Father being passed over in silence, he says that he himself does what he does with the Father: so that we may understand that the Son is no more to be separated from the Father's work, when, the Father being passed over in silence, he says that he himself works something; than the Father is separated from the Son's work, when, the Son being passed over in silence, the Son is said to work that which they nonetheless work equally.

When, therefore, the Son passes over in silence, in the Father's work, his own working, he commends his lowliness, that it may be more salutary for us: but when, in turn, in his own work he passes over in silence the Father's working, they commend his equality, lest he be believed inferior. In this way, then, here too he neither makes himself foreign to the Father's work, although he said, *The glory which you gave me*; because he himself too gave it to himself: nor does he make the Father foreign to his work, although he said, *I have given to them*; because the Father too gave it to them. For the works not only of the Father and the Son, but also of the Holy Spirit, are inseparable. But just as from this, that he asked the Father for all his own, he wished this to be done, *That all may be one*: so from this his own benefit too, which he says, *The glory which you gave me, I have given to them,* he nonetheless wished this to be done; for he at once subjoined: *That they may be one, as we also are one.*

**4.** Then he added: *I in them, and you in me, that they may be perfected into one*[^16]. Where he briefly intimated himself the mediator between God and men. For this was not said as though the Father were not in us, or we were not in the Father; since he said elsewhere, *We will come to him, and will make our dwelling with him*[^17]: and here a little before he did not say, *I in them and you in me,* which he said now; nor, They in me and I in you; but, *You in me and I in you, and they in us.* What he now says, then, *I in them and you in me,* was said from the person of the Mediator, just as that which the Apostle says, *You are Christ's, but Christ is God's*[^18]. But what he added, *That they may be perfected into one,* shows that the reconciliation which is brought about through the Mediator is led to this, that we may fully enjoy a perfect blessedness, to which nothing can now be added. Whence that which follows, *That the world may know that you sent me,* I do not think is to be taken as though he had again said, *That the world may believe*: for sometimes "to know" is put for that which is "to believe," as is that which he says a little above, *And they have known truly that I came forth from you, and have believed that you sent me*[^19]. He said afterward *they have believed,* which he had first said *they have known.* But here, since he is speaking of the consummation, such a knowledge is to be understood as will be by sight, not such as is now by faith. For an order seems to be preserved in that which he said a little before, *That the world may believe*; but here, *That the world may know.* For there, although he said, *That all may be one,* and *be one in us,* he did not yet say, *Let them be perfected into one*; and so he subjoined, *That the world may believe that you sent me*: but here, *That they may be,* he says, *perfected into one*; and then he did not add, *That the world may believe*; but, *That the world may know that you sent me.* For so long as we believe what we do not see, we are not yet so perfected as we shall be when we shall merit to see what we believe.

Most rightly, then, there: *That the world may believe*; here: *That the world may know*: yet both there and here, *that you sent me*; that we may know that, as far as pertains to the inseparable love of the Father and the Son, this we now believe, which by believing we tend to know. But if he should say, That they may know that you sent me, it would be worth just as much as this which he says, *That the world may know.* For they are the world, not the enduring enemy, such as is the world predestined to condemnation; but the world made a friend from an enemy, for whose sake *God was in Christ reconciling the world to himself.* Therefore he said, *I in them and you in me*: as if he said: I in them to whom you sent me; and you in me, reconciling the world to yourself through me.

## He who loves the Only-begotten surely loves also his members.

**5.** Therefore there follows also that which he says: *And you have loved them as you also loved me*[^20]. For in the Son the Father loves us, because in him he chose us before the foundation of the world. For he who loves the Only-begotten surely loves also his members, which he adopted into him through him. Nor are we for that reason equal to the only-begotten Son through whom we were created and re-created; because it was said, *You have loved them as you also loved me.* For he who says, As that, so this, does not always signify equality: but sometimes only, Because that is, this also is; or, Because that is, that this also may be. For who would say that the Apostles were sent into the world by Christ in altogether the same way as he himself was sent by the Father? For — to be silent about other differences, which it would be long to recall — they certainly were sent when they were already men; but he himself was sent that he might be a man: and yet above he says, *As you sent me into the world, I also have sent them into the world*[^21]; as if he said: Because you sent me, I sent them. So also in this place: *You have loved them,* he says, *as you loved me*; which is nothing other than: You have loved them, since you have also loved me.

For he who loves the Son would not fail to love the members of the Son; nor is there another cause for loving his members, except that he loves him. But he loves the Son according to the divinity, because he begot him equal to himself; he loves him also according to that whereby he is man, because the only-begotten Word himself was made flesh, and on account of the Word the flesh of the Word is dear to him: but us he loves because we are the members of him whom he loves; and that we might be this, for this reason he loved us before we were. Wherefore incomprehensible is the love with which God loves, nor changeable. For not from the time when we were reconciled to him through the blood of his Son did he begin to love; but before the foundation of the world he loved us, that with his Only-begotten we too might be his sons, before we were anything at all. That we were reconciled to God through the death of his Son, then, let it not be so heard, let it not be so taken, as though for this reason the Son reconciled us to him, that he might now begin to love those whom he hated; as an enemy is reconciled to an enemy, so that thereafter they are friends, and those who hated one another love one another in turn: but to him already loving us we were reconciled, with whom on account of sin we had enmities. Whether I say this truly, let the Apostle bear witness: *God commends,* he says, *his love toward us, in that, while we were yet sinners, Christ died for us*[^22].

**6.** Wherefore incomprehensible is the love with which God loves, nor changeable. For not from the time when we were reconciled to him through the blood of his Son did he begin to love; but before the foundation of the world he loved us, that with his Only-begotten we too might be his sons, before we were anything at all. That we were reconciled to God through the death of his Son, then, let it not be so heard, let it not be so taken, as though for this reason the Son reconciled us to him, that he might now begin to love those whom he hated; as an enemy is reconciled to an enemy, so that thereafter they are friends, and those who hated one another love one another in turn: but to him already loving us we were reconciled, with whom on account of sin we had enmities. Whether I say this truly, let the Apostle bear witness: *God commends,* he says, *his love toward us, in that, while we were yet sinners, Christ died for us*[^23].

He had, then, love toward us, even when, exercising enmities against him, we wrought iniquity: and yet it was most truly said to him, *You hate, O Lord, all who work iniquity*[^24]. Accordingly, in a wondrous and divine manner, even when he hated us, he loved us: for he hated us as not such as he himself had made us; and because our iniquity had not in every part consumed his work, he knew at once both to hate in each of us what we had made, and to love what he had made. And this indeed can be understood of him in all things, to whom it is truly said, *You hate nothing of the things you have made*[^25]. For he would not have willed anything to be which he hated, nor would that at all be which the Almighty had not willed to be, unless even in that which he hates there were also that which he loves. For rightly he hates and disapproves the vice, as foreign to the rule of his art: yet he loves his own benefit even in the vicious, whether by healing or his judgment by condemnation. So God both hates nothing of the things he has made: for, being the founder of natures, not of vices, the evils which he hates, he himself did not make; and of those same evils, whether by healing them through mercy, or ordering them through judgment, the things themselves which he does are good.

Since, therefore, he hates nothing of the things he has made, who could worthily utter how much he loves the members of his Only-begotten; and how much more the Only-begotten himself, in whom are created all things visible and invisible, which, ordered in their kinds, he loves most orderly? For he leads the members of the Only-begotten to equality with the holy angels by the bounty of his grace: but the Only-begotten, since he is Lord of all, is without doubt Lord of the angels, by the nature whereby he is God, not equal to the angels, but rather to the Father; but by the grace whereby he is man, how does he not exceed the excellence of any angel whatever, since he is one person of flesh and the Word?

**7.** Although there are not lacking those who even prefer us to the angels; because for us, they say, not for the angels, Christ died. Which is what else than to wish to glory in impiety? For *Christ,* as the Apostle says, *in due time died for the impious*[^26]. Here, then, not our merit, but the mercy of God is commended. For what kind of thing is it to wish to be praised for this, that one was so detestably sick by his own vice, that he could not be healed otherwise than by the death of the physician? This is not the glory of our merits, but the medicine of our diseases. Or do we for this reason prefer ourselves to the angels, because, although the angels too sinned, nothing of such a kind was expended for them whereby they might be healed? As if something, even small, had been expended for them, and more for us. But if this too had been done, it could still be asked whether it had been done for this reason, that we had stood more excellently, or that we lay more desperately. But since we know that the Creator of all goods conferred no grace for the repairing of the evil angels, why do we not rather understand that their fault was judged the more damnable, the more sublime was their nature? For they ought to have sinned so much less than we, as they were better than we. But now, in offending the Creator, they stood the more execrably ungrateful for his benefit, the more beneficently they were created; nor was it enough for them to be deserters of him, unless they became deceivers of us too. This great good, then, he will confer on us, who loved us as he loved Christ, that for the sake of him whose members he willed us to be, we may be equal to the holy angels, to whom we were both created inferior by nature, and made more unworthy by sin, we who ought to have become their fellows, of whatever sort.
