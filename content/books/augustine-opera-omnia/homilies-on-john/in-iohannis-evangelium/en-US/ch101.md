# Tractate 101

## *A woman when she gives birth has sorrow, because her hour has come*[^1].

## This fruit of all her labor the Church now travails to bring forth by desiring, then will bring forth by beholding; now she travails by groaning, then will bring forth by rejoicing; now she travails by praying, then will bring forth by praising.

**1.** These words of the Lord, where he says, *A little while, and you will no longer see me, and again a little while, and you will see me, because I go to the Father*[^2], were so obscure to the disciples, before that which he says was fulfilled, that, asking among themselves what it was that he said, they confessed that they did not know at all. For the Gospel follows: *Some of his disciples, then, said to one another: What is this that he says to us: A little while, and you will not see me, and again a little while, and you will see me, and because I go to the Father? They said, then: What is this that he says: A little while? We do not know what he is saying*[^3]. For this is what moved them, that he said, *A little while, and you will not see me, and again a little while, and you will see me.* For in the preceding, because he had not said, *A little while,* but had said, *I go to the Father, and you will no longer see me*[^4], he seemed to them to have spoken openly, and they did not ask one another anything about it. Now, then, what was then obscure to them, and was soon made manifest, is now certainly manifest to us: for after a little he suffered, and they did not see him; again, after a little he rose again, and they saw him. But that which he says, *You will no longer see me* — because by that word, that is, *no longer,* he wished it to be understood that they would not see him thereafter — we have explained how it is to be taken there, where he said, the Holy Spirit *will convict the world concerning righteousness, because I go to the Father, and you will no longer see me*[^5]: namely, because they would not thereafter see Christ mortal.

**2.** *But Jesus knew,* as the Evangelist next says, *that they wished to question him, and he said to them: You inquire among yourselves about this, because I said: A little while, and you will not see me, and again a little while, and you will see me. Amen, amen, I say to you, that you will weep and lament; but the world will rejoice: but you will be made sorrowful, but your sorrow will be turned into joy*[^6]. And this can be taken thus: that the disciples were made sorrowful at the death of the Lord, and at once made glad at the resurrection: but the world, by which name are signified the enemies by whom Christ was slain, certainly rejoiced when Christ was slain, when the disciples were made sorrowful. For by the name of the world the malice of this world can be understood, that is, of the men who are friends of this world. Whence the apostle James says in his epistle: *Whoever wishes to be a friend of this world is made an enemy of God*[^7]: by which enmities with God it came about that not even his Only-begotten was spared.

**3.** Then he adds and says: *A woman when she gives birth has sorrow, because her hour has come; but when she has brought forth the child, she no longer remembers the anguish, for joy, because a man has been born into the world: and you therefore now indeed have sorrow; but I will see you again, and your heart will rejoice, and your joy no one will take from you*[^8]. Nor does this comparison seem difficult to understand; since its application is at hand, he himself explaining why it was said. For the travail is compared to sorrow, but the birth to joy; which is wont to be the greater then, when not a girl, but a boy is born. But what he says, *Your joy no one will take from you,* because their joy is Jesus himself, is what the Apostle signifies: *Christ, rising from the dead, dies no more, and death will no longer have dominion over him*[^9].

**4.** Thus far in this chapter of the Gospel, on which we discuss today, all has run as it were with easy understanding: a keener attention is needed in what follows. For what is it that he says, *And in that day you will ask me nothing*[^10]? This word "to ask" (rogare) signifies not only to petition, but also to question; and the Greek Gospel, from which this is translated, has such a word as can be understood in both ways, so that this ambiguity is not resolved even from there: although, even if it were resolved, no question would for that reason remain none. For we read that the Lord Christ, after he rose, was both questioned and asked. For he was questioned by the disciples, when about to ascend into heaven, when he would present himself, and when the kingdom would be Israel's[^11]: but when he was already in heaven, he was asked by holy Stephen, that he might receive his spirit[^12]. And who would dare even to think or say that Christ sitting in heaven is not to be asked, and Christ remaining on earth was asked? that the immortal one is not to be asked, the mortal one ought to have been asked? Nay rather, dearly beloved, let us ask him to loose for us the knot of this question himself, by shining in our hearts to see what he says.

**5.** For I think that what he says, *But I will see you again, and your heart will rejoice, and your joy no one will take from you,* is to be referred, not to that time in which he rose and showed them his flesh to be beheld and touched: but rather to that of which he had already said, *He who loves me will be loved by my Father; and I will love him, and will manifest myself to him*[^13]. For he had already risen, had already shown himself to them in the flesh, was already sitting at the right hand of the Father, when this same apostle John, whose this Gospel is, said in his epistle: *Dearly beloved, now we are sons of God, and it has not yet been manifested what we shall be: we know that when it has been manifested, we shall be like him, because we shall see him as he is*[^14]. That vision is not of this life, but of the future; not temporal, but eternal. *But this is eternal life,* life itself saying it, *that they may know you,* he says, *the one true God, and Jesus Christ whom you have sent*[^15]. Of this vision and knowledge the Apostle says: *We see now through a mirror in an enigma, but then face to face: now I know in part, but then I shall know as I also have been known*[^16].

This fruit of all her labor the Church now travails to bring forth by desiring, then will bring forth by beholding; now she travails by groaning, then will bring forth by rejoicing; now she travails by praying, then will bring forth by praising. And therefore a male child; since to this fruit of contemplation all the offices of action are referred. For it alone is free; because it is sought for its own sake, and is not referred to anything else. Action serves this: for to this is referred whatever is done well, because it is done for the sake of this; but it itself is held and possessed, not for the sake of anything else, but for its own sake. There, then, is the end that suffices us. It will therefore be eternal: for an end does not suffice us, unless it be one that has no end. This had been breathed into Philip when he said: *Show us the Father, and it suffices us*[^17]. In which showing the Son too promised himself, saying: *Do you not believe that I am in the Father, and the Father in me*[^18]? Concerning this, then, which suffices us, we most rightly hear: *Your joy no one will take from you.*

## This little while seems long to us, because it is still going on.

**6.** By this too I judge that what was said above is better understood: *A little while, and you will no longer see me, and again a little while, and you will see me.* For this whole span in which the present age flies by is a little while: whence this same evangelist says in his epistle, *It is the last hour*[^19]. For he added, *because I go to the Father*: which is to be referred to the former saying, where he says, *A little while, and you will no longer see me*; not to the latter, where he says, *and again a little while, and you will see me.* For by going to the Father, he was going to bring it about that they would not see him. And through this it was not said because he was going to die, and, until he should rise, to withdraw from their sight; but because he was going to go to the Father, which he did after he rose, and, having conversed with them for forty days, ascended into heaven. To those, then, who then saw him bodily, he said, *A little while, and you will no longer see me,* because he was going to go to the Father, and thereafter they were not going to see him mortal, such as they saw him when he was speaking these things. But what he added, *and again a little while, and you will see me,* he promised to the whole Church: as he promised to the whole, *Behold, I am with you until the consummation of the world*[^20]. The Lord does not delay his promise: a little while, and we shall see him, where we shall ask nothing, question nothing; because nothing will remain to be desired, nothing will lie hidden to be sought. This little while seems long to us, because it is still going on: when it is ended, then we shall feel how little it was. Let our joy, then, not be such as the world has, of which it was said, *But the world will rejoice*: nor yet, in the travail of this desire, let us be sad without joy, but, as the Apostle says, *rejoicing in hope, patient in tribulation*[^21]; because the woman in travail herself, to whom we are compared, rejoices more over the offspring soon to come, than she is saddened by the present pain.

But let this be the end of this sermon: for what follows has a most troublesome question, and is not to be confined by brevity, that it may be more conveniently explained, if the Lord wills.
