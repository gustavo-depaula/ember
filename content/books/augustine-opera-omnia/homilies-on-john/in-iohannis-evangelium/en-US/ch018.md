# Homily 18

## *The Father loves the Son, and shows him everything that he himself will do*[^1].

## Return to the heart; see there what you may perceive about God, because there is the image of God. In the inner man Christ dwells, in the inner man you are renewed to the image of God, in his image recognize its author. I knock with you, I am fed with you.

**1.** John the Evangelist, among his fellows and partners the other evangelists, received this special and proper gift from the Lord (upon whose breast he reclined at the supper[^2], that by this it might be signified that he drank deeper secrets from his innermost heart): that he should say of the Son of God those things which can perhaps stir up the attentive minds of little ones, but cannot fill those not yet capable; while to certain more grown-up minds, coming to a certain inwardly mature age, he gives in these words something by which they may both be exercised and fed.

You have heard it as it was read, and you remember whence this sermon has come. For yesterday it was read that the Jews wished to kill Jesus for this reason, *because he not only broke the Sabbath, but also said that God was his Father, making himself equal to God*[^3]. What displeased the Jews, this pleased the Father himself. This without doubt also pleases those who honor the Son as they honor the Father; for if it does not please them, they will displease. For God will not be greater because he pleases you, but you will be less if he displeases you. Against this slander of theirs, coming either from ignorance or from malice, the Lord speaks—not at all what they may grasp, but something by which they may be stirred and disturbed, and perhaps, even disturbed, may seek the physician. But he said things that would be written down, so that they might also afterward be read by us. Let us, then, have seen what was done in the hearts of the Jews when they heard these things: what is done in us when we hear these things, let us think on the more.

For heresies have not arisen, and certain dogmas of perversity ensnaring souls and casting them headlong into the deep, except while the good Scriptures are understood not well; and what is understood in them not well is also rashly and boldly asserted. And so, dearly beloved, we ought to hear these things very cautiously, for the grasping of which we are little ones; and with a devout heart and with trembling, as it is written, holding to this rule of soundness: that what, according to the faith with which we have been imbued, we have been able to understand, we may rejoice over as over food; but what, according to the sound rule of faith, we have not yet been able to understand, we may put away doubt and defer understanding—that is, that even if we do not know what something is, we may yet in no way doubt that it is good and true.

And I too, brothers, who have undertaken to speak to you, must be considered by you—who it is that has undertaken, and what he has undertaken: for I have undertaken to treat divine things, a man; spiritual things, a carnal one; eternal things, a mortal one. Let vain presumption also be far from me, dearly beloved, if I wish to dwell soundly in the house of God, which is *the Church of the living God, the pillar and foundation of truth*[^4]: according to my own measure I take in what I set before you; where it is opened, I am fed with you; where it is closed, I knock with you.

**2.** The Jews, then, were stirred up and indignant; rightly indeed, that a man dared to make himself equal to God; but wrongly for this reason, that in the man they did not understand God. They saw the flesh, they did not know God; they discerned the dwelling, they were ignorant of the dweller. That flesh was a temple, God dwelt within. Jesus, then, was not making the flesh equal to the Father, was not comparing the form of a servant to the Lord; not what was made for our sake, but what he was when he made us. For who Christ is—I speak to Catholics—you know, because you have believed well: not the Word only, nor the flesh only; but *the Word was made flesh, that he might dwell among us*. I rehearse about the Word what you know: *In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God*: here is equality with the Father. But *the Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us*[^5]: in respect of this flesh the Father is greater.

So the Father is both equal and greater: equal to the Word, greater than the flesh; equal to him through whom he made us, greater than him who was made for our sake. To this sound catholic rule, which you ought especially to know, which you who know it should hold, from which your faith ought by no means to slip, which is to be wrung from your heart by no arguments of men, let us direct the things we understand; and the things we perhaps do not understand, let us defer to be directed to this rule at some time, when we shall be fit.

We know, then, the Son of God equal to the Father, because we know in the beginning God the Word. Why, then, did the Jews wish to kill him? *Because he not only broke the Sabbath, but also called God his Father, making himself equal to God*: seeing the flesh, not seeing the Word. Let the Word, then, speak even against them through the flesh, and let the inner dweller sound through his dwelling, that he who can may know who dwells within.

**3.** What, then, does he say to them? *Jesus, accordingly, answered and said to them*—who were stirred up that he made himself equal to God—*Amen, amen I say to you, the Son cannot do anything of himself, except what he sees the Father doing*[^6]. What the Jews answered to this is not written; and perhaps they kept silent. Yet certain persons, who wish to be held Christians, do not keep silent, and in a manner conceive from these words certain things to be said against us: which are not to be despised, both for their sake and for ours.

For the Arian heretics, saying that—not through the flesh, but before the flesh—the Son himself who took on the flesh is less than the Father is, and is not of the same substance as the Father is, take from these words a handle for slander, and answer us: You see that the Lord Jesus, when he noticed that the Jews were moved by this, that he made himself equal to God the Father, added such words as to show that he was not equal. For it moved the Jews, they say, against Christ, that he made himself equal to God; and Christ, wishing to correct them from this commotion, and to show them that the Son is not equal to the Father—that is, equal to God—said, as if to say: Why are you angry? why are you indignant? I am not equal, for the Son cannot do anything of himself, except what he sees the Father doing. For he who cannot, they say, do anything of himself, except what he sees the Father doing, is surely less, not equal.

**4.** In this twisted and crooked rule of his heart, let the heretic hear us not yet rebuking, but still as it were inquiring, and let him unfold to us what he perceives. For I suppose, whoever you are (for let us make him as it were present here), you hold with us that *in the beginning was the Word*.

I hold it, he says.

And that *the Word was with God*?

This too, he says, I hold.

Follow on, then, and hold this more firmly, that *the Word was God*.

This too, he says, I hold; but that God is greater, this God is less.

Now this smacks of something pagan, I know not what: I supposed I was speaking with a Christian. If there is a God greater, and there is a God less, we worship two gods, not one God.

Why? he says. And do not you say that there are two gods equal to each other?

This I do not say: for this equality I so understand that I understand there also undivided love; and if undivided love, then perfect unity. For if the love which God has sent to men makes of the many hearts of men one heart, and makes the many souls of men one soul, as it is written in the Acts of the Apostles of those who believed and loved one another, *They had one soul and one heart toward God*[^7]: if, then, my soul and your soul, when we are of one mind and love each other, become one soul, how much more are God the Father and God the Son, in the spring of love, one God?

**5.** But attend to these words, by which your heart was moved, and recall with me what we were inquiring about the Word. We now hold: *the Word was God*: I add another thing, that when he had said, *This was in the beginning with God*, the Evangelist at once added, *All things were made through him*[^8]. Now I stir you up by inquiring, now I move you against yourself, and call you against yourself: only hold in memory these things about the Word, that *the Word was God, and all things were made through him*. Now hear the words by which you were moved to say the Son is less—namely, because he said, *The Son cannot do anything of himself, except what he sees the Father doing*.

So it is, he says. Unfold this for me a little.

You understand it thus, as far as I suppose: that the Father does certain things, while the Son watches how the Father does them, that he too may be able to do the things that he has seen the Father doing. You have set up as it were two craftsmen: the Father and the Son so, as also a master and a pupil, in the way that craftsman fathers are wont to teach their sons their craft. See, I come down to your carnal way of thinking, I think for the moment as you do: let us see whether this thought of ours finds a way out consistent with the things we have already spoken of the Word, alike, and alike perceive—that the Word is God, and all things were made through him.

Set up, then, the Father as it were a craftsman doing certain works, but the Son as it were a pupil who cannot do anything except what he sees the Father doing: for he watches in a manner the hands of the Father, that as he has seen him fashion, so he too may fashion something like it in his own works. But all those things that this Father does, and wishes that the Son should watch him and himself do the like—through whom does he do them? Come now, you must stand by your earlier opinion, which you reviewed with me and held with me: that in the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God, and all things were made through him. You, then, when you have held with me that all things were made through the Word, again, by a carnal taste and a childish impulse, you make for yourself in your mind a God doing, and a Word watching, so that when God has done it, the Word too may do it.

For what does God do apart from the Word? For if he does anything, then not all things were made through the Word; you have lost what you held: but if all things were made through the Word, correct what you understood badly. The Father did it, and he did it only through the Word: how does the Word watch, that it may see the Father doing without the Word what the Word may likewise do? Whatever the Father did, he did it through the Word: either it is false, *All things were made through him*. But it is true, *All things were made through him*. Did it seem too little to you, perhaps? *And without him was made nothing*[^9].

## Both of us, unlearned, let us inquire of the Master.

**6.** Withdraw, then, from that prudence of the flesh, and let us inquire in what way it was said, *The Son cannot do anything of himself, except what he sees the Father doing*. Let us inquire, if we are worthy to lay hold of it. For I confess, it is a great matter, altogether arduous, to see the Father doing through the Son—the Father and the Son not doing separate works, but the Father doing whatever work through the Son, so that no works are done either by the Father without the Son or by the Son without the Father: because *all things were made through him, and without him was made nothing*.

These things being most firmly established in the foundation of faith, now what is it to see that *the Son cannot do anything of himself, except what he sees the Father doing*? You seek, as I suppose, to know the Son doing; seek first to know the Son seeing. For surely what does he say? *The Son cannot do anything of himself, except what he sees the Father doing*. Attend to what he said, *except what he sees the Father doing*. The seeing goes before, and the doing follows: for he sees in order to do. Why do you already seek to know how he does, while you do not yet know how he sees? Why do you run to what is later, leaving what is first? He said himself seeing and doing; not, doing and seeing: for he cannot do anything of himself, except what he sees the Father doing.

Do you wish me to unfold for you how he does? Unfold for me how he sees. If you cannot unfold this, neither can I that: if you are not yet fit to perceive this, neither am I that. Let each of us, then, inquire, let each knock, that each may deserve to receive. Why, as if learned, do you slander the unlearned? I to the doing, you to the seeing, both of us unlearned—let us inquire of the Master, let us not in his school childishly quarrel.

Yet together we have already learned that all things were made through him. Therefore it is manifest that the Father does not do other works, which the Son sees, that he himself may do similar ones; but the Father does the same works through the Son, because all things were made through the Word. But how God does, who knows? I do not say how he made the world, but how he made your eye, to which, clinging carnally, you compare invisible things with visible. For you think such things of God as you have been accustomed to see with these eyes. But if God could be seen with these eyes, he would not say, *Blessed are the pure in heart, for they will see God*[^10]. Therefore you have the eye of the body for seeing a craftsman, but you do not yet have the eye of the heart for seeing God: and so what you are wont to see in a craftsman, you wish to transfer to God. Set earthly things on the earth; the heart upward.

## Conduct leads to understanding.

**7.** What then, dearly beloved? Are we going to unfold what we have asked—how the Word sees, how the Father is seen by the Word, what the seeing of the Word is? I am not so bold, so rash, as to promise to unfold this both for me and for you: somehow I guess at your measure, but I know my own. If, then, it is agreeable, let us not delay longer; let us run through the reading, and see the carnal hearts disturbed by the words of the Lord—disturbed to this end, that they may not remain in what they hold.

Let some plaything be wrung from them, as from children, with which they distract themselves to their harm, that more useful things may be implanted in those who are more grown-up, that those who were creeping on the earth may be able to make progress. Rise, seek, sigh, pant with desire, and knock at what is closed. But if we do not yet desire, do not yet long, do not yet sigh, we will either cast pearls to anyone at all, or we ourselves will find only pearls of whatever sort.

Let me, then, dearly beloved, have stirred up desire in your heart. Conduct leads to understanding: a kind of life leads to a kind of life. One life is earthly, another heavenly; one life is of cattle, another of men, another of angels. The life of cattle seethes with earthly pleasures, seeks only earthly things, is bent down and cast forth in them: the life of angels is heavenly only: the life of men is midway between that of angels and that of cattle. If a man lives according to the flesh, he is compared to cattle; if he lives according to the spirit, he is joined to the angels.

When you live according to the spirit, inquire even in that angelic life whether you are little or grown. For if you are still little, the angels say to you: Grow; we eat bread, you are nourished with milk, the milk of faith, that you may come to the food of his form. But if there is still a longing for filthy pleasures, if frauds are still planned, if lies are not avoided, if lies are heaped up with perjuries—does so unclean a heart dare to say: Unfold to me how the Word sees, even if I could, even if I myself already saw?

But further, if perhaps I am not in these ways of conduct, and yet am far from that seeing—how much farther is he who is not yet caught up by that supernal desire, weighed down by earthly desires?

There is a great difference between one who turns away and one who desires; and again a great difference between one who desires and one who enjoys. You live like cattle: you turn away. The angels: they enjoy fully. But you, if you do not live like cattle, you now do not turn away: you desire something, and do not grasp it; you have begun, by that very desire, the life of the angels. Let it grow in you, and be perfected in you; and may you grasp this, not from me, but from him who made both me and you.

**8.** Yet the Lord did not leave us just anyhow, who wished it to be understood that in what he said, *The Son cannot do anything of himself, except what he sees the Father doing*, the Father does not do other works which the Son sees, and the Son other works when he has seen the Father doing—but the same works both the Father and the Son. For he went on to say, *For whatever things he does, these the Son likewise does*[^11].

Not, when he has done them, does the Son likewise do other things; but *whatever things he does, these the Son likewise does*. If the Son does these things which the Father does, then the Father does them through the Son: if through the Son he does the things which the Father does, then the Father does not do some things and the Son other things; but the works of the Father and the Son are the same. And how does the Son too do the same things? Both the same and likewise. Lest perhaps the same, but unlike: *the same*, he says, *and likewise*. And how could he do the same things not likewise? Take an example, which I think is not too great for you: when we write letters, our heart makes them first, and then our hand. Surely you all cried out approval just now, except because you recognized it? What I said is certain, and manifest to us all. Letters are made first by our heart, then by our body: the hand serves the commanding heart; the heart and the hand make the same letters: surely the heart not other, the hand not other? The hand indeed makes the same letters, but not likewise: for our heart makes them intelligibly, but the hand visibly.

See how the same things are made unlike. So it was too little for the Lord to say, *Whatever things the Father does, these the Son also does*, unless he added, *and likewise*. For what if you understood it in this way—as whatever things the heart does, these the hand also does, but not likewise? But here he added, *these the Son likewise does*. If he both does these things and does them likewise, awake; let the Jew be restrained, let the Christian believe, let the heretic be refuted: the Son is equal to the Father.

**9.** *For the Father loves the Son, and shows him everything that he himself does*[^12]. See, there it is, *shows*. He shows to one who is as it were what? Surely as it were to one seeing. We come back to that which we cannot unfold, how the Word sees. See, man was made through the Word; but man has eyes, has ears, has hands, diverse members in the body: through the eyes he can see, through the ears he can hear, through the hands work; diverse members, diverse offices of the members. That member cannot do what another can: yet, because of the unity of the body, the eye sees both for itself and for the ear, and the ear hears both for itself and for the eye. Is anything of the kind to be supposed in the Word, since all things are through him? And Scripture said in a Psalm: *Understand, you who are foolish among the people; and you fools, be wise at last. He who planted the ear, will he not hear? Or he who formed the eye, does he not consider?*[^13] If, then, the Word formed the eye, because all things are through the Word; if the Word planted the ear, because all things are through the Word: we cannot say, The Word does not hear, the Word does not see; lest the Psalm rebuke us and say, *You fools, be wise at last*.

And so, if the Word hears and the Word sees, the Son hears and the Son sees: yet are we to seek even in him eyes and ears in different places? Does he hear from one place, see from another; and can his ear not do what the eye does, and the eye not do what the ear can? Or is he wholly sight, and wholly hearing? Perhaps so: indeed not perhaps, but truly so; provided, however, that both his seeing and his hearing be in a way far other than ours. Both seeing and hearing are together in the Word, nor is hearing one thing there and seeing another; but the hearing is sight, and the sight hearing.

## Return, return to the heart.

**10.** And we, who hear in one way and see in another—how do we know this? We return, perhaps, to ourselves, if we are not transgressors, to whom it was said: *Return, transgressors, to the heart*[^14]. Return to the heart: why do you go away from yourselves, and perish from yourselves? Why do you go the ways of solitude? You wander astray; return.

To where?

To the Lord. It is quick: first return to your heart; an exile from yourself, you wander outside; you do not know yourself, and you ask by whom you were made! Return, return to the heart, take yourself away from the body: your body is your dwelling; your heart perceives even through your body, but your body is not what your heart is; leave even your body, return to your heart.

In your body you found eyes in one place, ears in another: in your heart do you find this? Or in your heart do you not have ears? Of which, then, did the Lord say, *He who has ears for hearing, let him hear*[^15]? Or in your heart do you not have eyes? Whence the Apostle says, *The eyes of your heart enlightened*[^16]. Return to the heart; see there what you perceive, perhaps, about God, because there is the image of God. In the inner man Christ dwells, in the inner man you are renewed to the image of God, in his image recognize its author.

See how all the senses of the body report inwardly to the heart what they have perceived outwardly: see how many ministers one inner ruler has, and what he does within himself even without these ministers. The eyes report to the heart white and black; the ears report to the same heart tuneful and discordant things; the nostrils report to the same heart fragrant and foul things; the taste reports to the same heart bitter and sweet things; the touch reports to the same heart smooth and rough things: and the heart reports to itself just and unjust things. Your heart both sees and hears, and judges the other things perceived by sense; and where the senses of the body do not reach, it discerns just and unjust, evil and good things.

Show me the eyes, ears, nostrils of your heart. Diverse are the things that are referred to your heart, and diverse members are not found there. In your flesh you hear in one place, see in another: in your heart you hear where you see. If this is the image, how much more powerful is he whose image it is? Therefore the Son both hears and the Son sees, and the very seeing and hearing is the Son: and for him to hear is the same as to be, and for him to see is the same as to be. For you to see is not the same as to be: because even if you lose your sight, you can be; and if you lose your hearing, you can be.

**11.** Do we suppose we have knocked? Has something been raised up in us by which we may, even faintly, surmise whence the light comes to us? I think, brothers, that when we speak these things, and when we meditate, we exercise ourselves. And when we exercise ourselves in them, and again are as it were bent back by our own weight to these accustomed things, we are like those with sore eyes, when they are brought out to see the light, if perhaps before they had no sight at all, and begin somehow to repair that very sight through the diligence of physicians. And when the physician wishes to prove how much health has come to them, he tries to show them what they desired to see and could not when they were blind: and now, the keenness of their eyes somehow returning, they are brought out to the light; and when they have seen, they are in a manner beaten back by the very brightness, and answer the physician who is pointing it out: Now, now I saw it, but I cannot see it.

What, then, does the physician do? He calls them back to accustomed things, and adds a salve, that he may nourish the desire toward that which was seen and could not be seen, and from that very desire he may be more fully cured; and if any biting remedies are applied for the repairing of health, he bears them bravely, so that, kindled with love of that light, he says to himself: When will it be that I may see with sound eyes what I could not with wounded and weak ones? He urges the physician, and asks that he may be cured.

Therefore, brothers, if perhaps something of the kind has been done in your hearts, if you have somehow raised your heart to see the Word, and, beaten back by its light, have fallen back to accustomed things: ask the physician to apply the biting salves, the commandments of righteousness. There is something for you to see, but there is not yet that by which you may see. You did not believe me before that there is something to see: led by a kind of reasoning you were brought; you drew near, you strained, you throbbed, you fled back. You know surely that there is something to see, but that you are not fit to see it. Therefore be cured. What are the salves? Do not lie, do not perjure, do not commit adultery, do not steal, do not defraud. But you are accustomed to it, and with some pain you are called back from the custom: this is what bites, but it heals. For I tell you more freely, out of fear both mine and yours: if you cease to be cured, and neglect to be fit to enjoy this light through the soundness of your eyes, you will love darkness; and by loving darkness you will remain in darkness; and by remaining in darkness you will even be cast into the outer darkness: *there will be weeping and gnashing of teeth*[^17]. If the love of light did nothing in you, let the fear of pain do it.

**12.** I think I have spoken sufficiently, and yet I have not finished the Gospel reading: if I were to say the rest, I would burden you, and I fear that even what has been drawn in may be poured out; let these things, then, suffice for your Charity. We are debtors, not now, but always, as long as we live; because for your sake we live. Yet console our weak, laborious, perilous life in this world by living well; do not sadden us and wear us out by your evil ways. For when we are offended by your evil life, if we flee from you, and separate ourselves from you, and do not come to you, will you not complain and say: If we were sick, you would have cured us; and if we were weak, you would have visited us? See, we cure, see, we visit; but let it not be done to us as you have heard from the Apostle: *I fear lest I have labored among you in vain*[^18].
