# Tractate 77

## *Peace I leave with you, my peace I give to you* (Jn 14:25-27).

## What does he leave us when he ascends from us but himself, so long as he does not depart from us? For he himself is our peace, who has made the two one. He himself, then, is our peace, both when we believe that he is, and when we shall see him as he is.

**1.** In the preceding passage of the holy Gospel, which this one that has just been read follows, the Lord Jesus had said that he and the Father would come to those who love him and would make their dwelling with them. And already earlier he had said of the Holy Spirit: *But you will know him, because he will abide with you and will be in you*[^1]; from which we understood that God the Trinity abides together in the saints as in his temple. But now he says: *These things I have spoken to you while abiding with you*[^2]. That dwelling, then, is one thing, which he promised would come; this is another, which he testifies is present. That one is spiritual and is rendered inwardly to minds; this one is bodily, presented outwardly to the eyes and ears. That one beatifies forever those who have been set free; this one visits in time those who are to be set free. According to that one the Lord does not depart from those who love him; according to this one he goes and departs. *These things,* he says, *I have spoken to you while abiding with you*[^3] — that is, by the bodily presence in which he was speaking with them visibly.

**2.** *But the Paraclete,* he says, *the Holy Spirit, whom the Father will send in my name, he will teach you all things and will bring to your remembrance all things whatsoever I have said to you*[^4]. Does the Son perhaps speak, and the Holy Spirit teach, so that when the Son speaks we take in the words, but when the Holy Spirit teaches we understand those same words? As if the Son were to speak without the Holy Spirit, or the Holy Spirit were to teach without the Son; or as if it were not the case that both the Son teaches and the Holy Spirit speaks, and that when God says and teaches anything, the Trinity itself says and teaches? But because there is a Trinity, it was fitting that its individual persons be introduced to us, and that we hear it distinctly but understand it inseparably. Hear the Father speaking where you read: *The Lord said to me, You are my Son*[^5]; hear him also teaching where you read: *Everyone who has heard from the Father and has learned comes to me*[^6]. The Son you have just heard speaking; for of himself he says: *Whatsoever I have said to you*[^7]; and if you wish to know him teaching as well, recall the master: *One,* he says, *is your Master, Christ*[^8]. As for the Holy Spirit, whom you have just heard teaching where it is said, *He will teach you all things*[^9], hear him also speaking where you read in the Acts of the Apostles that the Holy Spirit said to blessed Peter: *Go with them, for I have sent them*[^10]. The whole Trinity, then, both speaks and teaches; but unless it were also commended to us individually, human weakness could in no way grasp it. Since, therefore, it is altogether inseparable, it would never be known to be a Trinity if it were always spoken of inseparably; for even when we say the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit, we certainly do not say them at the same time, although they themselves cannot but be at the same time. As for what he added, *will bring to your remembrance*[^11], we ought to understand also this: that we are commanded not to forget those most salutary admonitions, which belong to the grace of which the Spirit reminds us.

## When about to go he promises peace; his own peace he will give us when he comes at the end.

**3.** *Peace,* he says, *I leave with you, my peace I give to you*[^12]. This is what we read in the prophet: *Peace upon peace*[^13]. Peace he leaves us when about to go; his own peace he will give us when he comes at the end. Peace he leaves us in this world; his own peace he will give us in the world to come. His peace he leaves us, in which, while we remain, we conquer the enemy; his own peace he will give us when we shall reign without an enemy. Peace he leaves us, that we may here love one another as well; his own peace he will give us where we can never disagree. Peace he leaves us, that we may not judge one another about our hidden things while we are in this world; his own peace he will give us when he will make manifest the thoughts of the heart, and then each one will have his praise from God. Yet in him and from him is our peace, whether the peace he leaves us when going to the Father, or the peace he will give us when he leads us to the Father. But what does he leave us when he ascends from us but himself, so long as he does not depart from us? *For he himself is our peace, who has made the two one*[^14]. He himself, then, is our peace, both when we believe that he is, and when we see him as he is. For if, *while we are in* a corruptible *body* that weighs down the soul, when *we walk by faith, not by sight*[^15], he does not abandon those who are on pilgrimage away from him, how much more, when we have come to that very sight, will he fill us from himself?

**4.** But why is it that where he says, *Peace I leave with you,* he did not add *my,* whereas where he says, *I give to you,* there he said *my*[^16]? Is *my* to be understood even where it is not stated, since what is said once can be referred to both? Or does something perhaps lie hidden here too, which is to be sought and asked for, and to be opened to those who knock? For what if he wished his own peace to be understood as the kind that he himself has? But this peace which he leaves us in this world should rather be called ours than his. For in him, who has no sin whatsoever, nothing in himself resists him; but we now have such a peace that in it we still say: *Forgive us our debts*[^17]. There is, then, some peace for us, since we delight in the law of God according to the inner man; but it is not full, because we see another law in our members at war with the law of our mind. Likewise among ourselves there is peace for us, because we trust one another that we love one another; but neither is that full, because we do not see one another's thoughts of the heart, and we suppose some things about one another, whether for better or for worse, that are not in us. And so this peace, even though it has been left us by him, is our peace; for unless it were from him, we should not have even such a peace, but he himself does not have such a peace. If we hold to the end the kind we have received, we shall have the kind he has, where nothing in us will resist us and nothing will lie hidden from one another in our hearts. Nor am I unaware that these words of the Lord can also be taken so that it seems a repetition of the same thought — *Peace I leave with you, my peace I give to you*[^18] — so that what he had said, *peace,* he repeated by saying, *my peace*; and what he had said, *I leave with you,* he repeated by saying, *I give to you.* Let each take it as he will; but it pleases me — and you too, I believe, my beloved brothers — so to hold to that peace in which we conquer the adversary in concord, that we may long for the peace where we shall have no adversary.

**5.** As for what the Lord added, saying, *Not as the world gives do I give to you*[^19] — what else does this mean but, I do not give to you as men give who love the world? They give peace to one another in order to enjoy fully, not God, but their friend the world, without the trouble of lawsuits and wars; and when they give peace to the righteous so as not to persecute them, the peace cannot be true where there is no true concord, because their hearts are disjoined. For just as one is called a partner who joins a share, so he is to be called of one accord who joins hearts. We, therefore, dearly beloved, to whom Christ leaves peace and gives us his own peace — not as the world does, but as he through whom the world was made does — that we may be of one accord, let us join our hearts to one another and have one heart on high, lest it be corrupted in the earth.
