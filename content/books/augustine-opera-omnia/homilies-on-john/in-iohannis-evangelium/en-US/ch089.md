# Tractate 89

## *If I had not come and spoken to them, they would not have sin*[^1]. — His coming became as salutary to those who believe as it became destructive to those who do not believe.

## The sin by which all sins are held.

**1.** The Lord had said above to his disciples: *If they persecuted me, they will also persecute you; if they kept my word, they will keep yours also: but all these things they will do to you on account of my name, because they do not know him who sent me*[^2]. But if we ask of whom he said this, we find that he had come to these words from what he had said: *If the world hates you, know that it hated me before you*[^3]; but now, in what he added, *If I had not come and spoken to them, they would not have sin,* he points more expressly to the Jews. Of these, then, he was saying those things too; for the very connection of the words indicates this. For of these he says, *If I had not come and spoken to them, they would not have sin,* of whom he was saying, *If they persecuted me, they will also persecute you; if they kept my word, they will keep yours also: but all these things they will do to you on account of my name, because they do not know him who sent me*: for to these words he joins this too: *If I had not come and spoken to them, they would not have sin.* The Jews, then, persecuted Christ, which the Gospel most evidently indicates; to the Jews Christ spoke, not to other nations: in them, therefore, he wished the world to be understood, which hates Christ and his disciples; nay rather, not in them alone, but he showed that these too belong to that same world.

What is it, then: *If I had not come and spoken to them, they would not have sin*[^4]? Were the Jews without sin before Christ had come to them in the flesh? Who, even the most foolish, would say this? But he wishes a certain great sin, not every sin, to be understood, as it were under a general name. For this is the sin by which all sins are held — which, if anyone does not have it, all his sins are forgiven him: and this is, that they did not believe in Christ, who came for this very reason, that he might be believed in. This sin, if he had not come, they certainly would not have. For his coming became as salutary to those who believe as it became destructive to those who do not believe: just as the head and prince of the Apostles himself, as they said of themselves, was *to some indeed an odor of life unto life, but to others an odor of death unto death*[^5].

**2.** But what he added, saying, *But now they have no excuse for their sin*[^6], can disturb those who ask whether those to whom Christ did not come, and to whom he did not speak, have an excuse for their sin. For if they have none, why is it said here that these have none for the reason that he came and spoke to them? But if they have one, do they have it so as to be set free from punishment, or so as to be more mildly punished? To these inquiries, according to my capacity, the Lord granting, I answer that they have an excuse, not for all their sin, but for this sin by which they did not believe in Christ — those to whom he did not come, and to whom he did not speak. But these are not in that number to whom he came in his disciples, and to whom he spoke through his disciples, which he does even now: for through his Church he comes to the Gentiles, and through the Church he speaks to the Gentiles. For to this belongs what he says: *He who receives you receives me*[^7]; and, *He who despises you despises me*[^8]. *Or do you wish,* says the apostle Paul, *to receive a proof of the Christ who speaks in me*[^9]?

**3.** It remains to inquire whether those who, before Christ came in the Church to the Gentiles, and before they heard his Gospel, were overtaken by the end of this life, or are being overtaken, can have this excuse. They certainly can, but not for that reason can they escape damnation. *For whoever sinned without the Law will also perish without the Law; and whoever sinned in the Law will be judged through the Law*[^10]. And indeed these words of the Apostle — since what he says, *they will perish,* sounds more terrible than what he says, *they will be judged* — seem to show that this excuse not only does not help at all, but even burdens the more. For those who will excuse themselves because they did not hear, *will perish without the Law.*

**4.** But whether those who, when they heard, despised, or even resisted — and that not only by contradicting, but also by persecuting with hatred those from whom they heard — are to be reckoned among those of whom something lighter seems to have sounded in what was said, *they will be judged through the Law,* is rightly asked. But if it is one thing to perish without the Law, another to be judged through the Law, and the former is the heavier, the latter the lighter; without doubt these are not to be placed in this lighter punishment, because they neither sinned in the Law, but altogether refused to receive the Law of Christ, and wished it, as far as concerned them, to be utterly nothing. But those sin in the Law who are in the Law, that is, who receive it, and confess it to be holy, and the commandment holy and just and good; but through weakness do not fulfill what they cannot doubt is most rightly commanded by it. These are they who can perhaps in some way be distinguished from the perdition of those who are without the Law: if, that is, what the Apostle says, *they will be judged through the Law,* is to be taken as though he said, they will not perish; which would be a wonder if it were so. For his discourse was not about unbelievers and believers, that he should say this, but about Gentiles and Jews: who, both alike, unless they are saved in that Savior who came to seek what had perished, will without doubt belong to perdition. Although it can be said that some will perish more heavily, others more lightly — that is, that some will suffer heavier, others lighter punishments in their perdition. For a man is said to perish to God whenever he is separated by punishment from that blessedness which God gives to his saints; but the diversity of punishments is as great as the diversity of sins; and how this stands the divine wisdom judges more deeply than human conjecture searches out or utters. These, certainly, to whom Christ came and to whom he spoke, do not have, for the great sin of unbelief, that excuse by which they could say: We did not see, we did not hear — whether that excuse would not be accepted by him whose judgments are inscrutable, or would be accepted, even if not so as to be freed from all damnation, yet certainly so as to be somewhat more leniently damned.

**5.** *He who hates me,* he says, *hates my Father also*[^11]. Here perhaps it may be said to us: Who can hate one whom he does not know? And certainly, before he said, *If I had not come and spoken to them, they would not have sin,* he had said to his disciples: *These things they will do to you, because they do not know him who sent me*[^12]. How, then, do they not know and yet hate? For if they suppose of him not what he is, but some other thing, then they are found to hate not him, but that which they feign, or rather, in erring, suspect. And yet, unless men could hate what they do not know, the truth would not have said both — that they both do not know and hate his Father. But how this can come about, if, by the help of the Lord, it can be shown through us — because this discussion must now be closed — it cannot be done now.
