# Tractate 78

## *If you loved me, you would indeed rejoice, because I go to the Father, for the Father is greater than I*[^1].

## The form of a servant was added, the form of God did not withdraw; the one was assumed, the other was not consumed. On account of the one he says, *The Father is greater than I*; but on account of the other, *I and the Father are one.* —  — 

**1.** We have received, brothers, the words of the Lord saying to his disciples: *Let not your heart be troubled, neither let it be afraid. You have heard that I said to you, I go away, and I come to you; if you loved me, you would indeed rejoice, because I go to the Father, for the Father is greater than I*[^2]. From this, then, their heart could be troubled and afraid, because he was going away from them, although he would come to them — lest perhaps in this interval the wolf should attack the flock through the shepherd's absence. But those from whom the man was departing, God was not abandoning; and this same Christ is both man and God. He went, then, by that which he was as man, and remained by that which he was as God; he went by that whereby he was in one place, he remained by that whereby he was everywhere. Why, then, should the heart be troubled and afraid, when he so forsook their eyes that he did not forsake their heart? Although even God, who is contained in no place, departs from the hearts of those who forsake him in their morals, not on foot; and comes to those who turn to him not in face but in faith, and draw near to him in mind, not in flesh.

But that they might understand that he had said, *I go away and I come to you*[^3], according to that which he was as man, he added, saying: *If you loved me, you would indeed rejoice, because I go to the Father, for the Father is greater than I.* By that, then, in which the Son is not equal to the Father, he was going to go to the Father, from whom he is to come to judge the living and the dead; but by that in which the only-begotten one is equal to the one who begot him, he never withdraws from the Father, but is wholly everywhere together with him in equal divinity, which no place contains. For *since he was in the form of God,* as the Apostle says, *he did not count it robbery to be equal to God*[^4]. For how could a nature be robbery which was not usurped but born? *But he emptied himself, taking the form of a servant*[^5] — not, then, losing that one, but taking this one. Emptying himself in such a way that here he appeared less than he remained with the Father. For the form of a servant was added, the form of God did not withdraw; the one was assumed, the other was not consumed. On account of the one he says, *The Father is greater than I*[^6]; but on account of the other, *I and the Father are one*[^7].

**2.** Let the Arian attend to this, and by attending become sound, lest by contention he become vain, or, what is worse, insane. For this is the form of a servant, in which the Son of God is less, not than the Father alone, but also than the Holy Spirit; and not only that, but even than himself, because this same one in the form of God is greater than himself. For the man Christ is not on that account not called the Son of God, seeing that even his flesh alone in the tomb deserved to be so named. For what else do we confess when we say that we believe in the only-begotten Son of God, who under Pontius Pilate was crucified and buried? And what of him but his flesh was buried, without the soul? And through this, when we believe in the Son of God who was buried, we assuredly call the Son of God also the flesh that alone was buried. This same Christ, then, the Son of God, equal to the Father in the form of God, because he emptied himself, not losing the form of God but taking the form of a servant, is greater even than himself; because the form of God, which was not lost, is greater than that of the servant, which was taken. What wonder, then, or what unworthiness is there, if, speaking according to this form of a servant, the Son of God says, *The Father is greater than I*[^8]; and speaking according to the form of God, this same Son of God says, *I and the Father are one*[^9]? For they are one, according to that which *the Word was God*; the Father is greater, according to that which *the Word was made flesh*[^10].

I will say something too that the Arians and the Eunomians cannot deny: according to this form of a servant, the boy Christ was even less than his parents, when as a little one he *was subject,* as is written, to his elders[^11]. What then, heretic? Since Christ is God and man, he speaks as man, and you slander God? In himself he commends the human nature; in him do you dare to deform the divine? Unbeliever, ungrateful one, do you for this reason diminish him who made you, because he says what he was made for your sake? For the Son, through whom man was made, equal to the Father, was made man in order to be less than the Father; and if this had not happened, what would man be?

## The human nature assumed by the Word is to be congratulated.

**3.** Let our Lord and Master say plainly: *If you loved me, you would indeed rejoice, because I go to the Father, for the Father is greater than I.* With the disciples let us hear the words of the teacher; let us not with strangers follow the cunning of the deceiver. Let us acknowledge the twofold substance of Christ: the divine, namely, by which he is equal to the Father; the human, by which the Father is greater. But both together are not two, but one Christ; lest God be a quaternity, not a Trinity. For just as one man is a rational soul and flesh, so one Christ is God and man; and through this, Christ is God, a rational soul, and flesh. We confess Christ in all these, Christ in each. Who, then, is he through whom the world was made? Christ Jesus, but in the form of God. Who is he who was crucified under Pontius Pilate? Christ Jesus, but in the form of a servant. Likewise concerning the individual parts of which man consists. Who was not abandoned in hell? Christ Jesus, but in the soul alone. Who, about to rise again, lay for three days in the tomb? Christ Jesus, but in the flesh alone. He is called Christ, then, in each of these as well. But all these are not two, or three, but one Christ. For this reason, then, he said: *If you loved me, you would indeed rejoice, because I go to the Father*[^12]; because the human nature is to be congratulated, in that it was so assumed by the only-begotten Word that it was established immortal in heaven, and so earth became sublime that incorruptible dust sat at the right hand of the Father. For in this way he said he would go to the Father. For assuredly he was going to him with whom he was. But this was to go to him and to depart from us: to change and make immortal what he took up as mortal from us, and to lift up to heaven that by which he was on earth for us. Who would not rejoice at this, who so loves Christ that he congratulates his own nature, now immortal in Christ, and hopes that he himself will be such through Christ?
