# Homily 17

*He who went down first into the pool after the moving of the water was made well*[^1].

## To go down into the troubled water is to believe humbly in the Lord's Passion. There one was healed, signifying unity; afterward, whoever came was not healed, because whoever is outside unity cannot be healed. He chose one to heal, that he might signify unity.

**1.** There ought to be no wonder that a miracle was done by God; for it would be a wonder if a man had done it. We ought to rejoice rather than wonder that our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ was made man, more than that, being God, he did divine things among men. For it is more for our salvation that he was made for the sake of men, than that he did things among men; and it is more that he healed the vices of souls, than that he healed the diseases of bodies that were to die. But because the soul itself did not know him by whom it was to be healed, and had eyes in the flesh with which to see things done bodily, but did not yet have sound eyes in the heart, by which it might know the hidden God, he did what it could see, that it might be healed in that by which it could not see.

He entered a place where there lay a great multitude of the sick, the blind, the lame, the withered; and although he was the physician both of souls and of bodies, and one who had come to heal all the souls of those who would believe, of those sick ones he chose one to heal, that he might signify unity. If we consider the doer with a mediocre heart, and as it were with merely human grasp and understanding, then, as far as power is concerned, he accomplished nothing great; and as far as kindness, he did little. So many lay there, and one was cured, when by one word he could have raised them all up. What, then, is to be understood, except that that power and that goodness were acting more upon what souls should understand in his deeds for eternal salvation, than upon what bodies should merit for temporal salvation?

For the health of bodies, which is truly awaited from the Lord, will be at the end, in the resurrection of the dead: then what lives will not die; then what is healed will not sicken; then what is satisfied will not hunger or thirst; then what is renewed will not grow old. But now, in those deeds of our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ, both the opened eyes of the blind were closed in death, and the bound limbs of the paralyzed were dissolved in death; and whatever was healed temporally in mortal members failed at the end; but the soul that believed made the passage to eternal life.

To the soul, then, that was to believe, whose sins he had come to forgive, to heal whose diseases he had humbled himself, he gave a great sign in this sick man healed. Of this matter and this sign the deep sacred meaning, as far as the Lord deigns to grant, I will speak as I can, with you attentive and helping our weakness by prayer. But whatever I cannot, he himself will supply in you, by whose help I do what I can.

**2.** About this pool, which was surrounded by five porticoes, in which lay a great multitude of the sick, I remember we have treated repeatedly; and I am about to say a thing which many will recognize with me rather than learn. Yet it is not amiss to repeat even what is known, that both those who did not know may be instructed, and those who knew may be confirmed. Accordingly, things as it were known are to be touched on briefly, not idly drummed in.

That pool and that water seem to me to have signified the people of the Jews. For that peoples are signified by the name of waters, the Apocalypse of John openly shows us: where, when many waters were shown to him, and he had asked what they were, he received the answer that they were peoples[^2]. That water, then, that is, that people, was shut in by the five books of Moses, as by five porticoes. But those books brought the sick to light, they did not heal them. For the Law convicted sinners, it did not absolve them. Therefore the letter without grace made guilty those whom grace, when they confessed, set free. For this the Apostle says: *For if a law had been given that could give life, righteousness would altogether be from the Law*. Why, then, was the Law given? He goes on and says: *But Scripture shut up all things under sin, that the promise by faith in Jesus Christ might be given to those who believe*[^3].

What is plainer? Have not these words expounded to us both the five porticoes and the multitude of the sick? The five porticoes are the Law. Why did the five porticoes not heal the sick?

Because *if a law had been given that could give life, righteousness would altogether be from the Law*.

Why, then, did they hold those whom they did not heal?

Because *Scripture shut up all things under sin, that the promise by faith in Jesus Christ might be given to those who believe*.

**3.** What, then, was being done, that in that troubled water those should be healed who could not be healed in the porticoes? For suddenly the water was seen to be troubled, and by whom it was troubled was not seen. You may believe that this was wont to be done by an angelic power, yet not without some signifying sacred meaning. After the water was troubled, one who could went down, and he alone was healed; after him, whoever should send himself down would do so in vain.

What, then, does this mean, except that one Christ came to the people of the Jews; and by doing great things, by teaching useful things, he troubled the sinners, troubled the water by his presence, and stirred them up to his Passion? But he troubled it while hidden. For *if they had known, they would never have crucified the Lord of glory*[^4]. To go down, then, into the troubled water is to believe humbly in the Lord's Passion. There one was healed, signifying unity; afterward whoever came was not healed, because whoever is outside unity cannot be healed.

## The number forty is sacred.

**4.** Let us see, then, what he wished to signify in that one whom, himself too keeping the mystery of unity, as I said before, he deigned of so many sick ones to heal as one. He found in his years a certain number of sickness: *he had been thirty-eight years in his infirmity*[^5]. How this number pertains rather to sickness than to health must be explained a little more carefully. I want you attentive; the Lord will be present, that I may speak fittingly, and that you may hear sufficiently.

The number forty is commended to us as sacred in a certain perfection. I think it is known to your Charity: the divine Scriptures very often attest it. Fasting is consecrated by this number: you know it well. For Moses fasted forty days, and Elijah as many, and our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ himself fulfilled this number of fasting. By Moses is signified the Law, by Elijah are signified the Prophets, by the Lord is signified the Gospel. Therefore on that mountain three appeared, where he showed himself to the disciples in the brightness of his countenance and his garment. For he appeared in the midst between Moses and Elijah, as though the Gospel had testimony from the Law and the Prophets. Whether, then, in the Law, or in the Prophets, or in the Gospel, the number forty is commended to us in fasting.

Now the great and general fasting is to abstain from iniquities and the unlawful pleasures of the world; this is the perfect fasting: *that, renouncing ungodliness and worldly desires, we may live temperately, and justly, and devoutly in this world*[^6]. What reward does the Apostle add to this fasting? He goes on and says: *awaiting that blessed hope, and the manifestation of the glory of the blessed God, and of our Savior Jesus Christ*[^7]. In this world, then, we celebrate as it were a Lent of abstinence, when we live well, when we abstain from iniquities and from unlawful pleasures. But because this abstinence will not be without reward, we await that blessed hope, and the revelation of the glory of the great God, and of our Savior Jesus Christ. In that hope, when out of hope it has been made a reality, we are to receive the reward of a denarius. For that very reward is paid to the laborers working in the vineyard, according to the Gospel[^8], which I believe you remember; for not everything is to be recounted, as though to the untaught and unskilled. The denarius, then, which received its name from the number ten, is paid; and, joined to the number forty, it makes fifty: whence with labor we celebrate Lent before Easter; but with gladness, as having received the reward, the fifty days after Easter. For to this as it were salutary labor of a good work, which pertains to the number forty, is added the denarius of rest and happiness, that it may become fifty.

**5.** The Lord Jesus himself also signified this much more openly, when after the resurrection he spent forty days on earth with his disciples[^9]; but on the fortieth day, when he had ascended into heaven, after ten days had passed he sent the reward of the Holy Spirit[^10]. These things were signified, and by certain significations the realities themselves were anticipated. We are fed by significations, that we may be able to come to the realities themselves that endure. For we are laborers, and we still labor in the vineyard: when the day is finished, the work finished, the reward will be restored. But what laborer endures to receive the reward, except one who is fed while he labors? For will you too give your laborer only the reward? Will you not also bring him something by which to repair his strength at his labor? Surely you feed the one to whom you are going to give the reward.

Accordingly, the Lord too feeds us as we labor in these significations of the Scriptures. For if that gladness of the sacred meanings to be understood is taken away from us, we fail in our labor, and there will be no one to come to the reward.

## The Law is fulfilled in the twofold commandment of love.

**6.** How, then, is the work perfected by the number forty? Perhaps because the Law was given in ten commandments, and the Law was to be preached throughout the whole world; which whole world is comprised in four parts—east and west, south and north—whence the denarius, multiplied by four, comes to forty. Or because through the Gospel, which has four books, the Law is fulfilled: for in the Gospel it was said, *I have not come to abolish the Law, but to fulfill it*[^11]. Whether, then, that cause, or this, or some other more probable one that lies hidden from us—it does not lie hidden from the more learned—it is yet certain that by the number forty is signified a certain perfection in good works, which good works are exercised especially in a certain abstinence from the unlawful desires of the world, that is, in the general fasting. Hear the Apostle too saying, *The fullness of the Law is love*[^12]. Love from where? Through the grace of God, through the Holy Spirit. For we would not have it from ourselves, as though making it for ourselves. It is the gift of God, and a great gift: *For the love of God*, he says, *has been poured out in our hearts through the Holy Spirit who has been given to us*[^13]. Love, then, fulfills the Law, and most truly it was said, *The fullness of the Law is love*. Let us seek this love, how it is commended by the Lord.

Remember what I proposed: I want to explain the number thirty-eight years in that sick man, why that thirty-eighth number is of sickness rather than of health. Therefore, as I was saying, love fulfills the Law. To the fullness of the Law in all works pertains the number forty; but in love two commandments are commended to us. Look, I beg you, and fix in memory what I say; do not be despisers of the word, lest your soul become a path, where the grains cast do not sprout: *And the birds of heaven will come*, he says, *and gather them up*[^14]. Take it in, and store it in your hearts. Two commandments of love have been commended by the Lord: *You shall love the Lord your God with your whole heart, and with your whole soul, and with your whole mind; and: You shall love your neighbor as yourself. On these two commandments depend the whole Law and the Prophets*[^15].

Deservedly that widow too put all her substance, two mites, into the gifts of God[^16]; deservedly too, for that sick man wounded by robbers, the innkeeper received two coins with which he might be healed[^17]; deservedly Jesus spent two days among the Samaritans, that he might confirm them in love[^18]. By this number two, then, when something good is signified, love above all, divided in two, is commended. If, then, the number forty has the perfection of the Law, and the Law is not fulfilled except in the twofold commandment of love, why do you wonder that he was sick who was two short of forty?

**7.** Let us therefore now see by what sacred sign this sick man is cured by the Lord. For the Lord himself came, the teacher of love, full of love, *making the word short upon the earth*, as was foretold of him[^19]; and he showed that on the two commandments of love depend the Law and the Prophets. From there, then, hung Moses with his forty, from there Elijah with his, this number the Lord brought in his testimony. This sick man is cured by the Lord present; but what does he first say to him? *Do you wish to be made well?*[^20] He answered that he had no man by whom he might be put into the pool[^21]. Truly he needed a man for his healing, but that man who is also God. *For there is one God, and one mediator of God and men, the man Christ Jesus*[^22]. The man, then, who was needed came: why should the healing be delayed? *Rise*, he says, *take up your pallet, and walk*[^23].

He said three things: *Rise, take up your pallet, and walk*.

But *Rise* was not the command of a work, but the working of healing. To him, now healed, he commanded two things: *Take up your pallet, and walk*. I ask you, why would not *Walk* suffice? Or surely, why would not *Rise* suffice? For when he had risen healed, he would not have remained in the place. Would he not have risen for this, that he might go away? It moves me, then, that he commanded two things, who found him lying two short: for as it were by commanding two things, he fulfilled what was lacking.

**8.** How, then, may we find in these two commands of the Lord those two commandments of love signified? *Take up*, he says, *your pallet, and walk*. What are those two commandments, brothers? Recall them with me. For they ought to be most well known, and not only come to mind when they are recalled by us, but never be erased from your hearts. Always think entirely that God and neighbor are to be loved: God with the whole heart, with the whole soul, and with the whole mind; and the neighbor as oneself. These are always to be thought of, these to be meditated on, these to be retained, these to be done, these to be fulfilled.

The love of God is first in the order of commanding; but the love of neighbor is first in the order of doing. For he who commanded you this love in two commandments would not commend to you the neighbor first, and afterward God; but first God, afterward the neighbor. But you, because you do not yet see God, by loving the neighbor earn to see him whom you love; by loving the neighbor you cleanse the eye for seeing God, as John says plainly: *If you do not love the brother whom you see, how will you be able to love God whom you do not see?*[^24] See, it is said to you: Love God. If you say to me, Show me the one I am to love—what shall I answer, except what John himself says: *No one has ever seen God*[^25]? And lest you suppose yourself altogether estranged from seeing God: *God*, he says, *is love; and he who abides in love abides in God*[^26]. Love, then, your neighbor; and look within yourself at the source from which you love your neighbor; there you will see, as you can, God.

Begin, then, to love your neighbor. *Break your bread for the hungry, and bring the needy without a roof into your house; if you see one naked, clothe him, and do not despise the household of your own seed*. But in doing these things, what do you obtain? *Then your light will break forth as the morning*[^27]. Your light is your God, the morning light to you, because he will come to you after the night of the world; for he neither rises nor sets, because he always abides. He will be the morning to you returning, who had made the setting to you perishing. Therefore, *Take up your pallet*, he seems to me to have said: Love your neighbor.

## Carry the one with whom you walk.

**9.** But it is still closed, and needs expounding, I think—why in the taking up of the pallet the love of neighbor is commended; unless perhaps this offends us, that by a pallet, a kind of dull and senseless thing, the neighbor is commended. Let the neighbor not be angry, if he is commended to us by a thing that is without soul and without sense. Our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ himself was called a cornerstone, that he might found two in himself. He was called also a rock, from which water flowed forth: *and the rock was Christ*[^28]. What wonder, then, if Christ is a rock, the neighbor is wood? Yet not just any wood: as neither was that just any rock, but one from which water had flowed forth for the thirsty; nor just any stone, but a cornerstone, which coupled in itself two walls coming from different directions. So neither should you take the neighbor as just any wood, but as a pallet.

What, then, in the pallet, I ask you? What, except that that sick man was carried by the pallet? But when made well, he carries the pallet. What was said by the Apostle? *Carry one another's burdens, and so you will fulfill the law of Christ*[^29]. The law of Christ, then, is love, and love is not fulfilled unless we carry one another's burdens. *Bearing with one another in love*, he says, *being eager to keep the unity of the Spirit in the bond of peace*[^30]. When you were sick, your neighbor carried you; you have been made well, carry your neighbor. *Carry one another's burdens, and so you will fulfill the law of Christ*. So, O man, you will fulfill what was lacking to you.

*Take up*, then, *your pallet*. But when you have taken it up, do not remain; *walk*. By loving the neighbor, and by having care for your neighbor, you make a journey. Where do you journey, except to the Lord God, to him whom we ought to love with the whole heart, with the whole soul, with the whole mind? For we have not yet come to the Lord, but we have the neighbor with us. Carry, then, the one with whom you walk, that you may come to the one with whom you desire to abide. *Take up*, then, *your pallet, and walk*.

**10.** This man did it, and the Jews were scandalized. For they saw a man on the Sabbath day carrying his pallet, and they did not bring a charge against the Lord that he had made him well on the Sabbath, so that he might answer them that if any of their cattle had fallen into a pit, surely on the Sabbath day they would pull it out and save their beast[^31]; so they were now no longer objecting that a man had been made well on the Sabbath day, but that he was carrying his pallet. If the healing was not to be deferred, was the work also to be commanded? *It is not lawful for you*, they say, *to do what you are doing, to take up your pallet*[^32]. And he held up the author of his healing to the slanderers: *He who*, he says, *made me well, he himself said to me: Take up your pallet, and walk*[^33]. Should I not receive a command from the one from whom I had received healing? And they: *Who is that man who said to you: Take up your pallet, and walk?*[^34]

**11.** But he who had been made well did not know who it was from whom he had heard this. *For Jesus, when he had done this and given the command, had withdrawn from him in the crowd*[^35]. See how this too is fulfilled. We carry the neighbor, and we walk, toward God; but the one toward whom we walk we do not yet see; and so that man too did not yet know Jesus. This sacred meaning is commended, that we believe in him whom we do not yet see; and, that he may not be seen, he withdraws into the crowd. It is hard to see Christ in a crowd: a certain solitude is necessary for our mind; by a certain solitude of attention God is seen. A crowd has noise; this seeing desires secrecy. *Take up your pallet*, carry, you who were carried, your neighbor; *and walk*, that you may arrive.

Do not seek Jesus in the crowd; he is not as one of the crowd: he goes before the whole crowd. That great fish rose first from the sea, and sits in heaven interceding for us; like a great priest, alone he entered into the inner part of the veil: the crowd stands outside. Walk you, who carry your neighbor; if you have learned to carry, you who were wont to be carried. In short, now you do not yet know Jesus, you do not yet see Jesus: what follows afterward? Because he did not cease to take up his pallet and to walk: *Jesus saw him afterward in the temple*[^36]. In the crowd he did not see him; in the temple he saw him. The Lord Jesus indeed saw him both in the crowd and in the temple; but that sick man does not recognize Jesus in the crowd, recognizes him in the temple. He arrived, then, at the Lord; he saw him in the temple, saw him in a consecrated place, in a holy place. And what does he hear from him? *Behold, you have now been made well; sin no more, lest something worse happen to you*[^37].

**12.** Then he, after he had seen Jesus, and recognized Jesus as the author of his healing, was not slow in evangelizing the one whom he had seen: *he went away, and told the Jews that it was Jesus who had made him well*[^38]. He was announcing, and they were raging; he was preaching his own salvation, they were not seeking their own salvation.

## The sacred meaning.

**13.** *The Jews were persecuting the Lord Jesus, because he did these things on the Sabbath*[^39]. Let us hear, then, what the Lord now answered the Jews. About men being made well on the Sabbath I have said what he is wont to answer—that they did not despise their own cattle on the Sabbath, either freeing them or feeding them. About the pallet being carried, what did he answer? A manifest bodily work had been done before the eyes of the Jews: not the health of the body, but a working of the body, which did not seem so necessary as health. Let the Lord, then, say openly the sacred meaning of the Sabbath, and that the sign of observing one day was given to the Jews for a time; but that the very fulfillment of the sacred meaning had come in him. *My Father*, he says, *works until now, and I work*[^40]. He sent a great tumult upon them; at the Lord's coming the water is troubled, but he who troubles it is hidden. Yet one great sick man is to be healed by the troubled water—the whole world, when the Lord has suffered.

**14.** Let us see, then, the answer of the Truth: *My Father works until now, and I work*. Did Scripture, then, say falsely that *God rested from all his works on the seventh day*[^41]? And does the Lord Jesus speak against this Scripture ministered through Moses, when he himself says to the Jews, *If you believed Moses, you would believe me also; for he wrote about me*[^42]? See, then, lest Moses meant to signify something by saying that God rested on the seventh day. For God had not been wearied in making his creation, and did not need rest like a man. How had he been wearied who had made it by a word? Yet that too is true, that God rested from his works on the seventh day; and this is true which Jesus says, *My Father works until now*.

But who can unfold in words—a man to men, a weak one to the weak, an unlearned one to those desiring to learn, and perhaps, if he knows anything, unable to bring it forth and unfold it to men who perhaps grasp it with difficulty, even if what is grasped could be unfolded? Who, I say, my brothers, can unfold in words how God both works at rest and rests in working? I beg you to defer this until you have made progress; for that seeing seeks the temple of God, seeks a holy place: carry the neighbor and walk; there you will see him, where you do not look for the words of men.

**15.** This perhaps we can rather say: that in the very fact that God rested on the seventh day, he signified by a great sacred sign our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ himself, who was speaking these things and saying, *My Father works until now, and I work*. For the Lord Jesus too is surely God. For he is the Word of God, and you have heard that *in the beginning was the Word*; and not just any Word, but *the Word was God*, and *all things were made through him*: perhaps it was signified that he would rest on the seventh day from all his works. For read the Gospel, and see how many things Jesus did. He worked our salvation on the cross, that all the things foretold by the prophets might be fulfilled in him: he was crowned with thorns, hung upon the wood; he said, *I thirst*, he received vinegar in a sponge, that what was said might be fulfilled, *And in my thirst they gave me vinegar to drink*[^43]. But when all his works were fulfilled, on the sixth day of the week, his head bowed, he gave up his spirit, and in the tomb on the Sabbath he rested from all his works.

It is, then, as if he said to the Jews: Why do you expect that I should not work on the Sabbath? The Sabbath day was commanded to you for my signification. You attend to the works of God; I was there when they were made, all things were made through me, I know: *My Father works until now*. The Father made light; but he said that light should be made: if he said it, he worked by a word; his Word was I, I am: through me the world was made in those works, through me the world is ruled in these works. My Father both worked then when he made the world, and works until now when he rules the world; therefore both through me he made it when he made it, and through me he rules it when he rules it. He said these things, but to whom? To the deaf, the blind, the lame, the sick, who did not recognize the physician, and, as if in a frenzy with mind lost, wished to kill him.

**16.** Accordingly, what did the Evangelist go on to say? *For this reason, then, the Jews sought all the more to kill him, because he not only broke the Sabbath, but also said that God was his Father*. Not in any ordinary way; but how? *Making himself equal to God*[^44]. For we all say to God, *Our Father who are in heaven*[^45]; we read that the Jews too said, *Since you are our Father*[^46]. Therefore they were not angry on this account, because he called God his Father, but because he did so in a way far other than men do.

See, the Jews understand what the Arians do not understand. For the Arians say that the Son is unequal to the Father, and from this the heresy has been driven out of the Church. See, those very blind ones, the very killers of Christ, yet understood the words of Christ. They did not understand him to be the Christ, nor did they understand him to be the Son of God; but yet they understood in those words that such a Son of God was being commended as would be equal to God. Who he was they did not know; yet they recognized that such a one was being proclaimed, because he called God his Father, making himself equal to God.

Was he not, then, equal to God? He did not make himself equal, but the one who begot him begot him equal. If he had made himself equal to God, he would fall through robbery. For he who wished to make himself equal to God when he was not, fell[^47], and from an angel became the devil; and he poured this pride into man, by which he himself had been cast down. For this he said to man, whose standing he, fallen, envied: *Taste, and you will be like gods*[^48]—that is, seize by usurpation what you were not made; for I too, by seizing, was cast down. He did not betray this, but persuaded it.

But Christ, equal to the Father, was born, not made: born of the substance of the Father. Whence the Apostle thus commends him: *Who, though he was in the form of God, did not count it robbery to be equal to God*. What is, *he did not count it robbery*? He did not usurp equality with God, but was in that equality in which he was born. And we, how should we come to a God who is equal? *He emptied himself, taking the form of a servant*[^49]. He did not, then, empty himself by losing what he was, but by taking what he was not. The Jews, despising this form of a servant, could not understand the Lord Christ to be equal to the Father; although they did not in the least doubt that he said this of himself, and therefore they raged: and yet he still bore with them, and sought the healing of those who raged.
