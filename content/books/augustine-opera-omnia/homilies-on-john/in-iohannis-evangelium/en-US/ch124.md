# Tractate 124

## *So I want him to remain until I come: what is that to you? You follow me* (Jn 21:19-25).

## The Church knows two lives, divinely preached and commended to her, of which one is in faith, the other in sight; one in the time of pilgrimage, the other in the eternity of abiding; one on the way, the other in the homeland. One was signified by the apostle Peter, the other by John.

**1.** It is no small question why the Lord, when he showed himself a third time to the disciples, said to the apostle Peter, *Follow me*; but concerning the apostle John, *So I want him to remain until I come; what is that to you?* To this question, as far as the Lord himself shall grant, whether to treat it or to solve it, we devote the last homily of this work. When, then, the Lord had foretold to Peter by what death he was to glorify God, *he says to him, Follow me. Peter, turning, saw that disciple whom Jesus loved following—the one who at supper had also leaned on his breast and said, Lord, who is it that will betray you? When Peter, then, saw him, he says to Jesus, Lord, and what about this man? Jesus says to him, So I want him to remain until I come; what is that to you? You follow me. So this saying went out among the brethren, that that disciple would not die. Yet Jesus did not say to him, He does not die; but, So I want him to remain until I come; what is that to you?*[^1] Behold how far in this Gospel the question is extended, which by its depth exercises not slightly the mind of one who searches it. For why is it said to Peter, *Follow me*, and not said to the others who were present together? And surely the disciples followed him as their master. But if it is to be understood of the passion, did Peter alone suffer for the Christian truth? Was there not present there, among those seven, the other son of Zebedee, the brother of John, who after his ascension is shown to have been killed by Herod? But someone may say that, since James was not crucified, it was rightly said to Peter, *Follow me*, who underwent not only death, but even the death of the cross, as Christ did. Let this stand, if nothing else more fitting can be found.

Why, then, was it said of John, *So I want him to remain until I come; what is that to you?* and it was repeated, *You follow me*; as though he therefore would not follow, because he wished him to remain until he comes? Who would easily believe that anything else was said than what the brethren who then were believed—namely, that this disciple would not die, but would remain in this life until Jesus came? But John himself removed this opinion, declaring by an open contradiction that the Lord did not say this. For why would he add: *Jesus did not say, He does not die*, except lest what was false should cling to the hearts of men?

**2.** But let him who pleases still resist, and say that what John says is true—that the Lord did not say that this disciple would not die—but that nonetheless it was signified by such words as he related him to have spoken; and let him assert that the apostle John is alive, and contend that in that tomb of his which is at Ephesus he is sleeping rather than lying dead. Let him take as argument that there the earth is said gently to bubble up and, as it were, to boil, and that this is caused by his breathing, whether he affirms it firmly or obstinately. For there cannot fail to be those who believe this, if there are not lacking those who even assert that Moses is alive; since it is written that his tomb is not found, and he appeared with the Lord on the mountain, where Elijah too was, whom we read was not killed but taken up. As if the body of Moses could not have been hidden somewhere so that men were utterly unaware where it was, and from there be raised up for an hour by divine power, when Elijah and he too were seen with Christ: just as for an hour many bodies of the saints arose when Christ suffered, and after his resurrection appeared to many in the holy city, as is written. But yet, as I had begun to say, if some deny that Moses is dead, whom that very Scripture, where we read that his tomb is found nowhere, nonetheless testifies without any ambiguity to be dead; how much more, on the occasion of these words where the Lord says, *So I want him to remain until I come*, is John believed to sleep alive beneath the earth? Whom they even report (which is found in certain Scriptures, though apocryphal), that when he had ordered a tomb to be made for him, he was present sound and whole; and when it had been dug and most carefully prepared, he laid himself there as on a couch, and at once was dead: but, as these think who so understand these words of the Lord, not dead, but lay like one dead; and when he was thought dead, was buried while sleeping; and so remains until Christ comes, and indicates his life by the welling up of dust: which dust is believed to be driven up, so as to rise from the bottom to the surface of the mound, by the breath of the one resting. I think it superfluous to struggle against this opinion. For let those who know the place see whether the earth there does or undergoes what is said; for in truth we have heard this not from frivolous men.

**3.** Meanwhile, let us yield to an opinion which we are not able to refute by certain proofs, lest another question arise that may be asked of us: why over the buried dead man the very earth seems in a manner to live and breathe. But is so great a question hereby solved, if by a great miracle, such as the Almighty can work, a living body is for so long in sleep beneath the earth, until the end of the age comes? Rather it becomes greater and more difficult: why, to the disciple whom he loved above the rest, so much that he was worthy to recline upon his breast, he should grant as a great gift a long sleep in the body: whereas blessed Peter, through the great glory of martyrdom, he freed from the burden of the body itself, and granted him what the apostle Paul said and wrote that he desired: *to be dissolved and to be with Christ*[^2]. But if, as is more commonly believed, holy John for this reason said that the Lord did not say *He does not die*, lest by those words which he spoke he should be thought to have meant this; and his body lies lifeless in his tomb like those of other dead men: it remains that, if what rumor has spread about the earth—that, the dust being repeatedly taken away, it grows up again—truly happens there, it happens either that his precious death may in this way be commended, since martyrdom does not commend it (for no persecutor killed him for the faith of Christ), or for some other reason that is hidden from us. Yet the question remains why the Lord said of a man who was to die: *So I want him to remain until I come.*

**4.** This too, in these two apostles, Peter and John, whom may it not move to inquiry: why the Lord loved John more, when Peter loved the Lord more? For wherever John mentions himself, so that, his own name being unspoken, he may be understood, he adds this, that Jesus loved him—as though he alone were loved, that by this sign he might be distinguished from the rest, whom surely he loved all: what then, except that he wished it understood that he was loved more, when he said this? Which God forbid that he should say falsely. But what greater indication of his greater love toward him could Jesus give than that a man, together with his fellow disciples a sharer of so great a salvation, alone reclined upon the breast of the Savior himself?

Further, that the apostle Peter loved Christ more than the others, many proofs can indeed be brought forward; but, not to go far afield, it appears clearly enough from the very third manifestation of the Lord, in the lesson a little earlier that precedes this one, where, questioning him, he said: *Do you love me more than these?*[^3] Which he surely knew, and yet asked, that even we who read the Gospel might know Peter's love toward the Lord, both with him questioning and with the other answering. But that in his answer, when Peter said, *I love you*, he did not add, more than these, he answered this which he knew of himself. For he could not know how much he was loved by any other, who could not see another's heart. Yet by his earlier words, in saying, *Yes, Lord, you know*, he himself sufficiently declared that the Lord, who knew, had asked what he asked. The Lord, then, knew not only that Peter loved, but also that he loved him more than they did. And yet if we propose, asking, which of two is the better—whether he who loves Christ more, or he who loves less—who will hesitate to answer that he who loves more is the better? Again, if we propose which of two is the better—whether he whom Christ loves less, or he whom he loves more—we shall without doubt answer that he who is loved more by Christ is the better. In the former comparison, then, which I put first, Peter is preferred to John; but in this other, John is preferred to Peter. And so we propose a third: which of two disciples is the better—he who loves Christ less than his fellow disciple, and is loved more than his fellow disciple by Christ? or he whom Christ loves less than his fellow disciple, while he himself loves Christ more than his fellow disciple does? Here clearly the answer hesitates, and the question grows. But as far as I myself understand it, I would easily answer that he is the better who loves Christ more, but happier whom Christ loves more; if I could see clearly how I should defend the justice of our Deliverer in loving less the one by whom he is loved more, and more the one by whom he is loved less.

## The two lives.

**5.** I will set out, then, in the manifest mercy of him whose justice is hidden, to discuss, as far as the strength he himself shall give, the solving of so great a question: for so far it has been proposed, not expounded. And of its exposition let this be the beginning: that we remember that in this corruptible body which weighs down the soul we live a wretched life. But we who are now redeemed through the Mediator, and have received the Holy Spirit as a pledge, have the blessed life in hope, though we do not yet hold it in reality. *But hope that is seen is not hope; for who hopes for what he sees? But if we hope for what we do not see, we wait for it with patience*[^4]. Now in the evils that each one suffers, not in the goods that he enjoys, there is need of patience. This life, then, of which it is written, *Is not human life upon earth a trial?*[^5] in which we daily cry to the Lord, *Deliver us from evil*[^6], a man is compelled to bear even when his sins are forgiven; although it was first sin that was the cause of his coming into this misery. For the penalty is longer than the fault, lest the fault be thought slight, if the penalty too ended with it. And by this—whether for the demonstration of the misery he owes, or for the amending of a fallible life, or for the exercise of necessary patience—the penalty holds a man for a time, even one whom the fault no longer holds liable to eternal damnation. This is the condition of these days, which we pass as evil in this mortality, though in it we love to see good days—a condition to be wept over, indeed, but not to be blamed.

For it comes from the just anger of God, of which Scripture, speaking, says: *Man, born of woman, of short life, and full of anger*[^7]—although the anger of God is not, like a man's, a disturbance of an agitated mind, but the tranquil settling of a just punishment. In this anger of his, God, *not restraining,* as is written, *his mercies*[^8], besides the other consolations of the wretched which he does not cease to furnish to the human race, in the fullness of time, at which he himself knew this was to be done, sent his only-begotten Son, through whom he created all things, that, remaining God, he might become man, and be the mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus: that those believing in him, the guilt of all their sins being loosed through the bath of regeneration—both the original sin which generation draws, against which especially regeneration was instituted, and the rest contracted by doing evil—might be freed from perpetual damnation, and might live in faith and hope and love, on pilgrimage in this age, and amid its laborious and perilous trials, walking, by the consolations of God both bodily and spiritual, toward the sight of him, holding to the way, which Christ was made for them. And because, even while walking in him, they are not without sins, which creep in from the weakness of this life, he gave the saving remedies of almsgiving, by which their prayer might be helped, where he taught them to say: *Forgive us our debts, as we also forgive our debtors*[^9].

This the Church does in blessed hope, in this toilsome life: of which Church the apostle Peter, on account of the primacy of his apostolate, bore by a figurative generality the person. For as regards what belongs to him properly, he was by nature one man, by grace one Christian, by more abundant grace one and the same first apostle: but when it was said to him, *I will give you the keys of the kingdom of heaven, and whatever you bind on earth shall be bound also in heaven; and whatever you loose on earth shall be loosed also in heaven*[^10], he signified the whole Church, which in this age is shaken by various trials, as by rains, floods, and tempests, and does not fall, because it is founded upon the rock, from which Peter took his name. For the rock is not from Peter, but Peter from the rock; just as Christ is not named from the Christian, but the Christian from Christ. For therefore the Lord says: *Upon this rock I will build my Church*[^11], because Peter had said, You are the Christ, the Son of the living God. Upon this rock, then, he says, which you have confessed, I will build my Church. For *the rock was Christ*[^12]; upon which foundation Peter himself was also built. For *no one can lay another foundation than that which has been laid, which is Christ Jesus*[^13]. The Church, then, which is founded in Christ, received from him in Peter the keys of the kingdom of heaven, that is, the power of binding and loosing sins. For what the Church is properly in Christ, that Peter is by way of signifying in the rock; by which signifying Christ is understood as the rock, Peter as the Church. This Church, then, which Peter signified, as long as it lives among evils, by loving and following Christ is freed from evils. But it follows more in those who strive for the truth even unto death. But it is to the whole company that it is said, *Follow me*, for which whole company Christ suffered; of whom the same Peter says: *Christ suffered for us, leaving us an example, that we should follow his footsteps*[^14]. Behold why it was said to him, *Follow me.*

But there is another life, immortal, which is not among evils: there we shall see face to face what here is seen through a mirror and in an enigma, when much progress has been made in the beholding of the truth. The Church, then, knows two lives, divinely preached and commended to her, of which one is in faith, the other in sight; one in the time of pilgrimage, the other in the eternity of abiding; one in labor, the other in rest; one on the way, the other in the homeland; one in the work of action, the other in the reward of contemplation; one turns away from evil and does good, the other has no evil to turn from, and has the great good to enjoy; one fights with the enemy, the other reigns without an enemy; one is brave in adversity, the other feels nothing adverse; one bridles carnal lusts, the other is free for spiritual delights; one is anxious with the care of conquering, the other is secure in the peace of victory; one is helped in temptations, the other, without any temptation, rejoices in the helper himself; one comes to the aid of the needy, the other is where it finds no one needy; one forgives others' sins, that its own may be forgiven it, the other neither suffers anything that it should forgive, nor does anything for which it should ask forgiveness; one is scourged by evils, lest it be lifted up in goods, the other, by so great a fullness of grace, lacks every evil, so that without any temptation of pride it cleaves to the highest good; one discerns good and evil, the other beholds the things that alone are good: therefore one is good, but still wretched; the other better and blessed.

The one was signified by the apostle Peter, the other by John. The whole of the one is carried on here, until the end of this age, and there finds its end: the other is deferred to be completed after the end of this age, but in the age to come has no end. Therefore it is said to the one, *Follow me*; but of the other, *So I want him to remain until I come; what is that to you? You follow me.* For what is this? As far as I understand, as far as I grasp, what is this, except: You follow me by imitation in the enduring of temporal evils; let him remain until I come to render eternal goods? Which can be said more plainly thus: Let perfect action follow me, formed by the example of my passion; but let contemplation, begun, remain until I come, to be perfected when I shall have come. For the godly fullness of patience follows Christ, reaching even unto death: but the fullness of knowledge remains until Christ comes, then to be made manifest. For here are endured the evils of this world in the land of the dying, there shall be seen the good things of the Lord in the land of the living. For what he says—*I want him to remain until I come*—is not to be understood as though he said, to stay behind or to abide; but, to wait: since what is signified by him will be fulfilled not now, but when Christ has come. But what is signified by this one, to whom it is said, *You follow me*, unless it be done now, there will be no arriving at that which is awaited.

But in this active life, the more we love Christ, the more easily we are freed from evil. But he himself loves us less, such as we now are; and therefore he frees us from this, that we may not always be such. There, however, he loves us more; since we shall not have anything that displeases him, or that he would take from us: nor for any other reason does he love us here, except to heal us and to transfer us from the things he does not love. Here, then, less, where he does not wish us to remain: there, more, whither he wishes us to pass, and from whence he does not wish us to perish. Let Peter, then, love him, that we may be freed from this mortality: let John be loved by him, that we may be preserved in that immortality.

**6.** But by this reasoning it is shown why Christ loved John more than Peter, not why Peter loved Christ more than John. For if Christ loves us more in the age to come, where we shall live with him without end, than in this from which we are rescued, in order that we may be there forever, it does not follow that we shall then love him less when we shall be better; since better we surely cannot in any way be except by loving him more. Why, then, did John love him less than Peter, if he signified that life in which he is to be loved much more, except because it was therefore said, *I want him to remain*, that is, to wait, *until I come*, because that very love, which then will be much greater, we do not yet have, but await it as to come, that when he himself has come, we may have it? For as the same apostle says in his Epistle: *It has not yet appeared what we shall be; we know that when he appears we shall be like him, for we shall see him as he is*[^15]. Then, therefore, we shall love more, because we shall see. But the Lord himself, knowing what that future life of ours will be like in us, loves more, by predestination, that life which is to come, so that by loving us he may bring us to it. Wherefore, since *all the ways of the Lord are mercy and truth*[^16], we know our present misery, because we feel it; and therefore we love more the mercy of the Lord, which we wish to be shown us in being freed from misery, and which we daily, especially for the remission of sins, beg and obtain: this was signified by Peter, loving more, but loved less; because Christ loves us less wretched than blessed. But the contemplation of the truth, such as it will then be, we love less, because we do not yet know nor have it: this was signified by John, loving less, and therefore awaiting—both for that contemplation itself, and for the love of it in us, such as is due to it, to be fulfilled—until the Lord comes; but loved more, because that which was figured by him makes one blessed.

## Let no one separate these illustrious apostles.

**7.** Yet let no one separate these illustrious apostles. Both were in what Peter signified; and both were to be in what John signified. By way of signifying, the one followed, the other remained: but by believing, both endured the present evils of this misery, both awaited the future goods of that blessedness. Nor they alone, but the whole holy Church does this, the bride of Christ, to be rescued from these trials, to be preserved in that felicity. These two lives Peter and John figured, each one of them: yet both walked in this present life for a time by faith, and both will enjoy that other life forever by sight. For all the saints, then, belonging inseparably to the body of Christ, that they might be steered through this most stormy life, Peter, the first of the apostles, received the keys of the kingdom of heaven for binding and loosing sins: and for those same saints, on account of the most secret quiet of that bosom of that other most hidden life, John the evangelist reclined upon the breast of Christ. For neither does the former alone, but the whole Church, bind and loose sins: nor did the latter alone drink from the fountain of the Lord's breast that which, by preaching, he might pour forth—In the beginning was the Word, the Word God with God, and the rest concerning the divinity of Christ, and the sublime things concerning the Trinity and unity of the whole Godhead, which in that kingdom are to be contemplated face to face, but now, until the Lord comes, are to be beheld in a mirror and in an enigma; but the Lord himself, according to each one's capacity, poured out this very Gospel throughout the whole world to be drunk by all his own.

There are those who have held—and these indeed not contemptible expounders of the sacred eloquence—that the apostle John was for this reason loved more by Christ: that he never took a wife and lived most chastely from his earliest boyhood. This indeed does not clearly appear in the canonical Scriptures: yet it too greatly favors the fittingness of this view, that by him was signified that life where there will be no marriages.

**8.** *This is that disciple who bears witness to these things and has written these things; and we know that his testimony is true. Now there are also,* he says, *many other things that Jesus did, which, if they were written one by one, I suppose that the world itself could not hold the books that would be written*[^17]. It is not by the extent of places that we are to believe the world could not hold them—how could they be written in it, if it did not bear what was written?—but perhaps they could not be comprehended by the capacity of the readers; although, the truth of the matters being safe, words often seem to exceed faith. This does not happen when something obscure or doubtful is set forth by giving its cause and reason: but when what is plain is either magnified or diminished, and yet there is no straying from the path of signifying the truth; since the words so exceed the matter they indicate that the intention of the speaker—who does not deceive, and who knows how far it is to be believed—is apparent, by whom something is either lessened or magnified in speaking beyond what is to be believed. This manner of speaking, by a Greek name, the masters not only of Greek but also of Latin letters call "hyperbole." This manner is found, as in this place, so in some other passages of the divine Scriptures: such as: *They have set their mouth against heaven*[^18]; and: *The crown of the hair of those who walk on in their sins*[^19]; and many such, which are not lacking in the holy Scriptures, like the other tropes, that is, modes of speech. Concerning which I would discuss more laboriously, were I not, with the evangelist ending his Gospel, also myself compelled to end my homily.
