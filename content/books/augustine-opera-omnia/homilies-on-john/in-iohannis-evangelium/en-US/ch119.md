# Tractate 119

## *There were standing by the cross of Jesus his mother . . .* (Jn 19:24-30).

## That wood where the limbs of the dying one were fastened was also the teaching chair of the master.

**1.** The Lord having been crucified, after the division of his garments, with the lot also cast, was completed, let us see what John the evangelist next relates. *The soldiers indeed,* he says, *did these things. But there were standing by the cross of Jesus his mother, and his mother's sister, Mary the wife of Clopas, and Mary Magdalene. When Jesus therefore saw his mother and the disciple whom he loved standing near, he said to his mother, Woman, behold your son; then he said to the disciple, Behold your mother. And from that hour the disciple took her to his own*[^1]. This, surely, is that hour of which Jesus, when about to turn water into wine, had said to his mother: *What have I to do with you, woman? My hour has not yet come*[^2]. He had foretold, then, this hour, which had not yet come, in which, about to die, he should acknowledge her from whom he had been born mortal. For then, about to do divine things, he repelled, as though unknown, the mother not of his divinity but of his weakness; now, however, already suffering human things, he commended with human affection her of whom he had been made man. For then he who had created Mary was being made known by his power; now what Mary had brought forth was hanging on the cross.

**2.** A moral lesson, therefore, is here suggested. He does what he admonishes ought to be done, and by his own example the good teacher instructed his own, that care should be bestowed by dutiful children upon their parents: as though that wood where the limbs of the dying one were fastened were also the teaching chair of the master. From this sound doctrine the apostle Paul had learned what he taught, when he said: *But if anyone does not provide for his own, and especially for his household, he has denied the faith and is worse than an unbeliever*[^3]. And what is so much one's own household as parents to children, or children to parents? Of this most salutary precept, then, the master of the saints himself set up an example from himself, when, not as God whom he had created and ruled, but as a man, for his mother, of whom he had been created and whom he was leaving behind, he provided in his stead, as it were, another son. For why he did this, what follows indicates; for the evangelist says: *And from that hour the disciple took her to his own*, speaking of himself. For so he is wont to mention himself—that Jesus loved him; he who indeed loved all, but this one above the rest and more familiarly, so that at supper he made him recline upon his breast; I believe, that he might in this way more deeply commend the divine excellence of this gospel, which he was to preach through him.

## He took her to his own—not lands, but duties.

**3.** But to what "own" did John take the Lord's mother? For surely he was among those who said to him: *Behold, we have left all and followed you*[^4]. But there too he had heard: Whoever leaves these things for my sake will receive in this age a hundredfold. That disciple, then, had a hundredfold more than he had left, among which to take in the mother of him who had given those things. But in that fellowship blessed John had received the hundredfold, where no one said anything was his own, but they had all things in common; as is written in the Acts of the Apostles. For so were the apostles, *as having nothing and possessing all things*[^5]. How, then, did the disciple and servant take the mother of his master and Lord to his own, where no one called anything his own? Or is it that, since a little later in the same book it is read: *For as many as were owners of lands or houses sold them and brought the proceeds and laid them at the apostles' feet; and distribution was made to each as anyone had need*[^6], we are to understand that there was so distributed to this disciple what was needful, that there too a portion was laid down for blessed Mary as for his mother? And ought we rather to take what was said—*From that hour the disciple took her to his own*—to mean that whatever was needful for her belonged to his care? He took her, then, to his own—not lands, which he possessed none of his own; but to duties, which by his own management he saw to be carried out.

**4.** Then he adds: *After this, Jesus, knowing that all was now finished, that the Scripture might be fulfilled, said, I thirst. A jar full of vinegar was standing there; so they put a sponge full of the vinegar on hyssop and held it to his mouth. When Jesus had received the vinegar, he said, It is finished. And bowing his head, he gave up his spirit*[^7]. Who can so arrange the things he does, as this man arranged the things he suffered? But the man, the mediator between God and men; the man of whom it is read in prophecy: And he is a man, and who shall know him? since the men through whom these things were done did not know the man God. For a man appeared who as God lay hidden; he who appeared suffered all these things, and the same one who lay hidden arranged all these things. He saw, then, that all things were finished which had to be done before he received the vinegar and gave up his spirit; and that this too might be finished which Scripture had foretold—*And in my thirst they gave me vinegar to drink*[^8]—*I thirst,* he says: as though he said: This much you have failed to do; give what you are. For the Jews themselves were the vinegar, having degenerated from the wine of the patriarchs and prophets; and, filled as from a full jar with the iniquity of this world, they had a heart like a sponge, deceitful, as it were, with its cavernous and twisting hiding-places. But the hyssop, on which they put the sponge full of vinegar—since it is a lowly herb and purges the breast—we fittingly take to be the humility of Christ himself; which they surrounded, and thought they had circumvented. Hence is that saying in the psalm: *You will sprinkle me with hyssop, and I shall be clean*[^9]. For by the humility of Christ we are cleansed; because unless he had humbled himself, becoming obedient unto death, even the death of the cross, surely his blood would not have been poured out for the remission of sins, that is, for our cleansing.

**5.** And let it not trouble anyone how the sponge could have been brought to the mouth of one who had been lifted up from the earth on the cross. For, as is read in the other evangelists—which this one omitted—it was done by means of a reed, so that such a drink in a sponge might be raised to the heights of the cross. But by the reed Scripture was signified, which was being fulfilled by this deed. For just as a tongue is called Greek or Latin, or any other, signifying the sound that is uttered by the tongue, so a reed can be called the letter that is written with a reed. But the sounds that signify the human voice we most usually call tongues; whereas to call Scripture a reed, since it is less usual, is for that very reason more mystically figured.

The impious people did these things; the merciful Christ suffered these things. He who did them did not know what he was doing; but he who suffered them not only knew what was being done and why it was being done, but also did good himself out of those who did evil.

**6.** *When Jesus had received the vinegar, he said, It is finished*[^10]. What, except what prophecy had foretold so long before? Then, since nothing remained that still had to be done before he died, as one who had power to lay down his life and to take it up again, all things being accomplished which he was awaiting to be accomplished, *bowing his head, he gave up his spirit*[^11]. Who sleeps when he wills, as Jesus died when he willed? Who puts off his garment when he wills, as he stripped himself of the flesh when he willed? Who so departs when he wills, as he died when he willed? How great a power is to be hoped for or feared in him as judge, if so great appeared in him as one dying?
