# Tractate 100

## *He will glorify me*[^1].

## The Holy Spirit did not glorify him with the true glory except in the holy catholic Church.

**1.** When the Lord promised that the Holy Spirit would come, he said: *He will teach you all truth*; or, as is read in some copies, *He will lead you into all truth. For he will not speak from himself, but whatever he will hear, he will speak*[^2]. On these Gospel words we have already discussed what the Lord granted: now attend to what follows. *And the things that are to come,* he says, *he will announce to you*[^3]. Nor must we linger here, since it is plain: for it has no question whose explanation should be demanded of us. But what he adds, *He will glorify me, because he will receive of mine, and will announce it to you*[^4], must not be passed over carelessly. For what he says, *He will glorify me,* can be understood thus: that by pouring out love in the hearts of believers, and by making them spiritual, he declared to them how the Son was equal to the Father, whom before they had known only according to the flesh, and thought of as a man like men. Or certainly, because through that very love, filled with confidence and fear being driven away, they announced Christ to men; and so his fame was spread throughout the whole earth. So that he said, *He will glorify me,* as though he said: He will take away fear from you, and will give you love, by which, preaching me more ardently, you will give to my glory the fragrance throughout the whole world, you will commend the honor. For what they were going to do in the Holy Spirit, he said that this same Spirit would do: as is also that, *For it is not you who speak, but the Spirit of your Father who speaks in you*[^5]. For the Greek word "doxasei" (δοξάσει) the Latin translators rendered, each in his own translation, one as *clarificabit* ("will make glorious"), another as *glorificabit* ("will glorify"): since that very thing which in Greek is called "doxa" (δόξα), whence the word is said, is rendered both "brightness" and "glory." For each one becomes bright by glory, and glorious by brightness; and through this, what is signified by either word is the same thing. But as the most distinguished ancient authors of the Latin language defined it, glory is frequent fame with praise concerning someone. And when this has been made in this world concerning Christ, it is not to be believed to have conferred anything great on Christ, but on the world. For to praise the good profits not the one praised, but those who praise.

**2.** But there is also a false glory, when those who praise are deceived by error, whether in things, or in men, or in both. For they are deceived in things, when they think that to be good which is evil: but in men, when they think him to be good who is evil: and in both, when both that which is a vice is thought a virtue, and the one who is praised for this does not have what is thought, whether he be good or evil.

For to give away one's goods to actors is a monstrous vice, not a virtue: and you know how frequent is fame with praise concerning such men; because, as it is written, *The sinner is praised in the desires of his soul, and he who does iniquitous things is blessed*[^6]. Here those who praise are not deceived in the men, but in the things: for the thing they believe to be good is evil. But those who are vicious in this evil of lavish giving are surely such as those who praise them do not suppose them to be, but discern them to be.

Further, if anyone feigns himself righteous, and is not, but whatever he is seen to do laudably before men, he does not do for the sake of God, that is, for the sake of true righteousness, but seeks and loves only glory from men; while those among whom his fame is frequent with praise think that he lives laudably only for the sake of God — they are not deceived in the thing, but are deceived in the man. For what they believe to be good, is good; but the one whom they believe to be good, is not good.

But if, for example, skill in the magic arts be thought a good, and while someone is believed to have freed his country by those very arts which he does not know at all, he obtains among impious men frequent fame with praise, which has been defined as glory; those who praise err in both: namely both in the thing, because they think that to be good which is evil; and in the man, because he is not what they think.

Wherefore in these three kinds the glory is false.

But when, concerning someone righteous through God and for the sake of God — that is, truly righteous — for the cause of righteousness itself there is frequent fame with praise; it is indeed true glory; yet it is not to be believed that the righteous man is made blessed by it, but those who praise are to be congratulated, because they judge rightly and love the righteous man. How much more, then, did the Lord Christ profit, by his glory, not himself, but those whom he profited by his death?

**3.** But his glory is not true among the heretics, among whom, nevertheless, he seems to have frequent fame with praise. This is not true glory, because they are deceived in both: for they both think that to be good which is not good, and they think Christ to be what Christ is not. For that the only-begotten Son is not equal to the one who begot him, is not good: that the only-begotten Son of God is only man, not God, is not good: that the flesh of Truth is not true flesh, is not good. Of these three which I have mentioned, the first the Arians hold, the second the Photinians, the third the Manicheans. But because none of their notions is good, and Christ is none of their notions, they are deceived in both: nor do they give true glory to Christ, although among them there seems to be frequent fame with praise concerning Christ. And absolutely all heretics, whom it would be too long to recall, who do not think rightly of Christ, err for this reason, because they do not think truly of good and evil things either.

The pagans too, since they are very many praisers of Christ, are themselves also deceived in both, who, not according to the truth of God, but rather according to their own suspicion, say that he was a man, a magician. For they blame the Christians as unskilled, but praise Christ as a magician, and so betray what they love: but Christ they do not love; since they love what Christ was not. Therefore they are deceived in both, because both to be a magician is evil; and Christ was not a magician, because he is good.

Wherefore, since nothing is to be said in this place of those who blame and blaspheme Christ — because we are speaking of his glory, by which he was glorified in the world — the Holy Spirit did not glorify him with true glory except in the holy catholic Church. For elsewhere, that is, whether among the heretics, or among certain pagans, his true glory cannot be on earth, even where there seems to be frequent fame concerning him with praise. His true glory, then, in the catholic Church is thus sung by the prophet: *Be exalted above the heavens, O God, and above all the earth your glory*[^7]. Since, therefore, after his exaltation the Holy Spirit was to come and to glorify him, this the sacred Psalm, this the Only-begotten himself promised would be, which we see fulfilled.

**4.** But as for what he says, *He will receive of mine, and will announce it to you,* hear it with catholic ears, perceive it with catholic minds. For the Holy Spirit is not, as certain heretics thought, less than the Son: as though the Son received from the Father, and the Holy Spirit from the Son, by certain degrees of natures. Far be it to believe this, far be it to say this, far be it to think this in Christian hearts.

Finally, he himself at once solves the question, and explains why he said this. *All things,* he says, *whatsoever the Father has are mine: therefore I said that he will receive of mine, and will announce it to you*[^8]. What more do you wish? The Holy Spirit, then, receives from the Father, whence the Son receives; because in this Trinity the Son is born of the Father, the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Father. But he who is born of no one, proceeds from no one, is the Father alone. But how the only-begotten Son said, *All things that the Father has are mine* (since it is certainly not in the way in which it was said to that son who was not only-begotten, but the elder of two, *You are always with me, and all that is mine is yours*[^9]), will be treated, if the Lord wills, with careful consideration in that place where the Only-begotten says to the Father, *And all that is mine is yours, and yours is mine*[^10]: so that this sermon may be closed; since what follows demands another beginning, in which it may be discussed.
