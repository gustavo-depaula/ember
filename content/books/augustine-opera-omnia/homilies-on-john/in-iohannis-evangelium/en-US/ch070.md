# Tractate 70

## *That where I am, you too may be* (Jn 14:3-10).

## He himself is the eternal life in which we are to be, when he will have received us to himself; and that same eternal life which he himself is, is in him, so that where he is, we too may be, that is, in him. — He himself is the eternal life in which we are to be.

**1.** The words of the holy Gospel, brothers, are rightly understood thus, if they are found to be in concord with what precedes: for what goes before ought to agree with what follows, when the truth speaks. The Lord had said above: *And if I go, and prepare a place for you; I come again, and will receive you to myself, that where I am, you too may be*: then he had added, *and where I go you know, and the way you know*; and he showed that what he said was nothing other than that they knew him. What it would be, then, to go to himself through himself, which he also grants to the disciples that they may go to him through him, we have already said, as we could, in the former discourse. And so, as for what he says: *That where I am, you too may be,* where were they to be but in him? And through this he too is in himself, and therefore they are there where he too is, that is, in him.

He himself, then, is the eternal life in which we are to be, when he will have received us to himself: and that same eternal life which he himself is, is in him, so that where he is, we too may be, that is, in him. *For as the Father has life in himself,* and surely the life which he has is nothing other than what he himself is who has this life: *so he has given to the Son to have life in himself*[^1], since he himself is the same life which he has in himself.

But shall we be the very life which he himself is, when we have begun to be in that life, that is, in him? Not at all, since he, by existing, has life as life, and is what he has, and what life is in him, he himself is in himself: but we are not the very life, but partakers of his life, and so we shall be there in such a way that in ourselves we cannot be what he himself is, but, we ourselves not being life, may have him as life, who himself has life, in that he himself is life.

Finally, he himself both is in himself unchangeably, and is in the Father inseparably: but we, when we wished to be in ourselves, were troubled toward ourselves; whence is that voice: *My soul is troubled toward myself*[^2]: and, changed for the worse, we could not even remain what we had been. But when through him we come to the Father, as he says: *No one comes to the Father except through me*; remaining in him, no one will be able to separate us either from the Father, or from him.

**2.** Connecting, then, what follows with what precedes: *If you have known me,* he says, *you have surely known my Father also.* This is what he says: *No one comes to the Father except through me.* Then he adds: *And from now on you will know him, and you have seen him*[^3]. But Philip, one of the Apostles, not understanding what he had heard: *Lord,* he says, *show us the Father, and it is enough for us*[^4]. To whom the Lord: *So long a time,* he says, *I am with you, and you have not known me, Philip? He who sees me sees the Father also*[^5]. Behold, he rebukes that for so long a time he was with them, and was not known. Had he not himself said: *And where I go you know, and the way you know*; and, when they said they did not know, had he not convicted them of knowing these things, by adding and saying: *I am the way, the truth, and the life*? How does he now say: *So long a time I am with you, and you have not known me?* since surely both where he was going, and the way they knew, for no other reason than that they surely knew him?

But this question is easily solved, if we say that some of them knew him, some did not, and among those who did not know him was Philip too: so that what he says, *And where I go you know, and the way you know,* is understood to have been said to those who knew; not to Philip, to whom it was said: *So long a time I am with you, and you have not known me, Philip?* To those, then, who already knew the Son, that also was said of the Father: *And from now on you will know him, and you have seen him*; for it was said on account of the complete likeness which he has with the Father, so that for this reason they might be said from now on to know the Father, because they knew the like Son. They already knew the Son, then, even if not all, certainly some of them, to whom it is said: *And where I go you know, and the way you know*: for he himself is the way. But they did not know the Father, therefore they hear: *If you have known me, you have known my Father also*: through me, surely, him also. For I am one, he is another. But lest they should think him unlike: *And from now on,* he says, *you will know him, and you have seen him.* For they saw his most like Son, but they had to be admonished that the Father too whom they did not yet see was such as is the Son whom they saw.

And to this purpose serves what is afterward said to Philip: *He who sees me sees the Father also.* Not that he himself was both Father and Son, which the catholic faith condemns in the Sabellians, who are also called Patripassians: but that the Father and the Son are so alike, that whoever knows one knows both. For we are wont to speak of two most alike in this way to those who see one of them, and wish to know what the other is like, that we say: You have seen this one, you have seen that one. So, then, it was said: *He who sees me sees the Father also*: not, surely, that he who is the Son is the Father, but that the Son in nothing whatever differs from the likeness of the Father. For unless the Father and the Son were two, it would not have been said: *If you have known me, you have known my Father also.*

For surely, because *no one,* he says, *comes to the Father except through me; if you have known me, you have known my Father also*: since I, through whom one comes to the Father, will lead you to him, that you may know him also. But because I am altogether most like him, *from now on you will know him,* when you know me: *and you have seen him,* if with the eyes of the heart you have seen me.

**3.** What, then, is it that you say, Philip: *Show us the Father, and it is enough for us*? *So long a time,* he says, *I am with you, and you have not known me, Philip? He who sees me sees the Father also.* But if to see this is too much for you, at least believe what you do not see. *For how do you say,* he says: *Show us the Father*? If you have seen me who am altogether like him, you have seen him to whom I am like. But if you cannot see, *do you not at least believe that I am in the Father, and the Father in me?* Here Philip could say: I indeed see you, and believe you to be most like the Father; but is he to be reproved and chided who, when he sees the like, wishes also to see him to whom he is like? I know indeed the like, but as yet I know the one without the other; it does not suffice me, unless I know also him to whom this one is like. *Show us,* then, *the Father, and it is enough for us.* But the master reproved the disciple for this reason, because he saw the heart of the one asking. For as though the Father were better than the Son, Philip desired to know the Father: and therefore he did not even know the Son, than whom he believed something to be better. To correct this notion it was said: *He who sees me sees the Father also. How do you say: Show us the Father?* I see how you say it: you do not seek to see another who is like, but you think him to be better. *Do you not believe that I am in the Father, and the Father in me?* Why do you desire to discern a distance in those who are alike? why do you long to know separately those who are inseparable?

Then he speaks not to Philip alone, but to them in the plural, things which are not to be cramped into narrow limits, that with his help they may be more carefully expounded.
