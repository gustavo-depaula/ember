# Tractate 116

## *And they struck him with the palms of their hands* (Jn 19:1-16).

## Thus that grain, to be multiplied, was sown in dreadful disgrace, that it might sprout up in wondrous glory. — His power being hidden, his patience is commended for our imitation.

**1.** When the Jews had cried out that they did not want Jesus released to them at the Passover, but Barabbas the robber—not the savior, but the slayer; not the giver of life, but the taker of it—*then Pilate took Jesus and scourged him*[^1]. Pilate is to be believed to have done this for no other reason than that the Jews, glutted with the injuries done to him, might think it enough for themselves and cease from raging even to his death. To this belongs the fact that the same governor also permitted his own cohort to do what follows; or perhaps even commanded it, though the evangelist passed this over in silence. For he said what the soldiers then did, yet he did not say that Pilate commanded it. *And the soldiers,* he says, *plaiting a crown of thorns put it on his head and clothed him in a purple robe. And they kept coming to him and saying, Hail, King of the Jews. And they struck him with the palms of their hands*[^2]. Thus were fulfilled the things Christ had foretold concerning himself; thus were the martyrs formed to endure all that the persecutors might please to do; thus, his fearsome power being for a little while hidden, his patience was first commended for our imitation; thus the kingdom which was not of this world overcame the proud world not by the savagery of fighting but by the humility of suffering; thus that grain, to be multiplied, was sown in dreadful disgrace, that it might sprout up in wondrous glory.

**2.** *Pilate went out again and said to them, Behold, I bring him out to you, that you may know that I find no cause in him. So Jesus came out, wearing the crown of thorns and the purple robe. And he said to them, Behold the man*[^3]. From this it appears that these things were done by the soldiers not without Pilate's knowledge, whether he ordered them or permitted them; for the reason, namely, that we mentioned above—that his enemies might most gladly drink in these mockeries of him and thirst no longer for his blood. Jesus comes out to them wearing the crown of thorns and the purple robe, not resplendent in command but full of reproach; and it is said to them: *Behold the man*: if you envy the king, now spare him, for you see him cast down; he has been scourged, crowned with thorns, clothed in a mocking robe, mocked with bitter taunts, struck with blows; his shame seethes, let envy grow cold. But it does not grow cold; rather it blazes hotter and increases.

**3.** *When the chief priests and the officers saw him, they cried out, saying, Crucify, crucify him. Pilate said to them, Take him yourselves and crucify him, for I find no cause in him. The Jews answered him, We have a law, and according to the law he ought to die, because he made himself the Son of God*[^4]. Behold another, greater envy. For that first one seemed slight—of one who had, as it were, aspired to royal power by an unlawful daring; and yet Jesus falsely usurped neither title, but both are true: he is both the only-begotten Son of God, and the king set up by him over Zion his holy mountain; and both would now be shown, did he not, the more powerful he was, the more prefer to be patient.

**4.** *When Pilate heard this word, he was the more afraid; and he entered the praetorium again and said to Jesus, Where are you from? But Jesus gave him no answer*[^5]. This silence of our Lord Jesus Christ is found, when the narratives of all the evangelists are compared, to have occurred not once: before the chief priests, and before Herod, to whom, as Luke indicates, Pilate had sent him to be heard, and before Pilate himself; so that the prophecy concerning him did not go before in vain: *Like a lamb before its shearer he was without voice, so he opened not his mouth*[^6]—precisely when he did not answer those questioning him. For although he often answered certain questions, yet on account of those in which he chose not to answer, the likeness to a lamb was given, so that in his silence he might be reckoned not guilty, but innocent. When, therefore, he was being judged, wherever he did not open his mouth, like a lamb he did not open it—that is, not like one ill-conscious who was being convicted of his own sins, but like a gentle one who was being sacrificed for the sins of others.

**5.** *Pilate therefore said to him, You will not speak to me? Do you not know that I have power to crucify you and power to release you? Jesus answered, You would have no power against me at all, unless it had been given you from above; therefore he who handed me over to you has the greater sin*[^7]. Behold, he answered; and yet wherever he did not answer, it was not as a guilty or deceitful man, but as a lamb—that is, as a simple and innocent one—that he did not open his mouth. Accordingly, where he did not answer, he was silent like a sheep; where he answered, he taught like a shepherd.

Let us learn, then, what he said—what he also taught through the Apostle: that *there is no power except from God*[^8]; and that he sins more who out of envy hands over an innocent man to a power to be killed, than the power itself if it kills him out of fear of another, greater power. For God had given Pilate such power that he was also under the power of Caesar. Therefore *You would have,* he says, *no power against me at all*—that is, however little you have—*unless* this very thing, whatever it is, *had been given you from above.* But since I know how great it is—for it is not so great that you are altogether free—*therefore he who handed me over to you has the greater sin.* For that man handed me over to your power out of envy, while you are about to exert that same power upon me out of fear. A man ought not to kill a man even out of fear, especially an innocent one; yet to do this out of zeal is much more evil than to do it out of fear. And so the truthful master does not say, He who handed me over to you has the sin, as though Pilate did not have it; but he says, *has the greater sin*, so that Pilate too might understand that he himself had sin. For that one is not nothing, just because this one is greater.

**6.** *From then on Pilate sought to release him*[^9]. What is the meaning of what is said, *from then on*, as though he had not sought to before? Read what comes above, and you will find that he had long been seeking to release Jesus. *From then on*, therefore, is to be understood as: On account of this—that is, for this reason, that he might not have the sin of killing an innocent man handed over to him, although sinning less than the Jews who had handed him over to be killed. *From then on*, then—that is, for this reason, that he might not commit this sin—he sought to release him, not now for the first time, but from the beginning.

**7.** *But the Jews kept crying out, saying, If you release this man, you are not Caesar's friend; for everyone who makes himself a king sets himself against Caesar*[^10]. They thought to instill a greater fear in Pilate, by terrifying him about Caesar, so that he would kill Christ, than earlier, where they said: *We have a law, and according to the law he ought to die, because he made himself the Son of God*[^11]. For their law he did not fear, so as to kill; but rather he feared the Son of God, so as not to kill. Now, however, he could not despise Caesar, the author of his own power, as he could the law of a foreign nation.

**8.** Still the evangelist continues and says: *Pilate, when he heard these words, brought Jesus out and sat down on the judgment seat, at a place called the Pavement, but in Hebrew Gabbatha. Now it was the preparation of the Passover, about the sixth hour*[^12]. As to the hour at which the Lord was crucified—because of the testimony of the other evangelist, who said, *Now it was the third hour, and they crucified him*[^13]—since a great dispute is wont to arise over this, we will, if the Lord wills, discuss it as far as we can when we have come to the very place where his crucifixion is related.

When Pilate, then, had sat down on the judgment seat, *He says to the Jews, Behold your king. But they cried out, Away with him, away with him, crucify him. Pilate said to them, Shall I crucify your king?*[^14] Still he tries to overcome the terror they had instilled about Caesar, wishing to break them by their own shame, by saying: *Shall I crucify your king?*—though he could not soften them by Christ's shame; but he is soon overcome by fear.

**9.** For *the chief priests answered, We have no king but Caesar. Then he handed him over to them to be crucified*[^15]. For he would clearly seem to be acting against Caesar if, to those professing that they had no king but Caesar, he wished to thrust another king by releasing unpunished the one whom, on account of these daring claims, they had handed over to him to be killed. *He handed him over to them, then, to be crucified.* But did he not also desire something else before, when he said, *Take him yourselves and crucify him*[^16]; or even earlier, *Take him yourselves and judge him according to your law*[^17]? But why were they so unwilling, saying, *It is not lawful for us to put anyone to death*[^18], and insisting in every way that he be killed not by them but by the governor, and therefore refusing to take him to be killed—if now they take him to be killed? Or, if this is not what happens, why is it said, *Then he handed him over to them to be crucified*? Or does it make any difference? It clearly does. For it is not said, Then he handed him over to them that they might crucify him; but, *to be crucified*—that is, to be crucified by the judgment and power of the governor. But the evangelist said he was handed over to them for this reason: to show that they were implicated in the crime from which they were trying to make themselves out as strangers; for Pilate would not have done this except to fulfill what he saw them desiring. As for what follows—*So they took Jesus, and led him away*[^19]—this can now be referred to the soldiers, the governor's attendants. For afterward it is said more clearly: *The soldiers, then, when they had crucified him*[^20]; although even if the evangelist attributes the whole to the Jews, he does so with reason; for they themselves took up what they most greedily demanded, and they themselves did whatever they extorted to be done. But these things that follow must be treated in another homily.
