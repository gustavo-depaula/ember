# Tractate 58

## *I have given you an example, that as I have done to you, so you also should do* (Jn 13:10-15).

## Let not a Christian disdain to do what Christ did. When the body is bent down to a brother's feet, the affection of humility itself is either stirred up in the heart, or, if it was already there, is strengthened.

**1.** We have already expounded to your Love, as the Lord deigned to grant, those words of the Gospel where the Lord, washing the feet of his disciples, says: *He who is once washed has no need but to wash his feet, but is clean all over*: now let us see what follows. *And you,* he says, *are clean, but not all.* What this is, lest we should inquire, the Evangelist himself made plain, adding: *For he knew who it was that would betray him; therefore he said: You are not all clean*[^1]. What is plainer than this? Accordingly, let us pass on to what follows.

**2.** *After, then, he had washed their feet, and taken his garments; when he had reclined again, he said to them: Do you know what I have done to you*[^2]? Now is the time for that promise to be repaid to blessed Peter: for it had been deferred, when, to him shuddering and saying: *You will not wash my feet forever,* it was answered: *What I am doing you do not know now, but you will know hereafter*[^3]. Behold, this is the very hereafter; now is the time for what was a little before deferred to be told.

And so the Lord, mindful that he had a while ago promised the knowledge of his deed, so unexpected, so wonderful, so to be shuddered at, and which, unless he himself had vehemently terrified him, was in no way to be allowed—that he, the master not only of them but of the angels, and the Lord not only of them but of all things, should wash the feet of his disciples and servants—since, then, he had promised the knowledge of this so great a deed, saying: *You will know hereafter*; he now begins to teach what it is that he did.

## It is profitable for us to serve the truth.

**3.** *You,* he says, *call me Master and Lord: and you say well; for so I am*[^4]. You say well, because you say true; for I am what you say. To man it is commanded: *Let not your own mouth praise you, but let the mouth of your neighbor praise you*[^5]. For it is dangerous to please oneself, for one who must beware of being proud. But he who is above all things, however much he praises himself, does not exalt himself, being exalted: nor can God rightly be called arrogant. For it is to our advantage to know him, not to his: nor does anyone know him, unless he himself who knows him reveals himself. If, then, by not praising himself, he should wish as it were to avoid arrogance, he will deny us wisdom.

And that he says he is a master, no one would blame, even one who believed him to be nothing other than a man; since he professes that which men themselves profess in whatever arts, so far without arrogance that they are called professors. But that he himself also says he is the lord of his disciples, though they are even according to the world freeborn, who would bear it in a man? But it is God who speaks. There is here no pride of so great a loftiness, no lie of the truth: it is profitable for us to be subject to that loftiness, profitable for us to serve the truth. That he says he is Lord is no fault in him, but a benefit to us.

The words of a certain secular author are praised, because he said: *While all arrogance is hateful, that of natural gift and eloquence is by far the most troublesome*: and yet that same man, when he spoke of his own eloquence: *I would call it perfect,* he said, *if I so judged; nor in truth would I fear the charge of arrogance.* If, then, that most eloquent man would not fear arrogance in the truth, how does the truth itself fear arrogance? Let him say he is Lord who is Lord, let him say what is true who is the truth; lest I not learn what is profitable, while he is silent about what he is.

The most blessed Paul, not indeed the only-begotten Son of God, but the servant and apostle of the only-begotten Son of God; not the truth, but a partaker of the truth; says freely and steadfastly: *And if I should wish to glory, I shall not be foolish; for I speak the truth*[^6]. For he would glory not in himself, but in the truth itself which is higher than he, and that both humbly and truthfully: since he himself also commanded *that he who glories should glory in the Lord*[^7]. Would the lover of wisdom, then, not fear folly, if he should wish to glory; and would wisdom itself fear folly in its own glory? He did not fear arrogance who said: *In the Lord shall my soul be praised*[^8]; and would the power of the Lord fear arrogance in its own praise, in which the soul of the servant is praised? *You,* he says, *call me Master and Lord: and you say well; for so I am.* Therefore you say well, because I am: for if I were not what you say, you would say ill, even if you praised me. How, then, would the truth deny what the disciples of the truth say? how would that from which they learned deny what those who learned say? How does the fountain deny what the one drinking proclaims? how does the light conceal what the one seeing declares?

## Let us, the humble, do to one another what the exalted one did humbly.

**4.** *If, then,* he says, *I, Lord and Master, have washed your feet, you also ought to wash one another's feet. For I have given you an example, that as I have done to you, you also should do*[^9]. This, blessed Peter, is what you did not know, when you would not allow it to be done. This he promised you should know hereafter, when, that you might allow it, your Master and your Lord terrified you, washing your feet. We have learned humility, brothers, from the Exalted one; let us, the humble, do to one another what the Exalted one did humbly.

Great is this commendation of humility: and brothers do this to one another, even by the visible deed itself, when they receive one another into hospitality; for among very many there is a custom of this humility, even to the deed by which it is seen expressed. Whence the Apostle, when he commended a widow well deserving: *If she has received guests,* he says, *if she has washed the feet of the saints*[^10]. And among the saints, wherever this custom is not, what they do not do with the hand, they do in the heart, if they are of the number of those to whom it is said in the hymn of the three blessed young men: *Bless the Lord, you holy and humble of heart*[^11]. But it is much better, and without controversy more true, that it should be done even with the hands; nor let a Christian disdain to do what Christ did. For when the body is bent down to a brother's feet, the affection of humility itself is either stirred up in the heart, or, if it was already there, is strengthened.

**5.** But apart from this moral understanding, we remember that we commended the loftiness of this deed of the Lord in this way, that, by washing the feet of disciples already washed and clean, the Lord signified, on account of the human affections amid which we live on the earth, that, however much we have advanced in the apprehension of righteousness, we should know that we are not without sin: which from time to time he washes away by interceding for us, when we pray the Father who is in heaven to forgive us our debts, *as we too forgive our debtors*.

How, then, can this pertain to that understanding which he himself afterward taught, where he expounded the reason of his deed, saying: *If, then, I, Lord and Master, have washed your feet, you also ought to wash one another's feet. For I have given you an example, that as I have done to you, so you also should do*! Can we say that even a brother will be able to cleanse a brother from the contagion of fault? Rather, let us know that in the loftiness of this deed of the Lord we too are admonished of this: that, having confessed our faults to one another, we should pray for one another, as Christ too intercedes for us. Let us hear the apostle James most plainly commanding this very thing, and saying: *Confess your faults to one another, and pray for one another*[^12]. For to this too the Lord gave us an example. For if he who has no sin, nor had, nor will have, prays for our sins; how much more ought we to pray for one another's? And if he forgives us, to whom we have nothing that we may forgive; how much more ought we to forgive one another, who are not able to live here without sin? For what does the Lord seem to signify in this loftiness of the sacrament, when he says: *For I have given you an example, that as I have done to you, so you also should do*; but what the Apostle most plainly says: *Forgiving among yourselves, if anyone has a complaint against another, even as the Lord has forgiven you, so also you*[^13]?

Let us, then, forgive one another our faults, and pray for one another's faults, and so in a certain way wash one another's feet. It is ours, by his gift, to apply the ministry of love and humility: it is his to hear, and to cleanse us from all defilement of sins through Christ, and in Christ; that what we forgive to others, that is, loose on earth, may be loosed in heaven.
