# Tractate 123

## *When he had eaten, Jesus said to Simon Peter, Simon son of John, do you love me more than these?* (Jn 21:12-19).

## To the threefold denial a threefold confession is rendered. Let it be the office of love to feed the Lord's flock, if it was the sign of fear to deny the shepherd.

**1.** In the fact that for the third time the Lord, after his resurrection, revealed himself to his disciples, the Gospel of the blessed apostle John comes to an end: in which we have already treated, as we were able, the earlier part, down to that point where it is related that a hundred and fifty-three fish were caught by the disciples to whom he showed himself, and that, although they were large, the nets were not broken. Then what follows must be considered, and, as far as the Lord helps, treated as the matter shall seem to require.

When that fishing was finished: *Jesus says to them, Come, eat. And none of those reclining dared to ask him, Who are you? knowing that it was the Lord*[^1]. If, then, they knew, what need was there to ask? But if there was no need, why is it said, *they did not dare*, as though there were need, but out of some fear they did not dare? The sense, then, is this: so great was the evidence of the truth by which Jesus appeared to those disciples, that none of them dared not only to deny, but not even to doubt; for if anyone had doubted, he would surely have had to ask. So, then, it was said, *None dared ask him, Who are you?* as if it were said: None dared doubt that it was he.

## The roasted fish is Christ who suffered.

**2.** *And Jesus comes and takes the bread and gives it to them, and the fish likewise*[^2]. Behold, it has been said also what they ate: of which meal we too will say something sweet and salutary, if he feed us too. Above it was related that these disciples, when they had gone ashore, *saw a charcoal fire laid, with a fish placed on it, and bread*[^3]. Here it is not to be understood that the bread too was placed on the coals, but only this is to be supplied: They saw. And if we repeat that word in the place where it is to be supplied, the whole can be said thus: They saw a charcoal fire laid, and a fish placed on it, and they saw bread. Or rather thus: They saw a charcoal fire laid, and a fish placed on it; they saw also bread. And at the Lord's command they brought also of the fish which they themselves had caught; which, although it was not expressed by the narrator that they did, yet that the Lord commanded it is not left unsaid. For he says: *Bring some of the fish that you have now caught*[^4]. And surely who would believe that, with him commanding, they did not do it? Hence, then, the Lord made a meal for those seven disciples of his, namely of the fish which they had seen placed on the coals, adding to it some of those which they had caught, and of the bread which it was likewise related that they had seen. The roasted fish is Christ who suffered. He himself is also the bread that came down from heaven. With him the Church is incorporated, for the sharing of everlasting blessedness. On account of which it was said: *Bring some of the fish that you have now caught*; that all of us who bear this hope, through that sevenfold number of disciples, by which in this place our whole company can be understood as figured, might know that we are made to share in so great a sacrament and to be joined to the same blessedness.

This is the Lord's meal with his disciples, with which John, though he had many other great things to say concerning Christ, concludes his Gospel by the contemplation, as I think, of great things. For here the Church, such as she will be in the good alone, is signified by the catch of a hundred and fifty-three fish; and to those who believe, hope, and love these things, the participation in so great a blessedness is shown by this meal.

**3.** *This was now the third time,* he says, *that Jesus was revealed to his disciples after he had risen from the dead*[^5]. This we ought to refer not to the showings themselves, but to the days (that is, the first day, when he rose; and after eight days, when the disciple Thomas saw and believed; and today, when he did this concerning the fish; but after how many days he did this is not stated): for on that first day he was seen not once, as the testimonies of all the evangelists, when compared, show; but, as has been said, his showings are to be reckoned according to days, so that this is the third; for the first is to be counted, and that one too, by reason of the one day, however many times and to whomever he showed himself on that day on which he rose; the second after eight days, and this the third, and then as often as he willed up to the fortieth day, on which he ascended into heaven, although not all are written.

## This was the end found by that denier and lover.

**4.** *When, therefore, they had eaten, he says to Simon Peter, Simon son of John, do you love me more than these? He says to him, Yes, Lord; you know that I love you. He says to him, Feed my lambs. He says to him again, Simon son of John, do you love me? He says to him, Yes, Lord; you know that I love you. He says to him, Feed my lambs. He says to him a third time, Simon son of John, do you love me? Peter was grieved because he said to him a third time, Do you love me; and he says to him, Lord, you know all things; you know that I love you. He says to him, Feed my sheep. Amen, amen I say to you, when you were younger, you girded yourself and walked where you wished; but when you grow old, you will stretch out your hands, and another will gird you and carry you where you do not wish. This he said to signify by what death he was to glorify God*[^6]. This was the end found by that denier and lover: lifted up by presuming, laid low by denying, cleansed by weeping, approved by confessing, crowned by suffering: this was the end he found, that he should die in perfect love for the name of him with whom he had promised, by a perverse haste, that he would die. Let him do, strengthened by his resurrection, what he had immaturely promised when weak. For this was fitting, that first Christ should die for Peter's salvation, then Peter for the preaching of Christ. It was preposterous, what human rashness had begun to dare, when the truth had arranged this order. Peter thought to lay down his life for Christ, when he himself, to be freed, was to be freed by his deliverer; whereas Christ had come to lay down his life for all his own, among whom was also Peter; which behold, has now been done. Now, then, let true firmness of heart to undergo death for the name of the Lord be taken up, the Lord himself giving it, not falsely presumed by us in our error. Now it is time that we should not fear the destruction of this life; because, with the Lord rising, the example of another life went before. Now it is time, Peter, that you should not fear death; because he lives whom you grieved over as dead, and whom, out of carnal love, you forbade to die for us. You dared to forestall your leader, you dreaded his persecutor: now that the price has been poured out for you, now it is time that you follow your purchaser, and follow him indeed even to the death of the cross. You have heard his words, whom you have already proved truthful; he himself foretold that you would suffer, who had foretold that you would deny.

**5.** But first the Lord asks what he knew—not once, but again and a third time—whether Peter loved him; nor does he hear anything so often from Peter but that he is loved; nor does he so often commend anything to Peter but that his sheep are to be fed. To the threefold denial a threefold confession is rendered, lest the tongue serve love less than it served fear, and lest imminent death seem to have drawn out more of the voice than present life. Let it be the office of love to feed the Lord's flock, if it was the sign of fear to deny the shepherd.

Those who feed the sheep of Christ with this mind—that they should wish them to be their own and not Christ's—are proved to love themselves, not Christ; out of a desire either to boast, or to dominate, or to acquire, not out of the love of obeying and helping and pleasing God. Against these, then, keeps watch this voice of Christ so often pressed home, against those whom the Apostle laments as seeking their own things, not the things of Jesus Christ. For what else is it to say, *Do you love me? Feed my sheep*, than if it were said: If you love me, do not think of feeding yourself, but feed my sheep as mine, not as yours; seek my glory in them, not yours; my dominion, not yours; my gains, not yours; lest you be in the fellowship of those who belong to the perilous times, lovers of self, and the rest that are connected to this beginning of evils? For when the Apostle had said, *For men will be lovers of self*, he went on to add: *lovers of money, haughty, proud, blasphemers, disobedient to parents, ungrateful, wicked, irreligious, without affection, slanderers, incontinent, harsh, without kindness, traitors, insolent, blinded, lovers of pleasure rather than of God; holding the appearance of piety, but denying its power*[^7]. All these evils flow, as from a fountain, from what he placed first, *lovers of self.* Rightly is it said to Peter, *Do you love me?* and he answers, *I love you*; and the reply is made to him, *Feed my lambs*; and this a second time, this a third. Where it is also shown that love and affection are one and the same thing: for the Lord too at the last did not say, *Do you love me?* but, *Are you fond of me?* Let us, then, love not ourselves, but him; and in feeding his sheep let us seek the things that are his, not the things that are ours. For in some inexplicable way, whoever loves himself and not God does not love himself; and whoever loves God and not himself, he it is that loves himself. For he who cannot live of himself surely dies by loving himself: he does not, then, love himself who, in order not to live, loves himself. But when he is loved of whom one lives, by not loving himself one loves himself the more, who for this reason does not love himself, that he may love him of whom he lives.

Let those, then, who feed the sheep of Christ not be *lovers of self*, lest they feed them as their own and not as his; and let them not wish to seek their own gains from them, like *lovers of money*; or to dominate them, like the *haughty*; or to glory in the honors they take from them, like the *proud*; or to advance so far as even to make heresies, like *blasphemers*; nor let them yield to the holy fathers, like those *disobedient to parents*; and to those who wish to correct them because they do not wish them to perish, let them not return evil for good, like the *ungrateful*: let them not destroy souls, both their own and others', like the *wicked*; let them not tear apart the maternal bowels of the Church, like the *irreligious*; let them not fail to have compassion on the weak, like those *without affection*; let them not try to stain the reputation of the saints, like *slanderers*; let them not fail to restrain the worst desires, like the *incontinent*; let them not engage in quarrels, like the *harsh*; let them not be unable to help, like those *without kindness*; let them not disclose to the enemies of the godly what they have learned ought to be kept hidden, like *traitors*; let them not disturb human modesty by shameless harassment, like the *insolent*; let them not fail to understand either what they say or about what they make assertions, like the *blinded*; let them not prefer carnal joys to spiritual gladness, like *lovers of pleasure rather than of God.* For these and suchlike vices, whether they all befall one man, or some dominate one and others another, sprout in a manner from that root, when men are *lovers of self.* Which vice is especially to be guarded against by those who feed the sheep of Christ, lest they seek their own things, not the things of Jesus Christ; and turn to the uses of their own desires those for whom the blood of Christ was poured out. The love of him, in one who feeds his sheep, ought to grow into so great a spiritual ardor that it overcomes even the natural fear of death, by which we are unwilling to die, even when we wish to be with Christ. For the apostle Paul too says he has a desire *to be dissolved and to be with Christ*[^8]: yet he groans, being burdened, and does not wish to be unclothed, but to be further clothed, that the mortal may be swallowed up by life.

And to this lover of his the Lord says: *When you grow old, you will stretch out your hands, and another will gird you and carry you where you do not wish. This he said to him, signifying by what death he was to glorify God. You will stretch out,* he says, *your hands*, that is, you will be crucified. But that you may come to this, *another will gird you and carry you*, not where you wish, but *where you do not wish.* First he said what would happen, and then how it would happen. For it was not when crucified, but surely when about to be crucified, that he was carried where he was unwilling: for when crucified he went not where he was unwilling, but rather where he was willing. For, being freed from the body, he wished to be with Christ; but, if it could be, he desired eternal life without the trouble of death: to which trouble he was led unwilling, but from it he was led out willing; unwilling he came to it, but willing he overcame it; and he left behind that affection of weakness by which no one wishes to die, so natural that not even old age could take it from blessed Peter, to whom it was said: *When you grow old*, you will be carried *where you do not wish.* For our consolation the Savior himself transferred this also into himself, saying: *Father, if it be possible, let this cup pass from me*[^9]: he who surely had come to die, and had no necessity of death, but a will, about to lay down his life by his own power, and again to take it up by his own power. But however great the trouble of death may be, the force of love ought to overcome it—the love by which he is loved who, although he is our life, willed even to endure death for us. For if there were no trouble of death, or only a little, the glory of the martyrs would not be so great. But if the good shepherd, who laid down his life for his sheep, made for himself out of those very sheep so many martyrs; how much more ought those to whom he entrusts the sheep themselves to be fed—that is, taught and ruled—to strive even unto death for the truth, and even unto blood against sin? And by this, the example of his passion going before, who does not see that shepherds ought rather to cleave to the shepherd by imitating him, if many sheep too have imitated him, under whom, one shepherd in one flock, the shepherds themselves are also sheep? For he made all his own sheep, for whom all he suffered; because he too, that he might suffer for all, was made a sheep.
