# Homily 20

## *Whatever the Father does, these same things the Son also does likewise*[^1].

## Understand, dearly beloved brothers, that the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit cleave to one another inseparably, that this Trinity is one God: and that all the works of the one God—these are the Father's, these are the Son's, these are the Holy Spirit's.

**1.** The words of our Lord Jesus Christ, especially those that John the Evangelist records—who not without cause reclined upon the Lord's breast, except that he might drink in the secrets of his deeper wisdom, and what by loving he had drunk, by evangelizing he might bring forth—are so secret and so deep in understanding that they trouble all who are perverse of heart, and exercise all who are right of heart. Accordingly let your Charity give heed to these few words that have been read. Let us see whether in some way we can, by the gift and help of him who willed his words to be recited to us—words that were then heard and written down, that now they might be read—what he means by what you have just heard him say: *Amen, amen I say to you, the Son cannot do anything of himself, except what he sees the Father doing: for whatever things the Father does, these same things the Son also does likewise*[^2].

**2.** But whence this discourse arose, you must be reminded, on account of the earlier part of the reading, where the Lord had cured a certain one among those who lay in the five porticoes of that pool of Solomon, to whom he had said, *Take up your pallet, and go to your house*[^3]. But this he had done on the Sabbath: whence the Jews, disturbed, slandered him as an overthrower and transgressor of the Law. Then he had said to them, *My Father works until now, and I work*[^4]. For they, taking the observance of the Sabbath carnally, supposed that God, after the labor of fashioning the world, as it were slept until this day; and that he had therefore sanctified that day, from which he began as it were to rest from his labors.

But there is a sacred meaning of the Sabbath, commanded to our ancient fathers, which we Christians observe spiritually: that we abstain from all servile work—that is, from all sin (because the Lord says, *Everyone who commits sin is a slave of sin*[^5])—and that we have rest in our heart, that is, spiritual tranquillity. And although in this world we strive after it, yet we shall not come to that perfect rest except when we have departed from this life. But it was said that God rested, because, after all things were perfected, he was now founding no creation. And Scripture called it "rest" for this reason, that it might admonish us that we shall rest after good works. For so we have it written in Genesis: *And God made all things very good, and God rested on the seventh day*[^6]: so that you, O man, when you attend to God himself resting after good works, may not hope for rest for yourself except when you have done good works: and just as God, after he made man on the sixth day to his image and likeness, and in him perfected all his works very good, rested on the seventh day; so do not you hope for rest, except when you have returned to the likeness in which you were made, which by sinning you lost. For God is not to be said to have labored, who said, and they were made. Who is there who, after such ease of work, would as it were after labor wish to rest? If he commanded and someone resisted him, if he commanded and it was not done, and he labored that it might be done, he might deservedly be said to have rested after labor: but since in the very book of Genesis we read, *God said: Let there be light; and there was light: God said: Let there be a firmament; and the firmament was made*[^7]; and the rest made at once at his word—to which the Psalm too bears witness, saying, *He himself spoke, and they were made; he himself commanded, and they were created*[^8]—how did he, after the world was made, seek rest as if to cease, who in commanding had never labored? Therefore those things are mystical, and set down for this reason, that we may hope for rest for ourselves after this life, but only if we have done good works.

Therefore the Lord, blunting the shamelessness and error of the Jews, and showing that they were not rightly minded about God, said to them, scandalized that on the Sabbath he was working the healing of men, *My Father works until now, and I work*[^9]: do not, then, suppose that on the Sabbath my Father so rested that from then on he does not work; but as he himself now works, I too work. But as the Father without labor, so also the Son without labor. God said, and they were made: Christ said to the sick man, *Take up your pallet, and go to your house*, and it was done.

## The works of the Father and the Son are inseparable.

**3.** Now the catholic faith holds that the works of the Father and the Son are not separable. This is what I wish, if I can, to speak to your Charity: but according to those words of the Lord, *Let him who can grasp it, grasp it*[^10]. But let him who cannot grasp it not ascribe it to me, but to his own slowness; and let him turn himself to the one who opens the heart, that he may pour in what he gives. Finally, even if anyone does not understand because it has not been said by me as it ought to have been said, let him pardon human frailty, and entreat the divine goodness. For we have within the Master, Christ. Whatever, through your ear and my mouth, you have not been able to grasp, turn yourselves in your heart to him who both teaches me what I say, and distributes to you as he deigns. He who knows what he gives, and to whom he gives, will be present to the one asking, and will open to the one knocking. And if perhaps he does not give, let no one say that he is forsaken. For perhaps he defers to give something, but he leaves no one hungering. For if he does not give at the hour, he exercises the seeker, he does not despise the asker. See, then, and attend to what I wish to say, even if perhaps I cannot.

The catholic faith holds this, established by the Spirit of God in his holy ones, against all heretical depravity: that the works of the Father and the Son are inseparable. What is it I have said? As the Father and the Son themselves are inseparable, so also the works of the Father and the Son are inseparable. How are the Father and the Son inseparable? Because he himself said, *I and the Father are one*[^11]. Because the Father and the Son are not two gods, but one God, the Word and he whose the Word is, one and only one, one God the Father and the Son embraced in love, and the one Spirit of their love is theirs, so that the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit are made a Trinity. Not, then, of the Father and the Son only, but also of the Holy Spirit, as the equality and inseparability of the persons, so also the works are inseparable.

Let me say yet more plainly what it is, the works are inseparable. The catholic faith does not say that God the Father made something, and the Son made something else: but what the Father made, this the Son too made, this the Holy Spirit too made. For all things were made through the Word: when he said, and they were made, they were made through the Word, they were made through Christ. For *in the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God: all things were made through him*[^12]. If all things were made through him, then *God said: Let there be light; and there was light*[^13]: in the Word he made it, through the Word he made it.

**4.** Behold, then, we have now heard the Gospel, when he answered the Jews vexed because *he not only broke the Sabbath, but also said that God was his Father, making himself equal to God*: for so it is written in the chapter above. When, then, the Son of God and the Truth answered such erring indignation of theirs, he said, *Amen, amen I say to you, the Son cannot do anything of himself, except what he sees the Father doing*[^14]. As though he said: Why are you scandalized, because I called God my Father, and because I make myself equal to God? I am so equal that he begot me: I am so equal that not he is from me, but I am from him. For this is understood in these words: *The Son cannot do anything of himself, except what he sees the Father doing*. That is, whatever the Son has by which to do, he has from the Father by which to do.

Why does he have from the Father by which to do?

Because he has from the Father that he is the Son.

Why does he have from the Father that he is the Son?

Because he has from the Father that he can, because he has from the Father that he is. For to the Son, to be is the same as to be able. To man it is not so. From the comparison of human weakness, lying far below, somehow lift up your hearts; and lest perhaps one of us touch the secret, and, as it were shuddering at the flashing of a great light, be wise in something, lest he remain unwise: yet let him not suppose that he is wholly wise, lest he grow proud, and lose what he was wise in. With a man, one thing is what he is, another what he can do. For sometimes a man both is, and cannot do what he wishes; but sometimes a man so is that he can do what he wishes: and so his being is one thing, his being able another. For if his being were the same as his being able, then when he willed he could.

But God, for whom there is not one substance by which he is, and another power by which he can, but whatever is his is consubstantial with him, and whatever he is, because he is God, he does not be in one way and be able in another; but he has being and being able together, because he has willing and doing together. Because, then, the power of the Son is from the Father, therefore also the substance of the Son is from the Father; and because the substance of the Son is from the Father, therefore the power of the Son is from the Father. There is not one power in the Son, and another substance: but the power is itself the same that is also substance; substance that he may be, power that he may be able. Therefore, because the Son is from the Father, he therefore said, *The Son cannot do anything of himself*. Because the Son is not from himself, therefore he cannot of himself.

## The Word of God exercises devout hearts.

**5.** For he seems as it were to have made himself lesser, when he said, *The Son cannot do anything of himself, except what he sees the Father doing*. Here heretical vanity lifts up its neck—namely, of those who say that the Son is lesser than the Father, of lesser power, majesty, capability—not understanding the mystery of the words of Christ. But let your Charity attend, and see how, in their carnal understanding, they are now disturbed in the very words of Christ. This I said a little before, that the Word of God troubles all perverse hearts, just as it exercises devout hearts, especially what is said through John the Evangelist. For lofty things are said through him, not just any things, not things easily understood.

Behold, now if the heretic perhaps hears these words, he lifts himself up, and says to us: See, the Son is lesser than the Father; see, hear the words of the Son, who says, *The Son cannot do anything of himself, except what he sees the Father doing*. Wait, as it is written: *Be meek in hearing the word, that you may understand*[^15]. For suppose me to be disturbed by these words, since I say that the power and majesty of the Father and the Son is equal, when I have heard, *The Son cannot do anything of himself, except what he sees the Father doing*. Disturbed by these words, I ask of you, who now seem to yourself to have understood: We know in the Gospel that the Son walked on the sea; where did he see the Father walking on the sea? Here now that man is disturbed. Set aside, then, what you had understood, and let us inquire together. What, then, do we do? We have heard the words of the Lord: *The Son cannot do anything of himself, except what he sees the Father doing*. He himself walked on the sea, the Father never walked on the sea. Surely the Son does not do anything except what he sees the Father doing.

**6.** Return, then, with me to what I was saying, lest perhaps it must be understood in such a way that we both come out of the question. For I, according to the catholic faith, see how I may come out without offense, without stumbling: but you, shut in on all sides, seek by what way to come out. See by what way you had come in. Perhaps you did not understand even this that I said, See by what way you had come in: hear him himself saying, *I am the door*[^16]. Not without cause, then, do you seek by what way to come out, and you do not find it, except because you did not come in through the door, but fell over the wall. Therefore, as you can, gather yourself from your downfall, and come in through the door, that you may come in without offense, and come out without error. Come through Christ, and do not bring from your own heart what you may say; but what he shows, that speak.

See how the catholic faith comes out of this problem. The Son walked on the sea, he set the feet of the flesh on the waves; the flesh walked, and the divinity guided it: when, then, the flesh walked and the divinity guided, was the Father absent? If he was absent, how does the Son himself say, *But the Father remaining in me, he himself does his works*[^17]? If, then, the Father, remaining in the Son, himself does his works; that walking of the flesh upon the sea was done by the Father, was done through the Son. Therefore that walking is a work of the Father and the Son, inseparable. I see both working there: neither did the Father forsake the Son, nor did the Son depart from the Father. So whatever the Son does, he does not do without the Father; because whatever the Father does, he does not do without the Son.

**7.** We have come out of here. See how rightly we say that the works of the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit are inseparable. For how do you understand it: behold, *God made light*[^18], and the Son saw the Father making light, according to your carnal understanding, you who wish therefore to understand him lesser, because he said, *The Son cannot do anything of himself, except what he sees the Father doing*? God the Father made light; what other light did the Son make? God the Father made the firmament, a heaven between the waters and the waters, and the Son saw him, according to your slow and gross understanding: because the Son saw the Father making the firmament, and said, *The Son cannot do anything of himself, except what he sees the Father doing*—give me another firmament. Or have you lost the foundation? But those *built upon the foundation of the apostles and prophets, Christ Jesus himself being the chief cornerstone*[^19], are made at peace in Christ; and they do not contend and err in heresy.

We understand, then, that the light was made by God the Father, but through the Son; the firmament was made by God the Father, but through the Son. For *all things were made through him, and without him was made nothing*. Shake out your understanding; nor is it to be called understanding, but plainly foolishness. God the Father made the world: what other world did the Son make? give me the world of the Son. This one in which we are, whose is it? tell us, by whom was it made? If you say, "By the Son, not by the Father," you have erred away from the Father: if you say, "By the Father, not by the Son," the Gospel answers you: *And the world was made through him, and the world did not know him*[^20]. Recognize, then, him through whom the world was made, and do not be among those who did not know him who made the world.

## What he can and what he is, the Word is from the Father.

**8.** The works of the Father and the Son are inseparable, then. But this is what *The Son cannot do anything of himself* would be, if he said: The Son is not from himself. For indeed, if he is the Son, he is born: if he is born, he is from him of whom he is born. But yet he begot him equal to himself. For nothing was lacking to the one begetting, nor did he seek time in order to beget, who begot a coeternal one; nor did he seek a mother in order to beget, who brought forth the Word from himself; nor had the begetting Father preceded the Son in age, so as to beget a lesser Son. And perhaps someone says that after many ages, in his old age, God received a Son. As the Father without old age, so the Son without increase: neither did the one grow old nor the other grow; but an equal begot an equal, an eternal an eternal.

How, says someone, an eternal an eternal?

As a temporal flame begets a temporal light. But the begetting flame is coeval with the light which it begets, nor does the begetting flame precede in time the begotten light; but from when the flame begins, from then the light begins. Give me a flame without light, and I give you God the Father without the Son.

This, then, is *The Son cannot do anything of himself, except what he sees the Father doing*; because the seeing of the Son is the same as to be born of the Father. His seeing is not one thing and his substance another: nor his power one thing, his substance another. The whole of what he is, is from the Father; the whole of what he can do, is from the Father: since what he can do and what he is, is one thing; and the whole is from the Father.

**9.** He goes on in his own words, and disturbs those who understand badly, that he may call back the erring to a right understanding. When he had said, *The Son cannot do anything of himself, except what he sees the Father doing*: lest perhaps a carnal understanding should creep in, and turn the mind aside, and a man make for himself as it were two craftsmen, one a master, the other a pupil as it were attending to the master—say, for instance, making a chest; so that as the one made a chest, this one too may make another chest according to the seeing which he beheld in the master at work: but lest the carnal understanding should reduplicate for itself something of the kind in that simple divinity, he went on to say, *For whatever things the Father does, these same things the Son also does likewise*[^21]. The Father does not do some things, and the Son other similar things, but the same things likewise. For he did not say: Whatever things the Father does, the Son also does other similar things: but, *Whatever things*, he says, *the Father does, these same things the Son also does likewise*.

What things the Father, these the Son too: the world the Father, the world the Son, the world the Holy Spirit. If three gods, three worlds: if one God the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit, one world was made by the Father through the Son in the Holy Spirit. These things, then, the Son does, which the Father also does, and he does them not unlike: both he does these things, and he does them likewise.

**10.** He had already said, he does these things; why did he add, he does them likewise? Lest another wrong understanding or error should arise in the mind. For you see a man's work; there is in man a mind and a body: the mind commands the body, but there is a great difference between the body and the mind: the body is visible, the mind invisible; between the power and strength of the mind, and of any body even though heavenly, there is a great difference. Yet the mind commands its body, and makes the body act: and what the mind seems to do, this the body too does. The body, then, seems to do this same thing which the mind does, but not likewise.

How does it do this same thing, but not likewise?

The mind makes a word within itself, commands the tongue, and brings forth the word which the mind made: the mind made it, the tongue made it; the lord of the body made it, the servant made it: but in order to make it, the servant received from the lord what he should make, and at the lord's command he made it. This same thing was made by both: but surely not likewise? How not likewise? says someone. See, the word which my mind made remains in me: what my tongue made, the air being struck, has passed away, and is not. When you have spoken a word in your mind, and it has sounded through your tongue, return to your mind, and see that there is the word which you made. Did it remain in your tongue as it remained in your mind? What sounded through your tongue, the sounding tongue made, the thinking mind made: but what the tongue sounded has passed away; what the mind thought, abides. The body, then, made this which the mind made; but not likewise. For the mind made what the mind may hold; but the tongue made what sounds, and through the air strikes the ear. Can you follow the syllables, and make them remain?

Not so, then, the Father and the Son; but he does these same things, and he does them likewise. If God made heaven which abides, the Son made this heaven which abides. If God the Father made man who dies, the Son made the same man who dies. Whatever abiding things the Father made, these the Son too made abiding; because he made them likewise: and whatever temporal things the Father made, these same temporal things the Son made; because not only did he make them, but he made them likewise. For the Father made through the Son, because the Father made all things through the Word.

## You do not touch God, unless you also pass beyond the mind.

**11.** Seek separation in the Father and the Son, you do not find it: but if you have risen up, then you do not find it; if you have touched something above your mind, then you do not find it. For if you are occupied with those things which the erring mind makes for itself, you are speaking with your images, not with the Word of God: your images deceive you. Transcend even the body, and be wise about the mind: transcend even the mind, and be wise about God. You do not touch God unless you also pass beyond the mind: how much less do you touch him, if you have remained in the flesh? Those, then, who are wise about the flesh, how far are they from being wise about what God is? for they would not be there, even if they were wise about the mind. A man recedes far from God when he is wise carnally, and there is a great difference between flesh and mind: yet there is a greater difference between the mind and God. You, if you are in the mind, are in the middle: if you look below, it is the body; if you look above, it is God. Lift yourself up from the body, pass beyond even yourself.

For see what the Psalm said, and be admonished how God is to be perceived: *My tears*, he says, *have become my bread day and night, while it is said to me daily: Where is your God?*[^22] As though the pagans should say: Behold our gods; where is your God? For they show what is seen; we worship what is not seen. And to whom should we show it? to a man who has no eyes to see with? For surely if they see their gods with their eyes; we too have other eyes by which we may see our God. Those very eyes are to be cleansed by our God, that we may see our God: *for blessed are the pure in heart, for they will see God*[^23].

So when he had said that he was disturbed, when it is said to him daily, *Where is your God?* *These things I remembered*, he says, *because it is said to me daily: Where is your God?* and as if wishing to lay hold of his God: *These things I remembered*, he says, *and I poured out my soul above myself*[^24]. That I might reach my God, about whom it was said to me, *Where is your God?* I did not pour out my soul above my flesh, but above myself: I transcended myself, that I might touch him. For he is above me, who made me: no one reaches him, except one who has passed beyond himself.

**12.** Consider the body; it is mortal, it is earthly, it is fragile, it is corruptible: cast it away. But perhaps the flesh is temporal. Consider other bodies, consider the heavenly bodies; they are greater, better, splendid: attend even to them, they are rolled from east to west, they do not stand still; they are seen with the eyes, not only by man, but even by the beast: pass beyond them too.

And how, you will say, do I pass beyond the heavenly bodies, while I walk on the earth?

You pass beyond, not by the flesh, but by the mind. Cast these too away: though they shine, they are bodies; though they gleam from heaven, they are bodies. Come, since perhaps you suppose you have nowhere to go, when you consider all these things.

And beyond the heavenly bodies, where am I to go, you say, and what am I to pass beyond by the mind?

Have you considered all these things?

I have considered them, you say.

By what did you consider them? Let the considerer himself appear. For the considerer of all these things, the discriminator, the distinguisher, and as it were the weigher in the balance of wisdom, is the mind. Without doubt the mind, by which you thought all these things, is better than all these things which you thought.

This mind, then, is spirit, not body: pass beyond it too. Compare the mind itself first, that you may see whither you pass; compare it to the flesh. Far be it that you should deign to compare them. Compare it to the brightness of the sun, the moon, the stars: greater is the brightness of the mind. First see the very swiftness of the mind. See whether the spark of the thinking mind is not swifter than the splendor of the shining sun. You see the rising sun with your mind: how slow is its motion compared with your mind? Quickly you were able to think what the sun is going to do. From east to west it is going to come, and now from the other side it rises tomorrow. When your thought has done this, it is still slow, and you have traversed all things.

A great thing, then, is the mind. But how do I say, it is? Pass beyond it too; because the mind itself too is changeable, although it is better than every body. Now it knows, now it does not know; now it forgets, now it remembers; now it wills, now it does not will; now it sins, now it is just. Pass beyond, then, all changeableness; not only all that is seen, but also all that is changed. For you have passed beyond the flesh which is seen, you have passed beyond heaven, the sun, the moon, and the stars which are seen; pass beyond all that is changed too. For now, these things being passed, you had come to your mind, but there too you found the changeableness of your mind. Is God changeable? Pass beyond, then, even your mind. Pour out your soul above yourself, that you may touch God, about whom it is said to you, *Where is your God?*

**13.** Do not suppose that you are going to do something that a man cannot. This John the Evangelist himself did. He transcended the flesh, transcended the earth which he trod, transcended the seas which he saw, transcended the air where the birds fly, transcended the sun, transcended the moon, transcended the stars, transcended all the spirits that are not seen, transcended his own mind by the very reasoning of his mind. Transcending all these things, pouring out his soul above himself, where did he arrive? What did he see? *In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God*[^25].

If, then, you do not see separation in the light, why do you seek separation in the work? See God, see his Word clinging to the Word that speaks: for he who speaks does not speak by syllables; but to shine forth with the splendor of wisdom, this is to speak. What was said of his Wisdom? *It is the brightness of the eternal light*[^26]. Attend to the brightness of the sun. It is in heaven, and it spreads its brightness through all lands, through all seas: and it is surely a bodily light. If you separate the brightness of the sun from the sun, separate the Word from the Father. I speak of the sun. One thin little flame of a lamp, which can be put out by a single breath, scatters its light over all that lies beneath. You see the light scattered, begotten by the little flame, you see the emission, you do not see the separation.

Understand, then, dearly beloved brothers, that the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit cleave to one another inseparably, that this Trinity is one God; and that all the works of the one God—these are the Father's, these are the Son's, these are the Holy Spirit's. The other things that follow, which pertain to the discourse of our Lord Jesus Christ himself in the Gospel, since a sermon is owed to you tomorrow too, be present that you may hear.
