# Tractate 55

## *Jesus, knowing that his hour had come that he should pass from this world to the Father, having loved his own who were in the world, he loved them to the end* (Jn 13:1-5).

What is *to the end* but unto Christ? *For Christ is the end of the law, unto righteousness for everyone who believes.* An end that perfects, not one that finishes off; an end to which we go, not where we perish. So altogether is it to be understood: *Christ our Passover has been sacrificed.* He himself is our end, into him is our passage.

**1.** The Lord's supper according to John, with his help, must be explained in the tractates due to it, and, as he shall grant us to be able, expounded.

*Now before the feast day of the Passover, Jesus, knowing that his hour had come that he should pass from this world to the Father, having loved his own who were in the world, he loved them to the end*[^1]. "Passover," brothers, is not, as some suppose, a Greek word, but a Hebrew one: yet most opportunely there occurs in this word a certain agreement of the two languages. For because "to suffer" is in Greek "paschein," therefore "Passover" was thought to be "passion," as if this word were named from passion: but in its own language, that is, in Hebrew, "Passover" means "passage": and therefore the people of God then first celebrated the Passover, when, fleeing from Egypt, they crossed the Red Sea.

Now, then, that prophetic figure is fulfilled in truth, when Christ is led like a sheep to the slaughter, with whose blood, smeared on our doorposts, that is, with whose sign of the cross, marked on our foreheads, we are freed from the perdition of this world as from the captivity or destruction of Egypt; and we make a most salutary passage, when we pass from the devil to Christ, and from this unstable world to his most firmly founded kingdom. For we pass over to the abiding God, lest we pass away with the passing world. Of this grace conferred upon us the Apostle, praising God, says: *Who has rescued us from the power of darkness, and transferred us into the kingdom of the Son of his love*[^2].

And so this word, that is, "Passover," which in Latin, as I said, is called "passage," the blessed Evangelist, as it were interpreting it for us, says: *Before the feast day of the Passover, Jesus, knowing that his hour had come that he should pass from this world to the Father.* Behold the Passover, behold the passage. From where, and to where? *From this* world, namely, *to the Father.* Hope was given to the members in their head, that they would surely follow as he passed over. What, then, of the unbelievers, and of those alien to this head and his body? Do not they too pass away, since they do not abide? They too plainly pass away: but it is one thing to pass from the world, another to pass away with the world; one thing to the Father, another to the enemy. For the Egyptians too passed; yet they did not pass through the sea to a kingdom, but in the sea to destruction.

## Love itself led him even unto death.

**2.** *Jesus,* then, *knowing that his hour had come that he should pass from this world to the Father, having loved his own who were in the world, he loved them to the end.* Surely so that they too might pass, from this world where they were, to their head who had passed hence, by love of him. For what is *to the end,* but, unto Christ? *For Christ is the end of the Law,* says the Apostle, *unto righteousness for everyone who believes*[^3]. An end that perfects, not one that finishes off: an end to which we go, not where we perish. So altogether is it to be understood: *Christ our Passover has been sacrificed*[^4]. He himself is our end, into him is our passage.

For I see that these words of the Gospel can also be taken in a certain human way, as though Christ loved his own even unto death, so that this should seem to be what *he loved them to the end* means. This is a human interpretation, not a divine: for he did not love us only up to this point, who always and without end loves us. Far be it that he ended his love by death, who was not ended by death. Even after death that proud and ungodly rich man loved his five brothers[^5], and is Christ to be thought to have loved us only up to death? Far be it, dearly beloved. By no means would he come to love us even unto death, if he ended his love for us by death.

Unless perhaps it is to be understood thus, *he loved them to the end*: that he loved them so much that he died for them. For this he attested, saying: *Greater love than this no one has, than that one lay down his life for his friends*[^6]. So indeed we do not forbid it to be understood, *he loved them to the end,* that is, love itself led him even unto death.

**3.** *And supper being ended,* he says, *the devil having now put it into the heart of Judas, the son of Simon Iscariot, to betray him: knowing that the Father had given all things into his hands, and that he came forth from God, and goes to God, he rises from supper, and lays aside his garments; and when he had taken a linen cloth, he girded himself. Then he puts water into a basin, and began to wash the feet of the disciples, and to wipe them with the linen cloth with which he was girded*[^7]. We ought not so to understand "supper being ended" as if it were already finished and over: for the supper was still going on, when the Lord rose and washed the feet of his disciples. For afterward he reclined again, and afterward gave the morsel to his betrayer, the supper surely not yet finished, that is, while there was still bread on the table. *Supper being made,* then, was said, meaning now prepared, and brought to the table and use of those dining.

## Judas, the seller of the Redeemer.

**4.** But as for what he says: *The devil having now put it into the heart of Judas, the son of Simon Iscariot, to betray him*; if you ask what was put into the heart of Judas: this surely, *to betray him.* This putting-in is a spiritual suggestion: it is done not through the ear, but through the thought; and through this, not bodily, but spiritually. For what is called spiritual is not always to be taken in praise. The Apostle knows certain spiritual things of wickedness in the heavenly places, against which he attests that we have a wrestling[^8]: but there would not be evil spiritual things, unless there were also evil spirits. For from spirit are spiritual things named. But how these come to pass, that diabolical suggestions are sent in, and mingled with human thoughts, so that a man reckons them as his own—whence does a man know? Nor is it to be doubted that good suggestions too are made by a good spirit thus secretly and spiritually: but it matters to which of them the human mind consents, being either forsaken by its merit, or helped by grace, of the divine help.

It had already come to pass, then, in the heart of Judas, through diabolical instigation, that the disciple should betray the master—but the master whom he had not learned to be God. He had already come to the banquet such as he was, a spy on the Shepherd, a plotter against the Savior, a seller of the Redeemer; he had already come such as he was, and was seen, and was borne with, and thought himself unknown: because in him whom he wished to deceive, he was deceived. But he, looking within into that very heart, knowingly used one who did not know.

**5.** *Knowing that the Father had given all things into his hands.* Therefore the betrayer also: for if he did not have him in his hands, he would surely not use him as he willed. Accordingly, the betrayer had already been handed over to him whom he desired to betray; and so by betraying he was doing evil, in such a way that from him whom he betrayed there came about a good which he did not know. For the Lord knew what he was doing for his friends, who patiently used his enemies: and so the Father had given all things into his hands, both evil things for use and good things for effect. *Knowing also that he came forth from God, and goes to God*: neither forsaking God, when he came forth thence; nor forsaking us, when he would return.

**6.** Knowing these things, then, *he rises from supper, and lays aside his garments; and when he had taken a linen cloth, he girded himself. Then he puts water into a basin, and began to wash the feet of the disciples, and to wipe them with the linen cloth with which he was girded.* We ought, dearly beloved, to attend carefully to the Evangelist's meaning. For, about to speak of so great a humility of the Lord, he first wished to commend his loftiness. To this pertains what he says: *Knowing that the Father had given all things into his hands, and that he came forth from God, and goes to God.* When, therefore, the Father had given all things into his hands, he washed not the hands but the feet of the disciples: and when he knew that he had come forth from God, and was going to God, he fulfilled the office not of God the Lord, but of a man the servant.

And to this pertains also that he wished to speak beforehand even of his own betrayer, who had already come such as he was, who was not unknown to him; that this too might add to the highest sum of humility, that he did not disdain to wash even the feet of him whose hands he already foresaw in the crime.

## That whole passion of his is our cleansing.

**7.** But what wonder if he rose from supper and laid aside his garments, who, when he was in the form of God, emptied himself? And what wonder if he girded himself with a linen cloth, who, taking the form of a servant, was found in fashion as a man[^9]? What wonder if he put water into a basin with which to wash the feet of the disciples, who poured out his blood upon the earth, with which to wash away the uncleanness of sins? What wonder if with the linen cloth with which he was girded he wiped the feet that he had washed, who with the flesh with which he was clothed made firm the footsteps of the Evangelists? And to gird himself with the linen cloth, indeed, he laid aside the garments he had: but to take the form of a servant when he emptied himself, he did not lay aside what he had, but took on what he did not have. About to be crucified, he was indeed stripped of his garments, and when dead was wrapped in linen cloths: and that whole passion of his is our cleansing.

About to suffer torments, then, he gave service beforehand; not only to those for whom he was to undergo death, but also to him who was to betray him to death. For so great is the usefulness of human humility that even the divine sublimity commended it by its own example: because proud man would perish forever, unless humble God should find him. *For the Son of man came to seek and to save what was lost*[^10]. But man had perished by following the pride of the deceiver; let him, then, when found, follow the humility of the Redeemer.
