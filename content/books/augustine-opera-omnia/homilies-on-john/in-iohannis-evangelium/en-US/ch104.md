# Tractate 104

## *These things I have spoken to you, that you may have peace in me*[^1].

## He commended this as the cause of his discourse, that they might have peace in him, for the sake of which is done all that we are Christians.

**1.** Before these things which we are now, with the Lord's help, about to treat, Jesus had said: *These things I have spoken to you, that you may have peace in me*[^2]: which we ought to take not as the more recent things said by him a little before, but as all things, whether whatever he spoke to them from the time he began to have them as disciples, or certainly from the time when, after supper, he began this admirable and long discourse. For he recalled such a cause why he spoke to them, that to that end may most rightly be referred either all the things he spoke to them, or especially those which he said when now about to die for them, as it were his last words, after he who was to betray him had gone out from the holy banquet. For he commended this as the cause of his discourse, that they might have peace in him, for the sake of which is done all that we are Christians.

For this peace will have no end of time, but it itself will be the end of all our devout intention and action. For its sake we are imbued with his sacraments, for its sake we are instructed by his wondrous works and discourses, for its sake we have received the pledge of his Spirit, for its sake we believe in him and hope, and are kindled with his love as much as he grants: by this peace we are consoled in all tribulations, by this we are freed from all tribulations: for its sake we bravely endure every tribulation, that in it we may happily reign without any tribulation. Rightly did he close with it those words which to the disciples who understood little were proverbs; but which they would understand when he had given them the promised Holy Spirit, of whom he says above: *These things I have spoken to you while abiding with you. But the Paraclete, the Holy Spirit, whom the Father will send in my name, he will teach you all things, and will bring to your remembrance all things whatsoever I have said to you*[^3]. This, doubtless, was going to be that hour in which he had promised that he would no longer speak in proverbs, but would announce openly concerning the Father. For these very words of his, the Holy Spirit revealing it, were no longer going to be proverbs to those who understood. For the only-begotten Son, while the Holy Spirit spoke in their hearts, was not going to be silent — he who said that in that hour he would announce to them openly concerning the Father, that which, surely, to them now understanding would not be a proverb. But this very thing too — how the Son of God and the Holy Spirit, nay rather the very Trinity, which works inseparably, speak together in the hearts of their own spiritual ones — is a word to those who understand, but a proverb to those who do not understand.

## The prayer which the Lord made for us, he made known also to us.

**2.** When, therefore, he had said why he had spoken all things, namely that they might have peace in him while having tribulation in the world, and had exhorted them to be confident, because he had overcome the world; the discourse which was to them being ended, he then directed his words to the Father, and now began to pray. For the Evangelist follows thus, saying: *These things Jesus spoke, and lifting up his eyes to heaven said: Father, the hour has come, glorify your Son*[^4]. The Lord, the only-begotten and coeternal with the Father, in and from the form of a servant, could, if there were need of it, pray in silence; but he wished so to present himself a suppliant to the Father that he remembered he was our teacher. Accordingly, the prayer which he made for us, he made known also to us; since not only the speaking of so great a master to them, but also his prayer to the Father for them, is the edification of the disciples. And if of those who were present to hear these things spoken, surely also of us who were to read them written down.

Wherefore this which he says, *Father, the hour has come, glorify your Son,* shows that all time, and what he should do or allow to be done at any time, was disposed by him who is not subject to time; since the things that were to be through individual times have their efficient causes in the wisdom of God, in which there are no times. Let it not, then, be believed that this hour came by the urging of fate, but rather by the ordering of God. Nor did a starry necessity bind together the passion of Christ: for far be it that the stars should compel the maker of the stars to die. Not, then, did time drive Christ to die, but Christ chose the time in which he should die: he who also established with the Father the time in which he was born of the Virgin, from whom he was born without time. According to which true and sound doctrine, the apostle Paul too says: *But when the fullness of time came, God sent his Son*[^5]; and God through the prophet says: *In an acceptable time I have heard you, and in the day of salvation I have helped you*[^6]; and again the Apostle: *Behold, now is the acceptable time, behold, now is the day of salvation*[^7]. Let him say, then, *Father, the hour has come,* he who with the Father disposed all hours: as if saying: *Father,* the hour which for the sake of men and among men we together appointed for my glorification, *has come; glorify your Son, that your Son also may glorify you.*

**3.** Some take the Son to have been glorified by the Father in this, that he did not spare him, but handed him over for us all. But if he is said to have been glorified by his passion, how much more by his resurrection? For in the passion his lowliness rather than his glory is commended, the Apostle being witness, who says: *He humbled himself, becoming obedient even to death, even the death of the cross*: then there follows, and he now speaks of his glorification: *Wherefore God also exalted him, and gave him a name which is above every name, that at the name of Jesus every knee should bow, of things in heaven, on earth, and under the earth, and every tongue should confess that the Lord Jesus Christ is in the glory of God the Father*[^8]. This is the glorification of our Lord Jesus Christ, which took its beginning from his resurrection. His lowliness, then, begins in the Apostle's discourse from that place where he says, *He emptied himself, taking the form of a servant*; and reaches as far as, *to the death of the cross.* But his glory begins from that place where he says, *Wherefore God also exalted him*; and reaches as far as, *is in the glory of God the Father.* For even the very word — if the Greek copies be inspected, from which language the apostolic Epistles were translated into Latin — which here is read "glory" (gloria), there is read by the word from which the term is derived in Greek so that it might say "doxasei," which the Latin translator renders *glorify*, though he could also say *make glorious*, which is worth just as much. And therefore in the Apostle's Epistle too, where there is "glory," "brightness" could be put: and if it were done, it would be worth just as much. But that there be no departure from the sounds of the words, just as from "brightness" comes "making glorious," so from "glory" is derived "glorification."

In order, then, that the mediator of God and men, the man Christ Jesus, might be glorified by the resurrection, he was first humbled by the passion: for he would not have risen from the dead, if he had not died. Lowliness is the merit of glory; glory is the reward of lowliness. But this was done in the form of a servant; for in the form of God there always was, and always will be, glory: nay rather, it was not, as though now it is not, nor will it be, as though it is not yet; but without beginning, without end, it always is glory. As for what he says, then, *Father, the hour has come, glorify your Son,* it is to be understood as though he said: The hour of sowing lowliness has come; do not defer the fruit of glory. But what does that mean which follows, *that your Son may glorify you*? Did God the Father too endure a lowliness of flesh or of passion, from which he ought to be glorified? How, then, was the Son going to glorify him, whose everlasting glory could neither be seen as less from the human form, nor be greater in the divine? But I do not wish to confine this question within this sermon, or to make it longer thereby.
