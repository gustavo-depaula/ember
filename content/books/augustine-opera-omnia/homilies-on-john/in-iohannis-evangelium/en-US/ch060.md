# Tractate 60

## *When Jesus had said these things, he was troubled in spirit* (Jn 13:21).

## So great a power is troubled, the firmness of the rock is troubled; or rather is it our weakness that is troubled in him? Yes indeed! Let servants believe nothing unworthy of their Lord, but recognize themselves as members in their head. He who died for us, the same was troubled for us.

**1.** No small question, brothers, is proposed to us from the Gospel of blessed John, where he says: *When Jesus had said these things, he was troubled in spirit, and testified, and said: Amen, amen I say to you, that one of you will betray me*[^1]. Was Jesus troubled, not in flesh, but in spirit, for this reason, that he was about to say: *One of you will betray me*? Did this then first come into his mind, or was it then first suddenly revealed to him, and the sudden newness of so great an evil troubled him? Was he not speaking of this a little before, saying: *He who eats bread with me will lift up his heel against me*? Had he not already said above: *And you are clean, but not all*? Where the Evangelist added: *For he knew who it was that would betray him*: whom he had already signified before, saying: *Did I not choose you twelve, and one of you is a devil*?

What is it, then, that now *he was troubled in spirit,* when *he testified, and said: Amen, amen I say to you, that one of you will betray me*? Was it because he was now about to point him out, so that he should not lie hidden among the rest, but be marked off from the rest, that therefore *he was troubled in spirit*? Or was it because the betrayer himself was now about to go out, to bring the Jews to whom the Lord would be betrayed by him, that the impending passion troubled him, and the near peril, and the betrayer's threatening hand, whose mind he had foreknown?

For this, that Jesus *was troubled in spirit,* is such as that also which he said: *Now is my soul troubled; and what shall I say? Father, save me from this hour; but for this cause I came to this hour*[^2]. As, then, his soul was troubled then, the hour of the passion drawing near; so now too, Judas being about to go out and to come, and so great a crime of the betrayer drawing near, *he was troubled in spirit.*

## It is our weakness that is troubled in him.

**2.** He was troubled, then, having power to lay down his life, and having power to take it up again[^3]. So great a power is troubled, the firmness of the rock is troubled: or rather is it our weakness that is troubled in him? Yes indeed: let servants believe nothing unworthy of their Lord, but recognize themselves as members in their head. He who died for us, the same was troubled for us. He, then, who died by power was troubled by power: he who transfigured the body of our lowliness to be conformed to the body of his glory[^4], transfigured also in himself the affection of our weakness, having compassion on us by the affection of his soul.

Accordingly, when the great, the strong, the sure, the unconquered one is troubled, let us not fear for him as though he were failing; he does not perish, but he seeks us. Us, I say, us altogether he so seeks: let us see ourselves in his being troubled, that when we are troubled, we may not perish through despair. When he who would not be troubled unless willing is troubled, he consoles him who is troubled even unwilling.

## Let the Christian mind be troubled, not by misery, but by mercy.

**3.** Let the arguments of the philosophers perish, who deny that disturbances of the mind fall upon the wise man. God has made foolish the wisdom of this world; and the Lord knows the thoughts of men, that they are vain[^5]. Let the Christian mind be troubled, indeed, not by misery, but by mercy: let it fear lest men perish to Christ, let it be saddened when anyone perishes to Christ; let it long for men to be gained to Christ, let it rejoice when men are gained to Christ: let it fear for itself too, lest it perish to Christ, let it be saddened that it is a pilgrim from Christ; let it long to reign with Christ, let it rejoice while it hopes to reign with Christ. These are surely the four which they call disturbances, fear and sadness, love and joy. Let the Christian mind have them for just causes, and let there be no consent to the error of the Stoic philosophers, or any like them: who indeed, just as they reckon vanity to be truth, so reckon insensibility to be soundness; not knowing that a man's mind, like a member of the body, is the more desperately diseased when it has lost even the sense of pain.

**4.** But someone says: Ought the Christian mind to be troubled even when death is impending? For where is that which the Apostle says, that he has a longing *to be dissolved and to be with Christ*[^6]; if that which he longs for can trouble him when it comes? It is indeed easy for those to answer this who call even joy itself a disturbance. For what if for this reason he is troubled when death impends, because he rejoices when death impends? But this, they say, is to be called gladness, not joy. What is this but to feel the same things, and to wish to change the names of things?

But let us lend our ear to the sacred Writings, and according to them rather, with the Lord's help, let us solve this question: nor, because it is written: *When Jesus had said these things, he was troubled in spirit,* let us say that he was troubled with gladness; lest he himself convict us by his own words where he says: *My soul is sorrowful even unto death*[^7]. Something of the kind is to be understood here too, when, his betrayer being then about to go out alone, and immediately to return with his associates: *Jesus was troubled in spirit.*

**5.** Most firm indeed are Christians, if there be any, who are by no means troubled when death impends: but are they more firm than Christ? Who, most insane, would say this? What, then, is it that he was troubled, but that he consoled the weak in his body, that is, in his Church, by the voluntary likeness of his own weakness: so that if any of his own are still troubled in spirit when death impends, they may look upon him, lest, thinking themselves reprobate for this very reason, they be swallowed up by the worse death of despair? How great a good, then, ought we to expect and hope for from the participation of his divinity, whose very being troubled makes us tranquil, and whose weakness makes us firm?

Whether, then, in this place he was troubled in pitying Judas perishing, or was troubled at his own death drawing near: yet it is in no way to be doubted that he was troubled not by weakness of mind, but by power; lest there arise in us a despair of salvation, when we are troubled not by power, but by weakness. For he indeed bore the weakness of the flesh, which weakness was consumed by the resurrection. But he who was not only man, but also God, surpassed by an ineffable distance the whole human race in fortitude of mind. He was not, then, troubled by anything compelling him, but he troubled himself; which was clearly expressed of him when he raised Lazarus: for there it is written that he troubled himself[^8], so that this may be understood even where it is not read written, and yet he is read to have been troubled. For he moved the human affection, when he judged it fitting, by power in himself, who took up the whole man by power.
