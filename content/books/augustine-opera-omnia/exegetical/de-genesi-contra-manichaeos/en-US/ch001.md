# Revisions I

## Chapter 10 (9): On Genesis: A Refutation of the Manichees, Two Books

**1.** Now that I was settled in Africa, I wrote two books *On Genesis: A Refutation of the Manichees.* For although in my earlier books, in whatever I argued in order to show that God is the supremely good and unchangeable creator of all changeable natures and that there is no nature or substance that is evil insofar as it is a nature and a substance, my purpose was vigilant against the Manichees, these two books were nonetheless published most openly against them in defense of the Old Law, which they attack with the fierce zeal of their insane error: in the first book, from the point where it is written, *in the beginning God made heaven and earth*[^1]*,* down to the completion of the seven days, where we read that God rested on the seventh day. In the second book, from the point where it is written, *this is the book of the creation of heaven and earth*[^2]*,* down to where Adam and his wife were sent out of paradise and a guard was set over the tree of life. Then, at the end of the book, I set the faith of the Catholic truth against the error of the Manichees, briefly and clearly summing up what they say and what we say.

**2.** As for what I said, that *that light does not feed the eyes of irrational birds but the pure hearts of those who believe in God and turn from the love of visible and temporal things to the fulfilling of his commandments, which all men can do if they will*[^3]*,* the new Pelagian heretics should not suppose that it was said in their sense. For it is altogether true that all men can do this if they will; but *the will is prepared by the Lord,* and it is increased by the gift of love to such a degree that they can—which was not said here because it was not necessary for the question then at hand.

As for what one reads there, that the blessing of God by which it was said, *increase and multiply*[^4], should be believed to have been changed into carnal fruitfulness after sin[^5]—if it cannot appear to have been said in any other way than that those human beings are thought not to have been going to have children unless they had sinned, I do not approve of it at all.

Nor does this follow, that it should be understood as to be taken only allegorically that the green plants and the fruit-bearing trees are given as food to every kind of beast and to all the birds and all the serpents in the book of *Genesis,* on the ground that there are also four-footed animals and birds that seem to live on flesh alone[^6]*.* For it could happen that they too were nourished by human beings on the fruits of the earth, if, on account of the obedience by which the human beings themselves served God without any iniquity, they had deserved to have all the beasts and birds serving them in every way.

Likewise, it may be troubling how I said of the people of Israel, *that people still served the Law, as it were amid the sea of the nations, by bodily circumcision and sacrifices*[^7], seeing that among the nations they could not offer sacrifice, just as we now see them remaining without sacrifices[^8]—unless perhaps the fact that they sacrifice a lamb at the Passover should be reckoned as a sacrifice.

**3.** In the second book, too, what I set down—that by the word *fodder* life can be signified[^9]*,* when the better manuscripts do not have *fodder* but *grass*—does not seem aptly said. For the word "grass" does not fit the signification of life as well as "fodder" does.

Likewise, I do not seem[^10] to have rightly called prophetic the words in which it is written, *why is earth and ashes proud?*[^11]*,* because it is not read in the book of one whom we are certain ought to be called a prophet.

Nor did I understand, as he intended, that passage of the Apostle where he adduces the testimony from *Genesis,* saying, *the first man Adam was made a living soul*[^12], when I was expounding what is written, *God breathed into his face the breath of life, and the man was made into a living soul*[^13]*.* For the Apostle adduced that testimony in order to prove that there is an "animal" body; but I thought it should be shown from this that the man was first made animal—not the body of the man alone[^14]*.*

As for what I said, that *sins harm no nature except their own*[^15]*,* I said it because one who harms a just man does not truly harm him, since he even increases his *reward in heaven*[^16], but by sinning he truly harms himself, because on account of the very will to harm he will receive that which he did to harm. The Pelagians, to be sure, can drag this statement over to their own teaching, and so say that the sins of others did not harm little children, because I said, *sins harm no nature except their own*—not considering that little children, who certainly belong to human nature, contract original sin precisely because human nature sinned in the first human beings, and thus no sins have harmed human nature except its own. For *through one man*, in whom all sinned, *sin entered into the world*[^17]; for I did not say "sins harm no man" but *sins harm no nature except their own.*

Likewise, in what I said a little later, that *there is no natural evil*[^18], they can look for a similar hiding place, unless this is taken to refer to a nature such as it was first created without fault; for that is what is truly and properly called the nature of man. But we use the word in a transferred sense when we also call "nature" the condition in which a man is born, according to which manner of speaking the Apostle said, *for we too were once by nature children of wrath, like the rest*[^19].

This work begins thus: *If the Manichees chose those whom they deceive.*
