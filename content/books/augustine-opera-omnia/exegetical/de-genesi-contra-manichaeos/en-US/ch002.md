# Book I

## About to write against the Manichees, Augustine will use a style lowered to the grasp of the less learned.

**1.** 1. If the Manichees chose those whom they deceive, we too would choose the words with which to answer them; but since they attack with their error both those learned in letters and the unlearned, and since, while they promise truth, they try to turn people away from the truth, their emptiness must be refuted not by ornate and polished speech but by plain facts.

For I was pleased by the opinion of certain truly Christian men who, though they are educated in the liberal arts, nonetheless, when they had read other books of ours that we published against the Manichees, saw that those books were either not understood by the less learned or understood only with difficulty; and they very kindly advised me not to abandon the common manner of speaking, if I intended to drive those so pernicious errors out of the minds even of the unlearned. For this customary and simple style is understood even by the learned, whereas the other is not understood by the unlearned.

## The books of the Old Testament are to be defended against the Manichees' censures, which lie in wait for the faith of the unlearned.

**1.** 2. The Manichees, then, are accustomed to disparage the Scriptures of the Old Testament, which they do not know, and by that disparagement to mock and deceive our weak and little ones, who do not find how to answer them; for there is no Scripture that cannot easily be faulted by those who do not understand it.

But for this reason divine providence permits there to be many heretics of various errors, so that, when they insult us and question us about things we do not know, we may at least in this way shake off our laziness and desire to know the divine Scriptures.

For this reason the Apostle also says, *there must be many heresies, so that those who are approved may become manifest among you*[^1]. For those are approved by God who are able to teach well, but they cannot be made manifest to men except when they teach; and they are unwilling to teach except those who seek to be taught. But many are too lazy to seek, unless through the troubles and insults of heretics they are, as it were, roused from sleep, and grow ashamed of their ignorance, and feel themselves to be in peril because of that ignorance of theirs.

If such men are of good faith, they do not yield to the heretics but diligently inquire what to answer them. Nor does God abandon them, so that those who ask receive, and those who seek find, and to those who knock it is opened[^2]. But those who despair of being able to find in Catholic teaching what they seek are worn down by errors; and if they inquire perseveringly, they return, after great labors, weary and thirsty and almost dead, to the very fountains from which they had strayed.

## The verse Genesis 1:1 is defended against those who carp at what God was doing before the creation of the world.

**2.** 3. The first book of the Old Testament, then, which is entitled *Genesis,* the Manichees are accustomed to fault in this way. As to what is written, *in the beginning God made heaven and earth*[^3]*,* they ask, "in what beginning?" and they say: "If God made heaven and earth in some beginning of time, what was he doing before he made heaven and earth? And why did it suddenly please him to make what he had never made before throughout the eternal ages?" To these we answer that God made heaven and earth in the beginning, not in the beginning of time but in Christ, when the *Word was with the Father, through whom all things were made and in whom they were made*[^4]. For our Lord Jesus Christ, when the Jews asked him who he was, answered, *the beginning, that is why I am even speaking to you*[^5].

But even if we believe that God made heaven and earth in the beginning of time, we must certainly understand that before the beginning of time there was no time. For God made the times as well; and therefore before he made the times, there were no times. We cannot, then, say that there was some time when God had not yet made anything. For how could there be a time that God had not made, when he himself is the maker of all times? And if time began to exist together with heaven and earth, no time can be found in which God had not yet made heaven and earth.

But when it is asked, "Why did it suddenly please him?" it is said as though some times had passed in which God did nothing. For no time could pass that God had not yet made, since none can be the maker of times except him who is before the times. Surely the Manichees themselves read the Apostle Paul, and praise and honor him; and by interpreting his Epistles badly they deceive many. Let them tell us, then, what the Apostle Paul said: *the acknowledgment of the truth which is according to godliness, in the hope of eternal life, which God who does not lie promised before the eternal ages*[^6]. For what could the eternal ages have had before them? Let them be compelled, then, to expound this, so that they may understand that they do not understand, when they rashly wish to fault what they ought to have sought out diligently.

## Against the Manichees it is shown why it suddenly pleased God to create the world.

**2.** 4. But suppose they do not say, "Why did it please God suddenly to make heaven and earth," but remove the word "suddenly" from it, and say only this: "Why did it please God to make heaven and earth?" For we do not say that this world is coeternal with God, since this world does not belong to that eternity to which God belongs; for God made the world, and so it was together with the very creation that God made that the times began to exist; and that is why they are called eternal times.

Yet the times are not eternal in the way that God is eternal, because God, who is the maker of times, is before the times: just as all the things that God made are *very good*, but they are not good in the way that God is good, because he made them, whereas these things were made; nor did he beget them out of himself, so that they would be what he himself is, but he made them out of nothing, so that they would not be equal either to him by whom they were made or to his Son through whom they were made; for that is just.

If, then, these men say, "Why did it please God to make heaven and earth?" they must be answered that those who desire to know the will of God should first learn the force of the human will. For they seek to know the causes of God's will, when God's will is itself the cause of all things that are. For if God's will has a cause, there is something that precedes God's will—which it is impious to believe. Whoever, then, says, "Why did God make heaven and earth?" must be answered, "Because he willed it." For God's will is the cause of heaven and earth, and therefore God's will is greater than heaven and earth. But whoever says, "Why did he will to make heaven and earth?" is seeking something greater than the will of God; yet nothing greater can be found.

Let human rashness, then, restrain itself, and not seek what does not exist, lest it fail to find what does exist. And if anyone desires to know the will of God, let him become a friend of God; for if anyone wished to know the will of a man whose friend he was not, everyone would laugh at his impudence and folly. But no one is made a friend of God except by the most purified character and by that end of the commandment of which the Apostle speaks: *the end of the commandment is love from a pure heart, and a good conscience, and faith unfeigned*[^7]; and if these men had this, they would not be heretics.

## Genesis 1:2 is defended: the earth invisible, etc.

**3.** 5. As for what follows in the book of *Genesis*: *but the earth was invisible and unformed*[^8]*,* the Manichees fault it by saying: "How did God make heaven and earth in the beginning, if the earth was already invisible and unformed?" Thus, when they want to disparage the divine Scriptures before knowing them, they fail to understand even the most obvious things.

For what could have been said more plainly than this was said: *in the beginning God made heaven and earth; but the earth was invisible and unformed;* that is: "In the beginning God made heaven and earth; but the earth itself, which God made, was invisible and unformed, before God arranged the forms of all things in their places and seats with an ordered distinction"—before he said, *let there be light,* and, *let there be a firmament,* and, *let the waters be gathered together,* and, *let the dry land appear*[^9]*,* and the rest, which are set forth in order in that same book in such a way that little ones can grasp them? All of which contain mysteries so great that whoever has learned them will either grieve over the emptiness of all the heretics because they are men, or laugh at it because they are proud.

## God's light is something far other than the light of this sun.

**3.** 6. There follows in that same book: *and there was darkness over the abyss*[^10]*.* This the Manichees fault, saying: "Was God, then, in darkness before he made light?" Truly it is they who are in the darkness of ignorance, and therefore they do not understand the light in which God was before he made this light. For they know no light except the one they see with the eyes of the flesh. And so this sun, which we behold in common not only with the larger beasts but even with flies and little worms, they so worship as to say that it is a particle of that light in which God dwells.

But let us understand that there is another light in which God dwells, from which comes that light of which we read in the Gospel: *it was the true light, which enlightens every man coming into this world*[^11]. For the light of this sun does not enlighten every man, but the body of a man and his mortal eyes, in which the eyes of eagles surpass us, since they are said to gaze at this sun far better than we do. But that light does not feed the eyes of irrational birds, but the pure hearts of those who believe in God and turn from the love of visible and temporal things to the fulfilling of his commandments. This all men can do if they will, because that light enlightens every man coming into this world. So there was darkness over the abyss before this light was made, of which light it is consequently said in this place.

## In defense of verse 3 it is shown that darkness is nothing.

**4.** 7. *And God said, 'let there be light*[^12]*.'* For where there is no light, there is darkness—not because darkness is anything, but the very absence of light is called darkness. Just as silence is not some thing, but where there is no sound, it is called silence. And nakedness is not some thing, but on a body where there is no covering, it is called nakedness. And emptiness is not anything, but a place where there is no body is called empty. So darkness is not anything, but where there is no light, it is called darkness.

We say this because they are accustomed to say: "Where did that darkness over the abyss come from, before God made light? Who had made or begotten it? Or if no one had made or begotten it, then darkness was eternal." As though darkness were something; but, as has been said, it received this name from the absence of light.

But because they themselves, deceived by their fables, believed there to be a race of darkness, in which they suppose there were bodies and forms and souls within those bodies, they therefore think that darkness is something; and they do not understand that darkness is not perceived except when we do not see, just as silence is not perceived except when we do not hear. But just as silence is nothing, so too darkness is nothing. And just as these men say that the race of darkness fought against the light of God, so another, equally empty-headed, could likewise say that a race of silences fought against the voice of God.

But we have not now undertaken to refute and convict those empty fancies. For now we have determined to defend the things they fault in the Old Testament, as far as the Lord deigns to grant us strength, and to show in them that the blindness of men can avail nothing against the truth of God.

## How to understand "the Spirit was borne over," etc., according to Genesis 1:2.

**4.** 8. As for what is written, *and the Spirit of God was borne over the water*[^13]*,* the Manichees are accustomed to fault it by saying: "Was the water, then, the dwelling place of the Spirit of God, and did it contain the Spirit of God?" They try to pervert everything with a perverse mind, and they are blinded by their own malice. For when we say, "the sun is borne over the earth," do we mean it to be understood that the sun dwells in the earth and that the earth contains the sun?

And yet the Spirit of God was not borne over the water in the way that the sun is borne over the earth, but in another way, which few understand. For that Spirit was not borne over the water through spatial distances, as the sun is borne over the earth, but through the power of his invisible sublimity.

But let these men tell us how the will of a craftsman is borne over the things that are to be fashioned. And if they do not comprehend these human and everyday things, let them fear God, and with a simple heart seek what they do not understand, lest, when they wish with sacrilegious words to cut down the truth they cannot see, the axe rebound upon their own legs. For that truth, which remains unchangeable, cannot be cut down; but whatever blows are aimed at it are struck back, and return with a greater stroke upon those who dare to fell what they ought to believe in order to deserve to understand.

## What is signified by the word "water" in Genesis 1:2.

**5.** 9. Then they ask, and in mockery they question: "Where did that water come from over which the Spirit of God was being borne? For was it written above that God made water?" If they asked this devoutly, they would find how it ought to be understood. For water was not named in this place in such a way that we should understand the water we can already see and touch; just as the earth that was called unformed and invisible was not such as the earth that can now be seen and handled. But when it was said, *in the beginning God made heaven and earth*[^14]*,* by the words heaven and earth the whole creation was signified, which God made and founded.

These things were given the names of visible things on account of the weakness of the little ones, who are less able to comprehend invisible things. First, then, matter was made, confused and formless, from which all things that are distinct and formed would be made—which I believe is called by the Greeks *χάος* (chaos). For so too in another place we read it said in the praises of God, *you who made the world out of formless matter*[^15]—which some manuscripts have as *out of unseen matter.*

## Formless matter is from nothing, and the rest from it.

**6.** 10. And therefore God is most rightly believed to have made all things out of nothing, because, even if all formed things were made out of that matter, this matter itself was nonetheless made out of nothing at all. For we ought not to be like those who do not believe that almighty God could make anything out of nothing, when they observe that smiths and craftsmen of any kind cannot fashion anything unless they have something from which to fashion it. For wood helps the carpenter, and silver helps the silversmith, and gold helps the goldsmith, and earth helps the potter to be able to complete his works.

For if they are not helped by the matter from which they make something, they can make nothing, since they do not themselves make the matter. For the carpenter does not make the wood, but makes something out of wood; and so too all other craftsmen of this kind. But almighty God needed to be helped by no thing that he himself had not made, in order to accomplish what he willed. For if, for the things he willed to make, he was helped by some thing that he himself had not made, he was not almighty—which it is sacrilegious to believe.

## Why formless matter was called invisible earth, water, and so on.

**7.** 11. That formless matter, then, which God made out of nothing, was first called "heaven and earth," and it was said, *in the beginning God made heaven and earth*[^16]—not because it already was this, but because it could be this; for heaven too is written to have been made afterward.

Just as, considering the seed of a tree, we might say that the roots and trunk and branches and fruit and leaves are there—not because they already are, but because they are going to be from it—so it was said, *in the beginning God made heaven and earth,* as if it were the seed of heaven and earth, when the matter of heaven and earth was still in confusion; but because it was certain that heaven and earth would come from it, that matter too was already called heaven and earth.

In this manner of speaking the Lord also speaks when he says, *now I will not call you servants, because the servant does not know what his master does. But I have called you friends, because all that I have heard from my Father I have made known to you*[^17]—not because it had already been done, but because it most certainly was going to be done. For a little later he says to them, *I still have many things to say to you, but you cannot bear them now*[^18]. Why, then, had he said, *all that I have heard from my Father I have made known to you,* except that he knew he was going to do this?

So too matter could be called heaven and earth, the matter from which heaven and earth had not yet been made, but yet was not to be made from anything else. Countless such expressions are found in the divine Scriptures. As in the custom of our speech, when we say that what we most certainly hope is going to happen, "consider it already done."

**7.** 12. But this still formless matter he willed also to call invisible and unformed earth, because among all the elements of the world the earth seems less beautiful than the rest. He called it invisible on account of its obscurity, and unformed on account of its formlessness. That same matter he also called "water," over which the Spirit of God was being borne, just as the will of the artisan is borne over the things to be fashioned. And although this can be reached by the understanding of a few, yet I do not know whether it can be expounded in human words even by a few men. But it was not absurd that this matter was also called water, because all things that are born in the earth—whether animals, or trees, or plants, and any like them—begin to be formed and nourished by moisture.

All these names, then—whether heaven and earth, or invisible and unformed earth and the abyss with darkness, or the water over which the Spirit was borne—are names of formless matter: so that the unknown thing might be introduced to the less learned by known terms; and not by one term but by many, lest, if there were one, it should be thought to be what men were accustomed to understand by that term.

It was called heaven and earth, then, because from it heaven and earth would come. It was called invisible and unformed earth, and darkness over the abyss, because it was formless and could be seen or handled in no form, even if there were a man to see and handle it. It was called water, because, easy and pliant, it lay beneath the one working, so that all things might be formed from it. But under all these names there was unseen and formless matter, out of which God founded the world.

## The Manichees' calumny concerning Genesis 1:4 is restrained.

**8.** 13. *And God said, 'let there be light.' And light was made*[^19]*.* This the Manichees are not accustomed to fault, but rather what follows: *and God saw the light, that it was good*[^20]*.* For they say: "So God did not know the light, or did not know the good." Wretched men, who are displeased that his works pleased God, when they see that even a human craftsman—for example, a worker in wood—although in comparison with the wisdom and power of God he is almost nothing, yet cuts and works the wood by hewing, shaping with the axe, planing, or turning and polishing, until it is brought, as far as he can, to the rules of the art, and pleases its craftsman.

Did he, then, not know the good simply because what he made pleases him? Assuredly he knew it within, in his mind, where the art itself is more beautiful than the things that are fashioned by the art. But what the craftsman sees within in the art, this he approves outside in the work, and that is perfect which pleases its craftsman.

*God saw*, then, *the light, that it was good:* by which words it is not shown that an unexpected good shone forth to God, but that the perfect work pleased him.

## What Christ willed to teach us by his wonder and his other reactions.

**8.** 14. What if it had been said, "God wondered at the light, that it was good"? How much would they cry out? How much would they wrangle? For wonder is indeed accustomed to arise from unhoped-for things, and yet these men read, and praise, that in the Gospel our Lord Jesus Christ wondered at the faith of believers[^21].

But who had made that faith in them, except he himself who wondered at it? And if another had made it, why would he wonder, who was foreknowing? If the Manichees solve this question, let them see that the other can be solved as well. But if they do not solve it, why do they fault these things which they refuse to let pertain to themselves, when they do not know the things they say pertain to themselves?

For what our Lord wonders at, he signifies to be a thing of wonder for us, who still need to be moved in this way. All such movements of his, then, are not signs of a disturbed mind, but of a teacher instructing.

Such too are the words of the Old Testament, which do not teach that God is weak, but rather coax our weakness. For nothing can be said worthily of God. Yet, so that we may be nourished and may come to those things which can be expressed in no human speech, things are said to us which we are able to grasp.

## How to take what is said in Genesis 1:4-5 about light and darkness.

**9.** 15. *And God divided between the light and the darkness, and God called the light day, and the darkness he called night*[^22]*.* Here it is not said, "God made the darkness," because darkness, as was said above, is the absence of light; yet a distinction was made between the light and the darkness. Just as we make a sound by crying out, but make silence by not sounding, since the cessation of sound is silence—yet by a certain perception we distinguish between sound and silence, and call the one sound, the other silence—so, just as we are rightly said to make silence, so in many places of the divine Scriptures God is rightly said to make darkness, because at whatever times and places he wills he either does not give light or withdraws it.

But all this was said for the sake of our understanding. For in what language did God call the light day, and the darkness night? Was it Hebrew, or Greek, or Latin, or some other? And so of all the things he named, one can ask in what language he named them. But with God there is pure understanding, without the noise and diversity of languages. *He called*, then, means "he caused to be called"; because he so distinguished all things and ordered them that they could both be discerned and receive names.

But afterward, in its place, we shall inquire whether it is truly to be taken in this way, that *God called*; for the more we advance in the Scriptures and grow accustomed to them, the more their expressions become known to us. For so we say, "that householder built this house," that is, caused it to be built; and many such things are found throughout all the books of the divine Scriptures.

## How it is rightly understood that one day began and passed, according to Genesis 1:5.

**10.** 16. *And evening was made, and morning was made, one day*[^23]*.* Here too the Manichees raise a calumny, since they think it was said as though the day had begun from evening. They do not understand that that work by which light was made, and a division was made between light and darkness, and the light was called day and the darkness night—this whole work, then, they do not understand to pertain to the day; and that it was after this work, as if the day were finished, that evening was made.

But because the night too pertains to its own day, one day is not said to have passed until, the night having also passed, morning was made: thus the remaining days are reckoned in succession from morning to morning. For now, when morning was made and one day was finished, the work begins which follows from that very morning already made, and after that work evening comes, then morning, and another day passes; and so in succession the remaining days pass.

## How the waters were divided by the firmament, according to Genesis 1:6-8.

**11.** 17. *And God said, 'let there be a firmament in the midst of the water, and let there be a division between water and water'; and so it was made. And God made the firmament, and God divided between the water that is above the firmament and the water that is below the firmament, and God called the firmament heaven; and God saw that it was good*[^24]*.* I do not remember that the Manichees are accustomed to fault this; nevertheless, as to the fact that the waters were divided so that some were above the firmament and some below the firmament, since we were saying that that matter was called by the word water, I believe that by the firmament of heaven the corporeal matter of visible things was separated from the incorporeal matter of invisible things.

For since heaven is a most beautiful body, every invisible creature surpasses even the beauty of heaven; and therefore perhaps the invisible waters are said to be above heaven, waters understood by a few to surpass heaven not by location of place but by the dignity of their nature—although on this matter nothing is to be rashly affirmed, for it is obscure and remote from the senses of men; but however it stands, before it is understood, it is to be believed.

*And evening was made, and morning was made, a second day*[^25]*.* Now all these things that are repeated are to be understood and treated as above.

## That the gathering of the waters, of which Genesis 1:9-10 speaks, is itself the forming of the waters.

**12.** 18. *And God said, 'let the water that is under heaven be gathered together into one gathering, and let the dry land appear'; and so it was made. And the water that was under heaven was gathered together into one gathering, and the dry land appeared. And God called the dry land earth, and the gathering of the water he called sea. And God saw that it was good*[^26]*.* In this place the Manichees say: "If the whole was full of waters, how could the waters be gathered into one?" But it was already said above that by the word waters that matter was called over which the Spirit of God was borne, from which God was going to form all things.

But now, when it is said, *let the water that is under heaven be gathered together into one gathering,* this is said so that that corporeal matter may be formed into the appearance that these visible waters have. For that gathering into one is itself the forming of these waters, which we see and touch. For every form is brought together to the rule of unity.

And as for what is said, *let the dry land appear,* what else is to be understood as meant, except that that matter receives the visible form that this earth now has, which we see and touch? Above, then, what was called invisible and unformed earth was the confusion and obscurity of matter being named; and what was called water over which the Spirit of God was borne was again the same matter being named. But now this water and this earth are formed out of that matter, which was called by these very names before it received these forms that we now see. It is indeed said that in the Hebrew idiom every gathering of waters, whether salt or fresh, is called sea.

## The complaint about Genesis 1:11 is rejected: why does the earth bring forth unfruitful and harmful things?

**13.** 19. *And God said, 'let the earth bring forth the plant of fodder bearing seed according to its kind and likeness, and the fruit-bearing tree making fruit, whose seed is in itself according to its likeness.' And so it was made. And the earth put forth the plant of fodder, bearing seed according to its kind, and the fruit-bearing tree making fruit, whose seed is in itself according to its likeness, according to its kind, upon the earth. And God saw that it was good. And evening was made, and morning was made, a third day*[^27]. Here they are accustomed to say: "If God commanded the plant of fodder and the fruit-bearing tree to be born from the earth, who commanded so many plants—thorny or poisonous—to be born, which are of no use for fodder, and so many trees that bear no fruit?"

These must be answered in such a way that no mysteries are opened to the unworthy, nor is it shown in what figure of things to come these things were said in this way.

So it must be said that through the sin of man the earth was cursed, so that it would bring forth thorns—not so that it might itself feel punishment, since it is without feeling, but so that it might always set before the eyes of men the guilt of human sin, by which they might be admonished at some time to turn away from sins and turn to the commandments of God.

Poisonous plants were created for the punishment, or for the testing, of mortals; and all this on account of sin, since we were made mortal after sin. But by unfruitful trees men are reproached, so that they may understand how shameful it is to be without the fruit of good works in the field of God, that is, in the Church; and that they may fear lest God forsake them, since they too forsake the unfruitful trees in their fields and give them no cultivation.

Before the sin of man, then, it is not written that the earth brought forth anything except the plant of fodder and the fruitful trees; but after sin we see many horrid and unfruitful things born from the earth, for the cause, I believe, that we have stated. For so it is said to the first man after he had sinned: *the earth will be cursed for you in all your works; in sorrow and groaning you will eat of it all the days of your life: it will bring forth thorns and thistles for you, and you will eat the fodder of your field: in the sweat of your face you will eat your bread, until you return to the earth from which you were taken; for earth you are, and to earth you will go*[^28]*.*

## Difficulties from Genesis 1:14-19 are resolved.

**14.** 20. *And God said, 'let there be lights in the firmament of heaven, so that they may shine upon the earth, and may divide between day and night, and may be for signs, and for seasons, and for days, and for years; and let them be for splendor in the firmament of heaven, so that they may shine upon the earth.' And so it was made. And God made two great lights, a greater and a lesser: the greater light for the beginning of the day, and the lesser light for the beginning of the night, and the stars. And God set them in the firmament of heaven, so that they may shine upon the earth, and may preside over the day and the night, and may divide between day and night. And God saw that it was good. And evening was made, and morning was made, a fourth day*[^29]*.*

Here they ask, first, how the lights—that is, the sun, the moon, and the stars—were made on the fourth day. For how could the three earlier days exist without the sun, when we see that now the day is spent by the rising and setting of the sun, and night comes upon us by the sun's absence, when from another part of the world it returns to the east? To these we answer that it could have been that the three earlier days were each reckoned over as great a span of time as the span in which the sun goes around, from the time it proceeds from the east until it returns again to the east. For men could perceive this span and length of time even if they lived in caves, where they could not see the rising and setting sun. And so it is perceived that this span could have occurred even without the sun, before the sun was made, and that this same span was reckoned over the three days, one by one.

This, then, is what we would answer, were we not called back by what is said there, *and evening was made, and morning was made,* which we now see cannot happen without the course of the sun. It remains, then, that we understand that within that very span of time the distinctions of the works themselves were so named—evening for the completion of a finished work, and morning for the beginning of a work to come—drawn, that is, from the likeness of human works, since for the most part they begin in the morning and end at evening. For the divine Scriptures have the custom of transferring words from human matters to divine matters.

## What is meant by "and let them be for signs and for seasons."

**14.** 21. Then they ask why it was said of the lights, *and let them be for signs and for seasons*[^30]*.* "For," they say, "could those three days have been without seasons, or do they not pertain to spans of time?"

But *for signs and seasons* was said so that through these lights the seasons might be distinguished and recognized by men; because if the seasons run on and are marked off by no divisions—divisions that are noted by the courses of the lights—the seasons can indeed run on and pass by, but they cannot be understood and discerned by men. Just as the hours, when the day is cloudy, do indeed pass by and complete their spans, but cannot be distinguished and noted by us.

## What is meant by "for the beginning of the day," etc.

**14.** 22. As for what was said, *and God made two lights; the greater light for the beginning of the day, and the lesser light for the beginning of the night*[^31]*,* it was said in place of saying, "for the rule of the day and for the rule of the night." For the sun does not merely begin the day and not also carry it through and end it; and the moon sometimes comes forth to us in the middle of the night or at the end of the night. If, then, those nights in which it does this are not begun by it, how was it made for the beginning of the night? But if by *beginning* you understand "principal place," and by "principal place" "rule," it is clear that through the day the sun holds rule, and the moon through the night, because, although the other lights then appear, the moon nonetheless surpasses them all by its brightness, and so is most rightly called their chief.

## The words "and let them divide between day and night" are explained.

**14.** 23. As for what was said, *and let them divide between day and night*[^32], a calumny can be made here, so that one says: "How had God already divided above between day and night, if the lights do this on the fourth day?" So, then, it was here said, *let them divide between day and night,* as if it were said: "Let them so divide day and night between themselves that the day is given to the sun, and the night to the moon and the other lights." These two had already been divided, but not yet among the lights, so that it might now be settled, in respect of the number of the lights, what should appear to men by day and what by night.

## That by the word water in Genesis 1:20-23 the misty air is designated.

**15.** 24. *And God said, 'let the waters bring forth creeping things of living souls, and flying things flying over the earth under the firmament of heaven.' And so it was made. And God made the great sea creatures, and every soul of the animals and crawling things that the waters brought forth according to each kind, and every kind of winged flying thing according to its kind. And God saw that they were good; and God blessed them, saying: 'increase and multiply, and fill the waters of the sea, and let the flying things multiply over the earth.' And evening was made, and morning was made, a fifth day*[^33]*.* Here they are accustomed to fault, asking—or rather raising a calumny—why it was written that the animals not only those that live in the waters, but also those that fly in the air, and all winged things, were born from the waters.

But let all whom these things trouble know that this misty and moist air in which the birds fly is, by the most learned men who diligently inquire into these matters, accustomed to be reckoned with the waters. For it thickens and becomes dense by the exhalations and, as it were, vapors of the sea and the earth, and from that moisture it grows somehow rich, so that it can bear up the flight of birds. That is why on clear nights it even gives dew, the drops of which dew are found in the morning on the plants.

For that mountain of Macedonia which is called Olympus is said to be of such height that on its summit neither is wind felt nor do clouds gather, because by its height it rises above all this moist air in which the birds fly; and therefore it is asserted that not even birds fly there. This is said to have been reported by those who, on account of certain sacrifices I know not which, were accustomed each year to climb the summit of the said mountain and to write certain marks in the dust, which they found intact in another year—which could not happen if that place were subject to wind or rain. Then, because the thinness of the air there did not give them breath, they could not last there unless they applied moist sponges to their nostrils, from which to draw a denser and accustomed breath; these men, then, reported that they had never at any time seen even a single bird in that place.

Not without reason, therefore, does the most trustworthy Scripture record that not only the fish and the other animals that are in the waters, but also the birds, were born from the waters; because they can fly through this air, which rises from the moisture of the sea and the earth.

## Why harmful living creatures were created (Genesis 1:24f.).

**16.** 25. *And God said, 'let the earth bring forth the living soul according to each kind of four-footed animals and serpents and beasts of the earth.' And so it was made. And God made the beasts of the earth according to their kind, and the cattle according to their kind, and all the crawling things of the earth according to their kind. And God saw that they were good*[^34]*.* The Manichees are accustomed to raise this question too, saying: "What need was there for God to make so many animals, whether in the waters or on the earth, that are not necessary to men? Many too are harmful and to be feared."

But when they say these things, they do not understand how all things are beautiful to their founder and craftsman, who uses them all for the governance of the universe, over which he holds dominion by the highest Law. For if an unskilled man enters the workshop of some craftsman, he sees there many tools whose purposes he does not know, and if he is very foolish, he thinks them superfluous. And if, being incautious, he falls into the furnace, or wounds himself with some sharp iron tool when he handles it badly, he thinks too that there are many harmful and noxious things there. Yet because the craftsman knows their use, he laughs at the man's foolishness, and, paying no heed to his inept words, presses on with his work in the shop.

And yet men are so foolish that, with a human craftsman, they dare not find fault with what they do not understand, but when they have seen these things they believe them to be necessary and established for some uses; whereas in this world, whose founder and administrator God is proclaimed to be, they dare to fault many things whose causes they do not see, and, in the works and instruments of the almighty craftsman, they want to seem to know what they do not know.

## That all things are beautiful in their kind: living creatures are either useful, or harmful, or superfluous.

**16.** 26. For my part, I confess that I do not know why mice and frogs were created, or flies or little worms; yet I see that all things are beautiful in their kind, although, on account of our sins, many seem to us hostile. For I consider the body and members of no animal in which I do not find measures and numbers and order pertaining to the unity of harmony. Whence all these come I do not understand, except from the highest measure and number and order, which consist in the very sublimity of God, unchangeable and eternal.

If these most talkative and most inept men would consider this, they would not weary us, but would themselves, by considering all beauties, both the highest and the lowest, praise God the craftsman everywhere; and since reason is nowhere offended, then, if perchance somewhere the bodily sense is offended, they would impute it not to a fault of the things themselves but to the deserts of our mortality.

And surely all animals are either useful to us, or harmful, or superfluous. Against the useful they have nothing to say. But by the harmful we are either punished, or tested, or terrified, so that we may love and desire not this life, subject to many dangers and toils, but another, better life, where there is the highest security, and may obtain it for ourselves by the deserts of godliness.

But as for the superfluous, what is it to us to inquire? If it displeases you that they are of no profit, let it please you that they do no harm; because, even if they are not necessary to our house, yet by them the wholeness of this universe is completed, which is much greater and much better than our house. For God administers this universe much better than each one of us administers his own house.

Make use, then, of the useful, beware of the harmful, leave aside the superfluous. In all things, however, when you see measures and numbers and order, seek the craftsman. And you will find no other than where the highest measure, and the highest number, and the highest order is, that is, God, of whom it was most truly said that he *arranged all things in measure, and number, and weight*[^35].

So perhaps you will reap a richer fruit when you praise God in the lowliness of the ant than when you cross a river on the height of some beast of burden.

## How man, made to the image of God, is to be understood according to Genesis 1:26.

**17.** 27. *And God said: 'let us make man to our image and likeness; and let him have power over the fish of the sea and the flying things of heaven, and over all cattle and wild beasts, and over all the earth, and over all the crawling things that crawl upon the earth*[^36]*,'* and the rest, down to the evening and morning by which the sixth day is completed[^37]. This question above all the Manichees are accustomed to stir up loquaciously, and to insult us for believing that man was made to the image and likeness of God. For they attend to the figure of our body, and unhappily ask whether God has nostrils and teeth and a beard, and inner members too, and the rest that are necessary in us. But to believe such things of God is ridiculous, indeed impious, and so they deny that man was made to the image and likeness of God.

To these we answer that these members are indeed often named in the Scriptures when God is being introduced to little ones who hear; and this not only in the books of the Old Testament but also in those of the New. For the eyes of God are mentioned, and the ears, and the lips, and the feet, and the Son is proclaimed to sit at the right hand of God the Father. And the Lord himself says, *do not swear by heaven, because it is God's throne; nor by the earth, because it is the footstool of his feet*[^38]. Likewise he himself says that by the finger of God he was casting out demons[^39].

But all who understand the Scriptures spiritually have learned to take by these names not bodily members but spiritual powers, like the helmet, and the shield, and the sword[^40], and many others. First, then, these heretics must be asked with what shamelessness they raise a calumny against the Old Testament from such words, when they see these very things placed in the New as well—or perhaps they do not see, but in their wrangling are blinded.

## That man was made to the image of God on account of the soul, by which he surpasses all the cattle.

**17.** 28. But let them nonetheless know that in Catholic teaching the spiritual faithful do not believe God to be bounded by a bodily form; and that man's being said to be made to the image of God is said with respect to the inner man, where reason and understanding are: from which he also has "power over the fish of the sea, and the flying things of heaven, and over all cattle and wild beasts, and over all the earth, and over all the crawling things that crawl upon the earth." For when he had said, *let us make man to our image and likeness,* he added at once, *and let him have power over the fish of the sea and the flying things of heaven*[^41]*,* and the rest: so that we might understand that man is said to be made to the image of God not on account of the body, but on account of that power by which he surpasses all the cattle.

For all the other animals are subject to man, not on account of the body, but on account of the understanding, which we have and they do not have—although our body too is fashioned in such a way as to indicate that we are better than the beasts and therefore like God. For the bodies of all animals, whether those that live in the waters, or on the earth, or those that fly in the air, are bent toward the earth and are not erect like the body of man. By this it is signified that our mind too ought to be erect toward the things above it, that is, toward the eternal spiritual things. So it is understood that it is chiefly through the mind—the erect form of the body bearing witness as well—that man was made to the image and likeness of God.

## Man's power over the beasts even after sin.

**18.** 29. Sometimes too they are accustomed to say: "How did man receive power over the fish of the sea and the flying things of heaven and all cattle and wild beasts, when we see men killed by many wild beasts, and harmed by many flying things, which we wish either to avoid or to capture and for the most part cannot? How, then, have we received power over these things?"

Here it must first be said to them that they greatly err who consider man after sin, when he has been condemned to the mortality of this life and has lost that perfection by which he was made to the image of God. But if his condemnation has so much force that he commands so many of the cattle—for although on account of the frailty of his body he can be killed by many wild beasts, yet by none can he be tamed, while he himself tames so many, indeed nearly all—if, then, this condemnation of man has so much force, what is to be thought of his kingdom, which is promised to him by the divine voice when he is renewed and set free?

## How the verse Genesis 1:28 is to be taken spiritually.

**19.** 30. As for what is written, *male and female he made them; and God blessed them, saying, 'increase and multiply, and beget, and fill the earth'*[^42], it is most rightly asked how the joining of male and female before sin is to be taken, and this blessing in which it was said, *increase and multiply, and beget, and fill the earth:* whether it is to be taken carnally or spiritually. For we are permitted to take it spiritually as well, so that it may be believed to have been changed into carnal fruitfulness after sin. For there was first a chaste joining of male and female, the one suited for ruling, the other for obeying; and a spiritual offspring of intelligible and immortal joys filling the earth, that is, giving life to the body and ruling it—that is, holding it so subject that it suffered from it no adversity, no trouble.

This is to be believed in this way because there were not yet sons of this world before they sinned. For *the sons of this world beget and are begotten,* as the Lord says, when, in comparison with the future life that is promised to us, he shows that this carnal generation is to be despised[^43].

## What it is, allegorically, to have dominion over the beasts (Genesis 1:28).

**20.** 31. And as for what was said to them, *have power over the fish of the sea, and the flying things of heaven, and all the crawling things that crawl upon the earth*[^44]—with the meaning preserved by which it is clear that man has dominion over all these animals by reason—it is yet rightly understood spiritually too, so that we should hold subject all the affections and movements of the mind that we have, similar to these animals, and have dominion over them through temperance and moderation. For when these movements are not ruled, they break out and go on into the foulest habits, and through diverse and ruinous pleasures they carry us off and make us like every kind of beast. But when they are ruled and subjected, they grow altogether tame and live in harmony with us.

For the movements of our mind are not foreign to us. They too are fed, along with us, by the knowledge of the best reasonings and ways of life, and of eternal life, as if by seed-bearing plants and fruit-bearing trees and green plants. And this is the happy and tranquil life of man, when all his movements consent to reason and truth; and they are called joys, and holy and chaste and good loves. But if they do not consent, then nonetheless, while they are managed negligently, they tear apart and scatter the mind and make life most wretched; and they are called disturbances, and lusts, and evil desires. Concerning these it is already commanded us that we crucify them in ourselves with as much effort as we can, until death is swallowed up in victory[^45]. For the Apostle says, *those who belong to Jesus Christ have crucified their flesh with its disturbances and desires*[^46].

For from this very thing anyone ought to be admonished that these things are not to be understood carnally, since the green plants and the fruit-bearing trees are given as food to every kind of beast, and to all the flying things and all the serpents, in Genesis, when we see that lions, and hawks, and kites, and eagles are not fed except on flesh and on the killing of other animals. This I believe also of some serpents, which are in sandy and desert places, where neither tree nor plant is born.

## Why in Genesis 1:31 it is said: very good.

**21.** 32. Certainly it is not to be passed over negligently that it was said, *and God saw all the things that he made, that they were very good*[^47]*.* For when he was dealing with the individual things, he said only, *God saw that it was good;* but when it was said of all things, it was too little to say *good,* unless *very* were also added. For if the individual works of God, when they are considered by the prudent, are found to have praiseworthy *measures and numbers and orders,* each established in its own kind, how much more *all things together,* that is, the universe itself, which is filled up by these individual things gathered into one?

For every beauty that consists of parts is much more praiseworthy in the whole than in a part: as in the human body, if we praise the eyes alone, or the nose alone, or the cheeks alone, or the head alone, or the hands alone, or the feet alone, and the rest, if we praise the beautiful parts singly and alone; how much more the whole body, to which all the members, which are beautiful singly, contribute their beauty—so that a beautiful hand, which even alone was praised in the body, if it be separated from the body, both itself loses its grace, and the rest without it are unseemly? So great is the force and power of wholeness and unity that even those things that are many goods please then, when in the whole they come together and meet in some unity. And "universe" took its name from unity.

And if the Manichees considered this, they would praise God, the author and founder of the universe; and what offends them in a part, on account of the condition of our mortality, they would refer back to the beauty of the whole, and would see how God made all things not only good but even very good. For in some adorned and well-composed discourse too, if we consider the individual syllables, or even the individual letters, which, as soon as they have sounded, pass away at once, we find in them nothing to delight us and to be praised. For that whole discourse is beautiful not from the individual syllables or letters, but from all of them.

## The rest of the seventh day (Genesis 2:2) by way of allegory.

**22.** 33. Now let us look also at this, which they are accustomed to deride with more shamelessness than ignorance: that it is written that God, having completed heaven and earth and all that he made, *rested on the seventh day from all his works*[^48], *and blessed the seventh day, and sanctified it, because he rested from his works.* For they say: "What need was there for God to rest? Was he perhaps wearied and tired by the works of the six days?" They add too the testimony of the Lord, where he says, *my Father works even until now*[^49]; and from this they deceive many unskilled people, whom they try to persuade that the New Testament is opposed to the Old Testament.

But just as those to whom the Lord says, *my Father works even until now,* supposed God's rest carnally, and, observing the sabbath carnally, did not see what the meaning of that day prefigured, so these men too, with a different intention indeed, yet alike do not understand the mystery of the sabbath.

For the one group, by observing it carnally, and the other, by execrating it carnally, have not known the sabbath.

Let each one, then, pass over to Christ, so that *the veil may be taken away,* as the Apostle says[^50]. For the veil is taken away when, the covering of likeness and allegory being removed, the truth is laid bare, so that it can be seen.

## What it signifies that God is said to have rested.

**22.** 34. First, then, the rule of this manner of speaking is to be observed and learned in many places of the divine Scriptures. For what else does it signify, that God is said to have rested from all his works which he made very good, except our rest, which he is going to give us from all our works, if we too have done good works? According to this same figure of speech the Apostle also says, *for we do not know what to pray for as we ought, but the Spirit himself intercedes for us with unutterable groanings*[^51]. For the Holy Spirit does not groan as though he were in need or suffered distress, who according to God intercedes for the saints; but because he himself moves us to pray when we groan, what we do at his moving he himself is said to do.

So too this is said: *the Lord your God tests you, that he may know whether you love him*[^52]. For it is not that he himself, from whom nothing is hidden, may know, but so that he may make us know how far we have advanced in his love, that he permits us to be tested. According to this same manner of speaking our Lord too says that he does not know the day and the hour of the end of the world[^53]. For what can there be that he does not know? But because he was usefully hiding this from the disciples, he said that he did not know it, because by hiding it he was making them not know it. According to this figure he also said that the Father alone knows that day, because he would make the same Son know it.

From this figure many questions in the divine Scriptures are solved without any difficulty for those who already know this kind of speech.

Our common usage too abounds in such expressions, as when we say a glad day, because it makes us glad; and a sluggish cold, because it makes us sluggish[^54]; and a blind ditch, because we do not see it; and a polished tongue, because it makes polished words; and finally we even call a time quiet from all troubles, in which we are quiet from all troubles.

And so God too was said to have rested from all his works which he made very good, because in him we shall rest from all our works, if we have done good works: because our good works themselves are to be attributed to him who calls, who commands, who shows the way of truth, who invites us even to will, and supplies the strength to fulfill what he commands.

## The six days of creation are like the six ages of the world, the first of which is infancy: from Adam to Noah.

**23.** 35. FIRST AGE. But why this rest is assigned to the seventh day, I think is to be considered more diligently. For I see throughout the whole text of the divine Scriptures certain six laborious ages, distinguished as if by their own fixed boundaries, so that in the seventh rest may be hoped for; and that these same six ages have a likeness to those six days in which were made the things that Scripture records God to have made.

For the first beginnings of the human race, in which it began to enjoy this light, are well compared to the first day on which God made light. This age is to be reckoned as the infancy, as it were, of the whole age of the world, which we ought to think of as one man in proportion to its greatness; because each man, when he is first born and comes forth to the light, passes the first age, infancy. This stretches from Adam to Noah, through ten generations. As the evening of this day comes the flood; because our infancy too is wiped out, as it were, by a flood of forgetfulness.

## The second age, like boyhood: from Noah to Abraham.

**23.** 36. SECOND AGE. And the morning begins from the times of Noah—the second age, like boyhood—and this age stretches to Abraham through another ten generations. And it is well compared to the second day on which the firmament was made between water and water; because the ark too, in which Noah was with his own, was a firmament between the lower waters in which it floated and the upper waters by which it was rained upon. This age is not wiped out by a flood, because our boyhood too is not wiped from memory by forgetfulness. For we remember that we were boys, but we do not remember being infants. The evening of this age is the confusion of tongues among those who were building the tower, and the morning comes from Abraham. But this second age too did not beget the people of God, because boyhood is not fit for begetting either.

**23.** 37. THIRD AGE. The morning, then, comes from Abraham, and the third age succeeds, like adolescence. And it is well compared to the third day, on which the earth was separated from the waters. For from all the nations—whose unsteady error, mobile like all winds by the empty teachings of idols, is well signified by the word sea—from this emptiness of the nations, then, and the waves of this world, the people of God was separated through Abraham, like the earth when it appeared dry, that is, thirsting for the heavenly rain of the divine commandments: which people, by worshiping one God, like a watered earth so that it could bear useful fruit, received the Holy Scriptures and Prophecies. For this age could already beget a people for God, because the third age too, that is, adolescence, can already have children.

And therefore it was said to Abraham: *I have set you as a father of many nations, and I will increase you very greatly, and I will set you among the nations, and kings will come forth from you. And I will set my covenant between me and you, and between your seed after you, throughout their generations, as an everlasting covenant; that I may be your God, and your seed's after you: and I will give to you and to your seed after you the land in which you dwell, all the land of Canaan, for an everlasting possession, and I will be their God*[^55]. This age extends from Abraham to David, through fourteen generations. Its evening is in the sins of the people, by which they transgressed the divine commandments, down to the wickedness of that worst king, Saul.

## The fourth age, like young manhood: from the reign of David to the migration to Babylon.

**23.** 38. FOURTH AGE. And from there the morning comes—the reign of David. This age is like young manhood. And indeed, among all the ages, young manhood reigns, and it is itself the firm adornment of all the ages; and therefore it is well compared to the fourth day, on which the lights were made in the firmament of heaven. For what more clearly signifies the splendor of the kingdom than the excellence of the sun? And the splendor of the moon shows the people obeying the kingdom, as if the synagogue itself, and the stars its princes, and all founded as if in a firmament, in the stability of the kingdom. Its evening, as it were, is in the sins of the kings, by which that nation deserved to be taken captive and to serve.

## The fifth age, like middle age: from the Babylonian captivity to Christ.

**23.** 39. FIFTH AGE. And the morning comes—the migration to Babylon, when in that captivity the people was gently settled in a foreign repose. And this age extends to the coming of our Lord Jesus Christ, that is, the fifth age—namely, the decline from young manhood to old age, not yet old age but no longer young manhood: which is the age of the older man, whom the Greeks call *πρεσβύτην*. For among them an old man is not called *πρεσβύτης* but *γέρων*. And indeed this age was so bent down and broken from the vigor of the kingdom among the people of the Jews, just as a man passes from young manhood to being older.

And it is well compared to that fifth day, on which the animals were made in the waters, and the flying things of heaven, after those men began to live among the nations, as if in the sea, and to have an uncertain and unstable seat, like flying birds. But there were plainly there also great sea creatures, that is, those great men who were able to master the waves of the world rather than to serve in that captivity. For they were not perverted by any terror to the worship of idols. Here indeed it is to be noted that God blessed those animals, saying, *increase and multiply, and fill the waters of the sea, and let the flying things multiply over the earth*[^56]: because indeed the nation of the Jews, from the time it was scattered among the nations, was greatly multiplied. The evening of this day, that is, of this age, as it were, is the multiplication of sins among the people of the Jews, because they were so blinded that they could not even recognize the Lord Jesus Christ.

## The sixth age, like old age: in it the new, spiritual man is born.

**23.** 40. SIXTH AGE. But the morning comes from the preaching of the Gospel through our Lord Jesus Christ, and the fifth day ends; the sixth begins, in which the old age of *the old man* appears. For in this age that carnal kingdom was vehemently worn down, when both the temple was cast down and the sacrifices themselves ceased; and now that nation, as far as the strength of its kingdom is concerned, drags out, as it were, its last span of life. Yet in this age, as in the old age of the old man, *the new man* is born, who now lives spiritually.

For on the sixth day it was said, *let the earth bring forth the living soul*[^57]*.* For on the fifth day it was said, *let the waters bring forth,* not the living soul, but *creeping things of living souls*[^58]; since creeping things are bodies, and that people still served the Law, as if in the sea of the nations, by bodily circumcision and sacrifices. But here he says the *living soul,* by which life eternal things now begin to be desired.

The serpents, then, and the cattle that the earth brings forth signify the nations that are now going to believe the Gospel firmly. Of these it is said, in that vessel which was shown to Peter in the *Acts of the Apostles: kill, and eat*[^59]*.* And when he said they were unclean, it was answered to him, *what God has cleansed, do not you call unclean*[^60].

Then man is made to the image and likeness of God, just as in this sixth age our Lord is born in the flesh, of whom it was said through the prophet, *he is also man, and who will know him*?[^61] And just as on that day there was *male and female,* so too in this age there is Christ and the Church. And on that day man is set over the cattle and the serpents and the flying things of heaven, just as in this age Christ rules the souls that obey him, which have come to his Church partly from the nations, partly from the people of the Jews; so that by him men might be tamed and made gentle—whether given over to carnal desire like cattle, or darkened by gloomy curiosity like serpents, or lifted up by pride like birds.

And just as on that day man and the animals that are with him are fed with seed-bearing plants and fruit-bearing trees and green plants, so in this age the spiritual man—whoever is a good minister of Christ, and imitates him well as far as he can—is fed spiritually, together with the people itself, on the nourishment of the Holy Scriptures and the divine Law: partly to conceive the fruitfulness of reasonings and discourses, as if by seed-bearing plants; partly for the benefit of the ways of human conduct, as if by fruit-bearing trees; partly for the vigor of faith, hope, and charity unto eternal life, as if by green plants, that is, flourishing ones, which can be parched by no heat of tribulations.

But the spiritual man is fed on these nourishments so as to understand many things; whereas *the carnal man, that is, the little one in Christ,* like a beast of God, so as to believe many things he cannot yet understand: yet they all have the same foods.

## The evening of the sixth age is the return of the Son of man over the earth.

**23.** 41. SEVENTH AGE. But the evening, as it were, of this age—which may it not find us, if it has not yet begun—is that of which the Lord says, *do you think that when the Son of man comes, he will find faith on the earth*?[^62] After this evening the morning will come, when the Lord himself is going to come in glory: then those will rest with Christ from all their works to whom it was said, *be perfect, as your Father who is in heaven is perfect*[^63]. For such men do works "very good." For after such works rest is to be hoped for in the seventh day, which has no evening.

In no way, then, can it be told in words how God made and founded heaven and earth and every creature that he founded; but this exposition through the order of the days indicates it as if it were a history of things done, in such a way as chiefly to observe the foretelling of things to come.

## Why the ages of the world are unequal.

**24.** 42. But if anyone is troubled that in these ages of the world we observe the first two ages unfolded in ten generations each, while the three following are each woven of fourteen generations, and this sixth is defined by no number of generations[^64], it is easy to see that in each individual man too the first two ages, infancy and boyhood, cling to the senses of the body. These bodily senses are five: sight, hearing, smell, taste, and touch; and the number five doubled—since the human sex, from which such generations arise, is twofold, male and female—the number five, then, as I said, doubled makes the number ten.

But from adolescence onward, where reason now begins to prevail in man, there are added to the five senses knowledge and action, by which life is ruled and administered, so that now the number seven begins to be: which, likewise doubled, on account of the twofold sex, stands out and appears in the fourteen generations that the three following ages have, namely those of the adolescent, the young man, and the older man. But the age of old age, just as in us it is defined by no fixed term of years, but, after those five ages, however long each one lives is reckoned to old age, so too in this age of the world the generations do not appear, so that the last day is also hidden, which the Lord usefully showed ought to lie concealed[^65].

## A deeper allegory of the seven days.

**25.** 43. Each one of us also has, in good works and a right life, these six days as if distinct, after which he ought to hope for rest.

On the first day, the light of faith, when he first believes in visible things, for the sake of which faith the Lord deigned to appear visibly.

On the second day, as it were the firmament of discipline, by which he discerns between carnal and spiritual things, as between the lower and the upper waters.

On the third day, by which he separates his mind, in order to bear the fruits of good works, from the corruption and waves of carnal temptations, as the dry land from the disturbances of the sea, so that he can now say, *with my mind I serve the Law of God, but with my flesh the law of sin*[^66].

On the fourth day, by which he now works and distinguishes spiritual understandings in that firmament of discipline, he sees what the unchangeable truth is, which shines like the sun in the soul; and how the soul becomes a partaker of that very truth, and furnishes order and beauty to the body, like the moon illuminating the night; and how all the stars—namely, the spiritual understandings—glitter and shine in the obscurity of this life, as in the night.

Made stronger by the knowledge of these things, let him begin on the fifth day to work in the actions of this most turbulent world, as if in the waters of the sea, for the benefit of the brotherly fellowship; and from bodily actions, which pertain to the sea itself, that is, to this life, to bring forth "creeping things of living souls," that is, works that profit living souls; and "great sea creatures," that is, the strongest actions, by which the waves of the world are broken and despised; and "the flying things of heaven," that is, voices preaching heavenly things.

But on the sixth day let him bring forth "the living soul from the earth," that is, from the very stability of his mind, where he has spiritual fruits, that is, good thoughts; let him rule all the movements of his soul, so that there may be in him "a living soul," that is, one serving reason and justice, not rashness and sin. So let man too be made to the image and likeness of God, male and female, that is, understanding and action, by whose union a spiritual offspring may fill the earth, that is, may subject the flesh, and the rest that was already said above concerning the perfection of man. But in these days, as it were, evening is in the very perfection of each work, and morning in the beginning of those that follow.

After the works very good of these, as it were, six days, let man hope for perpetual rest, and understand what this is: *God rested on the seventh day from all his works*[^67]: because he himself works these good things in us, who commands us to work; and he himself is rightly said to rest, because after all these works he himself will grant us rest. For just as a householder is rightly said to build a house, when he does this not by his own work but by the work of those whom he commands as they serve him, so too he is rightly said to rest from his works, when, after the completion of the building, he permits those whom he commanded to be at leisure and to enjoy a pleasant repose.
