# Preface

Who does not know that in the holy Scriptures—that is, the legal, the prophetic, the evangelical, and the apostolic, endowed with canonical authority—some things are so set down that they are only to be known and believed, as it is that *in the beginning* God *made heaven and earth*[^1], and that *in the beginning was the Word*[^2], and whatever divine or human deeds are narrated simply to be known; while other things are commanded in such a way that they should be observed and done, or forbidden, so that they should not be done, as it is: *Honor your father and mother*[^3]; and: *You shall not commit adultery*[^4]? Now of those things that are written by way of command and prohibition, some are veiled in the mysteries of the sacraments, things which were enjoined upon that people of the Old Testament to be done in great number, and which are not now done by the Christian people but are only required and treated to be understood—such as the Sabbath for a visible rest[^5], such as the unleavened bread without yeast, the Passover in the slaying of a lamb[^6], such as the many kinds of sacrifices and of foods to be avoided, and the new moons, and the yearly solemnities[^7], which the Jews observe even to this day; and those ordinances of justification which do not properly pertain to works of righteousness but are understood to signify something. For what Christian is compelled in the seventh year to restore his slave to freedom, and, if the slave is unwilling to depart, to bore through his ear with an awl against the doorpost[^8], and other things of this kind? But other things are to be done even now, if they have been commanded to be done; and not to be done, if they have been forbidden—such as those I have mentioned: *Honor your father and mother*; and: *You shall not commit adultery*. Concerning those things, then, that are so set down in the sacred writings, whether by commanding, forbidding, or permitting, as still to pertain even now—that is, in the time of the New Testament—to the practice of a devout life and to conduct, I have undertaken to compose this work which I have taken in hand; so that, as far as God helps me, I may gather all such things from the canonical books and, that they may easily be inspected, may collect them into one place as it were a *Mirror*. For it was fitting that they be set down by our authors in the way they are set down, so that precepts figured in narratives or proper to the discussions themselves, and proper things mingled with figured ones, while the order of events is preserved, or answer is made to adversaries, or those who are to be taught are instructed, or those who disdain what is ready and open are in a manner renewed by the discovery of what is hidden. We, however, in this work neither lead the unbeliever nor build him up to the faith; nor do we exercise the wit and attention of learners by certain wholesome difficulties; but we admonish the one who, already believing, has resolved to obey God, that he may inspect himself here and consider how far he has advanced in good conduct and works, and how much he still lacks. For thus he can both give thanks for the things he has, and, for the things he does not have so as to have them, take sufficient action; and on account of preserving the former and attaining the latter, apply the care and prayers of faithful devotion. But in all these things which I have determined to set forth for inspection, whatever shall seem to be contrary to one another must afterward be explained and resolved by questions proposed. Of course, the punishments of misdeeds and the rewards of right deeds, though I have thought some of them worth recalling, yet who does not know that in the New Testament they are unlike those of old? Let us begin, then, our starting point from the very Law that was given through Moses, of the divine precepts such as we have promised to recall.
