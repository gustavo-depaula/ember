# Book V: Expressions in Deuteronomy

**1.** (1, 7) *Usque ad flumen magnum, flumen Euphratem* (as far as the great river, the river Euphrates). It did not say "as far as the great river Euphrates."

**2.** (1, 17) *Et iudicium quod durum fuerit a vobis, afferetis illud ad me* (and the judgment that shall be hard from you, you shall bring it to me). It did not say "that shall be hard for you," but *a vobis* (from you), that is, so hard that it cannot be judged by you.

**3.** (1, 35) *Si videbit aliquis virorum istorum terram optimam hanc, quam iuravi patribus eorum praeter Chaleb filius Iephone; hic videbit eam* (if any of these men shall see this most excellent land which I swore to their fathers, except Caleb the son of Jephone; he shall see it). In the book of *Numbers* too we noted this expression[^1], that it did not say "except Caleb the son [accusative] of Jephone," but *filius* (the son [nominative]).

**4.** (2, 7) *Dominus enim Deus vester benedixit te in omni opere manuum tuarum* (for the Lord your [plural] God has blessed you [singular] in every work of your [singular] hands). It did not say "has blessed you [plural] in every work of your [plural] hands," although it had said beforehand *vester* (your [plural]), not "your [singular]."

**5.** (2, 24) *Nunc ergo surgite et promovete et pertransite vos vallem Arnon; ecce tradidi in manus tuas Seon regem Esebon* (now therefore rise and set out and pass over, you [plural], the valley of Arnon; behold, I have delivered into your [singular] hands Sihon king of Heshbon). It did not say "into your [plural] hands," but passed from the plural to the singular.

**6.** (4, 7) *Quoniam quae est gens magna cui est ei Deus appropians illis?* (for what is the great nation to which there is to it a God drawing near to them?) Two things are to be noted here: either that it says *cui est ei* (to which there is to it), or that it does not say "drawing near to it," but *appropians illis* (drawing near to them).

**7.** (4, 12) *Et similitudinem non vidistis sed vocem* (and a likeness you did not see, but a voice), although a voice cannot be seen; but it used this word in a general way, as though "to see" pertained to every sense of the body.

**8.** (4, 5. 14) *In quam vos ingredimini illo hereditare eam* (into which you are entering thither to inherit it); it would be complete even if *illo* (thither) were not added.

**9.** (4, 20) *Et eduxit vos de fornace ferrea ex Aegypto* (and he brought you out of the iron furnace, out of Egypt); by the iron furnace he wished a hard tribulation to be understood. According to this it is also said of Joseph in the *Psalms*: *ferrum pertransit animam eius*[^2] (the iron passed through his soul).

**10.** (4, 22) *Et non transeo Iordanem hunc* (and I do not cross this Jordan), as though there were also another Jordan. From this expression I think that "this world" too is often said, as though there were another.

**11.** (4, 25) *Si autem genueris filios et filios filiorum tuorum* (but if you shall beget sons and the sons of your sons); the expression is to be noted, where it says that grandfathers too beget grandsons.

**12.** (4, 29) *Et quaeretis ibi Dominum Deum vestrum et invenietis eum, quando exquiretis eum ex toto corde tuo et ex tota anima tua in tribulatione tua* (and you [plural] shall seek there the Lord your [plural] God and shall find him, when you [singular] shall seek him out with your [singular] whole heart and with your [singular] whole soul in your [singular] tribulation); it did not say "with your [plural] whole heart and with your [plural] whole soul in your [plural] tribulation."

**13.** (4, 32) *Interrogate dies priores, qui fuerunt priores te* (ask the former days, which were before you); not "men" but "days" is to be understood. Likewise this too is to be noted, that here as well, when it had said *interrogate* (ask) in the plural, it introduced *priores te* (before you) in the singular, not "before you [plural]."

**14.** (4, 34) *Si et tentavit Deus ingressus accipere sibi gentem de media gente* (whether God has even attempted, entering in, to take for himself a nation from the midst of a nation); *tentavit* (attempted) is set down for what is "willed." Or what else? But what it says, *de media gente* (from the midst of a nation), is to be understood as "from the midst of the nations," the singular being put for the plural; just as we read written *serpentem, ranam,* and *locustam* (the serpent, the frog, and the locust) for "serpents" and "frogs" and "locusts."

**15.** (4, 34) *Secundum omnia, quae fecit Dominus Deus vester in Aegypto coram te vidente* (according to all that the Lord your [plural] God did in Egypt before you [singular] looking on); nothing is lacking even if *vidente* (looking on) is not added.

**16.** (5, 5) *Et ego stabam inter Dominum et vos in tempore illo annuntiare vobis verba Domini, quoniam timuistis a facie ignis et non ascendistis in montem, dicens: 'ego sum Dominus Deus tuus*,' et cetera (and I was standing between the Lord and you at that time to announce to you the words of the Lord, because you feared before the face of the fire and did not go up the mountain, saying: "I am the Lord your God," and so on); it put *dicens* (saying) for "when he said."

**17.** (5, 14) *Et advena, qui incolit in te* (and the stranger who dwells in you); it is to be taken as said to the people, not as though to one man, because a stranger dwells within a people.

**18.** (5, 15) *Propter hoc constituit tibi Dominus Deus tuus ut observes diem Sabbati et sanctificare eum* (on this account the Lord your God appointed for you that you should keep the Sabbath day and to sanctify it). If it did not have *et* (and), it would not seem an unusual expression: "that you should keep the Sabbath day, to sanctify it"; or at least if it were thus: "that you should keep the Sabbath day and may sanctify it";

or thus: "on this account the Lord your God appointed for you to keep the Sabbath day and to sanctify it." But as it stands, *ut observes diem Sabbati et sanctificare eum* (that you should keep the Sabbath day and to sanctify it) is an unusual expression and to be noted.

**19.** (6, 13) *Attende tibi, ne dilatetur cor tuum et obliviscaris Domini Dei tui* (take heed to yourself, lest your heart be enlarged and you forget the Lord your God); it is to be noted that an enlarging of the heart can be spoken of in a bad sense as well; for exultation is understood in an enlarging, to which narrowness, that is, sadness, is contrary. But each can be taken both in a good and in a bad sense.

**20.** (6, 20) *Et erit cum interrogaverit te filius tuus cras dicens* (and it shall be, when your son shall ask you tomorrow, saying); it put *cras* (tomorrow) for any future time whatever.

**21.** (7, 1) *Septem gentes magnas et multas* (seven nations great and many); how, then, "many," if seven? But it said *multas* (many) for "having a multitude."

**22.** (7, 2) *Non dispones ad eos testamentum* (you shall not arrange a covenant with them), although it was speaking of nations; therefore it put *testamentum* (covenant) for "pact."

**23.** (7, 3-4) *Et filiam eius non sumes filio tuo; discedere enim faciet filium tuum a me et serviet diis aliis et irascetur indignatione Dominus in vos* (and you shall not take his daughter for your son; for he will make your son depart from me and he will serve other gods and the Lord will be angry with indignation against you); it did not say "I will be angry," but as though it spoke of another.

**24.** (9, 1) *Civitates magnas et muratas usque in caelum* (cities great and walled up to heaven); said as a hyperbole.

**25.** (9, 4) *Ne dicas in corde tuo: 'cum consumpserit Dominus Deus tuus gentes istas ante faciem tuam, dicens: 'propter iustitias meas induxit me Dominus hereditare terram bonam istam*.' (do not say in your heart, "when the Lord your God shall have consumed these nations before your face," saying, "for my righteous deeds the Lord brought me in to inherit this good land.") The order is "*do not say in your heart* saying."

**26.** (9, 28) *Ne quando dicant inhabitantes terram 'unde eduxisti nos inde'* (lest at any time the inhabitants of the land say, "from where you brought us out, from there"); after the manner of Scripture *inde* (from there) is added.

**27.** (11, 3) *Quae fecit virtutem Aegyptiorum* (which he did to the power of the Egyptians). Some Latin translators, understanding this too little, were unwilling to say *virtutem* (the power [accusative]), but *virtuti* (to the power [dative]) or *exercitui* (to the army), since what the Greek says, δύναμιν, some understood as "army"; but the expression is elegant: *quae fecit virtutem eorum* (which he did to their power) is to be understood as "what he did to it," since he reduced it to nothing. But because it was said in the plural, it is therefore somewhat obscure.

**28.** (11, 6) *Quos aperiens terra os suum deglutivit eos et domos eorum et tabernacula eorum* (whom the earth, opening its mouth, swallowed them up and their houses and their tents). Two expressions are to be noted here: that *eos* (them) was added, when what it also says above, *quos* (whom), could have sufficed; and that, when it had said *domos eorum* (their houses), it added *et tabernacula eorum* (and their tents), as though they had other houses in the desert besides tents. But doubtless it wished "houses" to be understood as the people belonging to them, just as it is said to the people itself: *et nunc tu, domus Iacob*[^3] (and now you, house of Jacob). Nor is this expression foreign to the Latin language; for the Romans too were called the house of Assaracus[^4], because they traced their origin from Assaracus the Trojan.

**29.** (11, 7) *Quoniam oculi vestri videbant omnia opera Domini magna, quae fecit in vobis hodie* (for your eyes were seeing all the great works of the Lord which he did among you today). Although it was speaking of those works that were done in the desert at the time when they were being led about there for forty years, yet it said *hodie* (today), by which it wished "this time" to be understood, however many years that same period could have had.

**30.** (11, 9) *Terram, quam iuravit Dominus patribus vestris dare eis et semini eorum post eos* (the land which the Lord swore to your fathers to give to them and to their seed after them). As though it said "that is, to their seed after them." For he did not give it to them as well, but he so gave it to them when he gave it to their seed.

**31.** (11, 13) *Si autem auditu audieritis omnia mandata eius, quae ego mando tibi hodie* (but if by hearing you shall hear all his commandments which I command you today). *Auditu* (by hearing) seems superfluous, but it is an expression most familiar to Holy Scripture.

**32.** (11, 14) *Et dabit pluviam terrae tuae in tempore suo matutinam et serotinam* (and he will give rain to your land in its season, the early and the late). Since the "morning time" is called day, did he perhaps wish the first season of the year to be understood from this? But *serotinum* (late) is less Latin, though it could not be more properly expressed from the Greek, which they call ὄψιμον; yet it is in common use in the Latin language too that something be called "late," but as what was done later than it ought. Here, however, he rather wished a season of the year to be understood.

**33.** (11, 15-16) *Et cum ederis et satiatus fueris, attende tibi ipsi, ne dilatetur cor tuum, et praevaricemini et serviatis diis aliis* (and when you shall have eaten and been filled, take heed to yourself, lest your heart be enlarged, and you transgress and serve other gods). We have already noted such expressions above, both that it passes from the singular to the plural, and that it wished *dilatetur* (be enlarged) to be understood in a bad sense, where it signified harmful prosperity.

**34.** (11, 24) *Et flumen magnum, flumen Euphratem* (and the great river, the river Euphrates); these repetitions are customary in the Scriptures and commend the matter fittingly.

**35.** (11, 25) *Timorem vestrum et tremorem vestrum imponet Dominus Deus vester super faciem universae terrae* (your fear and your trembling the Lord your God will lay upon the face of the whole earth); not the fear and trembling by which they themselves fear and tremble, but by which they are feared and make others tremble.

**36.** (12, 17) *Non poteris manducare in civitatibus tuis decimationem frumenti tui* (you shall not be able to eat in your cities the tithe of your grain). Instead of what is "you shall not be permitted."

**37.** (13, 16) *Et incendes civitatem in igni* (and you shall burn the city in fire); we would say more customarily "with fire."

**38.** (14, 24) *Si autem longe fuerit via a te* (but if the way shall be far from you), as if it said "the way will be long"; it put an adverb for a noun.

**39.** (15, 6) *Et fenerabis gentes multas* (and you shall lend to many nations). Scripture calls "lending" money given on loan, even if interest is not received; whence there is also that saying in the *Psalm*: *beatus vir, qui miseretur et commodat*[^5] (blessed is the man who has mercy and lends). For our translators preferred to render it thus, who thought the sense rather than the words should be followed; for in the Greek it has δανείζει, which is "he lends."

**40.** (15, 6) *Et principaberis gentium multarum, tui autem non principabuntur* (and you shall rule over many nations, but of you they shall not rule). As though it said "they shall not rule over you, that is, the nations"; for it is the genitive singular case of this pronoun that it says, *tui* (of you)—whose dative is "to you"—not the nominative plural, whose genitive is "of yours."

**41.** (15, 7) *Si autem fuerit in te egenus in fratribus tuis* (but if there shall be in you a needy man among your brothers). It is said not to one man but to the people: therefore *in te* (in you).

**42.** (15, 7-8) *Si autem fuerit in te egenus in fratribus tuis in una civitatum tuarum in terra, quam Dominus Deus tuus dat tibi, non avertes cor tuum neque constringes manum tuam a fratre tuo egente; aperiens aperies manus tuas ei, fenus fenerabis ei, quantumcumque postulat et quantum eget* (but if there shall be in you a needy man among your brothers in one of your cities in the land which the Lord your God gives you, you shall not turn away your heart nor close your hand from your needy brother; opening you shall open your hands to him, with a loan you shall lend to him, however much he asks and however much he needs).

Here, certainly, since it enjoins works of mercy, the cruelty of usury is by no means to be undertaken; whence it appears that what it says, *fenus fenerabis ei* (with a loan you shall lend to him), it wished to be understood as giving on loan what he might ask. But what it says, *aperiens aperies manus tuas* (opening you shall open your hands), is of the same kind as what follows, *fenus fenerabis* (with a loan you shall lend): it is a customary expression in Holy Scripture.

**43.** (15, 17) When it had given the command about piercing the ear of a servant, *et ancillam*, it says, *tuam facies similiter* (and your maidservant you shall do likewise), putting the accusative for the dative. It did not say "to your maidservant you shall do," which the usage of our expression demanded.

**44.** (15, 21) *Si autem fuerit in eo vitium, claudum aut caecum, vel omne vitium malum* (but if there shall be in it a blemish, lame or blind, or any evil blemish). It did not say "lameness" or "blindness" (for these are blemishes), but *claudum et caecum* (lame and blind), which are not blemishes but animals that have blemishes. This too is to be noted, that it says *vitium malum* (an evil blemish), as though there could be a good blemish.

**45.** (16, 4) *Et non dormiet de carnibus, de quibus immolaveritis vespere die primo usque in mane* (and there shall not sleep of the flesh which you shall have sacrificed in the evening on the first day until morning). Instead of what is "it shall not remain that night," it said *non dormiet* (shall not sleep).

**46.** (17, 1) *Non offeres Domino Deo tuo vitulum vel ovem in quo est in ipso vitium* (you shall not offer to the Lord your God a calf or a sheep in which there is in it a blemish). The customary expression would be "in which there is a blemish"; but this one is more customary in the Scriptures.

**47.** (17, 5) *Et lapidabis eos in lapidibus, et morientur* (and you shall stone them with stones, and they shall die); which by our usage would be said "with stones," not *in lapidibus* (with stones [in the stones]).

**48.** (17, 16) *Non multiplicabit sibi equum* (he shall not multiply for himself a horse). It put *equum* (a horse) for "horses" or for "cavalry"; whence some translated *equitatum* (cavalry).

**49.** (17, 15) *Non poteris constituere super te hominem alienum, quia non est frater tuus* (you shall not be able to set over you a foreign man, because he is not your brother). *Non poteris* (you shall not be able) is said for "you shall not be permitted."

**50.** (18, 16) *Secundum omnia quae petisti a Domino Deo tuo in Coreb in die convocationis dicentes* (according to all that you asked of the Lord your God at Horeb on the day of the assembly, saying). When it had said above *petisti* (you asked [singular]), it added *dicentes* (saying [plural]), not "saying [singular]."

**51.** (20, 4) *Quoniam Dominus Deus vester, qui praecedit vobiscum* (for the Lord your God, who goes before with you); it did not say "before you."

**52.** (22, 6) *Si occurreris nido avis ante faciem tuam in via* (if you shall meet a bird's nest before your face on the way); it is to be noted that it said *occurreris* (you shall meet) even of one not walking.

**53.** (22, 8) *Si autem aedificaveris domum novam, facies coronam solario tuo et non facies homicidium in domo tua, si cadat qui cecidit ab eo* (but if you shall build a new house, you shall make a parapet for your roof and you shall not commit homicide in your house, if there should fall the one who fell from it), that is, "the one who fell should fall from the roof." It is an exceedingly unusual expression.

**54.** (24, 2-3) *Et abiens fuerit viro alteri, et oderit eam vir novissimus* (and going away she shall be to another man, and the last man shall hate her). It is to be noted that, of two, the later one is called *novissimum* (the last). Such an expression is also in the Gospel, when it is asked which of two brothers did the will of the father, and the answer is *novissimus* (the last), although there were two[^6].

**55.** (24, 6) *Non pignerabis molam neque superiorem lapidem molae, quia animam iste pignerat* (you shall not take in pledge a millstone nor the upper stone of a mill, because this one takes a soul in pledge), instead of what is "because you take a soul in pledge, if you should do it." Then it is to be noted that it said *animam* (a soul) for that life which the soul has in the body; from which expression there is also that saying in the Gospel: *nonne anima plus est quam esca*[^7] (is not the soul more than food).

**56.** (24, 7) *Si autem deprehendatur homo furans animam ex fratribus suis filiis Israel* (but if a man shall be caught stealing a soul from among his brothers, the sons of Israel); it put *animam* (a soul) for "a man." Then it follows: *et opprimens eum vendiderit* (and oppressing him he shall sell him); which is an expression no less to be noted, for it was unwilling to say "her," which would be more consistent, since it had said *animam* (a soul), but *eum* (him), that is, the man, in whose signification it had said *animam* (a soul).

**57.** (24, 10) *Si debitum fuerit in proximo tuo debitum quodcumque* (if there shall be a debt owed by your neighbor, any debt whatever); the repetition of the word makes the expression worth noting.

**58.** (25, 7) *Si autem noluerit homo accipere uxorem fratris sui, et ascendet mulier in porta ad senatum et dicet: 'non vult frater viri mei suscitare nomen fratris sui in Israel, noluit frater viri mei*.' (but if the man shall not wish to take his brother's wife, then the woman shall go up at the gate to the council and shall say, "my husband's brother does not wish to raise up his brother's name in Israel, my husband's brother has refused.") Although Scripture loves repetitions of words, this repetition is yet more unusual; but it more elegantly displays the feeling of a complaint.

**59.** (27, 21) *Maledictus omnis, qui dormierit cum omni pecore* (cursed is everyone who shall sleep with any beast); instead of what is "shall lie." Then *cum omni pecore* (with any beast) for what is "with whatever beast."

**60.** (28, 48) *Et servies inimicis tuis, quos immittet Dominus Deus tuus super te* (and you shall serve your enemies, whom the Lord your God will send upon you). The Greek has λατρεύσεις, where the Latin rendered *servies* (you shall serve). Now Scripture is wont to put this word for that servitude which is owed to God; whence those are called idolaters who serve "idols" with that servitude;

therefore this word is here used unusually. Although it could also have this sense, that it threatened things so grievous that they would be compelled to serve their enemies, exceedingly proud, as though they were gods.

**61.** (28, 49) *Gentem cuius non audies vocem eius* (a nation whose you shall not hear its voice). This too is to be noted, that it said both *cuius* (whose) and *eius* (its), as the Scriptures are wont to speak, and that what it says, *cuius non audies vocem* (whose voice you shall not hear), is for what is "whose tongue you shall not understand."

**62.** (28, 54-55) *Mollis in te et tenera valde fascinabit oculo suo fratrem suum et uxorem quae est in sinu eius et qui sunt reliqui filii quicumque relicti fuerint illi, ita ut det uni ex eis a carnibus filiorum suorum de quibuscumque edet eo, quod non derelictum sit ei quidquam in angustia et tribulatione, qua tribulabunt te inimici tui in omnibus civitatibus tuis* (the man soft among you and very delicate will look with grudging eye upon his brother and the wife who is in his bosom and the remaining children, whoever shall be left to him, so as to give to none of them of the flesh of his children, of which he will eat, because nothing has been left to him in the straits and tribulation with which your enemies will afflict you in all your cities).

It is to be noted how it is here said *fascinabit eum* (he will bewitch him) for "he will grudge him," as though one were left behind for this purpose, that it be necessary to give him of the flesh of the children, which necessity compels to be taken as food by the parents. For in *Proverbs* too, where the Latin manuscripts have *non cenabis cum viro invido*[^8] (you shall not dine with an envious man), the Greek has ἀνδρὶ βασκάνῳ; βάσκανος means "bewitching".

**63.** (28, 63) *Et erit sicut laetatus est Dominus in vobis bene facere vobis* (and it shall be, just as the Lord rejoiced over you to do good to you). The Greek has *bene facere vos* (to do good you [accusative]), and it does not even seem to be a Greek expression. Yet the accusative is understood as put for the dative; whence the Latin translators preferred to say *vobis* (to you) rather than *vos* (you).

**64.** (29, 2) *Vos vidistis omnia, quae fecit Dominus Deus vester in terra Aegypti coram vobis Pharaoni et omnibus servis eius et omni terrae illius, tentationes magnas, quas viderunt oculi tui* (you saw all that the Lord your God did in the land of Egypt before you, to Pharaoh and to all his servants and to all that land, the great trials which your eyes saw); it is to be noted that those plagues too are called *tentationes* (trials).

**65.** (30, 4) *Si fuerit dispersio tua a summo caeli usque ad summum caeli, inde congregabit te Dominus Deus tuus* (if your dispersion shall be from the top of heaven to the top of heaven, from there the Lord your God will gather you). It is remarkable if it does not say *a summo caeli usque ad summum caeli* (from the top of heaven to the top of heaven) in the way it is wont also to say "from the end of the earth to the end of the earth"; perhaps for this reason, because, according to the fact that this air too is called "heaven," it is joined to the earth.

**66.** (30, 12-13) *Non in caelo est, dicens: 'quis ascendet in caelum et accipiet nobis illud, et audientes illud faciemus?' Neque trans mare est, dicens: 'quis transfretabit nobis trans mare et accipiet nobis illud, et audientes illud faciemus?'* (it is not in heaven, saying, "who will go up into heaven and take it for us, and hearing it we will do it?" Nor is it across the sea, saying, "who will cross the sea for us and take it for us, and hearing it we will do it?") It put *dicens* (saying) for what is "so that you may say," a new expression.

**67.** (31, 8) *Et Dominus qui comitatur tibi tecum* (and the Lord who accompanies to you with you).

**68.** (31, 16) *Et dixit Dominus ad Moysen: 'ecce tu dormies cum patribus tuis'* (and the Lord said to Moses, "behold, you shall sleep with your fathers"); by this word it signified his death.

**69.** (31, 27) *Amaricantes eratis quae ad Deum* (you were embittering the things that pertain to God); that is, "you were embittering the things that are God's."

**70.** (31, 29) *Scio enim, quia post obitum meum iniquitate iniquitatem facietis* (for I know that after my death you will do iniquity an iniquity); but it is said in Greek by one word, *iniquitatem facietis* (you will do iniquity), which is ἀνομήσετε.

**71.** (31, 29) *Et occurrent vobis mala novissimum dierum* (and evils will meet you, the last of days); for thus the Greek has it, instead of "in the last days" or "in the last of days."

**72.** (32, 6) *Nonne hic ipse pater tuus possedit te et fecit te et creavit te?* (is not this same one your father, who possessed you and made you and created you?) The order is to be noted; for it seems it ought first to have said *creavit te et fecit te* (created you and made you), and then *possedit te* (possessed you); for how is one who does not exist understood to be able to be possessed?

**73.** (32, 14) *Cum adipe renum tritici* (with the fat of the kidneys of wheat). This metaphor is exceedingly unusual, that *renes tritici* (the kidneys of wheat) be understood as, so to speak, the inner parts of the wheat from which the flour is cast out; for it also said *adipem* (fat), for by this word the Greeks are wont to call flour as well; for the Greek has this in *Exodus*, where it is written *elevantes farinam super humeros suos*[^9] (carrying the flour upon their shoulders), since the Greek translator put στέατα, which is "fat."

**74.** (32, 20) *Filii in quibus non est fides in eis* (children in whom there is no faith in them); an expression customary to the Scriptures.

**75.** (32, 36) *Quoniam iudicabit Dominus populum suum et in servis suis consolabitur* (for the Lord will judge his people and will be comforted in his servants); for "he will comfort his servants," unless perhaps he says that he himself is comforted by a transferred word, as it were from the indignation and offense by which the wicked offend him. But his comfort is to be understood thus, not after the manner of men; just as neither is his anger nor zeal nor other such things.

**76.** (32, 37) *Ubi sunt dii eorum in quibus fidebant in ipsis* (where are their gods in whom they trusted in them); it would be complete even without *in ipsis* (in them).

**77.** (32, 40) *Et iurabo dexteram meam* (and I will swear my right hand); that is, "by my right hand."

**78.** (32, 42) *Laetamini caeli simul cum eo, et adorent eum omnes angeli Dei* (rejoice, O heavens, together with him, and let all the angels of God worship him). Other manuscripts have *et adorent eum omnes filii Dei* (and let all the sons of God worship him). But that those who are angels in the heavens are called *filios Dei* (sons of God) is not easily found in Holy Scripture.
