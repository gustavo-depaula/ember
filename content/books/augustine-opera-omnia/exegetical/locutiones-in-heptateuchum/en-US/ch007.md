# Book VI: Expressions in Joshua

**1.** (1, 14) *Vos autem transibitis expediti priores fratribus vestris, omnis fortis* (but you shall pass over unencumbered, ahead of your brothers, every strong man); that is, everyone who is strong among you.

**2.** (3, 4) *Ut sciatis viam quam ibitis eam* (that you may know the way which you shall go it); it would be complete even if it did not have *eam* (it).

**3.** (5, 13) *Et factum est cum esset Iesus in Iericho* (and it came to pass when Joshua was in Jericho), an expression to be noted; for they were not yet in that city, whose gates had been shut against them; whence it came about that they could not enter it unless its walls fell when the ark of the Lord was carried around. Therefore *in Iericho* (in Jericho) is said for "in the land that belonged to Jericho."

**4.** (6, 1) Of Jericho it is said, when it was shut up, *nec quisquam ex illa prodibat neque introibat* (and no one went forth out of it nor came in); certainly "into it" is understood, not "out of it"; this is called in Greek ζεῦγμα κατ’ ἔλλειψιν.

**5.** (6, 2) The Lord says to Joshua: *ecce ego trado tibi subiugatam Iericho et regem eius qui est in ea, potentes fortitudine* (behold, I deliver to you Jericho subdued and its king who is in it, mighty in strength). It is to be wondered at that here it did not put the connective conjunction, so as to say "and mighty in strength," which Scripture is wont to put so constantly that it is found even where it impedes the sense of the customary expression. Or are both Jericho and its king called *potentes fortitudine* (mighty in strength)?

**6.** (6, 25; 4, 9) *Et Raab meretricem et domum paternam eius vivificavit Iesus, et commorata est in Israel usque in hodiernum diem* (and Joshua kept alive Rahab the harlot and her father's house, and she has dwelt in Israel until the present day). It is to be noted how Scripture says *usque in hodiernum diem* (until the present day), which it constantly says. For of those twelve stones too, which were set up where the Jordan had flowed down on the lower side and on the upper side had stood still while the ark or the people crossed, it is said that they were there *usque in hodiernum diem* (until the present day); which seems to sound as though these things were narrated through this Scripture after a very long time, and these books were not written down at the time when these events were most recent. But whoever thinks this, what will he say of this harlot, who certainly lived as long as the lifespan of one human being, and yet it is said *usque in hodiernum diem* (until the present day)? Therefore this is said of those things which are so established that they were not afterward changed by those who established them; just as someone is said to be sent into perpetual exile because he is not punished with this penalty for a predefined time, not because anyone can be perpetual in exile, since the man himself cannot be perpetual. So therefore this harlot too was not commanded to dwell in Israel for a time; therefore it is said *usque in hodiernum diem* (until the present day).

**7.** (7, 11) The Lord says to Joshua: *Peccavit populus et transgressus est Testamentum meum, quod disposui ad eos* (the people has sinned and has transgressed my covenant which I appointed to them). It is to be noted how it calls *testamentum* (covenant) this which it had commanded, that Jericho should be under the ban and that no one should appropriate anything from it for himself.

**8.** (8, 1-2) The Lord, speaking to Joshua, says among other things: *ecce dedi in manus tuas regem Gai et terram ipsius, et facies Gai sicut fecisti Iericho et regi eius et praedam pecorum praedaberis tibi* (behold, I have given into your hands the king of Ai and his land, and you shall do to Ai as you did to Jericho and its king, and the spoil of the cattle you shall plunder for yourself).

The expression is especially to be noted, not only because it said *praedaberis tibi* (you shall plunder for yourself), as though that spoil were to belong to one, when it was to belong to all—for such expressions are most customary when God speaks as though to one what he speaks to a people—but this is new here, because Scripture had said beforehand *et dixit Dominus ad Iesum* (and the Lord said to Joshua), who is established to have been one man. Yet what it said, *praedaberis tibi* (you shall plunder for yourself), was certainly not said in such a way that the spoil was commanded to belong to this one man, but to the whole people.

**9.** (8, 12) *Et insidiae erant civitati a mari* (and the ambush was for the city from the sea). One who does not know thinks this city was a seaside one. But it is the custom of the Scriptures to call the western side "from the sea" or "toward the sea," because, from that side, the land where these things were taking place has the sea nearer than from the other sides.

**10.** (8, 18) *Extende manum tuam in gaeso quod est in manu tua contra civitatem* (stretch out your hand with the javelin that is in your hand toward the city). This expression would not be worth noting except on account of the noun, which is obscure to those in whose usage it is not. For what *gaeson* may mean is not easily understood. The translator Symmachus is reported to have called this a shield; but the *Septuagint* translators, according to whom we are treating these matters, who put *gaeson*, I wonder whether in the Greek language too they wished a "spear" or a "Gallic lance" to be understood; for those things are called *gaesa* which Virgil too mentions, where he says of the Gauls depicted on the shield of Aeneas: *duo quisque Alpina coruscant gaesa manu*[^1] (each brandishes two Alpine javelins in his hand).

**11.** (8, 22) *Et facti sunt inter medium castrorum, hi hinc et hi hinc* (and they came to be in the midst of the camps, these on this side and these on that side).

Two things are to be noted in this expression: one, that, although the enemies who were being conquered were in the midst, it is said as though of these that these were on this side and these on that side, whereas it was rather the Israelites who had placed them in the midst to be struck down;

the other, that Scripture says they came to be in the midst of the camps, calling *castra* (camps) even the drawn-out battle lines set up in combat, although they are not usually called camps except where an army halts to remain; unless perhaps they were called camps for this reason, that they went with all their belongings.

**12.** (8, 27) *Exceptis pecoribus et spoliis quae erant in civitate praedati sunt filii Israel secundum praeceptum Domini, quemadmodum constituit Dominus Iesu* (except the cattle and spoils which were in the city, the sons of Israel took as plunder according to the command of the Lord, as the Lord appointed for Joshua). It is said thus, *exceptis pecoribus et spoliis praedati sunt* (except the cattle and spoils they took as plunder), as though they did not plunder these, whereas they rather plundered these; and they were therefore said to be excepted because these alone were not destroyed in that battle.

**13.** (9, 7) The Israelites, answering the Gibeonites, said: *Vide, ne in me habites, et quomodo disponam tibi Testamentum?* (see, lest you dwell in me, and how shall I arrange a covenant for you?) We have already noted such expressions above. For this, *vide ne in me habites* (see, lest you dwell in me), is what is "with me," that is, in the land which God had promised them. And here too they seem to have spoken as though to one man, although more than one envoy came to them; but in their manner, rendering answers to a nation and a people, they speak in the singular number, just as for the most part God or their own leader does to them as well. It is also to be noted that Scripture calls a pact of peace *Testamentum* (a covenant), in its own manner indeed; for it often speaks thus.

**14.** (10, 17) *Et nuntiatum est Iesu dicentes: 'inventi sunt quinque reges absconditi in spelunca'* (and it was announced to Joshua, saying, "the five kings have been found hidden in a cave"); instead of what is "they announced, saying."

**15.** (10, 25) *Ita faciet Dominus omnibus inimicis vestris, quos vos debellabitis eos* (so the Lord will do to all your enemies, whom you will war down them); nothing would be lacking to this sentence even if it did not have *eos* (them).

**16.** (11, 19-20) *Et omnes cepit in bello, quia per Dominum factum est confortari cor eorum, ut obviam irent ad bellum ad Israel, ut exterminarentur* (and he took them all in war, because it came about through the Lord that their heart was strengthened, so that they went out to battle against Israel, that they might be destroyed); it is to be noted that *confortari cor* (that the heart be strengthened) is not always to be taken in a good sense.

**17.** (14, 6) *Tu scis verbum, quod locutus est Dominus ad Moysen hominem Dei de me et te* (you know the word which the Lord spoke to Moses the man of God concerning me and you); it did not say "his man."

**18.** (17, 16) *Et dixerunt filii Ioseph: 'non sufficit nobis mons et equus electus et ferrum Chananaeo qui habitat in Baethsan.'* (and the sons of Joseph said, "the mountain is not enough for us, and there is choice horse and iron to the Canaanite who dwells in Beth-shan.") It said *equum electum* (choice horse) for "choice horses," which is not customary in our speech; whence some Latin translators were unwilling to render *equum* (horse), but "cavalry." But *equus electus* (choice horse) was said for "horses" just as in our usage "the soldier" is said for "soldiers."

**19.** (19, 33-34) *Et facti sunt exitus illorum Iordanes. Et revertentur fines ad mare* (and their goings out came to be the Jordan. And the borders will return toward the sea). It did not say "returned" or at least "return," as it is wont to say of borders, but *revertentur* (will return), as though it were still future, though this is a narration of past things. And in almost all the divisions of the lands themselves, tribe by tribe, Scripture speaks in such a way as to use verbs of the future tense.

**20.** (20, 9) *Refugere illuc qui percusserit animam nolens* (that he may flee thither who shall have struck a soul unwillingly). Here we ought to understand *animam* (a soul) either as "a man" or as "the life of the flesh," which comes about through the soul; by which expression that too was said by the brothers of Joseph: *non percutiamus eius animam*[^2] (let us not strike his soul), that is, "let us not kill him"; for if the soul could be struck by a slayer, the Lord would not say *nolite timere eos, qui corpus occidunt, animam autem non possunt occidere*[^3] (do not fear those who kill the body but cannot kill the soul).

**21.** (21, 2) *Mandavit Dominus in manu Moysi* (the Lord commanded in the hand of Moses). An expression most familiar to the Scriptures; for thus it is said "the word that came to be in the hand of this or that prophet," that is, given into his power of speaking.

**22.** (21, 40) *Et accepit Iesus cultros petrinos, in quibus circumciderat filios Israel* (and Joshua took stone knives, in which he had circumcised the sons of Israel). It did not say "with which he had circumcised," but *in quibus* (in which).

**23.** (22, 7) *Et ubi dimisit eos Iesus in domos suas, et benedixit eos dicens* (and when Joshua sent them away to their homes, and he blessed them, saying). The connective conjunction, that is, *et* (and), seems superfluous here; for it could be said thus: "and when Joshua sent them away to their homes, he blessed them, saying."

**24.** (22, 8) Likewise another expression is to be noted: that, when Scripture had said *et benedixit eos Iesus dicens* (and Joshua blessed them, saying) and by these words had made the hearers attentive as to what Joshua said in blessing, it added only this: *in divitiis multis abierunt in domos suas; et pecora multa valde et argentum et aurum et vestem multam valde diviserunt praedam inimicorum suorum cum fratribus suis* (in great riches they went away to their homes; and very many cattle and silver and gold and very much clothing—they divided the spoil of their enemies with their brothers); which appears to belong rather to one narrating than to one blessing.

**25.** (22, 10) *Aedificaverunt ibi aram super Iordanem, aram magnam videre* (they built there an altar by the Jordan, an altar great to look upon); this expression is known in Latin speech too.

**26.** (22, 11) *Et audierunt filii Israel dicentium* (and the sons of Israel heard of those saying). It did not say "saying [dative]" or "saying [accusative/nominative]."

**27.** (22, 27) *Servire servitutem Domini* (to serve the service of the Lord). In this expression two things are to be noted: *servire servitutem* (to serve a service), which is also found as a Latin expression, and *servitutem Domini* (the service of the Lord), that is, which is owed or rendered to the Lord.

**28.** (22, 30) *Et audientes Phinees sacerdos et omnes principes synagogae, qui erant cum illo, verba, quae locuti sunt filii Ruben et filii Gad et dimidium tribus Manasses, et placuerunt illis* (and Phinehas the priest and all the leaders of the assembly who were with him, hearing the words which the sons of Reuben and the sons of Gad and the half-tribe of Manasseh spoke, and they pleased them). *Audientes* (hearing) is put for "they heard," or else there is an extra *et* (and) here. And yet not without inconsistency, because "when they had heard" should have been said.

**29.** (23, 1) *Et Iesus senior provectus diebus* (and Joshua, an elder, advanced in days). We noted this said of Abraham, that in the Scriptures not only one who is below the age of *senis* (an old man) is called *seniorem* (an elder), but also one who is *valde senem* (very old)[^4]; whence not every elder can be called an old man, but every old man can be called an elder.

**30.** (24, 7) *Et induxit super illos mare et operuit super illos* (and he brought over them the sea and covered over them); it did not say "covered them," although some translators preferred to say this.

**31.** (24, 17) *In omnibus gentibus quas transivimus per ipsas* (among all the nations which we passed through them); these expressions are customary in the Scriptures and not to be noted as often as they occur.

[^1]: Virgil, Aeneid 8.661–62.
[^2]: Gen 37:21.
[^3]: Matt 10:28.
[^4]: See Questions 1.35; 1.70.
