# Book VII: Expressions in Judges

**1.** (1, 1) *Et factum est postquam defunctus est Iesus, interrogabant filii Israel in Domino* (and it came to pass after Joshua died, the sons of Israel inquired in the Lord); it did not say "the Lord," which the usage of our speech demanded.

**2.** (1, 3) *Et bellemus in Chananaeo* (and let us make war in the Canaanite). It did not say "against the Canaanite," or "facing the Canaanite," or at least "into the Canaanite."

**3.** (2, 8) *Et mortuus est Iesus filius Nave servus Domini, filius centum decem annorum* (and Joshua the son of Nun, the servant of the Lord, died, a son of one hundred ten years). This repetition—that, when it had said *filius Nave* (son of Nun), it repeated *filius* (son) and then added *centum decem annorum* (of one hundred ten years)—is unusual, in that it said *filius centum decem annorum* (a son of one hundred ten years) rather than "a man of one hundred ten years"; but yet it is found elsewhere too.

**4.** (2, 10) *Et omnis generatio appositi sunt ad patres suos* (and the whole generation, they were gathered to their fathers). The expression is to be noted, that it did not say "it was gathered," because they were many.

**5.** (2, 14) *Tradidit eos in manu praedantium* (he delivered them in the hand of plunderers); it did not say "into the hand," which the Latin expression seems to require.

**6.** (2, 18) *Et cum suscitavit Dominus eis iudices, et erat Dominus cum iudice* (and when the Lord raised up judges for them, and the Lord was with the judge); by the customary expression there is an extra *et* (and) here, for it would be complete: "and when the Lord raised up judges for them, the Lord was with the judge,"

this too is to be noted, that, passing from the plural to the singular, it did not say "with the judges," but *cum iudice* (with the judge), that is, with each judge.

**7.** (2, 19) *Et factum est, cum moreretur iudex, et revertebantur et iterum corrumpebant super patres suos* (and it came to pass, when the judge died, and they would turn back and again would corrupt themselves beyond their fathers). The correct expression could be thus, according to the usage of our speech: "and it would come to pass, when the judge died, they would turn back and again would corrupt themselves beyond their fathers." If it did not have *et* (and), it would be complete: "when the judge died, they would turn back and again would corrupt themselves beyond their fathers," that is, "more than their fathers."

**8.** (2, 20) *Propter quod tanta dereliquit gens haec Testamentum meum* (because this nation has so greatly forsaken my covenant). It said *tanta* (so great [a quantity]) for "so much," that is, so greatly—a noun for an adverb, which is found also in Latin expressions, especially poetic ones.

**9.** (2, 20) *Et non obaudierunt vocis meae* (and they did not obey my voice [genitive]); it is rather a Greek expression.

**10.** (3, 10) *Factus est super eum Spiritus Domini* (the Spirit of the Lord came to be upon him). Since the *Spiritus Domini* (Spirit of the Lord) certainly did not come to be, but came to be upon him, it is said as if it were said: it came about that he was upon him. From which expression there is also read in the Gospel: *ante me factus est*[^1] (he came to be before me), that is, it came about that he was before me; which would be said another way: "he was set ahead of me."

**11.** (3, 21) *Et sumpsit Aod gladium de super femore suo dextro* (and Ehud took the sword from upon his right thigh). It could be rendered thus, what the Greek has, ἀπὸ ἄνωθεν; for the expression is less Latin.

**12.** (3, 17) *Erat autem Eglom vir exilis valde* (now Eglon was a very slender man). This was said by irony, that is, the following words indicate it by a contrary expression; for when he was struck, it is said that the fat closed over the wound, because the sword was not drawn out of his belly.

**13.** (3, 31) *Et percussit alienigenas in sescentos viros* (and he struck the foreigners to six hundred men); it did not say "six hundred men of the foreigners," or at least "the foreigners in six hundred men."

**14.** (4, 6) *Nonne praecepit Dominus Deus Israel tibi: 'et perges in montem Thabor?'* (has not the Lord God of Israel commanded you: "and you shall go to Mount Tabor?") It did not say "that you should go," which seems to be the consequence, but by an unusual expression, *nonne praecepit tibi; 'et perges'* (has he not commanded you: "and you shall go"), so that we understand the word that was placed above, and it be "have you not gone?"

Or by the customary expression it has an extra *et* (and); for when this conjunction is removed, the sense runs: "has not the Lord God of Israel commanded you: you shall go to Mount Tabor *et accipies tecum decem millia virorum* (and you shall take with you ten thousand men)?" and so on.

**15.** (4, 8) *Quoniam nescio diem, in quo Dominus prosperat angelum mecum* (for I do not know the day on which the Lord prospers the angel with me); whether he prospers the acts of his angel who is with me, or makes things prosperous for me through the angel.

**16.** (4, 13) *Omnes currus suos nongentos currus ferreos* (all his chariots, nine hundred iron chariots). Wherever the pause is made, the repetition is elegant: whether *omnes currus suos* (all his chariots), and then there follow *nongentos currus ferreos* (nine hundred iron chariots); or *omnes currus suos nongentos* (all his nine hundred chariots), and then be introduced *currus ferreos* (iron chariots).

**17.** (4, 15) *Et pavefecit Dominus Sisaram et omnes currus eius et omnia castra eius in ore gladii in conspectu Barach* (and the Lord terrified Sisera and all his chariots and all his camp by the edge of the sword in the sight of Barak). How did he terrify the chariots, unless you understand those who were in the chariots?

**18.** (4, 15) *Et pavefecit Dominus Sisaram et omnes currus eius et omnia castra eius in ore gladii in conspectu Barach* (and the Lord terrified Sisera and all his chariots and all his camp by the edge of the sword in the sight of Barak). He terrified by the edge of the sword, as if he said "he slew by the edge of the sword"; *pavefecit* (he terrified), then, when they were being cut down by the sword.

**19.** (4, 16) *Et descendit Sisara de super curru suo* (and Sisera came down from upon his chariot); it could be said in Latin thus, what is in Greek ἀπὸ ἄνωθεν.

**20.** (4, 18) *Et cooperuit eum in pelle sua* (and she covered him in her skin); it did not say "with a skin" or "with a skin [de + ablative]"; for thus the Greek has it: ἐν τῇ δέρρει.

**21.** (4, 20) *Et erit si quis venerit ad te et dixerit: 'est hic vir?' Et dices: 'non est*.' (and it shall be, if anyone comes to you and says, "is there a man here?" and you shall say, "there is not.") With the conjunction removed it has a consistent sense: "if anyone comes to you and says, 'is there a man here?' you shall say, 'there is not.'"

**22.** (4, 24) *Et pergebat manus filiorum Israel pergens* (and the hand of the sons of Israel went on, going); it is a customary and frequent expression.

**23.** (4, 24) *Et indurabatur in Iabin regem Chanaan* (and it was hardening against Jabin king of Canaan); this word *indurabatur* (was hardening) is used in a new way, for "it was becoming strong and exceedingly powerful."

**24.** (4, 26) *Perforavit caput eius et percussit* (she pierced his head and struck him); that is, she struck his head and pierced it.

**25.** (4, 31) *Sic pereant omnes inimici tui, Domine; et diligentes eum sicut ortus solis in potentia eius* (so let all your enemies perish, O Lord; and let those who love him be as the rising of the sun in its power); it did not say "those who love you," although it certainly wished it to be understood of the Lord.

**26.** (6, 3) *Et factum est quando seminavit vir Israel* (and it came to pass when the man of Israel sowed); it does not signify one man, but the nation itself.

**27.** (6, 3) *Ascendebat Madian et Amalech et filii orientis et ascendebant super eum* (Midian and Amalek and the sons of the East would come up, and they would come up against him); this repetition of the word does not seem to have the customary elegance or feeling.

**28.** (6, 9) *Et liberavi vos de manu Aegypti* (and I delivered you from the hand of Egypt); by *manum* (hand) it signifies power, by *Aegyptum* (Egypt) the Egyptians.

**29.** (6, 13) *Et dixit ad eum Gedeon: 'in me, Domine, mi'* (and Gideon said to him, "on me, my Lord"); "attend" is understood, that is, "attend to me." And this expression is most familiar to the Scriptures.

**30.** (6, 13) *Et si est Dominus nobiscum, et ut quid invenerunt nos omnia mala ista?* (and if the Lord is with us, and why have all these evils found us?) It has an extra conjunction *et* (and), as Scripture is wont to speak; if it is removed, the sentence is complete: "and if the Lord is with us, why have all these evils found us?" Although the earlier conjunction too could be removed with the sentence kept intact, so that, when it had said *in me, Domine mi* (on me, my Lord), there would then follow *si est Dominus nobiscum, ut quid invenerunt nos omnia mala ista?* (if the Lord is with us, why have all these evils found us?); for the usage of our expression rather requires this.

**31.** (6, 25) *Et vitulum annorum septem* (and a calf of seven years). Here it is manifest how Scripture calls calves; for an ox of seven years, according to the usage of our expression, is certainly not a calf. According to this expression, then, it was said of Shamgar that he slew *sescentos viros praeter vitulos boum*[^2] (six hundred men besides the calves of the oxen), that is, "besides the oxen."

**32.** (6, 34) *Et cecinit in cornea* (and he sounded on the horn); "trumpet" is understood; for the Greek does not have *tuba* (trumpet), but only *cornea* (horn).

**33.** (7, 12) *Camelis eorum non erat numerus, et erant sicut arena quae est ad labium maris* (their camels had no number, and they were as the sand which is at the lip of the sea). It is agreed to be said as a hyperbole, with regard to multitude. But this rendering, where it put *labium maris* (the lip of the sea) for "the shore," is constant in the Scriptures, but rare in the Latin manuscripts, because most rendered *litus* (shore), wishing rather to put down what *labium* (lip) signified than *labium* (lip) itself. For if the *Septuagint* translators had wished to say "shore," the Greek language would not have lacked a word to say it.

**34.** (7, 16) *Et divisit trecentos viros per tria principia* (and he divided the three hundred men into three beginnings); it put *principia* (beginnings) for "parts."

**35.** (8, 1) *Et dixerunt ad Gedeon vir Ephraem* (and the man of Ephraim said to Gideon); that is, the men of the tribe of Ephraim.

**36.** (8, 1) *Quid verbum hoc fecisti nobis, ut non vocares nos cum exires pugnare in Madian?* (what is this word you have done to us, that you did not call us when you went out to fight in Midian?) It put *verbum* (word) for "deed."

**37.** (9, 4) *Et dederunt septuaginta argenti* (and they gave seventy of silver); "by weight" is understood, or something of the kind; the rendering from the Hebrew has *septuaginta pondo* (seventy by weight).

**38.** (9, 4) *Et conduxit in ipsis Abimelech viros* (and Abimelech hired men in them); it said *in ipsis* (in them) for "out of them," that is, out of the seventy by weight of silver he hired men.

**39.** (9, 4) *Viros inanes et perturbatos* (empty and disordered men). It put *inanes* (empty) for "light men," to whom "weighty men" are contrary, whence it is said: *in populo gravi laudabo te*[^3] (in a weighty people I will praise you).

**40.** (9, 5) *Et intravit in domum patris sui in Ephratha et interfecit fratres suos filios Ierobaal septuaginta viros super lapidem unum* (and he entered the house of his father in Ophrah and killed his brothers, the sons of Jerubbaal, seventy men, upon one stone). Because it had already said above that the sons of Jerubbaal, that is, of Gideon, were seventy, and now says that these seventy were killed, although two fewer were killed—that is, the one who killed them and the younger Jotham who hid, of whom it says by way of addition *et remansit Ioatham filius Ierobaal iunior, quoniam abscondit se* (and Jotham the younger son of Jerubbaal remained, because he hid himself)—and on this account it put the whole number for nearly the whole.

**41.** (9, 23) *Et spreverunt viri Sicimorum in domo Abimelech* (and the men of Shechem despised in the house of Abimelech); it did not say "they despised the house of Abimelech."

**42.** (9, 34) *Et insidiati sunt super Sicimam quattuor principia* (and they lay in ambush against Shechem, four beginnings); that is, four parts. We have already noted this expression above too[^4].

**43.** (9, 43) *Et accepit populum et divisit eum in tria principia* (and he took the people and divided them into three beginnings); it said *accepit* (he took) of that people who were with him; and here it put *tria principia* (three beginnings) for "three parts."

**44.** (9, 55) *Et abierunt vir in locum suum* (and the man went away to his place); that is, "each one."

**45.** (11, 8) *Et eris nobis in caput omnibus habitantibus Galaad* (and you shall be to us for a head to all the inhabitants of Gilead); it did not say "you shall be our head"; for thus Scripture is wont to speak. Whence there is: *esto mihi in Deum protectorem et in domum refugii*[^5] (be to me for a protecting God and for a house of refuge), and many things of this kind.

**46.** (11, 34) *Et haec unica ei, et non est ei praeter ipsam filius aut filia* (and this one was an only child to him, and there is not to him besides her a son or daughter); this is what was said, *unica ei* (an only child to him), but the statement is repeated for the sake of affection.

**47.** (13, 2) *Et non est ei praeter ipsam filius aut filia* (and there is not to him besides her a son or daughter); *non est ei* (there is not to him) is said for "there was not to him"; for past things are being narrated.

**48.** (13, 2) *Et uxor eius sterilis et non pariebat* (and his wife was barren and did not bear), when what was said, *sterilis* (barren), could have sufficed.

**49.** (13, 8) *Et precatus est Manue Dominum et dixit: 'in me, Domine, homo Dei, quem misisti, veniat nunc iterum ad nos'* (and Manoah prayed to the Lord and said, "on me, O Lord, the man of God whom you sent, let him now come again to us"); *in me* (on me) is said in such a way that "attend" is understood.

**50.** (15, 8) *Et percussit eos tibiam super femur* (and he struck them, shin upon thigh). This expression is unusual; yet it is an expression, not some place of a wound on the body, as I explained in the *Questions*[^6]; this is: he struck them so as to cause amazement, to cause astonishment.

**51.** (15, 10) *Et dixerunt vir Iuda: 'quare ascendistis super nos'* (and the man of Judah said, "why have you come up against us"); it put the singular for the plural; for the men of Judah said it, not one man.

**52.** (15, 12) *Ne forte occurratis in me vos* (lest perhaps you fall upon me); instead of what is "lest you kill me." This expression too is explained in the *Questions*[^7].

**53.** (15, 13-14) *Et reduxerunt eum de petra, et venit usque ad Maxillam* (and they brought him back from the rock, and he came as far as Jawbone); the place was so named afterward from that deed which Samson himself did there, when, fighting with the jawbone of a donkey, he laid low a thousand. This, then, is said by prolepsis, because this history was written down after all those deeds.

**54.** (15, 14) *Sicut stuppa cum olefecerit ignem* (like tow when it has smelled fire); a metaphor from the animate to the inanimate; for tow does not have a sense of smell, but it is said as if it were said "when it has sensed the fire." Although this too which I said, "has sensed," pertains to the same metaphor; but what was said, *olefecerit* (has smelled), pertains to the swiftness to be understood.

**55.** (15, 15) *Et extendit manum suam et accepit eam* (and he stretched out his hand and took it); even if it did not say *extendit manum suam* (he stretched out his hand), we would certainly understand that it was taken with the hand stretched out.

**56.** (16, 2) *Et nuntiatum est Gazaeis dicentes* (and it was announced to the men of Gaza, saying); it did not say "they announced, saying," or "it was announced by those saying."

**57.** (16, 7) *Si ligaverint me in septem nervis humidis nondum siccatis* (if they bind me with seven moist sinews not yet dried); it did not say "with seven sinews," but *in septem nervis* (with seven sinews [in + ablative]).

**58.** (16, 7) *Si ligaverint me in septem nervis humidis nondum siccatis, et infirmabor* (if they bind me with seven moist sinews not yet dried, and I shall be weakened); it would be complete even if it did not have *et* (and).

**59.** (16, 9) *Et insidiae ei sedebant in cubiculo* (and the ambush was sitting for him in the chamber); which some rendered *obsidebant* (were besieging); but the Greek has ἐκάθητο, that is, *sedebat* (it was sitting), because *insidiae* (ambush) is said in Greek in the singular number, and it cannot be said in Latin "an ambush [singular]." But the expression was to be noted, because it is said *insidiae sedebant* (the ambush were sitting), for those "who were lying in ambush"; for they themselves were sitting.

**60.** (16, 10) *Nunc ergo indica mihi in quo ligaberis* (now therefore tell me by what you may be bound); it did not say "with what you may be bound," or "from what you may be bound."

**61.** (16, 11) *Si ligaverint me in funibus novis* (if they bind me with new ropes); it did not say "with ropes."

**62.** (16, 11) Likewise: *si ligaverint me in funiculis novis in quibus non est factum opus, et infirmabor* (if they bind me with new cords on which no work has been done, and I shall be weakened); it would be complete even if *et* (and) were not put before.

**63.** (16, 15) *Hoc tertium fefellisti me* (this third you have deceived me); it put *tertium* (a third) for "a third time," or "thrice"—a noun, that is, for an adverb, which is also wont to be done elegantly in Latin expressions.

**64.** (16, 26) *Dimitte me, et palpabo columnas, super quas domus confirmata est super eas* (let me go, and I will feel the columns upon which the house is established upon them).
