# Book II: Expressions on Exodus

**1.** (1:12) *And they grew strong exceedingly, exceedingly*.

**2.** (1:21) What is the meaning of what is said of the midwives: *and they made themselves houses, because they feared God*? For it was said before: *God did good to the midwives*[^1]. And to this it seems to belong that they made themselves houses by fearing God, as though the benefits of God profited them to this end, that they should make themselves houses.

Is it to be understood that they did not previously have houses? Or does this word pertain to riches, or rather to some suitable means of a household? For similar to this seems what Jacob too says, after the fourteen years of his servitude, to his father-in-law, who wished to keep him still with him: *now therefore when shall I too make a house for myself?*[^2] For he had said that Laban's flocks had increased under him; as Laban himself too had confessed, saying: *God blessed me at your coming in*[^3]; when, therefore, he says *when shall I too make a house for myself?* it is implied: "as you too have." For this seems to be the force of his saying *I too*, so that to the very making of the house there seems to belong the wage that is consequently proposed to be set for Laban.

**3.** (1:22) *And every female, keep it alive*, for so the Greeks have it; the Latin do not have: *it*.

**4.** (2:1-2) *Now there was a certain man of the tribe of Levi, and he took to himself of the daughters of Levi*; a wife is understood, which some Latin translators thought should even be added; for there follows: *and he had her, and she conceived*.

**5.** (2:3) What *thibin* is, is difficult to know, for this reason: that neither did the Greek translator render this word from the Hebrew, nor the Latin from the Greek, but each rendered it as he found it.

**6.** (2:14) *But Moses feared, and said: 'if this matter has been so divulged.'* Two things are to be noted in this expression: first, that the sentence is left hanging, and so dismissed; then, that it put *word* for "deed."

**7.** (2:25) *And he looked upon the children of Israel, and was made known to them*; *was made known* is put in place of this: that he did among them the things by which they would understand God's care for them.

**8.** (3:7) *Seeing I have seen the affliction of my people, who are in Egypt*.

**9.** (3:11-12) *And Moses said to God: 'who am I, that I should go to Pharaoh king of Egypt, and that I should lead the children of Israel out of the land of Egypt?' And he said: 'for I will be with you,'* so the Greek has it; but the Latin said: *and who am I*, and did not say *that I should go, and that I should lead*, but: *that I may go, and lead*. And as for what the Greek has, *and he said, for I will be with you*, it is certainly understood that he said it to Moses; but all this the Latin added, and said: *and God said to Moses*.

**10.** (3:16) God says what Moses is to say to the children of Israel: *visiting I have visited you, and whatever has befallen you in Egypt*; the Greek has: *with a visitation I have visited you*.

**11.** (3:18) God says to Moses concerning the children of Israel: *and they will hear your voice*; the Greek has: *and they will hearken to your voice*, because "hearkening" is also said of a man.

**12.** (3:22) Whereas the Latin has: *the woman will ask of her neighbor, and of her lodger, vessels of silver and gold and clothing*, the Greek has: *of her fellow dweller*, that is: συσκήνου, which some Latin translated: *of her chamber companion*.

**13.** (4:1) Moses says: *'what if they do not believe me, nor hearken to my voice'*; which the Latin translated: *hear*.

**14.** (4:4) *Stretch out the hand, and take hold of the tail*, the Greek has; but the Latin translated: *your hand and its tail*.

**15.** (4:4) *And stretching out his hand he took hold of the tail, and it became a rod in his hand*.

**16.** (4:5) *And he said to him: 'that they may believe you, because the Lord God of their fathers has appeared to you'*; the Greek does not have: *and he said to him*, but continued at once: *that they may believe you*;

that is, the words of God, after that miracle was performed; as though he were still speaking, and had only now completed the sense of his words. For the order of the words is: *stretch out the hand and take hold of the tail,* that they may believe you; but the narrative of the deed was interposed, and afterward there was returned: *that they may believe you*.

**17.** (4:6) Whereas the Latin has: *and his hand became leprous like snow*, the Greek does not have *leprous*, but only: *it became like snow*.

**18.** (4:8) *But if they do not hear the voice of the first sign*, so the Greek has it; but the Latin: *your voice of the first sign*; so too there follows: *they will believe you at the voice of the following sign*.

**19.** (4:9) *And the water that you take from the river will be blood upon the dry land*, or rather *upon the dry*, that is, upon that which is dry; which the Latin said: *blood upon the earth*.

**20.** (4:10) Moses says: *I beg you, Lord, I am not eloquent, before yesterday and the day before*.

**21.** (4:17) *And this rod you shall take in your hand, with which you shall do signs with it*; when it could be said, "with which you shall do signs"; or else: "this rod you shall take in your hand, and you shall do signs with it"; but as it is, both were said by the customary expression of the Scriptures.

**22.** (4:18-19) *Now after those many days, the king of Egypt died; and the Lord said to Moses in Midian: 'go, proceed into Egypt, for all who sought your soul are dead.'* Many kinds of expression in these words are to be noted; first: *go, proceed into Egypt*, as though it would not suffice to say only *go*, or only *proceed*; then: *for all who sought your soul are dead*, when Scripture had said that only the king of Egypt died, and it had been said before of him alone that he sought to kill Moses. Or did he die last, after his other enemies? But if it is so, it is not a matter of expression, but of sense. Likewise *who sought your soul* is said in the Scriptures not only in a bad sense, but also in a good one; for, as here, it is said in a bad sense also in the *Psalms*: *let them be confounded and ashamed who seek my soul*; in a good sense: *flight has perished from me, and there is no one who seeks after my soul*[^4]; unless perhaps some difference is said to lie between "seeking" and "seeking after," so that the former is to be understood in a bad sense, the latter in a good one.

**23.** (5:10) *And they said to the people, saying: 'thus says Pharaoh'*; which expression the Latin was loath to render.

**24.** (5:21) *May God see you and judge, because you have made our odor abominable openly before Pharaoh, and openly before his servants, to give a sword into his hands, that he may kill us*; for so the Greek has it; but the Latin which we read as the best says: *that you should give a sword into his hands*; which solecism was made by no necessity of translation, since it is not in the Greek.

**25.** (6:4) *I established my covenant with them, so as to give them the land of the Canaanites, and the land which they dwelt in, in which also they dwelt in it*; for so the Greek has it, which certainly seems to sound absurd even in the Greek language;

and yet the authority of the *Septuagint* translators is so great, who were not loath to speak thus. For what then? Does a sense lie hidden here too? But if there is none, the expression itself is to be noted, lest, found elsewhere, it impede the sense, or compel one to seek something where it is not to be sought.

**26.** (6:5) Whereas the Latin has: *I have heard the groaning of the children of Israel, in the way that the Egyptians afflict them*, the Greek has: καταδουλοῦνται αὐτούς, which could be rendered: *reduce them to servitude*; for it cannot be done in a single word.

**27.** (6:9) *And Moses spoke thus to the children of Israel, and they did not hearken to Moses out of faintness of mind and out of hard labors*; it says *hearkened*, not: "heard."

**28.** (6:12) *For I am uneloquent*, which Moses says to the Lord—the Greek said ἄλογος, not: "unskilled," as if ἀμαθής, or ἀπαίδευτος.

**29.** (6:26) *These are the Aaron and Moses, to whom God said to them that they should lead the children of Israel out of the land of Egypt*; for so the Greek has it.

**30.** (6:30) *Behold I am of a thin voice, and how will Pharaoh hearken to me?* It is to be noted that he said *will hearken*, not: "will hear."

**31.** (7:6) *And Moses and Aaron did as the Lord commanded them, so they did*; what would be lacking if it were not said: *so they did*?

**32.** (7:9) *If Pharaoh speaks to you, saying: 'give us a sign or a wonder,' then you shall say to Aaron your brother: 'take the rod,'* did not the custom of our manner of speech, and a certain completeness of it, require that it be said thus: "if Pharaoh speaks to you, saying: 'give us a sign or a wonder,' you shall say to Aaron your brother: 'take the rod'"? What, then, is added there—*and*—except by some peculiarity of the Hebrew manner of speech? For it is reported not to be Greek either.

**33.** (7:11) The Latin do not seem to me to have rendered conveniently enough the σοφισταὶ of Pharaoh, so as to say: *wise men*; for wise men are called σοφοί. The Latin translator could have said "sophists," since there is no way to say it in Latin; and so we now use this word as Latin, just as we say "philosophy" not only in Greek but also in Latin: the most eloquent authors of Latin letters called them "sophists."

**34.** (7:12) *And the rod of Aaron swallowed up their rods*; as if it said: "the serpent of Aaron."

**35.** (7:15) As to what is said of Pharaoh: *behold, he goes out to the water*; the Greek has: *upon the water*.

**36.** (7:16) *Let my people go that they may serve me in the desert, and behold, you have not hearkened until now*; how often is it said *you have hearkened*, when it is said of a man?

**37.** (7:22) *And the enchanters of the Egyptians did likewise with their sorceries. And Pharaoh's heart was hardened, and he did not hearken to them, as the Lord had said*; for it is Scripture's usage to say *he hearkened*, even when it speaks of a man.

**38.** (8:1) *Let my people go that they may serve me*; not: "that it may serve," a manner of speaking which is hardly found except where the singular thing itself consists of many. For *people* is said by a singular name, but consists of many. So too is: *let all the earth worship you*[^5]; because *all the earth* was said for "the men who are in the earth."

**39.** (8:2) *Behold I strike all your borders with frogs*; for so the Greek has it, by a most elegant expression, so that the frogs themselves are understood to be the plague by which the land of Egypt is struck.

**40.** (8:3-4) *And the river will belch forth frogs, and going up they will enter into your houses and into the storerooms of your chambers, upon your beds, and into the houses of your servants, and of your people, and into your kneading bowls, and into your ovens; and upon you and upon your people and upon your servants the frogs will go up*.

It is to be noted that when it had said *into the houses* etc., it kept the accusative case; *and upon the beds* etc., where it said *upon*, it likewise kept the accusative; but at *into the kneading bowls* and *into the ovens* it shifted to the ablative;

for the Greek too changed the preposition, which the Latin does not change. For it has: εἰς τοὺς οἴκους *into the houses*; but *into the kneading bowls* ἐν τοῖς φυράμασιν.

in which it would be remarkable if there were not also a sense, not merely an expression, so that perhaps it wishes the frogs to be understood as having arisen in the kneading bowls and in the ovens, not as having gone up or rushed in from outside; and because it foretells that the river will belch them forth, it wishes them thence to fill all things.

**41.** (8:6) *And Aaron stretched out his hand over the waters of Egypt and brought forth frogs, and the frog was brought forth and covered the earth*; the repetition was carried over from the plural to the singular number; for *frog* is certainly put for the multitude of frogs; but somehow, through the custom of speaking with which men's senses are imbued, what is said in the singular often seems more than what is said in the plural; for more is grasped when one says, for example, "there is a soldier there" than "there are soldiers there"; "there is a fish there" than "there are fish there."

**42.** (8:14) *And they gathered them in heaps upon heaps*; this repetition signifies the multitude of heaps, and is familiar to the Scriptures.

**43.** (8:16) Whereas the Latin have: *stretch out with the hand your rod, and strike with the earth*; not: *strike the earth*; the Greek has: *the mound of earth*, if indeed it is rightly rendered by this word what is said: τὸ χῶμα τῆς γῆς.

**44.** (8:18) *And the enchanters did likewise with their sorceries, to bring forth gnats, and they could not.* It is to be noted: *they did* is said in place of this: "they tried to do." For certainly, if they did likewise, beyond doubt they brought forth gnats; but because there follows: *to bring forth, and they could not*; therefore they did not do likewise, but tried to do likewise. Or if perhaps they too, although they acted by *sorceries*, nevertheless stretched out a rod with the same manner of acting, which Scripture did not express, to this is to be referred: *they did likewise*.

**45.** (8:21) *And upon the land upon which they are upon it.*

**46.** (8:22) *And on that day I will make glorious the land of Goshen, in which my people are upon it*.

**47.** (8:29) *And Moses said: 'I will go out from you, and will pray to God, and the dog-fly will go out from Pharaoh and from his servants'*; as though he said *I will go out from you* to one person, and there followed concerning another Pharaoh, from whom the dog-fly was to go out.

**48.** (9:1) *Let my people go, that they may serve me*.

**49.** (9:18. 24) *Behold, I will rain this hour tomorrow much hail. And the hail was very, very much*.

**50.** (9:29. 28) *And the voices and the hail will cease*. It is to be noted that Scripture is accustomed to call thunders *voices*, which Pharaoh too above called *voices of God*, saying: *pray for me to the Lord, that the voices of God may cease to be made*; where there is also that expression in his saying: *let the voices cease to be made*.

**51.** (10:2) *That you may recount in the ears of your sons, and of your sons' sons, whatever I have mocked the Egyptians*;

it is to be noted in what way *I have mocked* is said, lest perhaps it be said in the way of: *this dragon which you formed to mock him*[^6], and that passage in *Job*: *this is the beginning of the Lord's creation, which he made to be mocked by his angels*[^7].

**52.** (10:4) *Behold, I bring this hour tomorrow much locust*. See how above it said "frog," where we said that more is generally understood when something of this kind is said in the singular than if it were said in the plural. For it is plain that *much locust* sounds more than if it said: "many locusts."

**53.** (10:8) *But who and who are they that will go?* We say daily, by a more familiar usage: "which and which will go?"

**54.** (10:15) *There was not left anything green nothing on the trees*; it should have been said in the manner of our expression: "there was not left anything green on the trees."

**55.** (10:16-17) Pharaoh to Moses: *I have sinned before the Lord your God and against you; take up therefore my offense*; this expression his brothers used also to Joseph, where they said: *receive the iniquity of the servants of the God of your father*[^8].

**56.** (10:23) Whereas the Latin have: *and no one saw his brother for three days*, the Greek has: *and no one did not see his brother*; it is also to be noted that any man whatever is called the brother of a man.

**57.** (10:24) Pharaoh says to Moses and Aaron: *Go and serve the Lord your God, except the sheep and oxen, leave behind*; for so the Greek has it: it is a very unusual expression, unless after a pause is added: *leave behind*, and "these" is supplied; so that the sense is: "go, except the sheep and oxen, and leave these behind"; for such an ellipsis is frequently made in the expressions of the Scriptures.

**58.** (10:26) *And we will not leave a hoof*; as though, the cattle having been led away, a hoof could remain: for what else is *we will not leave a hoof* but: "not even a hoof will we leave"?

**59.** (10:28) Pharaoh to Moses: *take heed to yourself to go on further to see my face*; in place of this: "take heed to yourself that you do not further see my face."

**60.** (11:2) *And let each one ask of his neighbor, and the woman of her neighbor, vessels of silver and gold and clothing*; it is to be noted that the Egyptians too are called the neighbors of the Hebrews.

**61.** (11:6-7) *And there will be a great cry throughout all the land of Egypt, such as has not been, and the like will not again be added; but among the children of Israel not a dog will growl with its tongue, from man even to beast*. It is a most elegant expression; for by *dog* it signified the lowest, whether of men or of beasts, thus conveying how great a quiet there would be among the Hebrews, while the Egyptians cried out in bitter bereavement.

**62.** (12:3) *Let each one take a sheep according to the houses of the fathers' families*. What it means by *fathers' families* is not easy to understand; whether the cities in which they were dwelling, or rather the numerous families propagated from the so-called paternity of each; for this is reported to be the more likely meaning in the Greek.

**63.** (12:4) *But if those in the house be few, so that they are not enough for the sheep*, that is, so that their fewness is not enough to consume the sheep, *he shall take in with him his neighbor next to him*, that is, the house itself shall take in a neighbor; he put "the nearest man" for "men," and the singular for the plural; for not one, but as many as suffice; since Scripture was speaking of consuming the sheep, it says *according to the number of souls, each one what suffices*; souls for "men," the whole from a part.

**64.** (12:7-8) *They shall take of the blood, and put it upon the two doorposts, and upon the lintel in the houses in which they will eat them in them*. When it said *in which*, it also said *in them*, by a most customary manner; but *they will eat them*, it is asked which, and "flesh" is understood; for this follows: *and they will eat the flesh this night roasted with fire*; which the Latin manuscripts have as: *who will eat the flesh* (feminine), but the Greeks have it (neuter), that is, flesh, but in the neuter gender; therefore in the Greek it is: in the houses, in which they will eat it (neuter) in them. And that expression—that it should say *them* before it stated what was to be understood in what follows as the thing it called *them*—is similar to the expression which we explained concerning the son of Moses[^9], when the angel wished to kill him, and we adduced an example from the *Psalm*: *its foundations are in the holy mountains; the Lord loves the gates of Zion*[^10]; for we do not understand whose foundations except from what follows; so too here: *in which they will eat them in them*, that is, in which houses they will eat them, is understood as the flesh, of which it speaks subsequently.

**65.** (12:22) *And you shall take for yourselves a little bunch of hyssop, and dip it in the blood that is by the door, and smear the lintel and both doorposts with the blood*. Here too *a little bunch of hyssop* certainly signifies several bunches, just as "frog" several frogs, and "locust" several locusts; but when this trope occurs in things that are not customarily spoken of so by us, it makes obscurity.

**66.** (12:26-27) *And it will be, when your sons say to you: 'what is this service?' And you shall say to them: 'this is the sacrifice of the Passover to the Lord'*; even if it did not have *and*, it would be complete: *you shall say to them*.

**67.** (12:28) *And the children of Israel coming did as the Lord commanded Moses and Aaron, so they did*; it would not add *so they did*, were it not the custom of the Scriptures.

**68.** (12:51) *And it came to pass, on that day the Lord led the children of Israel out of the land of Egypt*.

**69.** (13:1) *And the Lord said to Moses, saying*.

**70.** (13:12) *Everything that opens the womb, the males*; it brought a plural number to a singular, because the very thing that was said, *everything that opens*, is taken certainly not in one but in many. Similar is the expression: *attend, my people*[^11]. Such too is what follows a little after: *everything that opens the womb of the herds and of the flocks, whatever will be born to you; everything whatever will be born to you*, this is the manner of the expression.

**71.** (13:13) *Everything that opens the womb of a she-ass*; the Greek has: *of an ass* (masculine); by that manner of expression which we have already noted in *Genesis*, where the masculine was put for the feminine, concerning the death of Sarah, Scripture saying: *Abraham rising up from his dead* (masculine), and: *I will bury my dead* (masculine)[^12]; and such things are often said there concerning the dead woman.

**72.** (13:15) *For this reason I sacrifice to the Lord everything that opens the womb, the males*; an expression similar to the one above.

**73.** (13:16) *And it will be for a sign upon your hand*; that is: upon your works; where there is perhaps a sense rather than an expression.

**74.** (14:27) *And the Lord shook off the Egyptians into the midst of the sea*; the Greek has: *and the Lord shook off the Egyptians the midst of the sea*.

**75.** (14:31) *And Israel saw the great hand which the Lord did to the Egyptians*.

**76.** (15:1) *Then Moses and the children of Israel sang this song to the Lord, and they said to say*; for so the Greek has it.

**77.** (15:22) *And they did not find water, that they might drink*; it could lack: *that they might drink*.

**78.** (15:24) *And the people murmured against Moses, saying* (plural), not "saying" (singular); but: *saying* (plural); for a people consists of many.

**79.** (16:1) *And all the assembly of the children of Israel came into the wilderness* (plural verb); it did not say "came" (singular), because an assembly consists of many.

**80.** (16:2) *All the assembly of the children of Israel was murmuring against Moses and Aaron*; this expression too is the same.

**81.** (16:4) *And the Lord said to Moses: 'behold, I will rain you loaves from heaven'*; being about to rain manna, he promised loaves. It is an expression by which *bread* is put for "nourishment"; and Scripture loves to say *loaves* in the plural rather than in the singular.

**82.** (16:7) *In the morning you shall see*, it says, *the glory [of the Lord], while he hears your murmuring against God*, that is "because you murmur against God"; which amounts to as much as if it said: "against God." Then it is to be noted that here in a novel manner "hearing" is said not of those praying but of those murmuring, since Scripture certainly rebukes the murmuring. As though it said this, that God took notice of their murmuring, and it called this very taking-notice of God "hearing."

**83.** (16:9. 12) *Approach before God; for he has heard your murmuring*; again it signifies "hearing" not of prayers and petitions but even of the murmuring of the wicked; this too God says afterward: *I have heard the murmuring of the children of Israel*.

**84.** (16:14) *And behold, on the face of the wilderness something fine like coriander*. Since God so often says *the face of the earth*, as here *the face of the wilderness*, it is remarkable how men so err, when they hear "face," as to suppose something such as a man's face or that of some living creature.

**85.** (16:16) *According to the number of your souls; let each one with those dwelling with you gather*. How often Scripture calls men "souls" (the whole from a part); but not readily "fleshes" for men, but "flesh," as is: *I will not fear what flesh may do to me*, which it says more openly in the same *Psalm*: *what shall man do to me*[^13]. Yet it would not say: "according to the number of your fleshes," as it said: *according to the number of your souls*.

**86.** (16:21) *And they gathered it morning by morning*; as *wells by wells*, *heaps by heaps*, so it is said: *morning by morning*.

**87.** (16:29) *Let none of you go out, each one from his place, on the seventh day*; what is interposed, *each one*, could also be left unsaid.

**88.** (17:1) *But there was no water for the people to drink*, that is: "for drinking."

**89.** (17:2) *And the people cursed at Moses*; that is, they assailed Moses with curses, that is, they cursed him; for this he himself said in what follows: *why do you curse me?*

**90.** (17:3) *And the people murmured at Moses, saying*.

**91.** (17:5) *And the rod with which you struck the river, take in your hand. With which you struck* it said in place of what we say: "with which you struck"; and this expression is most frequent in the Scriptures.

**92.** (18:12) *And Jethro the father-in-law of Moses took burnt offerings and sacrifices for God*; *took* in place of this: "offered." Or is it perhaps a sense, not an expression, so that he is understood to have taken what Moses had offered—although Moses is not read above to have sacrificed, nor Aaron, nor any of the Hebrews who were led out of the land of Egypt, but only it is written above that Moses set up an altar and called it: *The Lord my refuge*? But this Jethro we read to have been a priest of Midian, that is, of the Midianite nation. It is remarkable, however, if at his coming Moses began to sacrifice and not rather Jethro himself, who was already a priest.

**93.** (18:18) *This matter is grievous to you*. "Is" is supplied.

**94.** (18:20) *And you shall show them the ways in which they will walk in them*.

**95.** (18:26) *But the grievous matter they referred upon Moses*; for so the Greek has it, which the Latin translated: to Moses. By that very expression the care itself is signified, by which it said *upon Moses*, as though a burden were signified to be laid upon him. *The grievous matter* is said for "a grievous question." Whence there follows: *but every light matter they judged themselves*.

**96.** (20:24) What the Greek has, ἐπονομάσω, is said more expressly *I shall name over* or: *I shall name upon*; which some, more customarily, rendered *I shall surname*. But *I shall surname* does not have a necessary meaning; yet *I shall surname* is said more closely than "I shall name." For this too some translators said.

**97.** (20:24) *And I will come to you and bless you*. Although above it spoke in the plural: *you (plural) shall make and sacrifice*, it says: *I will come to you* (singular), as though to Israel itself, that is, to the people.

**98.** (21:1-2) *And these are the ordinances which you shall set before them. If you buy a Hebrew slave*, and the rest. The expression is to be noted, how, when this was said to Moses—that is: *these are the ordinances which you shall set before them*—the rest is said to the people themselves as much as to Moses: *if you buy a Hebrew slave*; for this was not said to Moses, but what he was to say to the people, he was saying to the people themselves.

**99.** (21:6) *His master shall bore his ear with an awl, and he shall serve him forever or to eternity*, which the Greek has εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα. See how Scripture in many places says, according to this Greek word, *forever* or *eternal*, where that eternity is not understood according to which eternal things are promised to us, or according to which, on the contrary, the wicked will be burned with eternal fire! For this slave, who could not live forever, could not serve forever; but *forever* is understood as said of a time of which there is no end. Or else something eternal is signified by this mystery.

**100.** (21:13) *I will give you a place, to which he may flee there, who has killed*.

**101.** (21:20) *If anyone strikes his slave or his maidservant with a rod*; which is: "with a rod."

**102.** (21:28) *The bull shall be stoned with stones*; as though it could be stoned except with stones. This expression differs somewhat from the one in which it would say, in the manner of the Scriptures: *with a stoning it shall be stoned*, but yet it is similar.

**103.** (21:29) *But if the bull was given to goring before yesterday and the day before*; it put for past time a part for the whole, however long that may have been, in the manner of the Scriptures.

**104.** (21:33-34) *But if anyone opens a pit or digs out a pit and does not cover it, and a calf or an ass falls in there, the owner of the pit shall make restitution*. This expression too is to be understood as the whole from a part; for it is not the case that, if a horse falls into the pit or a sheep, he will therefore not make restitution because this is not written.

**105.** (21:34) *But what has died shall be his*, in place of: "shall be his (genitive)."

**106.** (22:5) *But if anyone grazes off a field or vineyard and lets his cattle graze off another field*, that is: "another's."

**107.** (22:26) *But if you take in pledge the garment of your neighbor, before sunset you shall return it to him*. It put the genus for the species; for it is said as if he had given a pledge of any garment, although Scripture testifies, from what follows, that it speaks specifically of the man who has only that garment with which to cover himself at night.

**108.** (23:20-21) *Behold, I send my angel before your face, to keep you on the way, to bring you into the land which I have prepared for you. Take heed to yourself and hearken to him*; God commands his angel to be hearkened to by the people—not, certainly, as one petitioning, but as one commanding.

**109.** (23:28) *And I will send hornets before you and it will cast out the Amorites*; it turned from the plural to the singular number. It is understood: the hornet will cast out the Amorites, as the frog, as the locust, not that it is one, but that by the singular the plural number is understood.

**110.** (23:30) *By parts I will cast them out from before you*.

**111.** (23:32-33) *You shall not make a pact with them and their gods; and they shall not settle in your land, lest they make you sin against me*; it did not say: "in me," yet it means the same.

**112.** (24:3) *And all the people answered with one voice, saying* (plural).

**113.** (24:10) *And they saw the place where the God of Israel had stood there. Where he had stood* could suffice, but these expressions are said to be Hebrew.

**114.** (25:13) *And you shall gild them with gold*. Similar is the expression: *it shall be stoned with stones*.

**115.** (25:20) *Cups in which they will offer in them*.

**116.** (26:10) *Two bases for one column on both its sides and two bases for one column on both its sides*. So as not to speak of them all, it speaks of two, after its manner of doubling, as *wells by wells, heaps by heaps*, *generations and generations*, and the like.

**117.** (26:29) *And the columns you shall gild with gold and you shall gild the bars with gold*.

**118.** (27:6) *And you shall bronze them with bronze*; such is the expression: *you shall gild with gold*.

**119.** (27:21) *Outside the veil which is over the covenant*. Speaking of the lamp to be lit, it says this, that is, that it should be lit outside, where the holy place is, not within the veil, which is over the covenant, that is, the Holy of Holies. Therefore it does not say *over* as though it were a roof or a vault or the firmament of heaven or the lid of the ark, but it called *over* even what was set up in place of a wall; just as we say that one reclines or stands "above" another, not at all that the one is carried by the other.

**120.** (27:21) *An everlasting ordinance throughout your generations*; in that sense of *everlasting* of which we have said much above.

**121.** (28:29) *And Aaron shall take the names of the children of Israel upon the breastpiece of judgment upon the breast, to one entering into the holy place*.

It would have been consistent to say: *entering into the holy place* (nominative), that is: "Aaron entering shall take," which some Latin, avoiding the solecism, rendered. But because the Greek too has *to one entering* (dative) and some Latin agree, I thought the expression should rather be noted than corrected.

**122.** (28:24) *And you shall put upon the breastpiece of judgment fringes, chained things*. And here some Latin, guarding against the solecism, rendered *chained fringes*; but the Greek has: τοὺς κροσσοὺς τὰ ἁλυσιδωτά. We are accustomed to call this an "absolute expression" when a neuter is applied to the masculine or feminine gender, as if we should say: "earthly justices are not stable things" (neuter).

**123.** (28:35) *And when Aaron begins to exercise the priesthood, his voice shall be heard, to him entering into the holy place in the sight of the Lord and going out*.

It said *his voice* of the little bells, which is rather their sound. *To him entering and going out*, in place of: "of him entering and going out," it put the dative for the genitive.

**124.** (29:13) *And the two kidneys and the fat which is upon them*, "is" is supplied, which some translators added.

**125.** (29:26) *And you shall separate it with a separation*.

**126.** (30:8) *And when Aaron lights the lamps, late he shall kindle upon it*. It said *late* for: "in the evening," and therefore the expression is to be noted: indeed the Greek has ὀψὲ. But "late" is not properly said except when the time has passed in which the thing said to be done *late* ought to have been done.

**127.** (30:12-13) *If you take the reckoning of the children of Israel in their visitation and each one shall give the redemption of his soul to the Lord and there shall be no ruin among them in their visitation and this is what they shall give you*.

This expression "hangs," because a copulative conjunction is interposed; which, if it were not interposed, it would not hang. It is placed in three spots, from any one of which, if it is removed, it makes the sentence complete.

Again, where it is said: *if you take the reckoning of the children of Israel in their visitation, and each one shall give the redemption of his soul to the Lord*; for if it had not said *and they shall give*, but had only said: *they shall give*, the sentence would be finished and would not hang; for it would be said thus: *if you take the reckoning of the children of Israel in their visitation, each one shall give the redemption of his soul to the Lord*.

But if this conjunction is placed here, it is to be removed below, so that it be said thus: *if you take the reckoning of the children of Israel in their visitation and each one shall give the redemption of his soul to the Lord and there shall be no ruin among them in their visitation, this is what they shall give*.

Or else in the middle, where it is said: *and there shall be no ruin among them*, if you remove the conjunction, the sentence will not hang; for the sense will be: *if you take the reckoning of the children of Israel in their visitation and each one shall give the redemption of his soul to the Lord, there shall be no ruin among them*. But since the same conjunction is placed everywhere, it makes the expression hang; therefore we thought it should be noted.

**128.** (32:1) *The people rose up against Aaron and they said to him*. It is a customary expression, because a people consists of many. Then it is to be noted that the whole is put for a part. For the Apostle, as was indeed the fact, said that certain of them did this, not the whole people, where he says: *nor let us serve idols as did some of them*[^14].

**129.** (32:1) *Rise up and make us gods, who may go before us*. Were they speaking to one seated? Or rather is the expression to be noted, on account of which it is often said: *rise up, O Lord*[^15], or: *arise, O God, judge the earth*[^16].

**130.** (32:10) *And now let me be and being angry with anger*, like: "by death he shall die"; Scripture loves to speak thus.

**131.** (32:24) *Whoever has gold things, take them off*; it did not say: what gold things. Whence the Latin translators said: *whoever has gold, let him take it off*.

**132.** (32:26) *Who is for the Lord? Let him come to me*.

**133.** (32:31) *They made themselves gods of gold*, of one calf. It put the plural number, then, for the singular; hence too is that: *these are your gods, O Israel, who led you out of the land of Egypt*. These expressions, where the plural is put for the singular, are certainly not found except in that kind of thing in which the thing can also be made or understood of many. For not because that calf was one was it therefore impossible that more should be made, or therefore unlike many idols. By this expression it is said that the robbers reviled the Lord[^17], when Scripture testifies that one did this[^18], but he was not the only robber. Indeed, even when this expression is made through proper names—in what manner it is made in the Scriptures we have not yet discovered—several are understood there, as some have said "Phaedras and Medeas," when there was one Phaedra and one Medea; but they called all those like "Phaedra" and "Medea" Phaedras and Medeas. Thus these expressions are inserted not without cause, nor anyhow and at random as is done faultily by the unskilled, but in a certain manner and by certain rules.

**134.** (33:1) *Go, go up from here, you and your people, whom you led out of the land of Egypt* (plural), not: "whom (singular) you led out"; which is so customary that it is rather rarely said otherwise.

**135.** (33:5) *And the Lord said: 'lay aside the robes of your glories and your ornament, and I will show you (singular) what I am going to do'*. It ended the expression from the plural to the singular number, because they were many and were a people,

just as it had said to them: *you (plural) are a stiff-necked people*; it did not say: "you (singular) are a people," but: *you (plural) are a people*, since *you* is of the plural number, but *people* of the singular number.

**136.** (34:9) *If I have found favor in your sight, let my Lord walk together with us*; as though he speaks of another. These expressions are frequent, but when it is said to God, it is thought to be said by the Father concerning the Son. But since such things were said to Pharaoh and to Joseph and to others in many places, the manner of speech was understood.

**137.** (34:15) *Lest at any time you make a pact with those who sit upon the land*; as if it said: "have their seats," which is: "dwell."

**138.** (34:17) *And you shall not make for yourself cast gods*. It is an expression signifying the whole from a part; for not because it named only cast ones did it permit carved, hammered, and molded ones to be made, or any kind of images or of manufactured gods whatever.

**139.** (34:19) *Everything that opens the womb, the males*. That is: "everything that opens the womb shall be mine, of those that are males."

**140.** (34:20) *The firstborn of the beast of burden you shall redeem with a sheep*. This too is the whole from a part; for it is not the case that, if the beast of burden be not just any animal whose flesh is rejected as unclean, it is therefore not to be redeemed, or to be redeemed with something other than a sheep.

**141.** (34:25) *You shall not slay the blood of my sacrificial victims upon leaven*; it is certainly an expression: "you shall slay the blood" in place of this: "by slaying you shall pour it out."

**142.** (34:25) *And the sacrifice of the feast of Passover shall not sleep until morning*. It said *shall sleep*: "shall remain"; for how shall the flesh of a slaughtered and cooked animal sleep? So when it is said: *why do you sleep, O Lord*[^19], it is said and understood by this kind of expression: "why do you delay," that is, do not take vengeance.

**143.** (34:28) Of Moses' fast of forty days Scripture speaks thus: *he did not eat bread and did not drink water*, the whole from a part, that is, by *bread* signifying all food and by *water* all drink.

**144.** (34:1) *Cut for yourself two tablets like also the first*, "were" is supplied; whence our translators thought this word should be added, since such an ellipsis is unusual in the Latin language.

**145.** (35:4) *And Moses said to all the assembly of the children of Israel, saying*; it would be complete even if it did not have: *saying*.

**146.** (35:21) *And they brought, each one what their heart brought*. It could be said more customarily: "and each one brought what his heart brought."

**147.** (35:21) *And those to whom it seemed good to their soul brought an offering to the Lord*, it did not say: "and as it seemed good to their soul."

**148.** (35:23) *And everyone with whom was found reddened rams' skins*, which would be said customarily: "and all with whom were found reddened rams' skins."

**149.** (35:24) *Everyone bringing an offering, silver and bronze, brought offerings to the Lord*. It would be said more customarily: "everyone brought" rather than "everyone brought" (plural).

**150.** (35:24) *And those with whom was found among them incorruptible wood; among them* was added in the manner of the Scriptures; for without it the sentence would be complete.

**151.** (35:25) *And every woman wise in mind to spin with the hands*, as if she could spin not with the hands; and: *wise to spin* is an elegant and unusual expression. Then: *every woman brought* (plural) is concluded, in the manner of those above, with a plural for a singular. Because "every woman brought" (singular) would be said more customarily.

**152.** (35:26) *And all the women to whom it seemed good in their understanding in wisdom spun goats' hair*. It is to be noted that Scripture often calls skill in these arts "wisdom," that is: σοφίαν.

**153.** (35:28) *And the compounds and the oil of anointing and the compound of incense*; we ought not to understand some *compounds* separately, but, when it had said *and the compounds*, through the copulative conjunction it did not join something else but explained what it had said, so that we might know which compounds: *and the oil*, it says, *of anointing and the compound of incense*. It calls them *compounds* because these were made up of many things.

**154.** (35:29) *And every man and woman whose understanding brought them to enter and do every work whatsoever the Lord commanded to be done by the hand of Moses, the children of Israel brought an offering to the Lord*. Therefore by *every man and woman* the *children of Israel* are understood. The other expressions are similar to those above.

**155.** (35:32-33) *To make gold and silver and bronze*, in place of this: "to make from gold and silver and bronze"; for they did not make gold but from gold. Such too is what it says a little after: *and to work woods*, that is: "from woods."

**156.** (35:35) *To do all the works of the sanctuary, to weave woven and varied work with scarlet and fine linen, to do every architectural work of variety*. See, even in woven things it calls it *architectural work*;

it would be remarkable unless it were for this reason: that they were being made for the tabernacle, which was like a building and was set up as a house. But what it means by *the works of the sanctuary* does not clearly appear—whether of the holy priest, since these things are said of his robe or robes, or of the holy God, in whose worship these were made, or *of the sanctuary*, as the holy place and the Holy of Holies were so called.

**157.** (36:8) *The shoulder-pieces joined together from both its sides*: it did not say "from both their sides," although it had said *those shoulder-pieces* (plural), not "shoulder-piece," as it usually says shoulder-piece; for it called it in the plural *shoulder-pieces*, as it does "robe" and the same "robes."

**158.** (36:37) *And Oholiab the son of Ahisamach of the tribe of Dan, who acted as architect of woven and sewn and many-colored things, to weave with scarlet and fine linen*.

By a novel manner woven things are said to be made by an architect. That too is to be understood as the whole from a part, in that it says: *to weave with scarlet and fine linen*; for from these we understand the rest as well, that is, purple and hyacinth.

**159.** (39:31) *And the children of Israel did, as the Lord commanded Moses, so they did*; it would be complete even if there were not added: *so they did*.

**160.** (40:14) *And Moses did all things that the Lord commanded him, so he did*; similarly, as was said of the children of Israel.
