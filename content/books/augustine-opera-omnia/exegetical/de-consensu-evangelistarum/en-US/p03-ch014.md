# On the words of the voice that came from heaven over the baptized one.

**14. 31.** Then Matthew goes on: *And having been baptized, immediately he came up out of the water. And behold, the heavens were opened to him, and he saw the Spirit of God descending like a dove and coming upon him. And behold, a voice from the heavens, saying: 'This is my beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased*[^1].' This the other two, Mark and Luke, narrate similarly, but about the words of the voice which came from heaven they vary the expression, the meaning nevertheless being preserved[^2]. For what Matthew says was said, *This is my beloved Son*, and what the other two say, *You are my beloved Son*[^3], serve to set forth the same meaning, as was treated above.

For the heavenly voice said one of these, but the evangelist wished to show that what was said served this end, *This is my Son*: that it might be indicated rather to those who were hearing that he was the Son of God; and thus he wished to report it as said, *You are my Son*, as if it were said to them, *This is my Son*. For it was not being indicated to Christ what he knew, but those who were present were hearing, on whose account the voice itself was made. But now, as for the fact that one says, *in whom I am well pleased*, another, *in you I am well pleased*, another, *in you it has pleased me*—if you ask which of these sounded in that voice, any one may be taken, provided you understand that those who did not report the same expression reported the same meaning.

This diversity of expressions is useful for this too, lest what was said in one way be too little understood and interpreted otherwise than the matter stands. For what was said, *in whom I am well pleased*, if anyone should wish to understand it so that God should seem to have been pleased in the Son, he is admonished by what was said, *in you I am well pleased*. If, again, from this one alone anyone should understand that in the Son the Father was pleasing to men, he is admonished by what was said, *in you it has pleased me*.

From which it sufficiently appears that whichever of the evangelists held even the words of the heavenly voice, the others varied the words to set forth the same meaning more familiarly, so that it is understood that this was said by all, as though it were said: "in you I have set my good pleasure," that is, "to carry out through you what has pleased me." But that which some codices have according to Luke—that this sounded in that voice which is written in the *Psalm*, *You are my Son, today I have begotten you*[^4]—although it is reported not to be found in the more ancient Greek codices, yet, if it can be confirmed by any copies worthy of credence, what else is to be understood than that both, in whatever order of words, sounded from heaven?

[^1]: Mt 3:16-17
[^2]: See Mk 1:10-11; Lk 3:22
[^3]: Mk 1:11; Lk 3:22
[^4]: Ps 2:7; Lk 3:22
