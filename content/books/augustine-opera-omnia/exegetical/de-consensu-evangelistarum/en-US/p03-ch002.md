# Why the begetters of Christ are recorded down to Joseph, when Christ was not born of his seed, but of the Virgin Mary.

**1. 2.** The evangelist Matthew began thus: *The book of the generation of Jesus Christ, the son of David, the son of Abraham*[^1]. By this beginning of his he shows sufficiently that he undertook to narrate the generation of Christ according to the flesh. For according to this Christ is the Son of man[^2], which he himself also most frequently calls himself, commending to us what he mercifully deigned to be for us. For that supernal and eternal generation, according to which the only-begotten Son of God is before all creation, because all things were made through him[^3], is so ineffable that what was said by the prophet is understood of it: *His generation, who shall declare it?*[^4] Matthew, then, follows out the human generation of Christ, recording the begetters from Abraham, whom he leads down to Joseph the husband of Mary, of whom Jesus was born. For it was not right that he should think Joseph ought, on this account, to be separated from his marriage with Mary, that Mary bore Christ not from his intercourse, but as a virgin.

For by this example it is grandly intimated to faithful married people that, even with continence preserved by equal consent, a marriage can endure and be called a marriage, with no mingling of the sex of the body, but with the affection of the mind guarded—especially since a son could even be born to them without any carnal embrace, which is to be employed only for the begetting of children. For Joseph was not, on this account, not to be called the father of Christ, that he had not begotten him by lying with Mary, since indeed he would rightly be the father even of one whom, not procreated from his own wife, he had adopted from somewhere.

**1. 3.** Christ was indeed thought to be the son of Joseph also in another way, as though begotten altogether from his flesh; but this was thought by those from whom Mary's virginity lay hidden. For Luke says: *and Jesus himself was beginning to be about thirty years of age, being, as it was thought, the son of Joseph*[^5]. Yet this same Luke did not at all hesitate to call not Mary alone his parent, but both his parents, where he says: *and the boy grew and was strengthened, full of wisdom, and the grace of God was in him. And his parents went every year to Jerusalem on the solemn day of the Pasch*[^6]. But lest anyone here suppose that by "parents" the blood relatives of Mary, together with his mother herself, are rather to be understood, what will he answer to that which this same Luke said above: *and his father and mother were marveling at the things which were said about him*[^7]? Since, therefore, he himself narrates that Christ was born not from the intercourse of Joseph, but from the Virgin Mary, whence does he call him his father, except because we rightly understand him also as the husband of Mary, without the mingling of flesh, by that very joining of marriage, and on this account also the father of Christ much more closely—who was born of his wife—than if he had been adopted to him from somewhere? Whence that is manifest which he says, *as was thought, the son of Joseph*[^8]—that he said it on account of those who thought him born of Joseph as other men are born.
