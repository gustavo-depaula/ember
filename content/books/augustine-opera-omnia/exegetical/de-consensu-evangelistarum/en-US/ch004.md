# Book III

## Prologue.

**1.** 1. Now since the narration of all four is occupied with that place at which they must necessarily walk together to the end and not greatly diverge from one another, even if somewhere perhaps one records something which another omits, it seems to me that we can more readily demonstrate the agreement of all the evangelists if from this point on we weave together everything of all of them and arrange it into a single narrative and form. Thus, then, I think that what we have undertaken can be more conveniently and easily set forth if we approach the narration, recording all things with the attestation of those evangelists who, of all these things, each recorded what he could or what he wished, in such a way, however, that all these things were said by all of them — things which it must be demonstrated are in no point contradictory to one another.

## On the Lord's supper and the betrayer of him made known.

**1.** 2. Let us therefore begin here, according to Matthew: *now as they were eating, Jesus took bread and blessed it and broke it and gave it to his disciples and said: 'Take and eat; this is my body*[^1].' This Mark and Luke too record[^2]. For the fact that Luke mentioned the cup twice — first before he gave the bread, then after he had given the bread — that which he said earlier he anticipated, as is his custom, while that which he set down in its own order he had not recorded earlier; and both joined together make this meaning, which is also theirs. John, however, said nothing in this place about the Lord's body and blood, but plainly attests that the Lord spoke about this much more fully elsewhere[^3]. Now, when he had recorded that the Lord rose from supper and washed the disciples' feet, the reason also being given[^4] why he did this for them — in which the Lord had still obscurely signified, through the testimony of Scripture, that he was being betrayed by one who ate his bread[^5] — he comes to this place which the other three convey alike: *when Jesus had said this,* he says, *he was troubled in spirit and testified and said: 'Truly, truly, I say to you that one of you will betray me.' The disciples therefore looked,* as the same John subjoins, *at one another, uncertain of whom he spoke*[^6]. *And being grieved,* as Matthew and Mark say, *they began to say to him one by one: 'Is it I?'*[^7]. *But he answered and said,* as Matthew continues, *he who dips his hand with me in the dish, he will betray me*. And the same Matthew continues, subjoining thus: *the Son of man indeed goes, as it is written of him; but woe to that man by whom the Son of man is handed over! It were good for that man if he had never been born*[^8]. In these things Mark too agrees, in this order as well. Then Matthew subjoins: *but Judas, who handed him over, answered and said: 'Is it I, Rabbi?' He said to him: 'You have said it*[^9].' Here too it is not stated whether it was he. For it can still be understood as though 'I did not say it'; and this too could have been so said by Judas, and answered by the Lord, that not all noticed.

**1.** 3. Then Matthew continues and inserts the mystery of the body and blood given by the Lord to the disciples, as Mark and Luke also do[^10]. But when he had handed over the cup, he spoke again about his betrayer, which Luke follows out, saying: *but behold, the hand of him who betrays me is with me on the table. And indeed the Son of man goes according to what has been determined; nevertheless, woe to that man by whom he is handed over!*[^11] Here now it is to be understood that there follows what John narrates and these others omitted, just as John omitted certain things which they said. When therefore, after the cup had been handed over, the Lord had said what Luke sets down: *but behold, the hand of him who betrays me is with me on the table,* and so on, there is joined what is according to John: *now there was reclining one of his disciples on the bosom of Jesus, whom Jesus loved; Simon Peter therefore beckoned to him and said to him: 'Who is it of whom he speaks?' And so, when he had leaned back on the breast of Jesus, he said to him: 'Lord, who is it?' Jesus answered: 'It is he to whom I shall give the dipped bread.' And when he had dipped the bread, he gave it to Judas, the son of Simon Iscariot. And after the morsel, then Satan entered into him*[^12].

**1.** 4. Here we must see that John does not seem to be opposed not only to Luke, who had already said that Satan entered into the heart of Judas when he made the pact then with the Jews to betray him for money received[^13], but also to himself, because he had already said above, before he received that morsel: *and when supper was ended, the devil having now put it into the heart of Judas to betray him*[^14]. For how does he enter into the heart, except by putting wicked persuasions into the thoughts of the wicked? But now we ought to understand that Judas was more fully possessed by the devil, just as, on the contrary, in good, those who had already received the Holy Spirit, when after the resurrection he breathed on them, saying: *receive the Holy Spirit*[^15], afterward received him more fully, surely, when he was sent from above on the day of Pentecost[^16]. After the morsel, then, Satan entered into him: *and,* as John himself records continuously, *Jesus said to him: 'What you are doing, do quickly.' But no one of those reclining knew for what purpose he said this to him. For some thought, because Judas had the money box, that Jesus said to him: 'Buy the things we need for the feast,' or that he should give something to the needy. When therefore he had received the morsel, he went out immediately. And it was night. When therefore he had gone out, Jesus says: 'Now the Son of man is glorified, and God is glorified in him. And God will glorify him in himself, and will glorify him at once*[^17].'

## On the foretold denial of Peter.

**2.** 5. *Little children, yet a little while I am with you. You will seek me, and, as I said to the Jews, 'Where I go, you cannot come,' so now I say to you. A new commandment I give you, that you love one another, as I have loved you, that you also love one another. By this all will know that you are my disciples, if you have love for one another. Simon Peter says to him: 'Lord, where are you going?' Jesus answered: 'Where I go, you cannot follow me now; but you will follow afterward.' Peter says to him: 'Why can I not follow you now? I will lay down my life for you.' Jesus answered: 'Will you lay down your life for me? Truly, truly, I say to you, the cock will not crow until you deny me three times*[^18].' This matter of the foretold denial of Peter is recorded not by John alone, from whose Gospel I have just now interposed these things, but also by the other three[^19]. They do not, however, all come to record it from one and the same occasion of the discourse. For Matthew and Mark subjoin it in altogether the same order and in the same place of their narration, both of them, after the Lord had gone out from that house where they had eaten the Passover, while Luke and John subjoin it before he had gone out from there. But we could easily understand either that those two set it down by recapitulation, or that these set it down by anticipation, were it not that we are rather troubled by the fact that they prefix such diverse words, and indeed meanings, of the Lord, moved by which Peter would put forth that presumption of dying either with the Lord or for the Lord — so that they rather compel us to understand that he expressed his presumption three times at different points of Christ's discourse, and that he was answered three times by the Lord, that before the cock crow he would deny him three times.

**2.** 6. For it is not incredible that, at intervals of time somewhat separated, Peter was moved to presume as well as to deny, or that the Lord answered him something three times in like manner, since indeed even continuously, with no other things or words interposed, after the resurrection he asks him three times whether he loves him, and to him, answering this same thing three times, he himself likewise enjoins one and the same command three times about feeding his sheep[^20].

But that this is more credible — that Peter showed his presumption three times and heard from the Lord three times about his denying him three times — is proved from the very words of the evangelists, which record diversely, and as diverse, the things said by the Lord, thus. Behold, let us remember what I have just now interposed from the Gospel of John. He had certainly said this: *little children, yet a little while I am with you. You will seek me, and, as I said to the Jews, 'Where I go, you cannot come,' so now I say to you. A new commandment I give you, that you love one another, as I have loved you, that you also love one another. By this all will know that you are my disciples, if you have love for one another. Simon Peter says to him: 'Lord, where are you going?'*

Surely it is clear here that Peter was moved by those words to say: *Lord, where are you going?* because he had heard him saying: *where I go, you cannot come.* Jesus answered this same Peter: *where I go, you cannot follow me now; but you will follow afterward.* Then he said: *why can I not*, he says, *follow you now? I will lay down my life for you*[^21]. To this presumption the Lord answered with his coming denial. Luke, however, when he had recorded that the Lord said: *Simon, behold, Satan has demanded to have you, that he might sift you like wheat; but I have prayed for you, that your faith may not fail, and you, when once you have turned, strengthen your brothers,* then subjoined that Peter answered: *Lord, I am ready to go with you both to prison and to death.*

*And he said: 'I tell you, Peter, the cock will not crow today until you have three times denied that you know me*[^22].' How different this is from that by which Peter was moved to presume, who does not see? But Matthew says: *and when a hymn had been said,* he says, *they went out to the Mount of Olives. Then Jesus says to them: 'You will all be made to stumble because of me this night. For it is written: I will strike the shepherd, and the sheep of the flock will be scattered*[^23]. *But after I have risen, I will go before you into Galilee*[^24].' Mark says exactly the same[^25]. What do these words or meanings have like those by which, according to John or according to Luke, Peter declared his presumption? And here too it continues thus: *but Peter answered and said to him: 'Even if all are made to stumble because of you, I will never be made to stumble.' Jesus said to him: 'Truly I say to you that this night, before the cock crows, you will deny me three times.' Peter said to him: 'Even if I must die with you, I will not deny you.' And all the disciples said likewise*[^26].

**2.** 7. These things Mark too records in nearly the same words[^27], except that he expressed what the Lord said not in general but more precisely, as to how it would be: *truly I say to you that you today, on this night, before the cock crows twice, will three times deny me*[^28]. Since therefore all say that the Lord foretold that Peter would deny him before the cock crowed, but not all say how often the cock would crow, Mark alone narrated this more expressly. Hence to some he seems not to agree with the rest, because they attend too little, and their attention is especially clouded when they clothe themselves with a hostile mind against the Gospel.

For Peter's whole denial is a threefold denial. For he remained in the same trepidation of mind and purpose of lying until, admonished of what had been foretold to him, he was healed by bitter weeping[^29] and grief of heart. But if this whole denial — that is, the threefold denial — were to begin after the first crowing of the cock, the three would seem to have spoken falsely, of whom Matthew said: *truly I say to you that this night, before the cock crows, you will deny me three times*[^30],

and Luke: *I tell you, Peter, the cock will not crow today until you have three times denied that you know me*[^31]. And John: *truly, truly, I say to you, the cock will not crow until you deny me three times*[^32]. For with different words and order of words they set forth the same meaning, that the Lord said that before the cock crowed Peter would deny him. Again, if he had completed the whole threefold denial before the cock began to crow, Mark would be found to have said in vain, in the person of the Lord: *truly I say to you that you today, on this night, before the cock crows twice, will three times deny me*[^33].

For what was the point of saying *before it crows twice,* when, if that whole threefold denial were completed before the first crowing of the cock, it would be found completed at once both before the second and before the third and before all the crowings of the cock of that night, which would be proved completed before the first? But because that threefold denial was begun before the first crowing of the cock, those three attended not to when Peter would complete it, but to how great it would be and when it would begin — that is, that it would be threefold and before the cock crowed — although in his mind it can be understood as whole even before the first crowing of the cock. For although, in the words of the one denying, it was begun before the first crowing but completed, that whole threefold denial, before the second crowing of the cock, yet in the disposition of his mind and in Peter's fear it was wholly conceived before the first.

Nor does it matter at what intervals of delay it was uttered in three voicings, since his heart possessed it whole even before the first crowing of the cock, imbibed with so great a dread, namely, that he could deny the Lord not only once but again and a third time when questioned — so that, to those attending more rightly and carefully, it is as when one has already committed adultery with a woman in his heart who has looked at her to lust after her[^34].

Thus, whenever Peter uttered in words the fear which he had conceived in his mind so vehemently that it could endure even to the third denial of the Lord, the whole threefold denial is to be reckoned to that time when a fear sufficient for a threefold denial seized him. From which, even if those words of denial, his breast struck by questions, were to begin to burst forth after the first crowing of the cock, not even so would it be said absurdly and falsely that he denied three times before the cock crowed, since before the cock crowed so great a fear had besieged his mind as could carry it through to the third denial. Much less, therefore, ought it to disturb anyone that the threefold denial, begun even in the three voicings of the one denying before the cock crowed, was yet not completed before the first crowing of the cock.

It is as if it were said to someone: 'This night, before the cock crows, you will write me a letter in which you will revile me three times'; surely, if he began to write it before any crowing of the cock and finished it after the first crowing, it would not for that reason have to be said that the prediction was false. Mark, therefore, spoke more plainly of the intervals of the voicings themselves, who said in the person of the Lord: *before the cock crows twice, you will three times deny me*. That it happened so will appear when we come to that same place of the Gospel narration, so that there too it may be shown that the evangelists agree with one another.

**2.** 8. But if the very words altogether be sought which the Lord said to Peter, they cannot be found, and they are sought in vain, since his meaning, for the sake of knowing which the words are produced, can be perfectly clear even in the differing words of the evangelists.

Whether, then, Peter, moved at different points of the Lord's discourses, declared his presumption three times one by one, and three times the Lord foretold to him his denial — as is the more probable inquiry — or whether by some order of narration the records of all the evangelists can be brought into one, by which it is shown that the Lord once foretold to the presuming Peter that he would deny him, no contradiction of the evangelists can be detected here, as there is none.

## On the things that were said by the Lord until he went out from the house where they had supped.

**3.** 9. Now, then, so far as we can, let us follow the order itself drawn from all of them. Since therefore, according to John, this had been foretold to Peter, the same John continues and weaves together the discourse of the Lord saying: *let not your heart be troubled. You believe in God; believe also in me. In my Father's house are many mansions*[^35],

and he narrates at length the rest of his discourse, splendid and most sublime[^36], until continuously he comes to that place where the Lord says: *righteous Father, the world has not known you, but I have known you, and these have known that you sent me; and I have made known to them your name, and will make it known, that the love with which you loved me may be in them, and I in them*[^37].

But when a *dispute had arisen among them as to which of them seemed to be the greater,* as Luke records, *he said to them: 'The kings of the gentiles lord it over them, and those who have authority over them are called benefactors. But you are not so; rather, let him who is greater among you become as the younger, and the leader as one who serves. For who is greater, he who reclines or he who serves? Is it not he who reclines? But I am in your midst as one who serves. And you are those who have continued with me in my trials. And I appoint for you, as my Father appointed for me, a kingdom, that you may eat and drink at my table in the kingdom, and sit on thrones judging the twelve tribes of Israel.'*

And the Lord said to Simon, as Luke himself subjoins: *'Behold, Satan has demanded to have you, that he might sift you like wheat; but I have prayed for you, that your faith may not fail, and you, when once you have turned, strengthen your brothers.' He said to him: 'Lord, I am ready to go with you both to prison and to death.' And he said: 'I tell you, Peter, the cock will not crow today until you have three times denied that you know me.' And he said to them: 'When I sent you without purse and bag and sandals, did you lack anything?' And they said: 'Nothing.' He said therefore to them: 'But now let him who has a purse take it, likewise also a bag, and let him who has none sell his tunic and buy a sword. For I tell you that this too which is written must yet be fulfilled in me: And he was reckoned among the unrighteous*[^38]. *For indeed the things that concern me have an end.' And they said: 'Lord, behold, here are two swords.' And he said to them: 'It is enough*[^39].'

*And when a hymn had been said,* as Matthew and Mark record, *they went out to the Mount of Olives. Then Jesus says to them: 'You will all be made to stumble because of me this night. For it is written: I will strike the shepherd, and the sheep of the flock will be scattered. But after I have risen, I will go before you into Galilee.'*

*But Peter answered and said to him: 'Even if all are made to stumble because of you, I will never be made to stumble.' Jesus said to him: 'Truly I say to you that this night, before the cock crows, you will deny me three times.' Peter said to him: 'Even if I must die with you, I will not deny you.' And all the disciples said likewise*[^40]. These things we have inserted according to Matthew, but Mark's are nearly the very same and the same number of words, except that there differs what we have already untangled above about the crowing of the cock[^41].

## On the things that were done in that estate or garden to which they came from that house after supper.

**4.** 10. Matthew therefore weaves the narration together and says: *then Jesus came with them to an estate which is called Gethsemane*[^42]. This Mark too says, and so does Luke, without expressly naming the estate[^43], when he says: *and going out he went, according to his custom, to the Mount of Olives. And the disciples too followed him. And when he had come to the place, he said to them: 'Pray that you do not enter into temptation*[^44].'

This is the place whose name they said was Gethsemane. There we understand was the garden which John records, narrating thus: *when Jesus had said these things, he went out with his disciples across the brook Kidron, where there was a garden, into which he entered, and his disciples*[^45]. Then, according to Matthew: *he said to the disciples: 'Sit here, while I go over there and pray.' And taking Peter and the two sons of Zebedee, he began to be sorrowful and troubled. Then he said to them: 'My soul is sorrowful even to death; remain here and watch with me.' And going forward a little, he fell on his face, praying and saying: 'My Father, if it is possible, let this cup pass from me; nevertheless, not as I will, but as you will.'*

*And he came to the disciples and found them sleeping, and he says to Peter: 'So, could you not watch one hour with me? Watch and pray, that you do not enter into temptation. The spirit indeed is willing, but the flesh is weak.' Again, a second time, he went away and prayed, saying: 'My Father, if this cup cannot pass unless I drink it, your will be done.' And he came again and found them sleeping, for their eyes were heavy. And leaving them, he again went away and prayed a third time, saying the same words. Then he came to his disciples and says to them: 'Sleep now and take your rest; behold, the hour has drawn near, and the Son of man is handed over into the hands of sinners. Rise, let us go; behold, he who will hand me over has drawn near*[^46].'

**4.** 11. These things Mark too weaves together in exactly that manner and order, constraining certain sentences somewhat more briefly and opening up something more. For this discourse according to Matthew seems as it were contradictory to itself, in that after the third prayer he comes to his disciples and says to them: *sleep now and take your rest; behold, the hour has drawn near, and the Son of man is handed over into the hands of sinners. Rise, let us go; behold, he who will hand me over has drawn near.*

For how, above, *sleep now and take your rest,* when he joins to it: *behold, the hour has drawn near,* and therefore says: *rise, let us go*? Moved by this apparent contradiction, those who read try to pronounce what was said, *sleep now and take your rest,* as though it were said by one reproaching, not by one permitting. Which would rightly be done, if it were necessary. But since Mark so recorded this that, when he had said: *sleep now and take your rest,* he added *it is enough,* and then brought in *the hour has come; behold, the Son of man is handed over*[^47], it is surely understood that after what was said to them: *sleep now and take your rest,* the Lord was silent for a while, so that what he had permitted might come to pass, and then brought in: *behold, the hour has drawn near.* Therefore after those words, according to Mark, there is placed *it is enough,* that is, 'what you have rested' is now enough. But because the interposition itself of the Lord's silence is not recorded, on that account it constrains the understanding, so that in those words another way of pronouncing them is sought.

**4.** 12. Luke, however, omitted how often he prayed; he did indeed say, what those others passed over, both that as he prayed he was strengthened by an angel, and that, as he prayed more at length, his sweat became bloody and drops ran down to the ground[^48].

When therefore he says: *and when he had risen from prayer and had come to his disciples*[^49], he did not express after which prayer; yet in no respect is he opposed to those two. John, on the other hand, after he says he entered the garden with his disciples, does not record what he did there until his betrayer came with the Jews to seize him[^50].

**4.** 13. These three, then, narrated the same matter just as even one man could three times, with some variety yet with no contradiction. Luke, however, opened up more plainly how far he had gone forward from them, that is, been drawn away, in order to pray, saying: *about a stone's throw*[^51]. Moreover, Mark first, in his own words, narrated that the Lord asked the same thing: *that, if it could be done, that hour might pass from him*[^52], that is, the hour of the passion, which he soon signified by the name of the *cup.*

Then he expressed the very words of the Lord thus: *Abba, Father, all things are possible to you; take this cup from me*[^53]. To which words, if you join what those two said, and what Mark himself also set down above in his own person, the meaning is made clear thus: *Father, if it can be done* — for *all things are possible to you* — *take this cup from me,* lest anyone suppose that he diminished the Father's power when he said: *if it can be done;* for he did not say 'if you can do it,' but: *if it can be done;* and that can be done which he wills. So, then, it was said: *if it can be done,* as if it were said: 'if you will.'

For Mark made clear in what sense *if it can be done* is to be taken, when he said: *all things are possible to you.* And the fact that they recorded that he said: *yet not what I will, but what you will* (which is just as much as if it were also said thus: *nevertheless, not my will, but yours be done*), sufficiently shows that *if it can be done* was said not from possibility but from the will of the Father, especially since Luke intimated this very thing more plainly; for he did not say: *if it can be done,* but: *if you will*[^54]. To which more open meaning what Mark set down is more openly joined, so that it is said thus: *if you will* — for *all things are possible to you* — *take from me this cup*.

**4.** 14. But that Mark himself records that he said not only *Father* but *Abba, Father,* this is *Abba* in Hebrew, which in Latin is *Father.* And perhaps the Lord said both on account of some mystery, wishing to show that he took on that sorrow in the person of his body, that is, the Church, to which he was made the cornerstone, as it came to him partly from the Hebrews, to whom belongs what he says *Abba,* partly from the gentiles, to whom belongs what he says *Father*[^55].

The apostle Paul too, not passing over this mystery, says: *in whom we cry, Abba, Father*[^56], and again he says: *God sent his Spirit into our hearts, crying, Abba, Father*[^57]. For it was fitting that the good Teacher and true Savior, having compassion on the weaker, should show in himself that his martyrs ought not to despair if perchance some sorrow from human frailty should creep into their hearts at the time of the passion, since they would overcome it by setting the will of God before their own will, because he knows what is expedient for those for whom he provides.

About this whole matter there is now no time to discourse more fully; for the question now is of the agreement of the evangelists, in whose diversity of words we learn, to our profit, that nothing else is to be sought in words, for the hearing of the truth, than the meaning of the speaker. For *Father* is the same as *Abba, Father,* but for intimating the mystery *Abba, Father* is plainer, while for signifying the unity *Father* suffices.

And the Lord is indeed to be believed to have said *Abba, Father;* but yet the meaning would not shine forth unless, with the others saying 'Father,' it were shown that those two churches, from the Jews and from the Greeks, are so two that they are also one. From that understanding, therefore, was it said: *Abba, Father,* by which the same Lord says elsewhere: *I have other sheep that are not of this fold,*

meaning surely the gentiles, since he had sheep also among the people of Israel. But because he went on to add: *I must bring them also, that there may be one flock and one shepherd*[^58], as much as *Abba, Father* means for the Israelites and the gentiles, so much does *Father* alone mean for the one flock.

## On the things that all record as done and said at his arrest.

**5.** 15. *While he was therefore still speaking,* as Matthew and Mark say, *behold, Judas, one of the twelve, came, and with him a great crowd with swords and clubs, from the chief priests and the elders of the people. Now he who handed him over had given them a sign, saying: 'Whomever I kiss, it is he; seize him.' And immediately, going up to Jesus, he said: 'Hail, Rabbi,' and kissed him*[^59]. And he said to him first what Luke says: *Judas, do you hand over the Son of man with a kiss?*[^60]

Then what Matthew says: *friend, for what have you come?*[^61] Then he said what John records: *whom do you seek? They answered him: 'Jesus of Nazareth.' Jesus says to them: 'I am he.' Now Judas, who handed him over, was standing with them. When therefore he said to them: 'I am he,' they drew back and fell to the ground. Again, therefore, he questioned them: 'Whom do you seek?' And they said: 'Jesus of Nazareth.' Jesus answered: 'I told you that I am he. If therefore you seek me, let these go their way.' That the word might be fulfilled which he had said: 'Of those whom you have given me, I have lost none*[^62].'

**5.** 16. *But seeing,* as Luke says, *those who were around him what was about to happen, they said to him: 'Lord, shall we strike with the sword?' And one of them struck,* which all four say, *the servant of the high priest and cut off his ear.* As Luke and John say, *the right ear*[^63]. He who struck, according to John, was Peter; and the one whom he struck was called Malchus[^64]. Then what Luke says: *Jesus answered and said: 'Permit even this far*[^65],' and he added what Matthew records: *put your sword back into its place. For all who take the sword will perish by the sword. Or do you think that I cannot ask my Father, and he will at once furnish me more than twelve thousand legions of angels? How then would the Scriptures be fulfilled, that it must so come to pass?*[^66] To these words can be joined what John records that he said in that place: *the cup which the Father has given me, shall I not drink it?*[^67] Then, as Luke says, he touched the ear of him who had been struck and healed him[^68].

**5.** 17. Nor let it be a stumbling block, as though it were contradictory, that Luke said the Lord answered the disciples asking whether they should strike with the sword: *permit even this far,* as though it were said after that striking, in such a way that what was done this far pleased him, but he did not wish more to be done — whereas in the words which Matthew set down it is rather understood that the whole deed by which Peter used the sword displeased the Lord.

For this is truer: that when they had asked him, saying: *Lord, shall we strike with the sword?* he then answered: *permit even this far,* that is, 'let not what is about to happen disturb you; they must be allowed to proceed this far, that is, to seize me and that the things written about me may be fulfilled.' But amid the delays of the words of those questioning the Lord and of him answering, Peter, in his eagerness to defend and in his greater agitation for the Lord, struck; but things that could happen at once could not also be said at once. For he would not say: *but Jesus answered,* unless he were answering their question, not Peter's deed.

For what he judged about Peter's deed, Matthew alone tells. Where also Matthew did not say: 'Jesus answered Peter: 'Put back your sword',' but said: *then Jesus said to him: 'Put back your sword,'* which the Lord clearly said after the deed. But what Luke set down: *but Jesus answered and said: 'Permit even this far*[^69],' is to be taken as answered to those who had asked; but because, as we said, between the words of those questioning and of the Lord answering the striking was done with one blow, he judged that it should be narrated in this order, so that he inserted it even between the words of the question and of the answer. This, then, is not contradictory to what Matthew said: *for all who take the sword will perish by the sword*[^70], that is, who will have used the sword. But it could seem contradictory if the Lord, by that answer, were shown to have approved a voluntary use of the sword, even up to one wound, and that not a fatal one. Although what was said to Peter too can be fittingly understood as a whole, so that both what Luke and what Matthew related are connected, as I recorded above: *permit even this far;* and: *put your sword back into its place. For all who take the sword will perish by the sword,* and so on. But how *permit even this far* is to be understood, I have already explained; and if it can be better understood otherwise, let it be so, provided only that the truth of the evangelists stands firm.

**5.** 18. Afterward Matthew continues and records that in that hour he said to the crowds: *have you come out as against a robber, with swords and clubs to seize me? Daily I sat among you teaching in the temple, and you did not seize me*[^71]. Then he added also the words which Luke sets down: *but this is your hour and the power of darkness*[^72]. *But all this happened,* as Matthew says, *that the Scriptures of the prophets might be fulfilled. Then all the disciples left him and fled*[^73], as Mark too says. *But a certain young man followed him, clothed in a linen cloth,* as the same Mark records, *and when they had seized him, leaving the linen cloth, he fled from them naked*[^74].

## On the things that were done while the Lord was being led to the house of the high priest.

**6.** 19. *But they, seizing Jesus, led him to Caiaphas the high priest, where the scribes and elders had gathered*[^75], as Matthew says. But first he was led to Annas, the father-in-law of Caiaphas, as John says[^76]. Mark and Luke, however, do not give the name of the high priest[^77]. He was led bound, while there were present in that crowd the tribune and the cohort and the officers of the Jews, as John records. *But Peter followed him at a distance, even into the courtyard of the high priest. And going inside, he sat with the officers, to see the end*[^78], as Matthew says. *And he was warming himself at the fire*[^79], as Mark says in that place of the narration. This Luke too records, that *Peter followed at a distance. And when a fire had been kindled in the middle of the courtyard and they were sitting around it, Peter was in the midst of them*[^80]. And John says that *Simon Peter and another disciple followed. Now that other disciple was known to the high priest, and he entered with Jesus into the courtyard of the high priest,* as John says. *But Peter stood at the door outside,* according to the same John. *The other disciple, therefore, who was known to the high priest, went out and spoke to the doorkeeper and brought Peter in*[^81], as the same John says. For so it came about that Peter too was inside, in the courtyard, as the others also say.

**6.** 20. *But the chief priests and the whole council,* as Matthew says, *were seeking false testimony against Jesus, that they might put him to death, and they found none, though many false witnesses came forward*[^82]. *For their testimonies did not agree*[^83], as Mark says, recording the same place. *But at last two false witnesses came,* as Matthew says, *and said: 'This man said: I can destroy the temple of God and rebuild it in three days*[^84].' Mark too records that others said: *we heard him saying: 'I will destroy this temple made with hands, and in three days build another not made with hands.' And* for this reason *their testimony did not agree*[^85], as Mark says in the same place. *And the high priest, rising, said to him: 'Do you answer nothing to the things these testify against you?' But Jesus was silent. And the high priest said to him: 'I adjure you by the living God to tell us whether you are the Christ, the Son of God.' Jesus says to him: 'You have said it*[^86],' so Matthew. But Mark says the same things in other words[^87], except that he is silent that the high priest adjured him, but shows that what Jesus says to him: *you have said it,* means as much as if he said: *I am he.* For it follows, as the same Mark says: *but Jesus said to him: 'I am he. And you will see the Son of man sitting at the right hand of Power and coming with the clouds of heaven*[^88].' This Matthew too says[^89], but he does not say that Jesus answered: '*I am he.*' *Then the high priest tore his garments, saying: 'He has blasphemed; what further need have we of witnesses?'* Which Matthew records and continues: *'Behold, now you have heard the blasphemy. What do you think?' And they answered and said: 'He deserves death.'* This Mark too attests. And Matthew continues: *then they spat in his face and struck him with their fists. And others struck him on the face with the palms of their hands, saying: 'Prophesy to us, O Christ, who is it that struck you?*[^90].' This Mark too says; he also records that they covered his face[^91]. Of these things Luke too attests[^92].

**6.** 21. These things the Lord is understood to have suffered until morning in the house of the high priest, to which he was first led, where Peter too was tested. But concerning Peter's testing, which took place amid these insults to the Lord, not all narrate in the same order; for Matthew and Mark first record the insults themselves, then the testing of Peter[^93]; Luke, however, sets forth first the testing of Peter, then at last these insults to the Lord[^94]; while John begins to tell the testing of Peter and interposes certain things about the insults to the Lord, and adds that from there he was sent to Caiaphas the high priest, and then recapitulates, to set forth the testing of Peter which he had begun, in the house to which he was first led, and returns to the order, where he shows how the Lord was led to Caiaphas[^95].

**6.** 22. Thus, then, Matthew continues: *but Peter was sitting outside in the courtyard, and a servant girl came to him, saying: 'You too were with Jesus the Galilean.' But he denied it before them all, saying: 'I do not know what you are saying.' And as he was going out the gate, another servant girl saw him and said to those who were there: 'This man too was with Jesus the Nazarene.' And again he denied it with an oath: 'I do not know the man.' And after a little while those standing by came up and said to Peter: 'Truly you too are one of them; for your speech too makes you known.' Then he began to curse and to swear that he did not know the man. And immediately the cock crowed*[^96]. So Matthew says. But it is understood that, after he had gone outside, when he had now denied once, the cock crowed first, which Matthew passes over and Mark says[^97].

**6.** 23. But he did not deny again outside, before the gate, but when he had returned to the fire; and when he had returned, there was no need to record. Mark, then, narrates it thus: *and he went outside before the courtyard, and the cock crowed. But again, when the servant girl saw him, she began to say to those standing by: 'This man is one of them.' But he again denied it*[^98].

But this servant girl is not the same, but another, as Matthew says. Surely this too is understood, that in the second denial he was accosted by two, namely by the servant girl whom Matthew and Mark record, and by another whom Luke records. For Luke narrates this thus: *but Peter followed at a distance. And when a fire had been kindled in the middle of the courtyard and they were sitting around it, Peter was in the midst of them. And when a certain servant girl saw him sitting by the light and had looked at him, she said: 'This man too was with him.' But he denied him, saying: 'Woman, I do not know him.' And after a little while another, seeing him, said: 'You too are one of them*[^99].'

This, then, which Luke says: *and after a little while,* Peter had already gone out the gate and the first cock had crowed, and he had already returned, so that, as John says, standing at the fire he denied again. For John, in Peter's first denial, not only is silent about the first crowing of the cock, like the rest except Mark, but also does not record that the servant girl recognized him sitting at the fire; for he says only this: *the servant girl who kept the door therefore says to Peter: 'Are you too one of this man's disciples?' He says: 'I am not*[^100].'

Then he interposes the things that were done with Jesus in that same house, which he thought should be recorded. Narrating thus: *now the servants and officers were standing at the coals, because it was cold, and warming themselves; and Peter too was with them, standing and warming himself*[^101].

Here, then, it is understood that Peter had already gone outside and returned; for at first he was sitting at the fire, but afterward, on returning, he had begun to stand.

But perhaps someone says: he had not yet gone out, but had risen to go out. This can be said by one who thinks that, outside before the gate, when questioned a second time, he denied. Let us therefore see what John says next: *the high priest therefore,* he says, *questioned Jesus about his disciples and about his teaching. Jesus answered him: 'I have spoken openly to the world; I have always taught in the synagogue and in the temple, where all the Jews come together, and in secret I have said nothing. Why do you question me? Question those who have heard what I spoke to them. Behold, these know what I said.' But when he had said these things, one of the officers standing by gave Jesus a blow, saying: 'Is that how you answer the high priest?' Jesus answered him: 'If I have spoken evil, bear witness of the evil; but if well, why do you strike me?' And Annas sent him bound to Caiaphas the high priest*[^102].

Here surely it is shown that Annas was a high priest; for he had not yet been sent to Caiaphas when it was already said to him: *is that how you answer the high priest?* And these two, Annas and Caiaphas, as high priests, Luke too records at the beginning of his Gospel[^103]. When these things have been said, John returns to what he had begun about Peter's denial, that is, to that same house where the things he narrated were done, and from which Jesus was sent to Caiaphas, to whom he was being led from the beginning, as Matthew said. Now John recorded these things which he interposed, recapitulating about Peter, and returning to that narration he says thus, so as to complete the threefold denial: *now Simon Peter was standing and warming himself. They said therefore to him: 'Are you too one of his disciples?' He denied it and said: 'I am not*[^104].'

In this place, then, we find that Peter denied the second time not before the gate but standing at the fire, which could not happen unless he had already returned after he had gone outside. For the other servant girl did not see him outside when he had already gone out, but saw him as he was going out, that is, when he was rising to go out, she noticed him and said to those who were there, that is, who were together at the fire inside in the courtyard: *and this man was with Jesus the Nazarene*[^105]. But he who had gone outside, hearing this, returned and swore to them, as they pressed against him, that he did not know the man[^106].

For Mark too says thus about the same servant girl: *and she began to say to those standing by: 'This man is one of them*[^107].' For she was speaking not to him, but to those who had remained there when he went out, yet in such a way that he could hear, from which, on returning and again standing at the fire, he resisted by denying their words. Then in what John says: *they said: 'Are you too one of his disciples?'*[^108] — which we understand was said to him as he returned and stood — this too is confirmed, that not only that other servant girl, whom Matthew and Mark record in this second denial, but also another, whom Luke records, dealt thus with Peter,

whence John says: *they said therefore to him.* Wherefore, whether it was that, after the servant girl said to those who were with her in the courtyard when he went out, *this man is one of them,* he, on hearing this, returned, so as to clear himself, as it were, by denying — or, what is more credible, he did not hear what was said about him as he was going outside, and after he returned the servant girl and that other man whom Luke records said to him: *are you too one of his disciples?* And he said: *I am not,*

with that man of whom Luke speaks more insistently pressing and saying: *you too are one of them,* to whom Peter said: *O man, I am not*[^109] — yet it is clearly gathered, when all the testimonies of the evangelists about this matter are compared, that Peter denied the second time not before the gate but inside in the courtyard at the fire, and that Matthew and Mark, who recorded that he had gone outside, for the sake of brevity were silent about his return.

**6.** 25. Now let us examine their agreement about the third denial, which we recall Matthew alone has already set forth. Mark therefore continues and says: *And after a little while, those standing by said to Peter: 'Truly you are one of them, for you too are a Galilean.' But he began to call down curses and to swear: 'I do not know this man of whom you speak.' And immediately the cock crowed a second time*[^110]. Luke, however, following, narrates this same thing thus: *and after an interval of about one hour, a certain other man affirmed, saying: 'Truly this man too was with him, for he too is a Galilean.' And Peter said: 'Man, I do not know what you are saying.' And immediately, while he was still speaking, the cock crowed*[^111].

John, following, sets forth Peter's third denial thus: *one of the servants of the high priest, a kinsman of him whose ear Peter had cut off, says: 'Did I not see you in the garden with him?' Again, therefore, Peter denied it, and immediately the cock crowed*[^112]. What, then, Matthew and Mark say, *after a little while,* how much time this was, Luke makes clear by saying: *and after an interval of about one hour.* But about this interval John is silent. Likewise, the fact that Matthew and Mark express in the plural, not the singular, those who dealt with Peter, while Luke says one, and John too one, namely a kinsman of him whose ear Peter had cut off,

it is easy to understand either that Matthew and Mark used the plural number for the singular by a customary expression, or that one especially, as knowing it and as having seen him, affirmed it, while the rest, following his confidence, pressed Peter at the same time, whence two evangelists set down the plural number by a shorthand, but the other two wished to signify only the one who was foremost in this.

Now, what Matthew asserts was said to Peter himself: *truly you too are one of them, and your speech makes you known*[^113], just as John asserts was said to the same Peter: *did I not see you in the garden with him?*[^114]

while Mark says that they spoke among themselves about Peter: *truly he is one of them, for he too is a Galilean*[^115], just as Luke too says, not to Peter but about Peter: *a certain other man affirmed, saying: 'Truly this man too was with him, for he too is a Galilean*[^116]' —

either we understand that those who say Peter was accosted kept to the meaning, for what was said about him in his presence meant as much as if it were said to him, or that it was done in both ways, and that some recorded the one manner, others the other. But we understand the crowing of the cock after the third denial to be the second, as Mark expressed it.

**6.** 26. Matthew therefore continues, saying thus: *and Peter remembered the word of Jesus, which he had said: 'Before the cock crows, you will deny me three times.' And going outside, he wept bitterly*[^117]. Mark, however, says thus: *Peter remembered the word that Jesus had said to him: 'Before the cock crows twice, you will deny me three times.' And he began to weep*[^118]. But Luke says thus: *and the Lord turned and looked at Peter. And Peter remembered the word of the Lord, as he had said: 'Before the cock crows, you will deny me three times.' And going outside, Peter wept bitterly*[^119].

John is silent about Peter's remembering and weeping[^120]. Surely, in what Luke says, that *the Lord turned and looked at Peter,* how it is to be taken must be considered more carefully. For although even inner courtyards are spoken of, yet Peter was in the outer courtyard among the servants, who were warming themselves at the fire together. But it is not credible that the Lord was heard by the Jews there, so that that looking was bodily.

For when Matthew had said: *then they spat in his face and struck him with their fists, and others struck him on the face with the palms of their hands, saying: 'Prophesy to us, O Christ, who is it that struck you?'* he continued, saying: *but Peter was sitting outside in the courtyard*[^121], which he would not say unless those things with the Lord were being done inside. And as far as is gathered in Mark's narration, they were being done not only in the inner parts but also in the upper parts of the house.

For after Mark narrated such things, he continued and said: *and as Peter was below in the courtyard*[^122]. As, therefore, by what Matthew said: *but Peter was sitting outside in the courtyard*[^123], he shows that those things were being done inside, so by what Mark said: *and as Peter was below in the courtyard,* he shows that what he had said was done not only in the inner parts but also in the upper parts. How, then, did the Lord look at Peter with a bodily face? Wherefore it seems to me that that looking was done divinely, so that it came into his mind how often he had now denied and what the Lord had foretold to him, and thus, with the Lord mercifully looking on, he repented and wept to his profit.

As we say daily, 'Lord, look upon me,' and 'The Lord looked upon him' of one who has been freed from some danger or trouble by the divine mercy, and as it is said: *look upon me and hear me*[^124], and: *turn, O Lord, and deliver my soul*[^125], so I judge it was said: *the Lord turned and looked at Peter, and Peter remembered the word of the Lord*[^126]. Indeed, since in their narrations they are more often wont to set down *Jesus* than *Lord,* here Luke set down 'Lord,' saying: *the Lord turned and looked at Peter, and Peter remembered the word of the Lord,* while Matthew and Mark, who were silent about that looking, said that he remembered not *the word of the Lord* but *the word of Jesus,* so that from this too we may understand that that looking was done by Jesus not with human eyes but divinely.

## On the things that were done in the morning, before he was handed over to Pilate.

**7.** 27. Matthew therefore continues and says: *but when morning had come, all the chief priests and the elders of the people took counsel against Jesus, that they might put him to death. And they led him bound and handed him over to Pontius Pilate the governor*[^127]. Mark likewise: *and immediately in the morning,* he says, *the chief priests, taking counsel with the elders and scribes and the whole council, bound Jesus and led him away and handed him over to Pilate*[^128]. Luke, however, after he had finished narrating Peter's denial, recapitulated the things that were done with the Lord, now, as it appears, around morning, and so wove the narration together: *and the men who held him mocked him, beating him, and they blindfolded him and struck his face and questioned him, saying: 'Prophesy to us, who is it that struck you?' and, blaspheming, they said many other things against him. And when day came, the elders of the people and the chief priests and the scribes gathered together, and they led him into their council, saying: 'If you are the Christ, tell us.' And he said to them: 'If I tell you, you will not believe me; and if I also question you, you will not answer me, nor release me. But from now on the Son of man will be sitting at the right hand of the power of God.' And they all said: 'Are you then the Son of God?' And he said: 'You say that I am.' And they said: 'What further need have we of testimony? For we ourselves have heard it from his own mouth.' And the whole multitude of them rose and led him to Pilate*[^129].

All these things Luke narrated, where it is understood — what Matthew and Mark too narrated[^130] — that the Lord was questioned whether he was the Son of God, and that he said: *I tell you, from now on you will see the Son of man sitting at the right hand of Power and coming on the clouds of heaven*[^131], done now as day was breaking, when Luke says: *and when day came*[^132];

and so he narrates similar things, even if he himself records something which they passed over. But we understand that during the night the dealing with the Lord through false witnesses, which Matthew and Mark briefly recorded[^133], was done, which Luke passed over, since he related the things that were done around morning. For they too, that is, Matthew and Mark, wove the narration together in the things that were done with the Lord up to morning[^134], but afterward returned to narrate Peter's denial, and when this was ended returned to morning, so as to weave the rest together from there, until they brought to a close the things that had been done with the Lord, but had not yet recorded what was done in the morning[^135]. John too, when he had recorded the things that were done, in such part as seemed good, and the whole of Peter's denial, says: *they led Jesus therefore from Caiaphas to the praetorium. And it was morning*[^136].

Here we understand either that there was some cause which compelled Caiaphas to be in the praetorium and not to be present, while the other chief priests were holding their examination of the Lord, or that the praetorium was in his house. Yet he was being led from the beginning to the very one to whom at the last he was brought. But because they were now bringing him as one already, as it were, convicted, and it had already seemed good to Caiaphas that Jesus should die, no delay was interposed but that he was handed over to Pilate to be put to death. The things, then, that were done with the Lord through Pilate, Matthew narrates thus.

**7.** 28. And first he ran on from there to record the end of Judas the betrayer, which he alone narrated, saying thus: *then Judas, who had handed him over, seeing that he was condemned, was moved by repentance and brought back the thirty pieces of silver to the chief priests and elders, saying: 'I have sinned in handing over innocent blood.' But they said: 'What is that to us? See to it yourself.' And throwing down the pieces of silver in the temple, he withdrew, and going away he hanged himself with a noose. But the chief priests, taking the pieces of silver, said: 'It is not lawful to put them into the treasury, since it is the price of blood.' And taking counsel, they bought with them the potter's field, for the burial of strangers. For this reason that field has been called Akeldama, the Field of Blood, to this present day. Then was fulfilled what was spoken through Jeremiah the prophet, saying: 'And they took the thirty pieces of silver, the price of him who was valued, whom the sons of Israel valued, and gave them for the potter's field, as the Lord appointed for me*[^137].'

**7.** 29. But if anyone is troubled that this testimony is not found in the writing of the prophet Jeremiah, and therefore thinks that something must be detracted from the evangelist's trustworthiness, let him first know that not all the manuscripts of the Gospels have 'what was spoken through Jeremiah,' but only 'through the prophet.' We could therefore say that those manuscripts are rather to be believed which do not have the name of Jeremiah. For this was spoken through a prophet, but Zechariah; whence the manuscripts which have the name of Jeremiah are thought to be faulty, because they ought to have had either 'Zechariah' or no name, as some do, yet 'through the prophet' saying it, who is surely understood to be Zechariah. But let whoever pleases use that defense; as for me, the reason why it does not please me is this: that both more manuscripts have the name of Jeremiah, and those who have considered the Gospel more carefully in the Greek copies report that they have found it so in the older Greek ones; and there was no cause why this name should be added, so that a fault should arise; but there was surely a cause why it should be removed from some manuscripts, namely that bold inexperience should do this, being troubled by the question that this testimony was not found in Jeremiah.

**7.** 30. What, then, is to be understood, except that this was done by the more hidden counsel of God's providence, by which the minds of the evangelists were governed? For it could have happened that, in the mind of Matthew as he was writing the Gospel, Jeremiah occurred for Zechariah, as is wont to happen, which, however, he would without any doubt have corrected, at least when admonished by others who, while he was still living in the flesh, could read this, had he not reflected that it was not in vain that to his recollection, which was governed by the Holy Spirit, the name of one prophet occurred for another, except that the Lord so determined that it should be written.

But why the Lord so determined, that first most useful reason ought most easily to be considered: that by this too it was conveyed that all the holy prophets, speaking by one Spirit, stand in marvelous agreement among themselves, so that this is much more than if all the sayings of all the prophets were spoken by the one mouth of one man, and that therefore whatever the Holy Spirit said through them ought to be received without doubt, and the several sayings are of them all, and all the sayings are of each one.

Since therefore both the things spoken through Jeremiah are as much Zechariah's as Jeremiah's, and the things spoken through Zechariah are as much Jeremiah's as Zechariah's, what need was there for Matthew to correct it, when, reading over what he had written, he found one name occurring to him for another, and not rather, following the authority of the Holy Spirit, by whom he surely felt his mind to be governed more than we do, to leave this written as the Lord, by admonishing him, had determined for him — to instruct us that there is so great a concord of their words among the prophets that we should not absurdly, but most fittingly, ascribe to Jeremiah too what we find spoken through Zechariah?

For if today someone, wishing to report the words of one man, were to say the name of another by whom they were not spoken, who is nevertheless a very close friend and most intimately joined in familiarity with the one whose words he wished to report,

and immediately, recollecting that he had said one for another, were so to recover himself and correct himself as nevertheless to say, 'I spoke well,' looking to nothing else than that there is so great a concord between the two — namely, between the one whose words he wished to report and the other whose name occurred to him in place of the former's name — that for the latter to have said this is as if the former had said it,

how much more was this to be understood of the holy prophets, and especially to be commended, that we should receive the books of them all as one book of one author, in which no discrepancy of things should be believed, as none would be found, and in which there would be a greater constancy of truth than if one man, however learned, were to speak all those things?

What argument, therefore, unbelieving or unskilled men try to take from this, as though to show a discordance of the holy evangelists, this rather the faithful and learned ought to take up to show the unity even of the holy prophets.

**7.** 31. There is another reason, which seems to me to be treated more carefully at another time, lest we extend the discourse further than the necessity of finishing this work demands, why this name of Jeremiah in the testimony of Zechariah was permitted to remain thus, or rather was commanded by the authority of the Holy Spirit.

There is in Jeremiah the account that he bought a field from his cousin and gave him silver[^138], not indeed under this designation of price which is set down in Zechariah, thirty pieces of silver, yet the purchase of a field is not in Zechariah; but that the evangelist interpreted the prophecy about the thirty pieces of silver as applying to this, which has now been fulfilled concerning the Lord, that this was his price, is clear; but that what Jeremiah said about the field bought also pertains to this could here be mystically signified, so that the name of Zechariah, who spoke of thirty pieces of silver, should not be set down here, but that of Jeremiah, who spoke of the field bought,

so that, the Gospel being read and the name of Jeremiah found, but Jeremiah being read and the testimony about the thirty pieces of silver not found, yet the field bought being found, the reader may be admonished to compare the two and from there to draw out the meaning of the prophecy, how it pertains to what was fulfilled in the Lord.

For what Matthew added to this testimony, when he said: *whom the sons of Israel valued, and gave them for the potter's field, as the Lord appointed for me*[^139], is found neither in Zechariah nor in Jeremiah. Whence it is rather to be taken as inserted elegantly and mystically in the person of the evangelist himself, because by the Lord's revelation he had learned that a prophecy of this kind pertained to this matter which was done concerning the price of Christ. For the deed of the field bought is, in Jeremiah, ordered to be put into an earthen vessel, and here the potter's field is bought with the price of the Lord, and this for the burial of strangers, as for the abiding rest of those who, sojourning in this world, are buried together with Christ through baptism[^140]. For the Lord told Jeremiah that that purchase of the field signified this, that there would be an abiding for those freed from captivity in that land. These things I have thought worth, as it were, sketching out, while admonishing what in these prophetic testimonies, brought into one and compared with the Gospel narration, is to be sought more carefully and attentively. These things Matthew interposed about Judas the betrayer.

## On the things that were done before Pilate.

**8.** 32. Then he continues and says: *but Jesus stood before the governor, and the governor questioned him, saying: 'Are you the king of the Jews?' Jesus says to him: 'You say so.' And when he was accused by the chief priests and elders, he answered nothing. Then Pilate says to him: 'Do you not hear how many testimonies they bring against you?' And he did not answer him a word, so that the governor marveled greatly. Now at the solemn feast the governor was accustomed to release to the people one prisoner whom they wished. And he had then a notable prisoner, who was called Barabbas.*

*When therefore they had gathered, Pilate said: 'Whom do you wish me to release to you, Barabbas or Jesus who is called Christ?' For he knew that they had handed him over out of envy. Now as he was sitting on the judgment seat, his wife sent to him, saying: 'Have nothing to do with that righteous man, for today I have suffered many things in a dream because of him.' But the chief priests and elders persuaded the crowds to ask for Barabbas and to destroy Jesus. And the governor answered and said to them: 'Which of the two do you wish to be released to you?' And they said: 'Barabbas.' Pilate says to them: 'What then shall I do with Jesus who is called Christ?' They all say: 'Let him be crucified!' The governor said to them: 'Why, what evil has he done?' But they cried out the more, saying: 'Let him be crucified!'*

*But Pilate, seeing that he was gaining nothing, but rather that an uproar was arising, took water and washed his hands before the people, saying: 'I am innocent of the blood of this righteous man; see to it yourselves.' And all the people answered and said: 'His blood be upon us and upon our children.' Then he released Barabbas to them; but Jesus he handed over to them, scourged, to be crucified*[^141]. These things Matthew narrated as done concerning the Lord through Pilate.

**8.** 33. Mark too agrees in nearly the same words and matters[^142]. But the words of Pilate, by which he answered the people asking that one prisoner be solemnly released, he reports thus: *but Pilate answered them and said: 'Do you wish me to release to you the king of the Jews?'*[^143]. Matthew, however, says thus: *when therefore they had gathered, Pilate said: 'Whom do you wish me to release to you, Barabbas or Jesus who is called Christ?'*[^144].

There is no question that he is silent about their having asked that someone be released to them; but it can be asked which words Pilate said, whether those reported by Matthew or those reported by Mark. For one thing seems to be: *whom do you wish me to release to you, Barabbas or Jesus who is called Christ?* Another: *do you wish me to release to you the king of the Jews?*

But because they called kings 'christs,' and he who said 'this one or that one' clearly asked whether they wished the king of the Jews, that is, the Christ, to be released to them, it makes no difference to the meaning that Mark was here silent about Barabbas, wishing to say only what pertained to the Lord, since indeed in their answer he too sufficiently showed whom they wished released to them: *the chief priests,* he says, *stirred up the crowd, that he should rather release Barabbas to them.* And he continues and says: *but Pilate again answered and said to them: 'What then do you wish me to do with the king of the Jews?'*[^145].

Whence it now sufficiently appears that what Mark wishes to show by saying *the king of the Jews* is what Matthew does by saying *Christ.* For 'christs' were called only kings of the Jews; for in that place too Matthew says: *Pilate says to them: 'What then shall I do with Jesus who is called Christ?'*[^146].

Mark therefore continues: *but they cried out again: 'Crucify him!'* which the other says: *they all say: 'Let him be crucified!'* And Mark continues: *but Pilate said to them: 'Why, what evil has he done?' But they cried out the more: 'Crucify him!'*[^147].

This Matthew did not say; but since he said: *but Pilate, seeing that he was gaining nothing, but rather that an uproar was arising*[^148], he also said that he washed his hands before the people, to signify himself innocent of the blood of the righteous man, which likewise both Mark and the others pass over. Matthew too sufficiently shows that the governor dealt with the people that he might be released.

Which Mark briefly signified in what he reports that Pilate said: *why, what evil has he done?* Then he too thus concludes the things that were done with the Lord through Pilate: *but Pilate,* he says, *wishing to satisfy the people, released Barabbas to them and handed over Jesus, scourged with whips, to be crucified*[^149]. These things, done before the governor, Mark narrated.

**8.** 34. Luke narrates the things done before Pilate thus: *and they began to accuse him, saying: 'We found this man perverting our nation and forbidding tribute to be given to Caesar and saying that he himself is Christ, a king*[^150].' This those two evangelists did not say, although they did say that they were accusing him.

So this one disclosed even the very charges which they falsely brought. But he was silent about what Pilate said to him: *do you answer nothing? See in how many things they accuse you*[^151]; but he plainly continues and says what those others too said: *but Pilate questioned him, saying: 'Are you the king of the Jews?' And he answered and said: 'You say so*[^152].'

But Matthew and Mark recorded this before they said that Jesus was accosted, in that he did not answer them as they accused him[^153]. Now it makes no difference to the truth in what order Luke related these things, just as it makes no difference if one is silent about something which another records,

as in what follows he says: *but Pilate said to the chief priests and the crowds: 'I find no cause in this man.' But they grew more insistent, saying: 'He stirs up the people, teaching throughout all Judea, beginning from Galilee even to here.' But Pilate, hearing of Galilee, asked whether the man were a Galilean, and when he learned that he was under Herod's authority, he sent him to Herod, who himself was also in Jerusalem in those days. Now Herod, seeing Jesus, was very glad. For he had been desiring for a long time to see him, because he heard many things about him, and he hoped to see some sign done by him. And he questioned him with many words. But he answered nothing. The chief priests and scribes too stood by, vehemently accusing him. But Herod with his army despised him and mocked him, clothing him in a white robe, and sent him back to Pilate. And Herod and Pilate became friends with each other that same day; for before they had been enemies of one another*[^154]. All these things — that is, that the Lord was sent by Pilate to Herod and what was done there — Luke alone indicates, even if he says something there similar to what may be found in other places of the narration in the others; but the rest wished to say only those things which were done before Pilate, until the Lord was handed over to be crucified.

Returning, then, to the things that were being done before the governor, from which he had digressed to narrate what was done before Herod, Luke continues thus: *but Pilate, having called together the chief priests and the magistrates and the people, said to them: 'You have brought me this man as one perverting the people, and behold, I, examining him before you, have found no cause in this man of those things of which you accuse him*[^155].'

From this we understand that he passed over how he asked the Lord what he would answer his accusers. '*But neither did Herod,* he says, *for I sent you to him, and behold, nothing worthy of death has been done by him. I will therefore chastise him and release him.' Now he was obliged to release one to them at the feast. But the whole crowd cried out together, saying: 'Away with this man, and release to us Barabbas,' who had been put in prison for a certain insurrection made in the city and for murder. But Pilate spoke to them again, wishing to release Jesus. But they shouted back, saying: 'Crucify, crucify him!' And he said to them a third time: 'Why, what evil has this man done? I find no cause of death in him; I will therefore chastise him and release him.' But they pressed on with loud voices, demanding that he be crucified, and their voices grew strong*[^156].

Matthew sufficiently attested, in very few words, to this effort of Pilate by which he dealt with them rather often, wishing that Jesus be released, where he says: *but Pilate, seeing that he was gaining nothing, but rather that an uproar was arising*[^157] — which he would not say unless Pilate had striven much, although he was silent about how often he tried to bring this about, to rescue Jesus from their fury.

And so Luke too concludes what was done before the governor: *and Pilate,* he says, *adjudged that their petition be granted. And he released to them the one who had been put in prison for murder and insurrection, whom they asked for; but Jesus he handed over to their will*[^158].

**8.** 35. Now let us consider the same things according to John, that is, the things done through Pilate. *And they themselves,* he says, *did not enter the praetorium, lest they be defiled, but might eat the Passover. Pilate therefore went out to them and said: 'What accusation do you bring against this man?' They answered and said to him: 'If this man were not an evildoer, we would not have handed him over to you*[^159].'

Here we must see that it is not contrary to what Luke says, that definite charges were brought against him, and he says what they were: *and they began,* he says, *to accuse him, saying: 'We found this man perverting our nation and forbidding tribute to be given to Caesar and saying that he himself is Christ, a king*[^160].' But in what I have just now recorded according to John, the Jews seem to have been unwilling to state the charges, when Pilate had said to them: *what accusation do you bring against this man?* For they answered: *if this man were not an evildoer, we would not have handed him over to you,*

namely, that, following their authority, he might cease to ask what was charged against him, but believe him guilty for this alone, that he had deserved to be handed over to him by them. We ought therefore to understand that both this was said and that which Luke recorded.

For many things were said and many answered, from which each of them plucked as much as seemed good and set down in his narration what he judged sufficient. For John too himself says certain things that were charged, which we shall see in their places. And so he continues: *Pilate therefore said to them: 'Take him yourselves and judge him according to your law.' The Jews therefore said to him: 'It is not lawful for us to put anyone to death,' that the word of Jesus might be fulfilled which he said, signifying by what death he was to die. Pilate therefore entered the praetorium again and called Jesus and said to him: 'Are you the king of the Jews?' Jesus answered: 'Do you say this of yourself, or did others tell you about me?*[^161].'

And this might seem not to agree with what is recorded by the others: *Jesus answered: 'You say so*[^162],' were it not that in what follows he shows that this too was said. Whence he shows that the things he now says were rather passed over in silence by the other evangelists than not said by the Lord. Attend, then, to the rest. *Pilate answered,* he says: *'Am I a Jew? Your nation and the chief priests handed you over to me; what have you done?' Jesus answered: 'My kingdom is not of this world; if my kingdom were of this world, my servants would fight, that I might not be handed over to the Jews; but now my kingdom is not from here.' Pilate therefore said to him: 'So you are a king?' Jesus answered: 'You say that I am a king*[^163].'

Behold, now we have come to what the other evangelists recorded. There follows, then, likewise, with the Lord still saying what the rest passed over in silence: *'For this I was born, and for this I came into the world, to bear witness to the truth. Everyone who is of the truth hears my voice.' Pilate says to him: 'What is truth?' And when he had said this, he went out again to the Jews and says to them: 'I find no cause in him. But you have a custom that I release one to you at the Passover. Do you wish, then, that I release to you the king of the Jews?' They all cried out again, saying: 'Not this man, but Barabbas.' Now Barabbas was a robber*[^164]. *Then Pilate took Jesus and scourged him. And the soldiers, plaiting a crown of thorns, put it on his head and clothed him in a purple robe. And they came to him and said: 'Hail, king of the Jews!' and they struck him with their hands. Pilate went outside again and says to them: 'Behold, I bring him out to you, that you may know that I find no cause in him.' Jesus therefore came out, wearing the crown of thorns and the purple robe. And he says to them: 'Behold the man.' When therefore the chief priests and the officers saw him, they cried out, saying: 'Crucify, crucify!' Pilate says to them: 'Take him yourselves and crucify him; for I find no cause in him.' The Jews answered him: 'We have a law, and according to the law he ought to die, because he made himself the Son of God*[^165].'

This can agree with what Luke records as said in the accusation of the Jews: *we found this man perverting our nation*[^166], so that there is added: *because he made himself the Son of God*. And so John continues and says: *when therefore Pilate heard this saying, he was the more afraid. And he entered the praetorium again and says to Jesus: 'Where are you from?' But Jesus gave him no answer. Pilate therefore says to him: 'Do you not speak to me? Do you not know that I have power to crucify you and power to release you?' Jesus answered: 'You would have no power at all against me unless it had been given you from above. Therefore he who handed me over to you has the greater sin.' From then on Pilate sought to release him. But the Jews cried out, saying: 'If you release this man, you are no friend of Caesar; everyone who makes himself a king opposes Caesar*[^167].'

With this can agree what Luke narrates in the same accusation of the Jews thus: when they had said: *we found this man perverting our nation,* he added: *and forbidding tribute to be given to Caesar and saying that he himself is Christ, a king*[^168]. From this is resolved that question by which it could be thought that John said no charge was brought against the Lord by the Jews, when they answered and said to him: *if this man were not an evildoer, we would not have handed him over to you*[^169].

John therefore continues and says: *Pilate, then, when he had heard these sayings, brought Jesus outside and sat on the judgment seat in a place called the Pavement, but in Hebrew Gabbatha. Now it was the preparation of the Passover, about the sixth hour. And he says to the Jews: 'Behold your king.' But they cried out: 'Away, away, crucify him!' Pilate said to them: 'Shall I crucify your king?' The chief priests answered: 'We have no king but Caesar.' Then therefore he handed him over to them to be crucified*[^170]. These things John narrated as done through Pilate.

## On the mockery with which he was mocked by Pilate's cohort, how the three who tell of it do not disagree — Matthew, Mark, and John.

**9.** 36. It is now consequent that we see the very passion of the Lord according to the testimony of the four evangelists, which Matthew thus begins: *then the soldiers of the governor, taking Jesus into the praetorium, gathered the whole cohort to him, and stripping him, they put a scarlet cloak around him, and plaiting a crown of thorns, they set it on his head and a reed in his right hand, and bending the knee before him, they mocked him, saying: 'Hail, king of the Jews*[^171].' This Mark, in the same place of the narration, says thus: *but the soldiers led him inside into the courtyard of the praetorium, and they call together the whole cohort, and they clothe him with purple and put on him a plaited crown of thorns, and they began to salute him: 'Hail, king of the Jews,' and they struck his head with a reed and spat on him, and, bending their knees, they worshiped him*[^172]. And so it is understood that what Matthew says, *they put a scarlet cloak around him,* is what Mark said: *they clothe him with purple.* For in place of the royal purple that scarlet cloak was used by the mockers, and there is a certain red purple very like scarlet. It can also be that Mark recorded the purple too which the cloak had, although it was scarlet. Luke passed this over. John, however, before he said that Pilate had handed him over to be crucified, recorded this, saying: *then Pilate took Jesus and scourged him, and the soldiers, plaiting a crown of thorns, put it on his head and clothed him in a purple robe, and they came to him and said: 'Hail, king of the Jews,' and they struck him with their hands*[^173].

Whence it appears that Matthew and Mark recorded this by recapitulation, not that it was done then, when Pilate had already handed him over to be crucified; for John says clearly enough that these things were done before Pilate, whence these recalled what they had passed over. To this pertains also what Matthew says next: *and spitting on him, they took the reed and struck his head. And after they had mocked him, they took off the cloak and put his own garments on him and led him away to crucify him*[^174]. This, that they took off the cloak and put his own garments on him, is understood to have been done at the end, when he was already being led away. Mark says this thus: *and after they had mocked him, they took off the purple from him and put his own garments on him*[^175].

## How it is not contradictory that the evangelists say the one who carried his cross was compelled.

**10.** 37. Matthew therefore continues: *and as they were going out, they found a man of Cyrene, Simon by name; this man they compelled to carry his cross*[^176]. Mark too: *and they lead him out,* he says, *to crucify him. And they compelled a passerby, Simon of Cyrene, coming from the country, the father of Alexander and Rufus, to carry his cross*[^177]. This Luke says thus: *and as they led him away, they seized a certain Simon of Cyrene, coming from the country, and laid the cross on him to carry it after Jesus*[^178]. But John narrates thus: *but they took Jesus and led him out. And bearing his own cross, he went out to the place called of the Skull, in Hebrew Golgotha, where they crucified him*[^179]. Whence it is understood that he himself carried the cross for himself when he was going out to the place mentioned. But that Simon was compelled on the way, of whom the three tell, to whom the cross was afterward given to carry up to the place. Thus we find that both happened: first what John said, then what the other three said.

## On the drink which they gave him before his crucifixion was recorded.

**11.** 38. And so Matthew continues: *and they came,* he says, *to the place called Golgotha, which is the place of the Skull.* In this place they most plainly agree[^180]. Then the same Matthew adds: *and they gave him wine to drink, mixed with gall, and when he had tasted it, he would not drink*[^181]. This Mark narrates thus: *and they gave him wine mixed with myrrh to drink, and he did not take it*[^182]. This is to be understood as what Matthew said: *mixed with gall.* For he put gall for bitterness, and wine mixed with myrrh is most bitter; although it can be that both gall and myrrh made the wine most bitter. As to what Mark says, *he did not take it,* it is understood: he did not take it to drink it. But he tasted it, as Matthew attests; so that what Matthew says, *he would not drink,* is what Mark said, *he did not take it;* but he was silent that he tasted it.

## On the dividing of his garments.

**12.** 39. Matthew continues: *but after they had crucified him, they divided his garments, casting lots, and sitting down they kept watch over him*[^183]. This same thing Mark says thus: *and crucifying him, they divided his garments, casting lots for them, who should take what*[^184]. This Luke says thus: *but dividing his garments, they cast lots, and the people stood watching*[^185]. It was said briefly by the three. But John sets this forth more distinctly, how it was done. *The soldiers therefore,* he says, *when they had crucified him, took his garments and made four parts, a part for each soldier, and also the tunic. Now the tunic was seamless, woven from the top throughout. They said therefore to one another: 'Let us not tear it, but cast lots for it, whose it shall be,' that the Scripture might be fulfilled which says: 'They divided my garments among themselves, and for my clothing they cast lots*[^186].'

## On the hour of the Lord's passion.

**13.** 40. Matthew continues: *and they put over his head the charge against him, written: 'This is Jesus, the king of the Jews*[^187].' But Mark, before he said this, says: *now it was the third hour, and they crucified him*[^188]. For he subjoins this when he had spoken of the dividing of the garments — a matter which is to be considered most carefully, lest it cause a great error. For there are those who think that the Lord was indeed crucified at the third hour, but that from the sixth hour darkness came over until the ninth, so that three hours are understood to have been completed from the time he was crucified until the darkness came.

And this could indeed be most rightly understood, were it not that John says that about the sixth hour *Pilate sat on the judgment seat in a place called the Pavement, but in Hebrew Gabbatha*[^189]. For it follows: *now it was the preparation of the Passover, about the sixth hour. And he says to the Jews: 'Behold your king.' But they cried out: 'Away, away! Crucify him.' Pilate said to them: 'Shall I crucify your king?' The chief priests answered: 'We have no king but Caesar.' Then therefore he handed him over to them to be crucified*[^190].

If, therefore, about the sixth hour, with Pilate sitting on the judgment seat, he was handed over to the Jews to be crucified, how was he crucified at the third hour, as some, not understanding the words of Mark, have thought[^191]?

**13.** 41. For let us first see at what hour he could have been crucified; then we shall see why Mark said he was crucified at the third hour. It was about the sixth hour when he was handed over to be crucified by Pilate sitting, as has been said, on the judgment seat.

For it was not yet the full sixth hour, but about the sixth, that is, the fifth was completed and something of the sixth had begun to be. But these people would never say 'the fifth and a quarter' or 'the fifth and a third' or 'the fifth and a half' or anything of the kind. For the Scriptures have this custom, that they put the whole for the part, especially in matters of time, as about those eight days, after which they say he went up the mountain[^192], of which, looking to the middle ones, Matthew and Mark said: *after six days*[^193]. Especially since the same John spoke so moderately that he did not say 'the sixth' but *about the sixth*[^194].

But if he did not say it thus, but only said 'the sixth,' we could understand it as the Scriptures are wont to speak, as I have said, the whole for the part,

so that, the fifth completed and the sixth begun, these things were done which are narrated at the crucifixion of our Lord, until, the sixth completed, while he hung, the darkness came which three evangelists attest, Matthew, Mark, and Luke[^195].

**13.** 42. Let us now ask, consequently, why Mark said, when he had recorded *that, crucifying him, they divided his garments, casting lots for them, who should take what,* and went on to add: *now it was the third hour, and they crucified him*[^196]. He had certainly already said: *and crucifying him, they divided his garments.* So too the rest attest that, when he was crucified, his garments were divided. If Mark wished to record the time of that deed, it would suffice to say: *now it was the third hour;* why did he add: *and they crucified him,* unless because he wished to signify something by recapitulation, which would be found upon inquiry when the Scripture itself was read in those times in which it was known to the whole Church at what hour the Lord was hung upon the wood, whence the error of this one might be corrected or his falsehood refuted? But because he knew that the Lord was hung by the soldiers, not by the Jews, as John says most plainly[^197], he wished secretly to show that they rather crucified him who cried out that he be crucified than those who, according to their office, furnished service to their commander.

It is understood, then, that it was the third hour when the Jews cried out that the Lord be crucified[^198], and it is most truly shown that they crucified him then, when they cried out, especially because they did not wish to seem to have done this and on that account had handed him over to Pilate,

which their words sufficiently indicate according to John. For when Pilate had said to them: *what accusation do you bring against this man? They answered and said to him: 'If this man were not an evildoer, we would not have handed him over to you.' Pilate said to them: 'Take him yourselves and judge him according to your law.' The Jews therefore said: 'It is not lawful for us to put anyone to death*[^199].' What, then, they especially did not wish to seem to have done, Mark shows that they did at the third hour, judging most truly that the tongue of the Jews was more the slayer of the Lord than the hands of the soldiers.

**13.** 43. But whoever says that it was not the third hour when the Jews first cried this out shows himself most insanely an enemy of the Gospel, unless perhaps he can resolve the same question otherwise; for he has no means of proving that it was not then the third hour, and therefore the truthful evangelist is rather to be believed than the contentious suspicions of men. 'Whence,' he says, 'do you prove that it was the third hour?' I answer: because I believe the evangelists. And if you too believe them, show how the Lord could be crucified both at the sixth hour and at the third. For about the sixth, that we must confess, we are pressed by John's narration; the third Mark records; and if each of us believes them, show how both could have happened otherwise, and I will most gladly acquiesce. For I love not my own opinion but the truth of the Gospel. And would that even more solutions of this question might be found by others! Until that is done, use this one with me, if you please. For if no other solution can be found, this alone will suffice; but if one can be found, when it has been shown, we shall choose it. Only do not think it a consequence that any one of all four evangelists lied, or erred at so great and so holy a height of authority.

**13.** 44. But if anyone says that he proves from this that it was not the third hour when the Jews cried that out — because, after the same Mark said: *but Pilate again answered and said to them: 'What then do you wish me to do with the king of the Jews?' But they cried out again: 'Crucify him*[^200],' no interval of delay is interposed by the same Mark in his narration, and one comes at once to this, that the Lord was handed over to the cross by Pilate, which John records as done about the sixth hour — let him who says this understand that many things were passed over which were done in between, while Pilate was acting, seeking how he might rescue him from the Jews and strive most insistently, by whatever means he could, against their most insane will.

For Matthew says: *Pilate says to them: 'What then shall I do with Jesus who is called Christ?' They all say: 'Let him be crucified!'* Then, we say, it was the third hour. But as Matthew continues and narrates: *but Pilate, seeing that he was gaining nothing, but rather that an uproar was arising*[^201], we understand that in Pilate's efforts to rescue the Lord and in the uproar of the Jews striving against him the time of two hours was spent, and that the sixth had begun, before whose ending were done the things that are narrated from the time when Pilate handed over the Lord until the darkness came.

But what Matthew records above: *now as he was sitting on the judgment seat, his wife sent to him, saying: 'Have nothing to do with that righteous man, for today I have suffered many things in a dream because of him*[^202]' — Pilate sat on the judgment seat afterward; but among the things which he narrated as done earlier, Matthew, recollecting what he said about Pilate's wife, wished to interpose it there, so as to lay the groundwork for the chief reason why Pilate was unwilling, even at the last, to hand him over to the Jews.

But Luke narrates that, when Pilate had said: *I will therefore chastise him and release him,* the whole crowd cried out: *away with this man, and release to us Barabbas*; but perhaps they had not yet said: *crucify.* *But Pilate again,* according to the same Luke, *spoke to them, wishing to release Jesus. But they shouted back, saying: 'Crucify him.'*

Then it is understood that it was the third hour. So, what Luke continues and says: *but he said to them a third time: 'Why, what evil has this man done? I find no cause of death in him; I will therefore chastise him and release him.' But they pressed on with loud voices, demanding that he be crucified, and their voices grew strong*[^203],

he too sufficiently signifies that there was a great uproar; and after how much delay he said to them a third time: *why, what evil has he done?* one may understand, as much as is enough for tracking out the truth; and afterward, when he says: *they pressed on with loud voices and their voices grew strong,* who does not see that this was done because they saw that Pilate was unwilling to hand the Lord over to them? And he who was greatly unwilling for this surely did not yield in a moment of time, but at least two hours and something more passed in that hesitation.

**13.** 46. Question John too, and see how great was that hesitation of Pilate and his refusal of so foul a service. For he narrates these things much more fully, although he too surely does not say all the things in which two hours and something of the sixth flowed past.

Yet, when he had scourged Jesus and permitted him to be ill-treated by the soldiers with the mocking robe and many mockeries[^204] (I believe, that in this way at least he might soften their fury, lest they persist in raging even to death), *Pilate went outside again and says to them: 'Behold, I bring him out to you, that you may know that I find no cause in him.' Jesus therefore came out, wearing the crown of thorns and the purple robe. And he says to them: 'Behold the man,'*

so that, seeing that, as it were, ignominious appearance, they might be appeased. But he adds and says: *when therefore the chief priests and the officers saw him, they cried out, saying: 'Crucify, crucify!'*

Then, we say, it was the third hour. Attend to what follows: *Pilate says to them: 'Take him yourselves and crucify him; for I find no cause in him.' The Jews answered him: 'We have a law, and according to the law he ought to die, because he made himself the Son of God.' When therefore Pilate heard this saying, he was the more afraid. And he entered the praetorium again and says to Jesus: 'Where are you from?'*

*But Jesus gave him no answer. Pilate therefore says to him: 'Do you not speak to me? Do you not know that I have power to crucify you and power to release you?' Jesus answered: 'You would have no power at all against me unless it had been given you from above. Therefore he who handed me over to you has the greater sin.' From then on Pilate sought to release him*[^205].

In this, then, that Pilate sought to release him, how much time do we suppose was spent, how many things passed over which were either said by Pilate or contradicted by the Jews, until the Jews said what would move him and make him yield?

For it follows thus: *but the Jews cried out, saying: 'If you release this man, you are no friend of Caesar; everyone who makes himself a king opposes Caesar.' Pilate therefore, when he had heard these sayings, brought Jesus outside and sat on the judgment seat in a place called the Pavement, but in Hebrew Gabbatha. Now it was the preparation of the Passover, about the sixth hour*[^206].

From that utterance of the Jews, then, by which they first said: *crucify,* when it was the third hour of the day, until this, that he sat on the judgment seat, through the intervening delays of Pilate's hesitation and of the tumultuous Jews, two hours passed, and, the fifth completed, something of the sixth had now begun. *He says,* then, *to the Jews: 'Behold your king.' But they cried out: 'Away, crucify him!'*[^207]. But not even now did Pilate, moved by fear of the accusation, easily yield. For then his wife had sent to him as he sat on the judgment seat, which Matthew anticipated[^208], who recorded only this before he came to that place of the narration, so as to set it there where he judged it opportune. And so, still trying whether he might gain anything, Pilate said to them: *shall I crucify your king? The chief priests answered: 'We have no king but Caesar.' Then therefore he handed him over to them to be crucified*[^209]. And while he proceeds, and while he is crucified with two robbers, while his garments are divided and that tunic is cast for by lot, and amid these things he is mocked with various revilings (for at the same time, while those things were being done, revilings too were being cast), the sixth hour was completed and the darkness came which Matthew and Mark and Luke record[^210].

**13.** 47. Now, then, let impious obstinacy fall, and let it believe that the Lord Jesus Christ was crucified both at the third hour by the tongue of the Jews and at the sixth by the hands of the soldiers, because in the tumult of the Jews and the heat of Pilate's dealings two hours and more passed from the utterance by which they said: *crucify.* But Mark himself, who is especially a follower of brevity, wished briefly to convey Pilate's will and effort for the Lord's life. For when he had said: *but they cried out again: 'Crucify him!'* — where he shows that they had already cried out, when they wished Barabbas to be released to them —

he added: *but Pilate said to them: 'Why, what evil has he done?'* In this way he briefly conveyed what was done over a long time. Yet he too, mindful of what he wished to be understood, did not say 'but Pilate said to them,' but said: *but Pilate kept saying to them: 'Why, what evil has he done?'*

For if he said 'said,' we would understand it as said once; but because he says *kept saying,* he sufficiently intimated to those who understand that it was said in many ways and often, until the sixth hour began.

Let us consider, then, how briefly Mark said this in comparison with Matthew, how briefly Matthew in comparison with Luke, how briefly Luke in comparison with John, while yet each recorded one thing and another, and finally how briefly John himself in comparison with the things that were done and the delays while they were being done; and let us believe, without the madness of resistance, that two hours and something could pass in that interval.

**13.** 48. But whoever says that Mark, if it were so, could have said, at the time when it was the third hour, when the voice of the Jews about crucifying the Lord had sounded, that it was the third hour, and could also say that they then crucified him, imposes laws too proudly on the narrators of the truth.

For he can in this way say that, if he himself were narrating these things, they ought to have been narrated by all in the same manner and order in which he narrated them. Let him therefore deign to have his own counsel overcome by the counsel of Mark the evangelist, who judged that this should be set in the place where it was suggested by divine inspiration. For their recollections were governed by the hand of him who governs the water, as it is written, however it pleased him[^211].

For human memory floats through various thoughts, nor is it in anyone's power what and when something comes into his mind. Since therefore those holy and truthful men had committed the, as it were, chance occurrences of their recollections, for the sake of the order of the narration, to the hidden power of God, to whom nothing is by chance, no man ought to say — far cast out from the eyes of God and far sojourning abroad — 'In this place ought to have been set' what he greatly fails to understand why God wished to be set in that place:

*for even if our gospel is veiled,* the Apostle says, *it is veiled in those who are perishing*[^212], and when he had said: *to some we are the fragrance of life unto life, to others the fragrance of death unto death,* he immediately added: *and who is sufficient for these things?*[^213] That is, 'Who is sufficient to understand how justly it is done?' Which the same Lord says: *I came that those who do not see may see, and that those who see may become blind*[^214].

For this is that depth of the riches of the wisdom and knowledge of God[^215], by which from the same lump one vessel is made for honor and another for dishonor, and it is said to flesh and blood: *O man, who are you to answer back to God?*[^216] Who, then, in this matter too, knows the mind of the Lord, or who has been his counselor[^217],

where he so governed the hearts of the remembering evangelists and exalted them at the summit of the Church with so great a height of authority that through these very things which can seem contradictory in them many were blinded, deservedly handed over to the desires of their heart and to a reprobate mind[^218], and many were exercised to refine a devout understanding according to the hidden justice of the Almighty? For the prophet says to the Lord: *your thoughts have become exceedingly deep; an unwise man does not know, and a fool does not understand these things*[^219].

**13.** 49. But I ask and admonish those who read these things, worked out by us with the Lord's help, that they remember this discourse, which I have thought to interpose in this place, in every similar difficulty of questions, lest the same things have to be repeated too often.

But anyone who, without the hardness of impiety, is willing to attend will easily see in how opportune a place Mark set this about the third hour, so that everyone may there recall at what hour the Jews crucified the Lord[^220] — they who wished to transfer the crime itself onto the Romans, whether the rulers or the soldiers, in the place where the deed of the soldiers, the servants, was recorded.

For he says: *and crucifying him, they divided his garments, casting lots for them, who should take what*[^221]. Who did this but the soldiers, as John makes clear[^222]? Therefore, lest anyone, turning the thought of so great a crime away from the Jews, should direct it upon those soldiers: *now it was,* he says, *the third hour, and they crucified him,* so that they are found rather to have crucified him whom the diligent inquirer will find could have cried out at the third hour that he be crucified, when he notices that what was done by the soldiers was done at the sixth hour.

**13.** 50. Although there are not lacking those who wish the preparation which John recorded, saying: *now it was the preparation, about the sixth hour*[^223], to be understood as the third hour of the day, at which Pilate sat on the judgment seat, so that the same third hour seems to have been completed when he was crucified, and, while he hung on the wood, three other hours passed and he gave up his spirit, so that from the hour at which he died, that is, the sixth of the day, the darkness came until the ninth. For they say that on that day, which the day of the Sabbath followed, it was the preparation of the Passover of the Jews, because the Days of Unleavened Bread already began from that Sabbath;

but yet that the true Passover, which was now being carried out in the Lord's passion, not of the Jews but of the Christians, began to be prepared — that is, to have its preparation — from the ninth hour of the night, in that the Lord was being prepared to be killed by the Jews. For *preparation* is the meaning of 'parasceve.'

From that ninth hour of the night, then, until his crucifixion there falls the sixth hour of the preparation according to John and the third hour of the day according to Mark, so that Mark did not record by recapitulation that hour at which the Jews cried out: *crucify, crucify!* but said outright that it was the third hour at which the Lord was fixed to the wood. What faithful person would not favor this solution of the question, if only some point can be conjectured from the ninth hour of the night from which we may fittingly understand the preparation of our Passover, that is, the preparing of the death of Christ, to have begun?

For if we say it began when the Lord was seized by the Jews, it was still the early parts of the night; if when he was led to the house of Caiaphas's father-in-law, where he was heard by the chief priests, the cock had not yet crowed, which we gather from Peter's denial, which, when it was now being heard, took place; but if when he was handed over to Pilate, it is most plainly written that it was already morning.

It remains, then, that we understand this preparation of the Passover, that is, the preparing of the Lord's death, to have begun when all the chief priests, before whom he was first heard, answered and said: *he deserves death,* which is found in Matthew and Mark, so that they are understood to have spoken later, by recapitulation, of Peter's denial what had been done earlier.

For it is not absurdly conjectured that at the time when, as I said, they pronounced him deserving of death, it could have been the ninth hour of the night. From which hour until the hour at which Pilate sat on the judgment seat there was about the sixth hour, not of the day, but of the preparation, that is, of the immolation of the Lord, which is the true Passover,

so that, at the full sixth of that preparation, which fell at the full third of the day, the Lord was hung upon the wood. Whether, then, this be rather chosen as the understanding, or whether Mark recalled the third hour and wished to record it especially with reference to the condemnation of the Jews in the Lord's crucifixion,

at which they are understood to have been able to cry out that he be crucified, so that we should take it that they rather crucified him than those by whose hands he was hung upon the wood,

just as that centurion drew nearer to the Lord than those friends whom he sent[^224] — without doubt the question is resolved about the hour of the Lord's passion, which is especially wont both to stir up the impudence of the contentious and to disturb the inexperience of the weak.

## On the two robbers crucified with him, how all agree.

**14.** 51. Matthew continues and says: *then two robbers were crucified with him, one on the right and one on the left*[^225]. And Mark likewise, and Luke[^226]. Nor does John raise any question, although he did not call them robbers. For he says: *and with him two others, one on this side and one on that, but Jesus in the middle*[^227]. But it would be contradictory if, when they had said they were robbers, he said they were innocent.

## On those who reviled the Lord.

**15.** 52. Matthew continues and says: *but those passing by blasphemed him, wagging their heads and saying: 'You who destroy the temple and rebuild it in three days, save yourself; if you are the Son of God, come down from the cross*[^228].' With this Mark agrees in nearly the same words[^229]. Then Matthew continues and says: *likewise also the chief priests, mocking him with the scribes and elders, said: 'He saved others, he cannot save himself. If he is the King of Israel, let him now come down from the cross, and we will believe in him. He trusts in God; let him deliver him now, if he wants him. For he said: 'I am the Son of God'*[^230].' Mark and Luke, although they use other words, yet agree to the same meaning, when one omits what another records[^231]. For about the chief priests, that they insulted the crucified Lord, they likewise do not keep silent, although Mark was silent about the elders, while Luke, because he said 'rulers' and did not add 'of the priests,' rather embraced all the chief men by a general name, so that there both scribes and elders can be understood.

## On the robbers' insult, how Matthew and Mark are not opposed to Luke, who said that one of them insulted, the other believed.

**16.** 53. Matthew continues and says: *but the robbers too, who were fixed with him, reproached him*[^232]. Nor does Mark differ, saying this same thing in other words[^233]. But Luke can be thought to be contradictory, unless we do not forget a sufficiently customary kind of expression.

For Luke says: *but one of the robbers who were hanging blasphemed him, saying: 'If you are the Christ, save yourself and us.'* And he continues and weaves it together thus: *but the other answered and rebuked him, saying: 'Do you not even fear God, since you are under the same condemnation? And we indeed justly, for we receive what our deeds deserve; but this man has done nothing wrong.' And he said to Jesus: 'Lord, remember me when you come into your kingdom.' And Jesus said to him: 'Truly I say to you, today you will be with me in Paradise*[^234].'

How, then, as Matthew says: *the robbers who were fixed with him reproached him*[^235], or, as Mark says: *and those who were crucified with him reviled him*[^236], since indeed one of them reviled him according to Luke's testimony, while the other both restrained him and believed in the Lord — unless we understand that Matthew and Mark, touching the place briefly, put the plural number for the singular,

just as in the *Epistle to the Hebrews* we read said in the plural: *they stopped the mouths of lions*[^237], when Daniel alone is understood to be signified, and said in the plural: *they were sawn in two*[^238], when it is handed down of Isaiah alone? In the *Psalm* too, what was said: *the kings of the earth stood up, and the rulers gathered together as one*[^239] and so on, is explained in the *Acts of the Apostles* as the plural number put for the singular[^240]. For those who adduced the testimony of that same *Psalm* understood *kings* on account of Herod, *rulers* on account of Pilate.

But because pagans too are wont to calumniate the Gospel, let them see how their own authors spoke of Phaedras, Medeas, and Clytemnestras, when there was a single one of each. And what is more usual, for example, than that someone should say, 'The peasants too insult me,' even if one insults?

For then what Luke made clear about the one would be contradictory, if they had said that both robbers reviled the Lord; for so the one could not be understood under the plural number. But since it was said: *the robbers,* or: *those who were crucified with him,* and 'both' was not added, not only, if both had done it, could this be said, but even, because one did it, it could be signified, by the customary manner of expression, through the plural number.

## On the drink of vinegar.

**17.** 54. Matthew continues and says: *now from the sixth hour darkness came over the whole land until the ninth hour*[^241]. This the other two attest[^242]; but Luke adds also the cause of the darkness, that is, that the sun was darkened. Matthew continues: *and about the ninth hour Jesus cried out with a loud voice, saying: 'Eli, Eli, lema sabachthani?' That is: 'My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?'*[^243]. *But some standing there and hearing it said: 'This man calls Elijah*[^244].'

Mark agrees in nearly the same words, yet to the same meaning not nearly but altogether[^245]. Matthew continues: *and immediately one of them, running, took a sponge, filled it with vinegar, put it on a reed, and gave it to him to drink*[^246]. So Mark too says: *but one ran and filled a sponge with vinegar and, putting it around a reed, gave it to him to drink, saying: 'Let be, let us see whether Elijah comes to take him down*[^247].'

This about Elijah Matthew narrated as said not by the one who offered the sponge with vinegar, but by the rest. For he says: *but the rest said: 'Let be, let us see whether Elijah comes to deliver him*[^248],'

whence we understand that both he and the rest said this. Luke, however, before he narrated the robber's insult, recorded this about the vinegar thus: *but the soldiers too mocked him, coming up and offering him vinegar, and saying: 'If you are the King of the Jews, save yourself*[^249].'

He wished to embrace at once what was done and said by the soldiers. Here it ought not to disturb anyone that he did not say one of them offered the vinegar; for he kept the kind of expression of which we treated above, putting the plural for the singular.

But this about the vinegar John too recorded, where he says: *afterward, knowing that all things were now finished, that the Scripture might be fulfilled, Jesus said: 'I thirst.' Now a vessel full of vinegar was standing there; and they, putting a sponge full of vinegar around a hyssop, offered it to his mouth*[^250]. But that in the same John it is found that he said *I thirst,* and that there was a *vessel full of vinegar* there, is no wonder if the rest were silent about it.

## On the utterances which the Lord made as he was about to die at once.

**18.** 55. Matthew continues: *but Jesus, crying out again with a loud voice, gave up his spirit*[^251]. Mark likewise: *but Jesus, having uttered a loud voice, breathed his last*[^252]. Luke, however, declared what he said with that loud voice; for he said: *and crying out with a loud voice, Jesus said: 'Father, into your hands I commend my spirit,' and saying this, he breathed his last*[^253]. John, however, just as he was silent about that first utterance, *Eli, Eli,* which Matthew and Mark related, was silent also about this one which Luke alone indicated, but those two signified by the name of *a loud voice,* that is: *Father, into your hands I commend my spirit,*

which Luke himself likewise attested that he said with a loud voice, so that we may understand this to have been the loud voice which Matthew and Mark recorded. But John said what none of those three did, that he said: *it is finished,* when he had received the vinegar, which we understand he said before that loud voice. For these are the words of John: *when therefore Jesus had received the vinegar, he said: 'It is finished,' and bowing his head, he gave up his spirit*[^254].

Between what he says, *it is finished,* and what he says, *and bowing his head, he gave up his spirit,* that loud voice was uttered which this one was silent about, but the other three recorded. For this appears to be the order: that he first said *it is finished,* when what had been prophesied of him was accomplished in him, and as if he were waiting for this, who surely would die when he willed, then, commending it, *he gave up his spirit.* But in whatever order anyone may judge it could have been said, this must be most diligently guarded against, lest it seem to anyone that any of the evangelists is opposed to another, if he either was silent about what another said or said what another was silent about.

## On the tearing of the veil.

**19.** 56. Matthew continues: *and behold, the veil of the temple was torn in two, from top to bottom*[^255]. And Mark thus: *and the veil of the temple was torn in two, from top to bottom*[^256]. Luke likewise says this: *and the veil of the temple was torn down the middle*[^257], but not in the same order.

For, wishing to join miracle to miracle, when he had said: *the sun was darkened,* he thought it should immediately be subjoined: *and the veil of the temple was torn down the middle,* anticipating, evidently, what was done as the Lord expired,

so that then, recapitulating, he might follow out both the drink of vinegar and that loud voice and the death itself, which are understood to have been done after the darkness, before the tearing of the veil.

For Matthew, who, when he had said: *but Jesus, crying out again with a loud voice, gave up his spirit,* immediately subjoined: *and behold, the veil of the temple was torn*[^258], sufficiently expressed that it was torn then, when Jesus had given up his spirit. But if he had not added *and behold,* but had simply said: *and the veil of the temple was torn,* it would be uncertain whether he and Mark had recorded this by recapitulation while Luke had kept the order, or whether Luke had recapitulated what they had set down in order.

## On the wonder of the centurion and of those who were with him.

**20.** 57. Matthew continues: *and the earth was shaken, and the rocks were split, and the tombs were opened, and many bodies of the saints who had slept arose; and coming out of the tombs after his resurrection, they came into the holy city and appeared to many*[^259]. These things which he alone said, there is no need to fear lest they seem to anyone contradictory to the rest. The same one continues: *but the centurion and those who were with him keeping watch over Jesus, seeing the earthquake and the things that were happening, were greatly afraid, saying: 'Truly this was the Son of God*[^260].'

Mark thus: *but when the centurion who stood opposite saw that he had thus expired, crying out, he said: 'Truly this man was the Son of God'*[^261] Luke thus: *but when the centurion saw what had happened, he glorified God, saying: 'Truly this man was righteous*[^262].' It is not contradictory that Matthew says the centurion and those who were with him marveled at seeing the earthquake, while Luke says he marveled at this, that he had expired when he had uttered that voice, showing how much power he had over when he should die.

For in that the same Matthew not only said *seeing the earthquake* but also added *and the things that had happened,* he showed that the place was open for Luke to say that the centurion marveled at the Lord's death itself, since this too is among those things which were then done wonderfully. Although, even if Matthew had not added it, it had to be understood that, since many wonderful things were done and the centurion and those with him could marvel at them all, it was free for the narrators to record at what each of them marveled, and that they are not opposed to one another when one said he marveled at this, another at that, since he had marveled at them all.

But that one says the centurion said: *truly this was the Son of God*[^263], while another says: *truly this man was the Son of God*[^264], will not disturb anyone to whom so many things said and explained alike above have not slipped away; for both forms of words converge to one meaning,

nor is it in any way contradictory that one was silent about *man,* another said it. Rather, that Luke does not say the centurion said: *he was the Son of God,* but: *he was righteous,* can be thought different; but we ought to understand either that both were said by the centurion and that they recorded the one, this one the other, or perhaps that Luke wished to express the meaning of the centurion, in what sense he said Jesus was the Son of God.

For perhaps the centurion had not understood him to be the Only-begotten, equal to the Father, but had said Son of God for this reason, that he had believed him righteous, as many righteous men have been called sons of God. But that Luke himself too said: *but when the centurion saw what had happened,* in that kind he included all the things that were done wonderfully in that hour, recording, as it were, one wonderful deed, of which all those miracles were, as it were, the members and parts. Now, that Matthew added those who were with the centurion, while the others were silent about this, to whom is it not clear, from the well-known rule, that there is no contradiction when one says what another is silent about? And that Matthew said: *they were greatly afraid,* while Luke did not say *he was afraid,* but: *he glorified God,* who does not understand that he glorified God by fearing?

## On the women who stood there.

**21.** 58. Matthew continues: *now there were there many women at a distance, who had followed Jesus from Galilee, ministering to him, among whom was Mary Magdalene and Mary the mother of James and Joseph, and the mother of the sons of Zebedee*[^265] Mark thus: *now there were also women looking on from a distance, among whom was Mary Magdalene and Mary the mother of James the younger and of Joseph, and Salome; and when he was in Galilee, they followed him and ministered to him, and many others who had come up with him to Jerusalem*[^266].

Among these I see nothing that can be thought contradictory. For what does it matter to the truth that they named some women together, some one by one? Luke too weaves his narration thus: *and all the crowd of those who were present together at that spectacle, and saw the things that were happening, returned, beating their breasts. But all his acquaintances stood at a distance, and the women who had followed him from Galilee, seeing these things*[^267].

Whence he agrees sufficiently with the two above about the presence of the women, although he names none of them by name. About the crowd too, which was present together and, seeing the things that were happening, beat their breasts and returned, he agrees with Matthew, although he separately appended: *but the centurion and those who were with him*[^268].

Wherefore he alone is found to have said only of his acquaintances, who stood at a distance. For John too recorded the presence of the women before the Lord had given up his spirit, narrating thus: *now there were standing by the cross of Jesus his mother and his mother's sister, Mary of Cleophas, and Mary Magdalene. When therefore Jesus saw his mother and the disciple standing whom he loved, he says to his mother: 'Woman, behold your son.' Then he says to the disciple: 'Behold your mother.' And from that hour the disciple took her to his own home*[^269].

In this matter, unless Matthew too and Mark had most plainly named Mary Magdalene, we could say that some were at a distance, others by the cross. For none of them except John recorded the Lord's mother.

Now, then, how is it understood that the same Mary Magdalene both stood at a distance with the other women, as Matthew and Mark say, and was by the cross, as John says, except that they were at such an interval that they could both be said to be near, because they were present in his sight, and at a distance in comparison with the crowd standing more closely around with the centurion and the soldiers? We can also understand that those who were present together with the Lord's mother, after he commended her to the disciple, had now begun to go away, to extricate themselves from the density of the crowd, and to watch from farther off the rest of what was done, so that the other evangelists, who recorded them after the Lord's death, recorded them as now standing at a distance.

## On Joseph, who asked Pilate for the Lord's body.

**22.** 59. Matthew continues: *but when evening had come, a certain rich man from Arimathea, named Joseph, came, who himself too was a disciple of Jesus. He went to Pilate and asked for the body of Jesus. Then Pilate ordered the body to be given back*[^270].

Mark thus: *and when evening had now come, because it was the preparation, that is, the day before the Sabbath, Joseph of Arimathea came, a noble councillor, who himself too was waiting for the kingdom of God, and he boldly went in to Pilate and asked for the body of Jesus. But Pilate marveled that he had already died. And summoning the centurion, he asked him whether he was already dead. And when he had learned it from the centurion, he granted the body to Joseph*[^271].

Luke thus: *and behold, a man named Joseph, who was a councillor, a good and righteous man (he had not consented to their counsel and deeds), from Arimathea, a city of Judea, who himself too was waiting for the kingdom of God — he went to Pilate and asked for the body of Jesus*[^272].

John, however, after he had narrated the breaking of the legs of those crucified with the Lord and the piercing of the Lord's side with a lance — which he alone said in full — subjoins also about Joseph, agreeing with the rest, narrating thus: *but after these things Joseph of Arimathea, because he was a disciple of Jesus, but secretly for fear of the Jews, asked Pilate that he might take away the body of Jesus. And Pilate permitted it. He came, therefore, and took the body of Jesus*[^273]. In this there is nothing in which any of them seems opposed to any other. But perhaps someone may ask how John himself does not disagree with himself, attesting with the rest that Joseph asked for the body of Jesus, and alone saying that he had been a secret disciple of the Lord for fear of the Jews. For it rightly raises a question why he who was a secret disciple for fear dared to ask for his body, which none of those who followed him openly dared. But it is to be understood that this man did it by the confidence of his rank, endowed with which he could enter familiarly to Pilate. But in that last service rendered to the funeral he cared less about the Jews, although he was wont, in hearing the Lord, to avoid their hostilities.

## On his burial.

**23.** 60. Matthew continues: *and Joseph, having taken the body, wrapped it in a clean linen cloth and laid it in his own new tomb, which he had hewn out in the rock. And he rolled a great stone to the door of the tomb and went away*[^274]. Mark thus: *but Joseph, having bought a linen cloth and taking him down, wrapped him in the linen cloth and laid him in a tomb which was hewn out of rock, and rolled a stone to the door of the tomb*[^275].

Luke thus: *and having taken him down, he wrapped him in a linen cloth and laid him in a hewn tomb, in which no one had yet been laid*[^276]. From these three no question of disagreement can arise. But John records that the Lord's burial was cared for not by Joseph alone, but by Nicodemus too. For, consequently beginning with Nicodemus, he narrates thus: *now Nicodemus too came, who had at first come to Jesus by night, bringing a mixture of myrrh and aloes, about a hundred pounds*[^277].

Then he continues, joining Joseph himself, and says: *they took the body of Jesus, therefore, and bound it in linen cloths with the spices, as is the custom of the Jews to bury. Now there was in the place where he was crucified a garden, and in the garden a new tomb, in which no one had yet been laid. There, therefore, on account of the preparation of the Jews, because the tomb was near, they laid Jesus*[^278].

Nor is anything contradictory here to those who understand rightly. For neither did those who were silent about Nicodemus affirm that the Lord was buried by Joseph alone, although they made mention only of him; nor, because they said that Joseph wrapped him in one linen cloth, did they on that account forbid us to understand that other linen cloths too could be brought by Nicodemus and added over, so that John narrated the truth, that he was wrapped not in one linen cloth but in linen cloths,

although, both on account of the napkin which was applied to the head and the bands with which the whole body was bound, because all were of linen, even if there was one linen cloth there, it could most truly be said: *they bound him in linen cloths.* For things woven from linen are generally called linen cloths.

## On the things that were done about the time of the Lord's resurrection.

**24.** 61. Matthew continues: *now Mary Magdalene was there, and the other Mary, sitting opposite the tomb*[^279]. Which Mark says thus: *but Mary Magdalene and Mary of Joseph saw where he was laid*[^280]. Whence it is clear that they disagree in nothing from this.

**24.** 62. Matthew continues: *now on the next day, which is after the preparation, the chief priests and the Pharisees gathered to Pilate, saying: 'Lord, we have remembered that that deceiver said while still living: 'After three days I will rise.' Command, therefore, that the tomb be guarded until the third day, lest perhaps his disciples come and steal him and say to the people: 'He has risen from the dead,' and the last error will be worse than the first.' Pilate said to them: 'You have a guard; go, guard it as you know how.' But they, going away, secured the tomb, sealing the stone, with the guards*[^281]. This Matthew alone narrates, with none of the others narrating anything that seems contradictory to it.

**24.** 63. Then the same Matthew continues and says: *but on the evening of the Sabbath, which dawns toward the first day of the Sabbath, Mary Magdalene came, and the other Mary, to see the tomb. And behold, a great earthquake occurred. For an angel of the Lord descended from heaven, and coming, rolled back the stone and sat upon it. Now his appearance was like lightning and his garment like snow. And for fear of him the guards were terrified and became like dead men. But the angel answered and said to the women: 'Do not fear; for I know that you seek Jesus who was crucified. He is not here, for he has risen, as he said. Come and see the place where the Lord was laid. And going quickly, tell his disciples that he has risen, and behold, he goes before you into Galilee; there you will see him. Behold, I have told you*[^282].'

With this Mark agrees[^283]. But it can be a stumbling block how, according to Matthew, the angel sat upon the stone rolled back from the tomb. For Mark says that as they entered the tomb they saw a young man sitting on the right, covered with a white robe, and were amazed — unless we understand either that Matthew was silent about that angel whom they saw on entering, while Mark was silent about the one whom they saw outside sitting upon the stone, so that they saw two and heard from the two, one by one, what the angels said about Jesus,

first from the one whom they saw outside sitting upon the stone, then from the one whom they saw, on entering the tomb, sitting on the right, into which they were exhorted to enter by the words of the one sitting outside, who said: *come and see the place where the Lord was laid*[^284], where, coming as was said and entering, they saw the one about whom Matthew is silent but Mark speaks, sitting on the right, from whom they likewise heard such things.

Or surely we ought to take it that they entered the tomb into some enclosure of a wall, by which it is credible that the place was then fortified, in some space before the rock, where, the rock being hewn out, the place of burial had been made, so that they saw, sitting on the right in that same space, the one whom Matthew says was sitting upon the stone, which by the earthquake he had rolled back from the door of the tomb, that is, from the place of the sepulcher, which had been dug out in the rock.

**24.** 64. Likewise it can be asked how Mark says: *but they, going out, fled from the tomb, for trembling and fear had seized them, and they said nothing to anyone, for they were afraid*[^285], when Matthew says: *and they went out quickly from the tomb with fear and great joy, running to tell his disciples*[^286], unless we understand that they dared to say nothing to anyone of the angels themselves, that is, to answer the things they had heard from them, or surely to the guards, whom they saw lying there. For that joy which Matthew records is not opposed to the fear of which Mark speaks. For we ought to have understood that both were done in their mind, even if Matthew himself had not spoken of the fear; but since he too says: *and they went out quickly from the tomb with fear and great joy,* he allows no question to remain from this matter.

**24.** 65. About the very hour too at which the women came to the tomb, a question arises not to be despised. For when Matthew says: *but on the evening of the Sabbath, which dawns toward the first day of the Sabbath, Mary Magdalene came, and the other Mary, to see the tomb*[^287], what is it that Mark says: *and very early on the first day of the week they came to the tomb, the sun being now risen*[^288]? In this indeed he does not differ from the other two, that is, Luke and John. For what Luke says, *very early at dawn*[^289], and John, *early, while it was still dark*[^290], this is understood to be what Mark says: *very early, the sun being now risen,*

that is, when the sky from the eastern part was now whitening, which surely does not happen except by the nearness of the rising sun; for that brightness which is wont to be called by the name of dawn is its own. Therefore it is not opposed to him who says: *while it was still dark.* For as day rises, some remnants of darkness are thinned out the more, the more the light rises. Nor is what he says, *very early,* to be taken as though the sun itself were now seen above the earth, but rather as we are wont to say to those to whom we wish to signify that something is to be done earlier. For when we have said *early,* lest they think we are speaking of the sun being already visible above the earth, we generally add to this *very early,* that they may understand that which is called the whitening time; although this too is usual, that after the cock crow has been often repeated, when men have begun to conjecture that day is now drawing near, they say, 'It is now morning,'

and when, after this word, they have observed, and now, with the sun rising, that is, now coming near at hand to these parts, they have seen the sky redden or whiten, those who were saying 'it is morning' add and say, 'It is very early.' But what does it matter, since whether in the one way or the other we understand nothing else to be called by Mark *morning* than what Luke called *at dawn,* and this *very early,* what the other called *very early at dawn,* and what John called *early, while it was still dark; but the sun now rising,* that is, by its rising now beginning to light up the sky?

How, then, Matthew agrees with these three, who said neither *at dawn* nor *morning,* but: *on the evening of the Sabbath, which dawns toward the first day of the Sabbath,* must be more attentively tracked out. For by the first part of the night, which is *evening,* Matthew wished to signify the night itself, at the end of which night the women came to the tomb. And this is understood to be the reason why he so named that same night, because already in the evening it was lawful to bring spices, the Sabbath being surely past.

Therefore, since they were hindered by the Sabbath from doing it before, he named the night from the time at which it began to be lawful for them to do it, at whatever time of that same night they wished. And so it was said *on the evening of the Sabbath,* as if it were said 'on the night of the Sabbath,' that is, on the night which follows the day of the Sabbath. Which his very words sufficiently indicate. For he says thus: *but on the evening of the Sabbath, which dawns toward the first day of the Sabbath.*

Which cannot be the case if we understand only the first little part of the night, that is, only the beginning of the night, to be signified by the word 'evening'; for the beginning itself does not dawn toward the first day of the Sabbath, but the night itself, which begins to be ended by the light. For the end of the first part of the night is the beginning of the second part, but the light is the end of the whole night.

Whence the evening cannot be said to dawn toward the first day of the Sabbath, unless by the name of *evening* the night itself, which the light ends, is understood.

And it is a customary manner of speaking in Divine Scripture to signify the whole by a part: by *evening,* then, he signified the night, whose end is the dawn. For at dawn those women came to the tomb; and on this account they came in that night which was signified by the name of *evening.* For the whole was signified by that name, as I have said; and therefore, in whatever part of that night they had come, they would surely have come in that night; since therefore they came in the last part of that night, they came without doubt in that night. *But on the evening which dawns toward the first day of the Sabbath* cannot be understood except as that whole night; therefore they came in that evening who came in that night, and they came in that night who came even in the last part of that night.

**24.** 66. For the very three-day span, in which the Lord died and rose again, cannot be rightly understood except by this manner of speaking, by which the whole is wont to be named by a part. For he himself says: *for as Jonah was in the belly of the whale three days and three nights, so will the Son of man too be in the heart of the earth three days and three nights*[^291]. But the times are reckoned either from when he gave up his spirit or from when he was buried, and it does not come out clearly unless we take the middle day, that is, the whole Sabbath day, that is, with its night, but understand those days which are placed at either end, that is, the preparation and the first day of the Sabbath, which we call the Lord's day, as the whole by a part.

For what does it avail that some, hemmed in by these straits and not knowing that this manner of speaking, that is, the whole by a part, is of the greatest value in resolving the questions of the Holy Scriptures, wished to count as a night those three hours from the sixth to the ninth in which the sun was darkened, and as a day three other hours in which it was again restored to the earth, that is, from the ninth until its setting?

For then follows the night of the coming Sabbath, which, reckoned with its day, will now be two nights and two days; and further, after the Sabbath follows the night of the first day of the Sabbath, that is, of the dawning Lord's day, on which the Lord then rose;

there will therefore be two nights and two days and one night, even if it could be understood as whole, and we did not show that that dawn is its last part; wherefore, not even with those six hours counted in, of which for three the sun was darkened and for three it shone, will the reckoning of three days and three nights hold together.

It remains, then, that by that most usual manner of speaking of the Scriptures, by which the whole is understood by a part, we find the last day to be the time of the preparation, on which the Lord was crucified and buried, and from that very last part we take the whole day with its night, which had already passed; but the middle, that is, the day of the Sabbath, not by a part but entirely whole;

and the third, again, from its first part, that is, from the night, the whole with its daytime; and thus there will be a three-day span, just like those eight days after which he went up the mountain.

Of which, attending to the whole middle ones, Matthew and Mark said: *after six days*[^292], what Luke said: *after eight days*[^293].

**24.** 67. Now let us see the rest, how they agree with Matthew. For Luke most plainly says that two angels were seen by the women who came to the tomb[^294], whereas we had understood single angels to be recorded by the two, one by Matthew, that is, the one who sat outside the tomb on the stone[^295], the other by Mark, that is, the one who sat inside the tomb on the right[^296].

But Luke narrates thus: *and it was the day of the preparation, and the Sabbath was dawning. But the women who had come with him from Galilee followed after, and saw the tomb and how his body was laid. And returning, they prepared spices and ointments; and on the Sabbath they rested according to the commandment*[^297]. *But on the first day of the Sabbath, very early at dawn, they came to the tomb, carrying the spices they had prepared, and they found the stone rolled back from the tomb, and going in, they did not find the body of the Lord Jesus.*

*And it happened that, while they were perplexed in mind about this, behold, two men stood by them in shining garments. But as they were afraid and bowed their faces to the ground, they said to them: 'Why do you seek the living among the dead? He is not here, but has risen. Remember how he spoke to you while he was still in Galilee, saying that the Son of man must be handed over into the hands of sinful men and be crucified and on the third day rise again.' And they remembered his words. And going out from the tomb, they told all these things to the eleven and to all the rest*[^298].

How, then, were single angels seen sitting, one according to Matthew outside upon the stone and the other according to Mark inside on the right[^299], when according to Luke two stood by them[^300], although they said similar things? We can indeed still understand one angel to have been seen by the women both according to Matthew and according to Mark, as we said above, so that we take it that they entered the tomb into some space, namely, which was fortified by some wall, so that it could be entered before that rocky place of the sepulcher,

and there saw the angel sitting upon the stone rolled back from the tomb, as Matthew says, so that this is the one sitting on the right whom Mark mentions; then inside, as they looked at the place where the Lord's body had lain, they saw two other angels standing, as Luke says, who spoke similar things to exhort their mind and build up their faith.

**24.** 68. But let us see also the things that John says, whether and how they agree with these. John, then, narrates thus: *but on the first day of the Sabbath Mary Magdalene came early, while it was still dark, to the tomb, and saw the stone taken away from the tomb. She ran, therefore, and came to Simon Peter and to the other disciple whom Jesus loved, and said to them:*

*'They have taken the Lord from the tomb, and we do not know where they have laid him.' Peter therefore went out, and that other disciple, and they came to the tomb. Now the two ran together, and that other disciple ran ahead more quickly than Peter and came first to the tomb. And when he had stooped down, he saw the linen cloths lying, yet he did not go in. Simon Peter therefore came, following him, and went into the tomb, and saw the linen cloths lying and the napkin which had been over his head, not lying with the linen cloths but folded up separately in one place. Then that disciple too, who had come first to the tomb, went in, and saw and believed. For he did not yet know the Scripture, that he must rise from the dead. The disciples, therefore, went away again to their own homes. But Mary stood outside at the tomb, weeping. As she wept, then, she stooped down and looked into the tomb, and saw two angels in white sitting, one at the head and one at the feet, where the body of Jesus had been laid. They say to her: 'Woman, why are you weeping?' She says to them: 'Because they have taken away my Lord, and I do not know where they have laid him.' When she had said this, she turned back and saw Jesus standing, and did not know that it was Jesus. Jesus says to her: 'Woman, why are you weeping? Whom do you seek?'*

*She, thinking that he was the gardener, says to him: 'Lord, if you have carried him away, tell me where you have laid him, and I will take him away.' Jesus says to her: 'Mary.' She, turning, says to him: 'Rabboni,' which means 'Teacher.' Jesus says to her: 'Do not touch me, for I have not yet ascended to my Father; but go to my brothers and say to them: I ascend to my Father and your Father, to my God and your God.' Mary Magdalene came, announcing to the disciples: 'I have seen the Lord, and he said these things to me*[^301].' In this narration of John, the day or time at which they came to the tomb agrees with the rest; that too, that two angels were seen, accords with Luke[^302]; but he says the angels were seen standing, while this one says sitting. And the rest, which they do not say, how they can here be understood not to disagree from the rest, and in what order they were done, unless they are diligently considered, can seem contradictory.

**24.** 69. Wherefore let us arrange all these things which were done about the time of the Lord's resurrection, according to the testimonies of all the evangelists, into a certain single narrative, as far as the Lord shall help us, how they could have been done. On the first day of the Sabbath, at dawn, as all agree, they came to the tomb; and there had already happened what Matthew alone records about the earthquake and the stone rolled back and the guards so terrified that in some part they lay like dead men[^303].

And, as John says[^304], Mary Magdalene came — without doubt more fervent in love than the other women who had ministered to the Lord, so that not undeservedly John recorded her alone, those who were with her being passed over in silence, as the others attest. She came, then, and saw the stone taken away from the tomb; and before she looked at anything more carefully, not doubting that the body of Jesus had been taken from there, she ran, as the same John says, and announced it to Peter and to John himself. For he is the disciple whom Jesus loved. And they began to run to the tomb, and John, arriving first, stooped down and saw the linen cloths lying, yet did not go in; but Peter, following, went into the tomb *and saw the linen cloths lying and the napkin which had been over his head, not lying with the linen cloths but folded up separately*[^305].

Then John too went in and saw likewise and believed what Mary had said, that the Lord had been taken from the tomb. *For they did not yet know the Scripture, that he must rise from the dead*[^306]. *The disciples, therefore, went away again to their own homes. But Mary stood outside at the tomb, weeping*, that is, before that rocky place of the sepulcher, but yet within that space which they had already entered; for there was a garden there, as the same John records[^307]. Then they saw the angel sitting on the right upon the stone rolled back from the tomb, about which angel Matthew and Mark narrate. Then he said to them: *do not fear; for I know that you seek Jesus who was crucified. He is not here. For he has risen, as he said. Come and see the place where the Lord was laid. And going quickly, tell his disciples that he has risen; and behold, he goes before you into Galilee; there you will see him. Behold, I have foretold you*[^308].

Mark too did not keep silent about things similar to these[^309]. At these words, as Mary wept, she stooped down and looked into the tomb *and saw two angels,* as John says, *in white sitting, one at the head and one at the feet, where the body of Jesus had been laid. They say to her: 'Woman, why are you weeping?' She says to them: 'Because they have taken away my Lord, and I do not know where they have laid him*[^310].' Here the angels are to be understood to have risen, so that they were even seen standing, as Luke records that they were seen[^311], and to have said, according to the same Luke, to the women who feared and bowed their faces to the ground: *why do you seek the living among the dead? He is not here, but has risen. Remember how he spoke to you while he was still in Galilee, saying that the Son of man must be handed over into the hands of sinful men and be crucified and on the third day rise again.* And they remembered his words[^312].

After these things Mary turned back. *And she saw Jesus standing,* as John says, *and did not know that it was Jesus. Jesus says to her: 'Woman, why are you weeping? Whom do you seek?' She, thinking that he was the gardener, says to him: 'Lord, if you have carried him away, tell me where you have laid him, and I will take him away.' Jesus says to her: 'Mary.' She, turning, says to him: 'Rabboni,' which means 'Teacher.' Jesus says to her: 'Do not touch me, for I have not yet ascended to my Father; but go to my brothers and say to them: I ascend to my Father and your Father and my God and your God*[^313].' Then she went out from the tomb, that is, from that place where was the space of the garden before the hewn-out rock, and with her the others, whom, according to Mark, trembling and fear had seized, and they said nothing to anyone[^314].

Then now, according to Matthew: *behold, Jesus met them, saying: 'Hail.' But they approached and held his feet and worshiped him*[^315]. For so we gather both that they had the address of the angels twice over as they came to the tomb, and that of the Lord himself, once, namely, there, when Mary thought he was the gardener[^316], and now again, when he met them on the way, so that by the very repetition he might strengthen them and revive them from fear. *Then, therefore, he said to them: 'Do not fear; go, tell my brothers to go into Galilee; there they will see me*[^317].' *Mary Magdalene came, therefore, announcing to the disciples*[^318] that she had seen the Lord and that he had said these things to her — not she alone, but the others too whom Luke records, *who told these things to the eleven disciples and to all the rest*[^319]. *And these words seemed before them like idle talk, and they did not believe them*[^320].

To these things Mark too attests; for after he recorded that they went out from the tomb trembling and afraid and said nothing to anyone[^321], he added that the rising Lord appeared in the morning on the first day of the Sabbath *first to Mary Magdalene, from whom he had cast out seven demons,* and that *she, going, announced it to those who had been with him, as they mourned and wept,* and that *they, hearing that he was alive and had been seen by her, did not believe*[^322]. Matthew surely inserted this too, as the women who had seen and heard all those things were going away: that some too of those guards who had lain like dead men came into the city. And that they announced to the chief priests all that had happened, that is, the things which they too could perceive; and that those, gathered with the elders, having taken counsel, gave abundant money to the soldiers, that they might say that his disciples had come and stolen him while they slept, promising also security from the governor, who had given them as guards; and that they, having taken the money, did as they had been instructed, and that this word was spread abroad among the Jews to this present day[^323].

## In that he afterward manifested himself to the disciples, how all the evangelists are not opposed to one another.

**25.** 70. Now, after the resurrection, how the Lord appeared to the disciples must be considered, not only so that the agreement of the four evangelists may shine forth from this matter too[^324], but also so that they may accord with the apostle Paul, who speaks of this matter in the *First Epistle to the Corinthians* thus: *for I delivered to you first of all what I also received, that Christ died for our sins according to the Scriptures, and that he was buried, and that he rose on the third day according to the Scriptures, and that he appeared to Cephas, then to the twelve; then he appeared to more than five hundred brothers at once, of whom most remain until now, but some have fallen asleep; then he appeared to James, then to all the apostles; and last of all, as to one untimely born, he appeared also to me*[^325].

But this order none of the evangelists kept. Whence it must be considered whether the order which they kept is not opposed to this one. For neither did he say all things, nor did they say all things; but whether the things which they all said are in no way opposed to one another, this only must be seen. For Luke alone of the four evangelists does not say that the Lord was seen by the women, but only angels[^326]. But Matthew says that he met them as they returned from the tomb. Mark too says that he was first seen by Mary Magdalene[^327], as John also does; but how he was seen by her he does not say, which is set forth by John[^328].

But Luke not only is silent, as I said, that he appeared to the women, but even says that two, of whom one was Cleophas, spoke such things with him, before they recognized him, as though the women had announced nothing other than that they had seen angels, who said that he was alive. For he narrates thus: *behold, two of them were going that same day to a village which was at the distance of sixty stadia from Jerusalem, named Emmaus, and they were talking with each other about all these things that had happened. And it happened that, as they talked and questioned together, Jesus himself, drawing near, went with them; but their eyes were held, that they should not recognize him. And he said to them: 'What are these words that you exchange with one another as you walk, and you are sad?'*

*And one, whose name was Cleophas, answered and said to him: 'Are you the only stranger in Jerusalem who does not know the things that have happened there in these days?' And he said to them: 'What things?' And they said: 'About Jesus of Nazareth, who was a prophet, mighty in deed and word before God and all the people; how the chief priests and our rulers handed him over to the condemnation of death and crucified him. But we were hoping that he was the one to redeem Israel; and now, besides all this, today is the third day since these things were done. But some women too of ours astonished us, who were at the tomb before light, and not finding his body, came saying that they had even seen a vision of angels, who say that he is alive. And some of ours went to the tomb, and found it just as the women had said, but him they did not see*[^329].'

These things, according to Luke, they narrate as they could remember and recall what had been said by the women, or by the disciples who had run to the tomb, when it was announced to them that his body had been taken from there. And Luke himself indeed says that only Peter ran to the tomb and, stooping down, saw the linen cloths alone lying, and went away wondering with himself at what had happened[^330]. But he records this about Peter before he narrates about these two whom he met on the way, after he had narrated about the women who had seen the angels and heard from them that Jesus had risen, as though Peter then ran to the tomb. But it is understood that Luke set this down about Peter by recapitulation. For Peter then ran to the tomb, when John too did, when it had been announced to them only by the women, chiefly by Mary Magdalene, about the body taken away; and she had announced it then, when she saw the stone torn away from the tomb; and afterward these things were done about the vision of the angels and of the Lord himself, who had appeared to the women twice over, once at the tomb and again, meeting them as they returned from the tomb[^331]. But this was before he had been seen by those two on the way, of whom one was Cleophas. For Cleophas too, speaking with the Lord, whom he did not yet recognize, did not say that Peter went to the tomb; but: *some of ours went,* he says, *to the tomb, and found it just as the women had said,* which he too is understood to have said by recapitulation, namely, what the women first announced to Peter and John about the Lord's body taken away. And on this account, although Luke himself said that Peter ran to the tomb, and he himself related that Cleophas said that some of them had gone to the tomb, he is understood to attest to John that two went to the tomb; but he recorded only Peter first, to whom Mary first announced it[^332]. Again, it can be a stumbling block that the same Luke says Peter, not going in but stooping down, saw the linen cloths alone and departed wondering[^333], when John says that he himself rather saw thus — that is, that the disciple whom Jesus loved did not go into the tomb, to which he had come first, but, when he had stooped down, saw the linen cloths lying; but he says that he himself afterward went in[^334]. So Peter too is to be understood first, stooping down, to have seen what Luke records and John is silent about; but afterward to have gone in — yet to have gone in before John went in, so that all are found to have spoken the truth without any contradiction.

**25.** 71. The order of things, then, that could have been — how the Lord, apart from what he had already said to the women, appeared also to the male disciples — must be woven together and shown according to the testimonies not only of the four evangelists, but also of the apostle Paul. Of all the men, then, he is understood to have appeared first to Peter — of all those, at least, whom the four evangelists and the apostle Paul recorded. But if he appeared to any of them before Peter, which all kept silent about, who would dare either to say or to deny it?

For neither did Paul say, 'He appeared first to Cephas,' but: *he appeared,* he says, *to Cephas, then to the twelve; then he appeared to more than five hundred brothers at once*[^335]. But thus it does not appear which twelve, just as neither which five hundred.

For it can be that these twelve, whoever they were, were from the crowd of disciples. For those whom he named apostles he would now call not twelve but eleven, as some manuscripts indeed have, which I believe troubled men corrected, thinking it was said of those twelve apostles who, Judas now being dead, were eleven.

But whether those manuscripts have it more truly which have eleven, or whether Paul wishes some other twelve disciples to be understood, or whether he wished that sacred number to stand even at eleven, because the number twelve in them was so mystical that into Judas's place none but another, that is, Matthias, could be substituted, to preserve the mystery of that same number[^336];

whichever of these it be, nothing arises from it that seems opposed to the truth or to any of these most truthful narrators. Yet it is probably believed that, after he appeared to Peter, he then appeared to those two, of whom one was Cleophas, about whom Luke narrates the whole[^337], but Mark briefly touches it thus: *but after these things,* he says, *he was shown in another form to two of them as they walked, going into the country*[^338]. For we not absurdly take it that that village could also be called a country, which is now called Bethlehem, which formerly was wont to be called a city, although now it is of greater honor by the name of the Lord, who was born in it, so famed throughout the churches of all nations; and in the Greek manuscripts indeed we find *field* rather than *village;* but by the name of *field* not only villages but also towns and colonies are wont to be called outside the city which is the head and, as it were, the mother of the rest, whence it is called the metropolis.

**25.** 72. But what Mark says, that the Lord appeared to them in another form[^339], this Luke says, that their eyes were held, that they should not recognize him[^340]. For something had befallen their eyes which was permitted to remain so until the breaking of the bread, for the sake of a certain mystery, so that in him another form was shown to them, and so they recognized him only in the breaking of the bread, as is shown by Luke's narration.

For according to the desert of their mind, still ignorant that it behooved Christ to die and rise, their eyes suffered something similar, not by the truth deceiving, but by their being unable to perceive the truth and supposing something other than the reality, lest anyone should think he has recognized Christ if he is not a partaker of his body, that is, of the Church, whose unity the Apostle commends in the sacrament of the bread, saying: *we who are many are one bread, one body*[^341],

so that, when he handed them the blessed bread, their eyes might be opened and they might recognize him[^342] — opened, that is, to the knowledge of him, the impediment being removed by which they were held, that they should not recognize him. For they did not walk with their eyes closed; but there was in them something by which they were not allowed to recognize what they saw, which both a mist and some moisture is wont to cause. Not that the Lord could not transform his flesh, so that there really was another form, not the one which they were wont to behold, since indeed even before his passion he was transformed on the mountain, so that his face shone like the sun[^343]; for he who from true water made true wine makes of whatever body he wishes a true body from a true one[^344],

but he had not done so when he appeared to those two in another form. For he did not appear as he was to those whose eyes were held, that they should not recognize him. But we not unfittingly take it that this impediment in their eyes was from Satan, that Jesus should not be recognized; but yet the permission was given by Christ until the sacrament of the bread, so that, the unity of his body being shared in, the impediment of the enemy is understood to be removed, so that Christ can be recognized.

**25.** 73. For it must be believed that these are the same of whom Mark too narrates[^345], because he says that they, going, announced it to the rest, as Luke says that they, rising that same hour, returned to Jerusalem and found the eleven gathered together, and those who were with them, saying that the Lord had truly risen and had appeared to Simon, and that then they too narrated the things that had happened on the way and how they had recognized him in the breaking of the bread[^346].

Already, then, there was a report that Jesus had risen, made by those women and by Simon Peter, to whom he had already appeared; for these two found people speaking of this, to whom they came in Jerusalem. And so it can be that out of fear they at first, on the way, were unwilling to say that they had heard he had risen, when they said only that angels had been seen by the women; for, not knowing with whom they were speaking, they could rightly be anxious lest, by boasting indiscriminately of Christ's resurrection, they fall into the hands of the Jews.

But what Mark says: *they announced it to the rest, and they did not believe them*[^347], when Luke says that those there were already speaking of how the Lord had truly risen and had appeared to Simon[^348], what is to be understood except that there were some there who would not believe this? But to whom is it not clear that Mark passed over what Luke set forth in narrating, that is, what Jesus spoke with them before they recognized him, and how they recognized him in the breaking of the bread? Since, as soon as he said that he appeared to them in another form as they went into the country, he immediately joined: *and they, going, announced it to the rest, and they did not believe them*,

as though they could announce one whom they had not recognized, or those to whom another form of him had appeared could recognize him. How, then, they recognized him, so that they could announce him, Mark without doubt passed over. Which is therefore to be committed to memory, that we may accustom ourselves to notice the manner of the evangelists, who so pass over what they do not record, and join together what they do record, that, for those who have no practice in this consideration, error chiefly arises from no other source, whereby they think that they do not agree with one another.

**25.** 74. Luke, then, continues and says: *but while they were saying these things, Jesus stood in their midst and says to them: 'Peace to you; it is I, do not fear.' But disturbed and terrified, they thought they were seeing a spirit. And he said to them: 'Why are you disturbed, and why do thoughts arise in your heart? See my hands and my feet, that it is I myself; handle me and see, for a spirit does not have flesh and bones, as you see me to have.' And when he had said this, he showed them his hands and feet*[^349].

This showing of the Lord after the resurrection John too is understood to have recorded, speaking thus: *when therefore it was evening on that first day of the Sabbath, and the doors were shut where the disciples were gathered for fear of the Jews, Jesus came and stood in the midst and said to them: 'Peace to you.' And when he had said this, he showed them his hands and his side*[^350]. And on this account to these words of John can be joined those which Luke says but the same John passes over. For Luke continues thus: *but while they still did not believe and wondered for joy, he said: 'Have you anything here to eat?' And they offered him a piece of broiled fish and a honeycomb. And when he had eaten before them, taking the remains, he gave them to them*[^351].

To these words likewise can be joined those which Luke is silent about but John says: *the disciples therefore rejoiced when they saw the Lord. He said to them again, therefore: 'Peace to you. As the Father sent me, I also send you.' When he had said this, he breathed on them and said to them: 'Receive the Holy Spirit; whose sins you forgive, they are forgiven them, and whose you retain, they are retained*[^352].'

To these again let us join those which John passed over, but Luke records: *and he said to them: 'These are the words which I spoke to you while I was still with you, that all things must be fulfilled which are written about me in the Law of Moses and the prophets and the Psalms.' Then he opened their understanding, that they might understand the Scriptures, and he said to them: 'Thus it is written, and thus it behooved Christ to suffer and to rise from the dead on the third day, and that repentance and the forgiveness of sins be preached in his name to all the nations, beginning from Jerusalem; but you are witnesses of these things. And I send the promise of my Father upon you; but stay in the city until you are clothed with power from on high*[^353].'

Behold how Luke too recorded the promise of the Holy Spirit[^354], which we do not find made by the Lord except in the Gospel of John. Which is not to be noticed in passing, that we may remember how the evangelists attest to one another even about certain things which they themselves do not say, and yet knew to have been said. After these things Luke passes over all that was done and does not record it at all, except when Jesus ascended into heaven[^355],

and yet he so joins it as though this followed those words which he said, though this was done on the first day of the Sabbath, on which day the Lord rose, but that on the fortieth day, as the same Luke narrates in the *Acts of the Apostles*[^356].

But as for John's saying that the apostle Thomas was not then with them[^357] — since, according to Luke, those two, of whom one was Cleophas, returning to Jerusalem, found the eleven gathered together and those who were with them[^358] — it must without doubt be understood that Thomas went out from there before the Lord appeared to them as they were saying these things.

**25.** 75. Hence now John records another showing of himself made by the Lord to the disciples after eight days, where Thomas too was, who had not seen him before. *And after eight days,* he says, *his disciples were again inside, and Thomas with them; Jesus came, the doors being shut, and stood in the midst and said: 'Peace to you.' Then he says to Thomas: 'Put your finger here and see my hands, and bring your hand and put it into my side, and do not be unbelieving, but believing.' Thomas answered and said to him: 'My Lord and my God.' Jesus says to him: 'Because you have seen, you have believed; blessed are those who have not seen and have believed*[^359].'

This second vision of the Lord made among the disciples, that is, the one which John records in the second place, we could recognize in Mark, briefly touching it, as is his wont, were it not that what he there says is a stumbling block: *at last he appeared to the eleven as they reclined*[^360] — not because John was silent about their reclining[^361], for he could pass this over, but because this one said *at last,* as though he no longer appeared to them after this, whereas John is still going to narrate a third showing of himself at the Sea of Tiberias;

then, because the same Mark says, reproaching them *their unbelief and hardness of heart, because they had not believed those who had seen him risen*[^362] — namely, those two to whom, as they went into the country, he appeared after he rose, and Peter, to whom he is found in Luke to have appeared first[^363], and perhaps also Mary Magdalene and the other women who were with her, when he appeared to them both at the tomb and met them on the way as they returned from it.

For the same Mark weaves the narration thus: when he had briefly recorded those two to whom he appeared as they went into the country, that they announced it to the rest and were not believed: *at last,* he says, *he appeared to the eleven as they reclined, and reproached their unbelief and hardness of heart, because they had not believed those who had seen him risen*[^364]. How, then, *at last,* as though they no longer saw him? For that is the last when the apostles saw the Lord on earth, when he ascended into heaven, which was done on the fortieth day after his resurrection[^365].

Was he then going to reproach them for not having believed those who had seen him risen, when by then they had so often seen him themselves after the resurrection, and especially on the very day of his resurrection, that is, the first day of the Sabbath, now toward night, as Luke and John record?[^366] It remains, then, that we understand the same day of his resurrection, that is, the first day of the Sabbath, when after dawn Mary saw him and the other women with her, when Peter too did, and when also those two, of whom one was Cleophas, whom the same Mark too seems to record,

when, now toward night, those eleven, apart from Thomas, and those who were with them, when these too narrated to them what they had seen — that now Mark too, in his usual way, wished to record it briefly and therefore said *at last,* because on that day this was the last thing, as the night-time was now beginning, after those who came from the village, where they had recognized him in the breaking of the bread, returned to Jerusalem and found, as Luke says, those eleven and those who were with them now conversing about the Lord's resurrection and that he had been seen by Peter, to whom these too narrated what had happened on the way and how they had recognized him in the breaking of the bread[^367].

But there were surely there some who did not believe, whence what Mark says is true: *they did not believe them*[^368]. To these, then, as Mark says, as they reclined[^369], and as they were still speaking of these things, as Luke says, the Lord stood in their midst and said to them: *peace to you*[^370], as Luke and John say. But the doors were shut when he entered to them, which John alone records. And so, to the words of the Lord which Luke and John said he then spoke to the disciples, is interposed also that reproach of which Mark speaks, that they had not believed those who had seen him risen.

**25.** 76. But this again is a stumbling block, how Mark says the Lord appeared to the eleven as they reclined[^371], if that is the time of the Lord's day, now at the beginning of night, which Luke and John mention[^372]. For John plainly says that the apostle Thomas was not then with them, who, we believe, went out from there before the Lord entered to them, after those two, returning from the village, conversed with the eleven themselves, as is found in Luke. But Luke, in his narration, gives room by which it can be understood that, while these things were being said, Thomas first went out from there and the Lord afterward entered. But Mark, who says: *at last he appeared to the eleven as they reclined*[^373], compels us to confess that Thomas too was there, unless perhaps, though one was absent, he nevertheless wished to call them eleven, because that same apostolic society was then called by this number, before Matthias was substituted into Judas's place[^374]. Or if it is hard to take it so, let us take this, then: that after his many showings, by which he presented himself to the disciples through forty days, he also at last appeared to the eleven as they reclined, that is, on the fortieth day itself, and, because he was now about to ascend from them into heaven, wished on that day especially to reproach them for this, that they had not believed those who had seen him risen, before they themselves saw him, since surely after his ascension, as they preached the Gospel, even the nations were going to believe what they had not seen. For after that reproach the same Mark continues and says: *and he said to them: 'Go into the whole world and preach the Gospel to every creature. He who believes and is baptized will be saved; but he who does not believe will be condemned*[^375].' Being therefore about to preach this, that he who does not believe will be condemned, since surely he will not have believed what he did not see, ought not they themselves first to have been rebuked, that, before they saw the Lord, they did not believe those to whom he had first appeared?

**25.** 77. But that this was the last bodily presentation of the Lord on earth to the apostles, this too admonishes us to believe, which the same Mark continues thus: *but these signs will follow those who have believed: in my name they will cast out demons, they will speak with new tongues; they will take up serpents, and if they drink anything deadly, it will not harm them; they will lay hands on the sick, and they will be well.* Then he subjoins: *and the Lord indeed, after he had spoken to them, was taken up into heaven and sat at the right hand of God. But they, going forth, preached everywhere, the Lord working with them and confirming the word by the signs that followed.*

When therefore he says: *and the Lord indeed, after he had spoken to them, was taken up into heaven*[^376], he seems sufficiently to show that this was the last discourse he had with them on earth, although he does not seem to confine it to this in every way. For he does not say, 'after he had spoken these things to them,' but: *after he had spoken to them,* whence he admits, if necessity should compel, that this was not the last discourse, nor was this the last day on which he was present with them on earth, but that what was said, *after he had spoken to them, he was taken up into heaven,* can pertain to all the things which he spoke with them in all those days. But because the things we have said above rather persuade that this was the last day, than that the eleven, who with Thomas absent were ten, should be understood, therefore, after this discourse which Mark records, with those words also joined consequently, whether of the disciples or of himself, which are recorded in the *Acts of the Apostles*, it must be believed that the Lord was taken up into heaven[^377], namely on the fortieth day after the day of his resurrection.

**25.** 78. John, however, although he confesses that he passed over many things which Jesus did[^378], yet wished also to record a third presentation of himself made to the disciples after the resurrection, at the Sea of Tiberias, namely to seven disciples — Peter, Thomas, Nathanael, the sons of Zebedee, and two others whom he did not express by name — when they were fishing, when at his command, casting their nets on the right side, they drew out a hundred and fifty-three great fish, when also he questioned Peter three times whether he was loved by him, and commended his sheep to him to be fed, and foretold his own passion[^379], and said about John himself: *so I wish him to remain until I come*[^380]. With this John ended his Gospel.

**25.** 79. Now it must be asked when he was first seen by the disciples in Galilee, because this too which John narrates in the third place was done in Galilee at the Sea of Tiberias[^381], which he easily sees who recalls that miracle of the five loaves[^382], which the same John begins to narrate thus: *after these things Jesus went away across the Sea of Galilee, of Tiberias*[^383]. But where can it be thought that he ought first to have been seen by the disciples after the resurrection except in Galilee, if the words of that angel be recalled who, according to Matthew, speaks thus to the women as they came to the tomb: *do not fear; for I know that you seek Jesus who was crucified; he is not here, for he has risen, as he said; come and see the place where the Lord was laid. And going quickly, tell his disciples that he has risen; and behold, he goes before you into Galilee; there you will see him. Behold, I have told you*[^384],

according to Mark—whether he was an angel or someone else: *do not*, he says, *be alarmed. You seek Jesus of Nazareth, who was crucified; he has risen, he is not here; behold the place where they laid him. But go, tell his disciples and Peter that he goes before you to Galilee; there you will see him, as he told you*[^385]?

These words seem to imply that Jesus was not going to show himself to the disciples after the resurrection except in Galilee. Yet Mark himself records no such showing; he says that early on the first day of the week Jesus first appeared to Mary Magdalene, and that she announced this to those who had been with him as they mourned and wept, but that they did not believe;

and after this he appeared to two of them as they were going into the country, and they announced it to the rest, which happened—as Luke and John jointly attest—in Jerusalem on that very day of the resurrection, now at nightfall. Then Mark comes to that manifestation of his which he calls the last, as they reclined, the eleven[^386]; and after this he says that he was taken up into heaven, which we know happened on the Mount of Olives, not far from Jerusalem. Nowhere, then, does Mark record the fulfillment of what he testifies was foretold by the angel.

Matthew, for his part, says nothing else and records no other place at all, either before or afterward, where the disciples saw the Lord after he had risen except in Galilee, in accordance with the angel's prediction. Then, after he had reported what was said to the women by the angel, and had added, as they went away, what was done about the guards who had been bribed to lie[^387], he goes straight on, as though nothing else followed (for indeed it had been so said by the angel: *he has risen, and behold he goes before you to Galilee, there you will see him*[^388], so that nothing else seemed bound to follow): *now the eleven disciples*, he says, *went to Galilee, to the mountain where Jesus had appointed them. And seeing him they worshiped him; but some doubted. And Jesus came and spoke to them, saying: 'All authority in heaven and on earth has been given to me. Go therefore and teach all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit, teaching them to observe all that I have commanded you. And behold, I am with you all days, even to the consummation of the age*[^389].' Thus Matthew closed his Gospel.

**25.** 80. And so, if the narratives of the others, when considered, did not compel a more careful inquiry, we would suppose nothing other than that after the resurrection the Lord was first seen by the disciples nowhere but in Galilee. Likewise, if Mark had been silent about that prediction of the angel[^390], someone might suppose that Matthew said the disciples went to the mountain in Galilee and there worshiped the Lord precisely so that what he had related as commanded and foretold through the angel might appear to be fulfilled.

As it is, however, both Luke and John show clearly enough that on the very day of his resurrection the Lord was seen by his disciples in Jerusalem[^391]—from which Galilee is so far away that he could not be seen by them in both places on one day—while Mark likewise relates that prediction of the angel but nowhere records that the Lord was seen by the disciples in Galilee after the resurrection. These facts strongly compel us to ask in what sense it was said: *behold he goes before you to Galilee, there you will see him*[^392].

For even Matthew himself, if he had not said at all that the eleven disciples went to the mountain in Galilee where Jesus had appointed them, and there saw and worshiped him, we would suppose that nothing of this prediction was fulfilled literally, but that the whole was foretold in a figurative sense—like that saying in Luke: *behold, today and tomorrow I cast out demons and accomplish cures, and on the third day I shall be perfected*[^393], which certainly was not fulfilled literally.

Again, if the angel had said: "*he goes before you to Galilee*, there you will see him first," or "there only will you see him," or "you will see him nowhere but there," Matthew would without doubt contradict the other evangelists. But since it was said: *behold he goes before you to Galilee, there you will see him*, and it was not stated when this would happen—whether as soon as possible, before he had been seen by them elsewhere, or after they had also seen him somewhere besides Galilee—and since the very statement that Matthew makes, that the disciples went to the mountain in Galilee, does not specify the day nor frame the order of narration so as to force us to understand nothing other than that this happened first: he does not in fact contradict the narratives of the others and leaves room for them to be understood and accepted. Nevertheless, that the Lord directed that he be seen not where he was first to show himself, but in Galilee, where he was necessarily seen afterward—both through the angel who said, *behold he goes before you to Galilee, there you will see him*, and through himself when he said, *go, tell my brothers to go to Galilee; there they will see me*[^394]—makes any believer intent on inquiring in what mystery the saying is to be understood.

**25.** 81. But first we must consider when he could have been seen even bodily in Galilee, since Matthew says: *now the eleven disciples went to Galilee, to the mountain where the Lord had appointed them, and seeing him they worshiped him. But some doubted*[^395]. For it is clear that it was not on the very day he rose; that he was seen in Jerusalem on that day at nightfall Luke and John agree most plainly[^396], though Mark not so plainly. When, then, did they see the Lord in Galilee? Not in the way John reports at the Sea of Tiberias[^397]; for then there were seven of them and they were found fishing—but in the way Matthew reports, where the eleven were on the mountain, to which Jesus had gone before them according to the angel's prediction.

For he seems so to narrate it that they found him there because he had certainly gone before them as appointed. It was therefore not on the very day he rose, nor in the following eight days, after which John says the Lord appeared to the disciples—where Thomas, who had not seen him on the day of his resurrection, first saw him. For surely, if within those same eight days the eleven had already seen him on the mountain in Galilee, how did Thomas, who was among the eleven, first see him after eight days? Unless someone says that it was not those eleven who were already then called "apostles," but eleven disciples out of the large number of disciples who were there. For only those eleven were as yet called apostles, but they were not the only disciples. It can therefore be that not all but some of them were there, and other disciples were with them so as to make up eleven, and that thus Thomas, who first saw the Lord after those eight days, was not there. For Mark, when he mentioned those eleven, did not say merely "the eleven," but: *he appeared*, he says, *to the eleven*[^398].

Luke too says: *they returned to Jerusalem and found the eleven gathered together, and those who were with them*[^399]. And he too shows that those eleven were the apostles. For when he added: *and those who were with them*, he made it clear enough that those eleven were named with greater eminence, with whom were the rest, and that hence those are understood who were already called apostles. So it could happen that out of the number of the apostles and of other disciples eleven disciples were made up who saw Jesus on the mountain in Galilee within those eight days.

**25.** 82. But another difficulty arises to stand in the way. For John, when he reported that the Lord was seen not on the mountain by the eleven but at the Sea of Tiberias by seven who were fishing, says: *this was now the third time that Jesus was manifested to his disciples after he had risen from the dead*[^400]. But if we take it that the Lord was seen by some eleven disciples within those eight days, before Thomas had seen him, then this at the Sea of Tiberias will not be the third manifestation but the fourth. Here we must take care that no one suppose John said "the third time" as though only three manifestations of his took place; rather, he is to be understood as having referred this to the number of days, not to the number of the manifestations themselves, nor to consecutive days, but to intervals, as he himself attests. For on the very first day of his resurrection, apart from his being seen by the women—that is, what is clear in the Gospel—he manifested himself three times: once to Peter,

again to the two of whom one was Cleopas, a third time to many as they were already conversing at the beginning of the night; but all this John, referring it to one day, counts as one. Then again, that is, on another day, when Thomas too saw him; and then a third time at the Sea of Tiberias, that is, on the third day of his manifestation, not at the third manifestation. And so we are compelled to understand that after all these things it happened that the eleven disciples saw him on the mountain in Galilee, according to Matthew, where he had gone before them as appointed, so that there might be fulfilled even literally what he had foretold both through the angel and through himself.

**25.** 83. We find, then, in the four evangelists that the Lord is recorded to have been seen by people after the resurrection ten times: once at the tomb by the women, again by the same women as they were returning from the tomb on the way[^401], a third time by Peter[^402], a fourth time by the two going to the village[^403], a fifth time by many in Jerusalem where Thomas was not present[^404], a sixth time where Thomas saw him[^405], a seventh time at the Sea of Tiberias[^406], an eighth time on the mountain in Galilee according to Matthew[^407], a ninth time, which Mark mentions, *last, as they reclined*, because they were no longer going to feast with him on earth[^408], a tenth time on that same day, no longer on earth but lifted up into a cloud as he ascended into heaven[^409], which Mark and Luke record—Mark indeed after the appearance to them as they reclined, continuing so as to say: *and the Lord, after he had spoken to them, was taken up into heaven*[^410]. Luke, however, passing over all that could have been done by him with the disciples during the forty days, joins silently to that first day of his resurrection, when he appeared to many in Jerusalem, the last day on which he ascended into heaven, narrating thus: *and he led them out to Bethany, and lifting up his hands he blessed them. And it came to pass, as he blessed them, that he withdrew from them and was carried up into heaven*[^411]. They therefore saw him, besides what they had seen on earth, also while he was being carried into heaven. So often, then, in the books of the Gospel is he recorded to have been seen by people before he had ascended into heaven: nine times, that is, on earth, and once in the air as he ascended.

**25.** 84. But not all things were written, as John acknowledges[^412]. For he was frequently in company with them during the forty days, before he ascended into heaven[^413]. Yet he had not appeared to them through all forty days in a row. For after the first day of his resurrection John says that another eight days intervened, after which he appeared to them again[^414], and a third time at the Sea of Tiberias[^415]—perhaps on the very next day, for nothing forbids it—and thereafter, when he willed, appointing for them, as he had also foretold beforehand, that he would go before them to the mountain in Galilee; and altogether throughout those forty days, as often as he willed, to whom he willed, and in whatever way he willed; just as Peter says, when he was preaching about him to Cornelius and those who were with him: *we who*, he says, *ate and drank with him after he rose from the dead, for forty days*[^416],

not that they ate and drank with him daily for forty days; for that would contradict John, who put those eight days in between, during which he was not seen by them, so as to be manifested a third time at the Sea of Tiberias. From then on, even if he was seen by them daily and feasted with them, nothing is contradicted. And perhaps for this reason it was said: *for forty days*, which are four tens, signifying the mystery either of the whole world or of the whole temporal age, because even those first ten days, in which the eight days fell, can—according to the manner of the Scriptures—be reckoned not inconsistently as the whole from a part.

**25.** 85. Let us compare, then, what the Apostle Paul says, to see whether it raises any difficulty. *He rose*, he says, *on the third day according to the Scriptures, and he appeared to Cephas*[^417]. He did not say "he appeared first to Cephas[^418]," for that would contradict what we read in the Gospel, that he first appeared to the women[^419].

*Then*, he says, *to the twelve*[^420]: to whomever, at whatever hour you please, yet on the very day of the resurrection.

*Then he appeared to more than five hundred brothers at once*[^421],

whether these were gathered with the eleven behind closed doors for fear of the Jews—from which Thomas had gone out when Jesus came to them—or after those eight days at whatever time, it presents nothing contrary[^422].

*Then*, he says, *he appeared to James*; but we should not take it that James saw him then for the first time, but in some manifestation properly and singularly his own.

*Then to all the apostles*[^423], and not to them then for the first time, but so that he might now associate with them more familiarly until the day of his ascension[^424]. *Last of all*, he says, *as to one untimely born, he appeared also to me*[^425], but this now from heaven, after no small time from his ascension.

**25.** 86. Now let us see what we had deferred: for the sake of what mystery, according to Matthew and Mark, the risen Lord gave this command: *I will go before you to Galilee, there you will see me*[^426],

which, although it was fulfilled, was nevertheless fulfilled only after many things, since it was so commanded (though without prejudging any necessity) that one expected either this alone or this first to have to take place. Beyond doubt, then, since this utterance is not that of an evangelist narrating that it happened so, but of the angel by the Lord's command, and afterward of the Lord himself—while it is the evangelist who narrates, but narrates that it was so said by the angel and by the Lord—it is to be taken as said prophetically.

For *Galilee* is interpreted as either "migration" or "revelation." Taking first the meaning of "migration," what else suggests itself to be understood by *he goes before you to Galilee, there you will see him*[^427], except that the grace of Christ was to migrate from the people of Israel to the nations?

And the apostles, preaching the Gospel to them, would in no way be believed unless the Lord himself prepared the way in the hearts of men; and this is what is understood by *he goes before you to Galilee*. And that they would marvel with joy, the difficulties broken through and overcome, as the door was opened to them in the Lord through the enlightenment of believers—this is what is understood by *there you will see him*; that is, "there you will find his members, there you will recognize his living body in those who have received you."

But according to the sense in which *Galilee* is interpreted as "revelation," he is now to be understood not in the form of a servant but in that form in which he is equal to the Father[^428], which he promised in John to those who love him, when he said: *and I will love him and will show myself to him*[^429]. Not, of course, according to what they already saw, and what even as he rose he afterward showed, with the scars, not only to be seen but also to be touched[^430], but according to that ineffable light by which he enlightens every man coming into this world, according to which it shines in the darkness and the darkness does not comprehend it[^431].

He went before us to that place from which, in coming to us, he did not depart, and to which, going before us, he did not abandon us. That will be the revelation, as it were the true Galilee, when we shall be like him; there we shall see him as he is[^432]. This will also be the more blessed migration from this world into that eternity, if we so embrace his commands that we deserve to be set apart at his right hand. For then those on the left will go into eternal burning, but the just into eternal life[^433].

From here they will migrate to that place, and there they will see him in a way that the ungodly do not see. For *the ungodly one will be taken away, that he may not see the glory of the Lord*[^434], and the ungodly will not see the light. *This, however*, he says, *is eternal life, that they may know you, the one true God, and Jesus Christ whom you have sent*[^435], as he is known in that eternity, to which he will lead his servants through the form of a servant, so that, set free, they may contemplate the form of the Lord[^436].
