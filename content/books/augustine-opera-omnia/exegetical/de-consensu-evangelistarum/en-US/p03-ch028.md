# On the raised daughter of the ruler of the synagogue, and the woman who touched the hem of his garment.

**28. 64.** Then Matthew goes on, still preserving the order of times: *as he was saying these things to them, behold, a certain ruler came and adored him, saying: 'My daughter has just now died, but come, lay your hand upon her, and she shall live*,' and the rest[^1], up to where he says, *and the girl rose. And this report went out into all that land*[^2].

The other two too, Mark and Luke, say this, but they now depart from this order. For they recall and insert it elsewhere—that is, in that place where he returns, by crossing the strait, from the region of the Gerasenes, after the demons were cast out and let go into the swine. For Mark joins this thus, after that deed among the Gerasenes: *and when Jesus had crossed over in the boat again across the strait*, he says, *a great crowd gathered to him, and he was beside the sea. And there came one of the rulers of the synagogue, named Jairus, and seeing him he fell down at his feet*, and the rest[^3].

And for this reason it is to be understood that this about the daughter of the ruler of the synagogue was indeed done when Jesus had crossed over in the boat again across the strait, but how long after does not appear. For unless there had been an interval, there would have been no time when there could have happened what Matthew just now narrated at the banquet in his own house[^4]; for, in the manner of the evangelists, he narrated as about another what had been done concerning himself and at his own house, after which deed nothing else immediately follows but this about the daughter of the ruler of the synagogue.

For he himself weaves it so that the very transition openly indicates that this is narrated as a consequence which was also done as a consequence, since indeed, when he had mentioned above what Jesus said about the new cloth and the new wine, he immediately subjoined: *as he was saying these things to them, behold, a certain ruler came*[^5]. And for this reason, if he came as he was saying these things, nothing else of his deeds and sayings was interposed.

But in the narration of Mark a place lies open where other things could have been interposed, as we have already shown. Similarly Luke too, when, after the miracle narrated among the Gerasenes, he passes to narrating about the daughter of the ruler of the synagogue, does not so pass as to resist Matthew, who shows that this was done after those similitudes about the cloth and the wine, by saying, *as he was saying these things*.

For he, when he had concluded what he had narrated as done among the Gerasenes, passes to something else in this manner: *now it came to pass*, he says, *when Jesus had returned, the crowd received him, for they were all awaiting him. And behold, a man whose name was Jairus, and he himself was a ruler of the synagogue, and he fell at the feet of Jesus*, and the rest[^6].

Thus it is understood that the crowd indeed received the Lord immediately, since they were awaiting him to return; but what he added—*and behold, a man whose name was Jairus*—is not to be taken as done immediately, but first that about the banquet of the publicans, as Matthew narrates[^7], to which Luke so joins this that nothing else can be understood to have been done as a consequence.

**28. 65.** In this narration, then, which we have now undertaken to consider, about the woman who suffered a flow of blood all these three evangelists agree without any question. For it makes no difference to the truth of the matter that something is left unsaid by one and said by another; nor that Mark says, *Who touched my garments?*[^8] and Luke, *Who touched me?*[^9] For one spoke in the customary way, the other in the proper way, yet both the same meaning. For we more customarily say, "You are tearing me," than, "You are tearing my garments," when nevertheless it is plain what we wish to be understood.

**28. 66.** But whereas Matthew narrates that the ruler of the synagogue announced to the Lord not that his daughter was about to die, or was dying, or was placed at the extremity of life[^10], but altogether dead, while those two say she was already near death, yet not yet dead[^11]—to such a degree that they say there came afterward those who announced her dead, and that for this reason the master ought no longer to be troubled, as though he were coming to keep her, by laying on his hand, from dying, not as one who could raise her when dead—

it must be considered, lest it seem to contradict, and it must be understood that, for the sake of brevity, Matthew rather wished to say that the Lord was asked to do what it is manifest that he did, namely, to raise her when dead; for he attended not to the words of the father about his daughter, but, what is more important, to his intention, and set down such words as his intention was.

For he had so despaired that he wished her rather to come to life again, not believing that she whom he had left dying could be found alive. Thus the two set down what Jairus said, but Matthew, what he wished and thought. Both, then, were asked of the Lord: that he should either make her well as she was dying or raise her when dead; but since Matthew had set out to say the whole briefly, he intimated that the father, beseeching, said what it is also certain he wished and Christ did. Indeed, if those two, or any of them, had mentioned that the father himself said what those coming from his house had said—that Jesus should no longer be troubled, because the girl was dead—the words which Matthew set down would contradict his thought. But now, as for that too—it is not read that he consented to those who announced it and forbade that the master should now come. And for this reason that too which the Lord said to him, *Do not fear, only believe, and she shall be saved*[^12], did not rebuke him as one distrusting, but more strongly confirmed him as one believing. For such was the faith in him as in the one who said, *I believe, Lord; help my unbelief*[^13].

**28. 67.** Since these things are so, through such varied but not contrary expressions of the evangelists we learn a thing plainly most useful and very necessary: that in anyone's words we ought to inspect nothing but the intention, which the words ought to serve, and that no one lies if he says in other words what he meant whose words he does not state—lest wretched word-catchers think the truth must be bound, as it were, to the tips of the letters, when surely not only in words, but also in all other signs of minds, nothing is to be inquired into but the mind itself.

**28. 68.** But as for the fact that some codices have, according to Matthew, *for the woman is not dead, but sleeps*[^14], when Mark and Luke testify that she was a girl of twelve years[^15], you may understand that Matthew spoke in the Hebrew manner. For in other places of the Scriptures too this is found, that not only those who had known a man, but altogether females, even untouched and intact, are called women, just as it is written of Eve herself, *he formed her into a woman*[^16], and that in the book of *Numbers*, where the women who had not known the bed of a male, that is, the virgins, are commanded to be kept[^17], lest they be killed; by which manner of speaking Paul too says that Christ himself was made of a woman[^18]. For this we understand better than that we should believe that girl of twelve years was already married or had known a man.
