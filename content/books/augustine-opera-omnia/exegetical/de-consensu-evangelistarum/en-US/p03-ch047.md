# That he walked upon the waters: how those who said this agree among themselves; and how they depart from that place where he fed the crowds with the five loaves.

**47. 99.** Matthew goes on and says: *and the crowd having been dismissed, he went up onto the mountain alone to pray. But when evening had come, he was there alone. But the boat in the midst of the sea was tossed by the waves. For the wind was contrary. But in the fourth watch of the night he came to them, walking upon the sea. And seeing him walking upon the sea, they were troubled, saying: 'It is a phantasm*[^1],' and the rest, up to where he says, *they came and adored him, saying: 'Truly you are the Son of God*[^2].'

Mark too, after the narrated miracle of the five loaves, continues in the same way: *and when evening had come, the boat was in the midst of the sea, and he was alone on the land. And he saw that they were toiling in rowing, for the wind was against them,* and so on[^3], in like manner, except that he said nothing about Peter walking on the water. But one must take care that this not be a stumbling block, what Mark said about the Lord when he was walking on the water: *and he meant to pass by them*[^4]. For how could they have understood this, except that he was going in another direction, meaning to pass them by as if they were strangers, since he was not recognized by them but was thought to be a ghost? And who is so dull that he would refuse to notice that this is to be referred to a mystical meaning? Yet when they were terrified and cried out, he came to their aid, saying: *take heart, it is I; have no fear*[^5]. How, then, did he mean to pass by those whom he so reassured in their fear, except that the intention of passing by served to draw out that cry which needed to be answered!

**47.** 100. John too lingers a little while with these. For after the narrated miracle of the five loaves, he likewise does not pass over in silence this matter of the struggling boat and of the Lord's walking upon the water, weaving it together thus: *Jesus therefore, when he perceived that they were about to come and take him by force to make him king, fled again to the mountain by himself. When evening came, his disciples went down to the sea. And getting into a boat, they came across the sea to Capernaum. It was now dark, and Jesus had not yet come to them. The sea rose because a strong wind was blowing,* and so on[^6]. Nothing here can seem contradictory, except that Matthew says that, having dismissed the crowds, he went up the mountain to pray there alone, while John says that he was on the mountain when he fed those same crowds with the five loaves.

But since John himself says that after that miracle he fled to the mountain lest he be seized by the crowds, who wanted to make him king, it is surely clear that they had come down from the mountain to the lower ground when those loaves were distributed to the crowds.

And therefore it is no contradiction that he went up the mountain again, as both Matthew and John say, except that Matthew said *he went up,* while John said *he fled,* which would be a contradiction only if in fleeing he did not go up.

Nor does this conflict, that Matthew said *he went up the mountain alone to pray*[^7], while John said: *when he perceived,* he says, *that they were about to come to make him king, he fled again to the mountain by himself*[^8]. For the reason for fleeing is not contrary to the reason for praying, since indeed the Lord, by this very act of transfiguring the body of our lowliness into himself, so as to make it conformed to the body of his glory[^9], teaches this too: that this is for us a great reason for praying, when there is a reason for fleeing.

Nor is this in opposition, that Matthew said he first ordered the disciples to get into the boat and go before him across the strait until he should dismiss the crowds[^10], and then, having dismissed the crowds, went up the mountain alone to pray; whereas John first records that he fled alone to the mountain, and then says: *when evening came,* he says, *his disciples went down to the sea, and getting into a boat*[^11], and so on.

For who does not see that John, recapitulating, afterward related as done by the disciples what Jesus had already ordered before he fled to the mountain, just as in speech one is wont to return in some way to something passed over? But because that return, made especially in the brevity and point of a moment, is not noted, those who hear it generally suppose that what is said afterward was also done afterward. So too those whom he had said got into the boat and came across the sea to Capernaum, he says the Lord came to them as they toiled on the sea, walking upon the water, which surely happened earlier, during that very voyage by which they were coming to Capernaum.

**47.** 101. Luke, however, after the narrated miracle of the five loaves, proceeds to something else and departs from this sequence. For he records nothing about that boat or about the Lord's way upon the water, but, having said: *and they all ate and were satisfied, and what was left over of the fragments was taken up, twelve baskets,* he then added: *and it happened that, as he was praying alone, the disciples were with him; and he asked them, saying: 'Who do the crowds say that I am?'*[^12]

thereupon narrating something else, not what those three related, who told of the Lord coming, walking upon the water, to the disciples as they sailed.

Nor should it be supposed that it was on that mountain to which Matthew said he went up to pray alone that he said to the disciples: *who do the crowds say that I am?*[^13]

(For Luke seems on this point to agree with Matthew, since he said *as he was praying alone,* where the other had said *he went up the mountain alone to pray*[^14]), but it was altogether elsewhere, when he was praying alone and the disciples were with him, that he asked this.

For Luke said he was alone, not without the disciples, as Matthew and John say, when they departed from him to go before him across the sea[^15]. For he most plainly added: *the disciples were with him.* Accordingly he said alone, that is, without the crowds, who did not dwell with him.
