# In the very miracle of the five loaves, how all four agree among themselves.

**46. 95.** Matthew, then, goes on, and to the very deed about the five loaves he leads his narration, in order, thus: *now when evening had come, his disciples came to him, saying: 'The place is desert, and the hour has now passed; dismiss the crowds, that, going into the villages, they may buy themselves food.' But Jesus said to them: 'They have no need to go; you give them to eat*[^1],' and the rest, up to where he says, *and the number of those who ate was five thousand men, besides women and children*[^2].

This miracle, then[^3], which all four evangelists mention and which are thought to differ somewhat among themselves, must be considered and treated, so that from it, for the rest of such cases too, the rules of expressions may be learned, by which, though so diverse, the same meaning is nevertheless retained and the same truth of things preserved. And the consideration is to be begun, indeed, not from Matthew according to the order of the evangelists, but rather from John, by whom this narration is so expressed that he even named the disciples with whom the Lord spoke about this matter. For he says thus: *when, therefore, Jesus had lifted up his eyes and seen that a very great multitude had come to him, he says to Philip: 'Whence shall we buy loaves, that these may eat?' But this he said, tempting him; for he himself knew what he was about to do. Philip answered him: 'Two hundred denarii worth of loaves are not enough for them, that each one may receive a little.' One of his disciples, Andrew, the brother of Simon Peter, says to him: 'There is one boy here who has five barley loaves and two fishes; but what are these among so many?' Jesus, therefore, said: 'Make the men recline.' Now there was much hay in the place. The men, therefore, reclined, in number about five thousand. Jesus, then, took the loaves, and when he had given thanks, distributed them to those reclining, and likewise of the fishes as much as they wished. And when they were filled, he said to his disciples: 'Gather up the fragments that are left over, lest they perish.' They gathered them up, therefore, and filled twelve baskets with the fragments from the five barley loaves which were left over by those who had eaten*[^4].

**46. 96.** Here it is not asked what this one said—of what sort the loaves were; for that they were barley loaves he was not silent, which the others were silent about. Nor is this asked, what this one did not say—that there were there also, besides the five thousand men, women and children, which Matthew says[^5]. And it ought now to be altogether certain, and to be held as a rule in such questions, that no one ought to be troubled when something is said by one which is passed over by another. But it is asked how the things which they said are all true, lest, while one narrates the narration of another, one exclude something else. For if the Lord, according to John's narration, the crowds having been observed, asked Philip, tempting him, whence food could be given them, it can trouble us how that be true which the others narrated—that the disciples first said to the Lord that he should dismiss the crowds, so that they could buy themselves food from the nearby places, to whom he answered, according to Matthew: *they have no need to go; you give them to eat*[^6]. To which Mark and Luke too agree, only passing over what he says, *they have no need to go*. It is understood, then, that after these words the Lord looked upon the multitude and said to Philip what John mentions, but these others passed over. Then what Philip answered in John, this Mark mentions was answered by the disciples, wishing it to be understood that Philip answered this from the mouth of the others—although they could also most customarily put the plural number for the singular. So what Philip says—*two hundred denarii worth of loaves are not enough for them, that each one may receive a little*[^7]—this is to say what Mark says: *going, shall we buy two hundred denarii worth of loaves and give them to eat?*[^8] But that the same Mark mentions the Lord said, *how many loaves do you have?*, the others passed over. And what Andrew, in John, suggested about the five loaves and two fishes[^9], this the others, putting the plural number for the singular, reported from the person of the disciples. And Luke indeed compressed the answer of Philip and the answer of Andrew into one sentence. For what he says—*we have no more than five loaves and two fishes*—he reported as Andrew's answer; but what he added—*unless perhaps we go and buy food for all this crowd*[^10]—seems to pertain to Philip's answer, except that he was silent about the two hundred denarii. Although this can be understood also in Andrew's own statement; for when he had said, *there is one boy here who has five barley loaves and two fishes*, he too added: *but what are these among so many?*[^11] This is to say, *unless perhaps we go and buy food for all this crowd.*

**46. 97.** From which whole variety of words, but concord of things and meanings, it sufficiently appears that we are wholesomely taught to seek in words nothing but the intention of those speaking, which all truthful narrators ought to be watchful to make plain, when they narrate something about a man, or about an angel, or about God; for the intention of these can be brought forth in words, lest they differ among themselves in anything about it.

**46. 98.** Indeed, it ought not to be passed over to direct the reader's attention in this place, and toward the other such cases that may perhaps occur, that Luke said they were ordered to recline by fifties, but Mark, both by fifties and by hundreds[^12]. This here does not trouble us for this reason, that one said a part, the other the whole.

For he who reported also about the hundreds reported what the other passed over; nothing, therefore, is contrary. But yet, if one mentioned only the fifties, the other only the hundreds, it would seem very much to be contrary; nor would it easily be discerned that both were said, but one mentioned by one, the other by the other; and yet, who would not confess that, more attentively considered, it ought to be found? This I have said for this reason, that there often arise some such things which seem, to those attending too little and judging rashly, contrary, and are not.
