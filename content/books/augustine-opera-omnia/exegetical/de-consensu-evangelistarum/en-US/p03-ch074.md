# On the discourse he had on the Mount of Olives.

**77.** 147. Matthew continues, saying: *and as he was sitting on the Mount of Olives, the disciples came to him privately, saying: 'Tell us, when will these things be, and what will be the sign of your coming and of the consummation of the age?' And Jesus answered and said to them: 'See that no one leads you astray. For many will come in my name, saying: I am the Christ, and they will lead many astray'*[^1], and so on down to where he says: *and these will go into eternal punishment, but the righteous into eternal life*[^2]. Now let us consider this lengthy discourse of the Lord according to the three evangelists Matthew, Mark, and Luke. For, keeping the same order, they weave these things together alike. Here indeed each says some things proper to himself, in which no suspicion of contradiction is to be feared; but about those things which they say alike, we must see that they nowhere be thought to be opposed to one another. For it cannot be said, if anything of the kind occurs, that it is something else said in like manner by the Lord elsewhere, since the narration of all three is occupied with the same place of all the events and times. Surely the fact that they do not all preserve the same order of the same sayings spoken by the Lord pertains in no way to the matter, either of understanding or of conveying it, so long as the things related as said by him are not opposed to one another.

**77.** 148. What Matthew therefore says: *and this Gospel of the kingdom will be preached in all the world as a testimony to all the nations, and then the end will come*[^3], Mark too records in the same order thus: *and the Gospel must first be preached to all the nations*[^4]. He did not say: *and then the end will come,* but this is what he signifies by saying *first,* that is: *and the Gospel must first be preached to all the nations,* because they had asked about the end. When therefore he says: *the Gospel must first be preached to all the nations,* he surely signifies *first,* before the end comes.

**77.** 149. Likewise, what Matthew says: *when therefore you see the abomination of desolation, which was spoken of by Daniel the prophet, standing in the holy place — let the reader understand*[^5], Mark says thus: *but when you see the abomination of desolation standing where it ought not — let the reader understand*[^6]. In which change of word he set forth the same meaning; for it is where it ought not because it ought not to be in the holy place. Luke, however, does not say: *when you see the abomination of desolation standing in the holy place,* or *where it ought not,* but says: *but when you see Jerusalem surrounded by an army, then know that its desolation has drawn near*[^7]. Then, therefore, the abomination of desolation will be in the holy place.

**77.** 150. But what Matthew says: *then let those who are in Judea flee to the mountains, and let him who is on the housetop not come down to take anything from his house, and let him who is in the field not turn back to take his cloak*[^8], Mark too records in nearly the same number of words[^9]. Luke, however, says: *then let those who are in Judea flee to the mountains*[^10].

This much like those two, but the rest otherwise. For he continues and says: *and let those who are in the midst of it depart, and let those who are in the country not enter it, for these are the days of vengeance, that all things which are written may be fulfilled*[^11].

It seems quite different, what those two said: *and let him who is on the housetop not come down to take anything from his house*[^12], and what this one says: *and let those who are in the midst of it depart,*

unless perhaps because there will be great confusion, with so great a danger pressing, that those whom the siege has shut in — which he signifies by saying: *those who are in the midst of it* — will be on the housetop, astonished and wishing to see what threatens or by what way they must escape.

But how does he say: *let them depart,* when above he said: *but when you see Jerusalem surrounded by an army?* For what follows: *let those who are in the country not enter it,* seems to belong to a fitting admonition, and it can be observed that those who are outside should not enter it; but those who are in the midst, how should they depart from an army when the city is already surrounded?

Or is to be *in the midst of it* this: when the danger will so press that one cannot escape in a temporal way to preserve the present life, and since then the soul ought to be ready and free, and not occupied and weighed down by fleshly desires — this is what is signified by what was said by the other two: *on the housetop,* or *upon the housetop,* so that what this one says, *let them depart,* that is, let them no longer be held by desire for this life, but be ready to migrate into another life;

this those two said: *let him not come down to take anything from his house,* that is, let him be inclined by no affection toward the flesh, as though about to receive some advantage from it,

and what this one says, *let those who are in the country not enter it,* that is, let those who by a good purpose of heart have already been placed outside it not again desire it with fleshly longing — this those said: *and let him who is in the field not turn back to take his garment,* as though to be wrapped again in the cares of which he had been stripped?

**77.** 151. But what Matthew says: *pray that your flight be not in winter or on the Sabbath*[^13], of this Mark said part and was silent about part: *but pray,* he says, *that it be not in winter*[^14]. Luke did not say this, yet he alone said something by which he seems to me to have illuminated this very meaning which these others set down obscurely. For he says: *but take heed to yourselves, lest perhaps your hearts be weighed down with dissipation and drunkenness and the cares of this life, and that day come upon you suddenly. For like a snare it will come upon all who sit upon the face of the whole earth. Watch therefore at all times, praying that you may be counted worthy to escape all these things that are to come*[^15].

This is understood to be the flight which Matthew records, which ought not to be in winter or on the Sabbath. Now to *winter* belong the cares of this life, which Luke set down openly, but to the *Sabbath* belong dissipation and drunkenness. For sad cares are like winter, whereas dissipation and drunkenness submerge and overwhelm the heart with fleshly gladness and luxury — an evil signified by the name of the *Sabbath* for this reason, that it was then, as it is now too, the worst custom of the Jews to abound in delights on that day, while they are ignorant of the spiritual Sabbath;

or if something else is to be understood in those words according to Matthew and Mark, let Luke too have said something else, provided only that no question of contradiction be raised. For we have not now undertaken to expound the Gospels, but to defend them against the calumnies of falsehood or deceit.

But the other things which Matthew set down in this discourse in common with Mark raise no question; and as for the things he has in common with Luke, Luke set them down not in this discourse, whose order agrees with this one, but elsewhere he either recalls such things and inserts them by anticipation, so as to record beforehand what was afterward said by the Lord, or he gives us to understand that they were said twice by the Lord, both now according to Matthew and then according to himself.
