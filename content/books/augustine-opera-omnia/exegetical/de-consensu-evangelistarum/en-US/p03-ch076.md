# On the supper in Bethany where the woman poured precious ointment over the Lord, how Matthew, Mark, and John agree with each other.

**79.** 154. Matthew continues from the place where we had made an end of the narration to be considered, and says: *then the chief priests and the elders of the people gathered together in the courtyard of the high priest, who was called Caiaphas, and took counsel to seize Jesus by guile and to kill him. But they said: 'Not on the feast day, lest perhaps there be an uproar among the people.' Now when Jesus was in Bethany, in the house of Simon the leper, a woman came to him having an alabaster jar of precious ointment, and poured it over the head of him as he reclined*[^1], and so on down to where he says: *what she has done will be told in memory of her*[^2]. Now let us consider the matter of the woman and the precious ointment which took place in Bethany. For although Luke records a similar deed and the name agrees of the man with whom the Lord was dining — for he too says it was Simon[^3] —

yet, since it is not against nature or against the custom of men that, if one man can have two names, much more two men can have one name, it is rather to be believed that this Simon was another, not the leper, in whose house this was done in Bethany. For neither does Luke say the event he narrates was done in Bethany, and although he does not record the city or village where it was done, yet his narration does not seem to be occupied with the same place. I therefore think nothing else is to be understood than that the woman who, as a sinner, came to the feet of Jesus and kissed them and washed them with tears and wiped them with her hair and anointed them with ointment — to whom the Lord, employing the likeness of the two debtors, said that many sins were forgiven her because she loved much — was indeed not a different woman then, but that this same Mary did this twice: once, namely, what Luke narrated, when, first approaching with that humility and those tears, she earned the remission of her sins. For this John too, although he did not narrate how it was done as Luke did, yet commended this same Mary by mentioning it, when he had now begun to speak of the raising of Lazarus, before he came to Bethany. Which he there narrates thus: *now a certain man was ill, Lazarus of Bethany, of the village of Mary and her sister Martha. And it was Mary who anointed the Lord with ointment and wiped his feet with her hair, whose brother Lazarus was sick*[^4]. In saying this John bears witness to Luke, who narrated that this was done in the house of a certain Pharisee, Simon[^5]; so then Mary had already done this. But what she did again in Bethany is another thing, which does not pertain to Luke's narration, but is narrated alike by the three, namely John, Matthew, and Mark[^6].

**79.** 155. Among these three, then — Matthew, Mark, and John — let us attend to how this agrees, concerning whom there is no doubt that they narrate the same event done in Bethany, where also the disciples, as all three record, murmured against the woman as though over the waste of most precious ointment. As to the fact, then, that Matthew and Mark say the Lord's head was anointed with that ointment, but John his feet, it is shown by that rule which we set forth when he fed the crowds with the five loaves that there is no contradiction.

For there, because there was not lacking one to record that they reclined both in fifties and in hundreds, while another said fifties[^7], it could not seem contradictory; but it could have seemed so if one had set down only hundreds, as another did fifties, and yet it would have had to be found that both took place.

By which example we ought to be instructed, as I admonished there, to understand that both took place even where the several evangelists record several things. Accordingly here too let us take it that the woman poured the ointment not only over the head but also over the feet of the Lord. Unless perhaps, because Mark records that the alabaster jar was broken and the head anointed[^8], someone is so absurd and calumnious as to deny that anything could have remained in the broken vessel with which to anoint the feet as well.

But when this man contends that it was so broken that nothing remained in it, striving against the truth of the Gospel, how much better and more reverently will another contend that it was not so broken as to pour out the whole, striving for the truth of the Gospel.

But if that calumniator is so obstinately blind as to try to break the concord of the evangelists over the broken alabaster jar, let him first take it that the feet were anointed before the jar was broken, so that there remained intact whatever was used to anoint the head as well, where that breaking would pour out the whole. For we recognize that provision is made for us in order, from the head, but in order too we ascend from the feet to the head.

**79.** 156. The rest of this matter seems to me to have no question. For the fact that the others say the disciples murmured about the pouring out of the precious ointment, while John records Judas, and that because he was a thief[^9], I think it clear that by the name of *the disciples* this same Judas was signified, by that manner of speech which we suggested concerning Philip in the matter of the five loaves[^10], the plural number being used for the singular. It can also be understood that the other disciples too either felt this or said it, or were persuaded of it when Judas said it, and that Matthew and Mark expressed in words the will of them all, but that Judas said it because he was a thief, while the rest spoke out of care for the poor; and that John wished to record it of that one alone, whose habit of stealing he believed should be intimated on this occasion.
