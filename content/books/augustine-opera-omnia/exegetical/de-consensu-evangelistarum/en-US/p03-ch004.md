# Why Matthew enumerates some forefathers of Christ, Luke others.

**3. 5.** But as for those whom it troubles that Matthew enumerates some forefathers, descending from David down to Joseph[^1], while Luke enumerates others, ascending from Joseph up to David[^2], it is easy for them to observe that Joseph could have had two fathers: one by whom he was begotten, the other by whom he was adopted. For the custom of adopting is an ancient one, even in that people of God, that they might make sons for themselves whom they had not themselves begotten. For, apart from the fact that Pharaoh's daughter had adopted Moses[^3] (for she indeed was a foreigner), Jacob himself adopted his grandsons born of Joseph, in most manifest words, saying: *Now therefore your two sons, who were born to you before I came to you, are mine: Ephraim and Manasseh shall be to me as Reuben and Simeon. But the children whom you beget afterward shall be yours*[^4]. Whence it also came about that there were twelve tribes of Israel, apart from the tribe of Levi, which served the Temple; for with it there were thirteen, while the sons of Jacob had been twelve. Whence it is understood that Luke took up, in his Gospel, the father of Joseph not by whom he was begotten, but by whom he had been adopted[^5], whose forefathers he might record upward until he came out to David.

For since it is necessary, both evangelists narrating the truth, namely both Matthew and Luke, that one of them held the origin of that father who had begotten, the other of him who had adopted Joseph, we understand it more probable that Luke held the origin of the one adopting than the one who refused to say that Joseph was begotten by him whose son he was narrating him to be. For he was more suitably called the son of him by whom he had been adopted, than he would be said to be begotten by him of whose flesh he was not born. But Matthew, saying, *Abraham begot Isaac, Isaac begot Jacob*[^6], and thus persevering in this word which is *begot*, until at the last he should say, *and Jacob begot Joseph*[^7], sufficiently expressed that he had led down the order of the begetters to that father by whom Joseph was not adopted, but begotten.

**3. 6.** And yet, even if Luke too had said that Joseph was begotten by Eli, not even thus ought this word to disturb us, so that we should believe anything other than that by one evangelist the begetting father, by the other the adopting father, was recorded. For it is not absurdly said that anyone has begotten, not by flesh but by love, the one whom he has adopted as a son for himself; or indeed even we, to whom God gave the power to become his sons[^8]—he did not beget us of his own nature and substance, as he begot the only Son, but surely adopted us by love[^9]. The apostle is understood often to use this word for no other reason than to distinguish the Only-begotten before all creation, through whom all things were made, who alone was born of the substance of the Father, according to the equality of divinity, altogether that which the Father is—whom he says was sent to take on flesh from that lineage in which we too are according to our nature, so that, by his partaking of our mortality through love, he might make us partakers of his divinity through adoption. For thus he says: *but when the fullness of time came, God sent his Son, made of a woman, made under the Law, that he might redeem those who were under the Law, that we might receive the adoption of sons*[^10]. And yet we are also said to be born of God, that is, having received the power to be made his sons, we who were already men, but are made so by grace, not by nature[^11]. For if we were sons by nature, we would never have been anything else.

For when John had said, *he gave them the power to become sons of God, to those who believe in his name*, he went on to say, *who were born not of bloods, nor of the will of the flesh, nor of the will of man, but of God*[^12]. Thus those whom he said were made sons of God, having received the power—which that adoption signifies which Paul mentions—the same he said were born of God[^13].

And, that he might show more openly by what grace it was done, *and the Word*, he says, *was made flesh and dwelt among us*[^14], as if he said: "What wonder is it, if they were made sons of God, since they were flesh, for whose sake the Only one was made flesh, since he was the Word?" With this great difference, indeed, that we, made sons of God, are changed for the better, but he, the Son of God, when he had been made the Son of man, was not changed for the worse, but took on what was inferior. James too says: *of his own will he begot us by the word of truth, that we might be some beginning of his creation*[^15]. Lest, namely, in that which he says *begot* we should think we are made that which he himself is, he therefore sufficiently shows that a certain primacy among creation was granted to us by this adoption.

**3. 7.** It would not, then, be alien from the truth, even if Luke said Joseph was begotten by the one by whom he had been adopted. For even thus he begot him, not that he might be a man, but that he might be a son, just as God begot us, that we might be his sons, whom he had made that we might be men. But the Only one he begot, not only that he might be the Son, which the Father is not, but also that he might be God, which the Father too is. But clearly, if Luke too had used this word, it would in every way be ambiguous which of them had recorded the adopting, which the father begetting from his own flesh; just as, even if neither of them said *begot*, but both this one called him the son of that man and that one of the other, it would be no less ambiguous which of them had recorded the one of whom he was born, which the one by whom he had been adopted. But now, since one says, *Jacob begot Joseph*[^16], the other, *Joseph, who was the son of Eli*[^17], even the very difference of the words has elegantly intimated what each had undertaken. But this could easily, indeed, as I said, occur to a religious man, who would judge that anything else whatsoever was rather to be sought than that he should believe an evangelist had lied; easily, I say, it would occur to him to see by what causes one man could have had two fathers. This would occur to those calumniators too, had they not preferred to wrangle rather than to consider.
