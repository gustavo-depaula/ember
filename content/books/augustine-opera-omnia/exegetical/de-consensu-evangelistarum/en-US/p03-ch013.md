# On the words of John among all four.

**12. 25.** Matthew, then, weaves together about John thus: *Now in those days came John the Baptist, preaching in the desert of Judea and saying: 'Repent, for the kingdom of heaven has drawn near.' For this is he who was spoken of through Isaiah the prophet, saying: 'A voice of one crying in the desert: prepare the way of the Lord, make straight his paths*[^1].' Mark too and Luke agree that this testimony of Isaiah is about John[^2]. For Luke mentioned several following words too from the same prophet, when he was narrating about John the Baptist. And John the Evangelist mentions that John the Baptist himself brought forward this same testimony of Isaiah about himself[^3], just as Matthew has now said certain words of John which the others did not say. *Preaching*, he says, *in the desert of Judea and saying: 'Repent, for the kingdom of heaven has drawn near.'* These words of John the others passed over. But now, as for what follows, which Matthew goes on and adds—*For this is he who was spoken of through Isaiah the prophet, saying: 'A voice of one crying in the desert: prepare the way of the Lord, make straight his paths'*—it is placed ambiguously, and it does not become clear whether Matthew mentioned this in his own person, or whether, still following the words of the same John, he added it, so that all this is understood to have been said by John: *Repent, for the kingdom of heaven has drawn near. For this is he who was spoken of through Isaiah the prophet*, and the rest. For this ought not to trouble us, that he does not say, "for I am he who was spoken of through Isaiah the prophet," but says, *For this is he who was spoken of*. For such a manner of speaking is wont to belong even to the evangelists Matthew and John themselves. For Matthew said, *he found a man sitting at the tax office*[^4], and did not say, "he found me"; and John says, *This is the disciple who bears testimony of these things and wrote these things; and we know that his testimony is true*[^5]—he did not say, "I am," or "my testimony is true." And the Lord himself very frequently says *the Son of man* or *the Son of God*[^6], and does not say "I"; and *it behooved*, he says, *Christ to suffer and to rise on the third day*[^7]—he does not say, "it behooved me to suffer." John the Baptist too, then, could, when he had said, *Repent, for the kingdom of heaven has drawn near*, add about himself what follows: *For this is he who was spoken of through Isaiah the prophet*, and the rest, so that, after his words, Matthew thus weaves the narrative: *Now John himself had a garment of camel's hair*, and the rest. And if this is so, it is no wonder if, when asked too what he said about himself, as John the Evangelist narrates, *I*, he says, *am the voice of one crying in the desert*[^8], just as he had already said, commanding that they repent. About his clothing, then, and his food, Matthew goes on thus, saying: *Now John himself had a garment of camel's hair and a leather belt around his loins. And his food was locusts and wild honey*[^9]. This Mark too says in almost the same words[^10], but the other two are silent.

**12. 26.** Matthew, then, goes on and says: *Then there went out to him Jerusalem and all Judea and all the region around the Jordan, and they were baptized by him in the Jordan, confessing their sins. But seeing many of the Pharisees and Sadducees coming to his baptism, he said to them: 'Brood of vipers, who has shown you to flee from the wrath to come? Bear, therefore, fruit worthy of repentance, and do not be willing to say within yourselves: We have Abraham as our father; for I say to you that God is able from these stones to raise up sons of Abraham. For already the axe is laid to the root of the trees; every tree, therefore, that does not bear good fruit is cut down and cast into the fire. I indeed baptize you in water unto repentance; but he who is to come after me is stronger than I, whose sandals I am not worthy to carry; he shall baptize you in the Holy Spirit and fire, whose winnowing fan is in his hand, and he shall thoroughly cleanse his threshing floor and gather his wheat into the barn, but the chaff he shall burn with unquenchable fire*[^11].'

All this Luke too says, expressing almost the same words of John[^12]. And where there is something different in the words, the same meaning is nevertheless not departed from—as when Matthew says that John said, *and do not be willing to say within yourselves: We have Abraham as our father*[^13], but the other, *and do not begin to say: 'We have Abraham as our father'*[^14]; the one, *I indeed baptize you in water unto repentance*[^15], the other interposes the question of the crowds, what they should do, and John answering them about good works as about the fruits of repentance[^16], which Matthew omitted;

then, while they were thinking in their hearts about him, whether he himself were the Christ, he says that he answered: *I indeed baptize you with water*[^17]—he did not say "unto repentance." Then Matthew: *but he who*, he says, *is to come after me is stronger than I*. But the other: *but there comes*, he says, *one stronger than I*[^18]. Likewise Matthew: *whose sandals*, he says, *I am not worthy to carry*[^19], but the other: *the strap of whose sandals I am not worthy to loose*[^20]. Which Mark too says, though he is silent about the rest. For after mentioning his clothing and food, he goes on and says: *and he preached, saying: 'There comes one stronger than I after me, the strap of whose sandals I am not worthy to stoop down and loose. I have baptized you with water, but he shall baptize you with the Holy Spirit*[^21].' About the sandals, then, this differs from Luke, that he added *stooping down*; but about the baptism it differs from both, because he did not say *and with fire*, but only *with the Holy Spirit*. For just as Matthew, so Luke too said, and in the same order, *he shall baptize you in the Spirit and fire*, except that Luke did not add *Holy*, as Matthew said, *in the Holy Spirit and fire*.

To these three John the Evangelist attests, when he says: *John bears testimony of him and cries out, saying: 'This was he of whom I said: He who comes after me has been made before me, because he was prior to me*[^22].' For thus he shows that he then said this, when they record that he said it to them; but that he repeated and recalled what he had already said, when he says, *This was he of whom I said: 'He who comes after me.'*

**12. 27.** If, then, it is asked which words John the Baptist rather said—whether those which Matthew, or those which Luke records him to have said, or those which Mark set down in the very few he recorded him to have said, being silent about the rest—he who prudently understands judges that this is in no way to be labored over, since the meanings themselves are necessary for the knowing of the truth, by whatever words they were expressed. For that one keeps one order of words, another another, is surely not contrary. Nor is it contrary if one says what another passes over. For as each one remembered, and as it was to each one's mind, whether more briefly or more at length, to express the same meaning, so it is manifest that they expressed it.

**12. 28.** And in this it sufficiently appears—which pertains most of all to the matter—that, since the truth of the Gospel, proceeding from the Word of God, which abides above all creation eternal and unchangeable, obtained the highest summit of authority through creation, dispensed by temporal signs and the tongues of men,

we ought not to think that anyone lies if, several persons recalling a thing which they heard or saw, the same thing is nevertheless indicated not in the same manner and the same words; or if the order of words is changed, or other words are brought forth for others which nevertheless mean the same; or if something is said less—either because it did not occur to the one recalling, or because it can be understood from other things that are said; or if, for the sake of narrating other things which one decides rather to tell, so that a fitting measure of time may suffice, each one undertakes that something is not to be set forth by him in full, but to be touched on in part; or if, to illuminate and make clear the meaning, he to whom authority of narrating has been granted adds nothing indeed of the things, yet something of the words; or if, holding the matter well, he does not attain, although he tries this, to enunciate from memory in full the very words too which he heard.

But whoever says that it ought certainly to have been granted to the evangelists, through the power of the Holy Spirit, that they should not differ in the kind of words, nor in the order, nor in the number, does not understand that, the more the authority of the evangelists excels, by so much the more, through them, the security of the rest of men who speak the truth was to be confirmed: so that, when several happen to narrate the same thing, no one of them can in any way be rightly accused of falsehood, if he has so differed from another that he can be defended even by the preceding example of the evangelists.

For since it is not lawful either to think or to say that any of the evangelists lied, so it will appear that neither did he lie to whom, in recalling, some such thing happened as is shown to have happened to them. And the more it pertains to the best morals to beware of falsehood, the more we ought to have been governed by so eminent an authority, that we should not think there are falsehoods when we find the narrations of certain persons varying among themselves as they have varied among the evangelists. At the same time too, which pertains most of all to faithful teaching, we should understand that the truth, not so much of words as of things, is to be sought or embraced, since we approve those who do not use the same expression, when they do not differ in the things and the meanings, as having stood firm in the same truth.

**12. 29.** What, then, in the things which I have set forth from the narrations of the evangelists, compared, is to be thought contrary? Is it that one said, *whose sandals I am not worthy to carry*[^23], but the others, *to loose the strap of his sandal*[^24]? For it seems to be different, not in words or in the order of words or in some kind of expression, but even in the thing itself, that it is one thing *to carry the sandals*, another *to loose the strap of the sandal*. With reason, then, it can be asked what John said he was not worthy to do—whether to carry the sandals or to loose the strap of the sandal. For if he said one of these, that one seems to have narrated the truth who could narrate this which he said; but he who narrated the other, even if he did not lie, will certainly be thought either to have forgotten, or to have said one thing for another.

But it is fitting that all falsehood be absent from the evangelists—not only that which is brought forth by lying, but also that which arises through forgetting. And so, if it pertains to the matter to understand something else from what is said, *to carry the sandals*, and something else from what is said, *to loose the strap of the sandal*, what else would you rightly suppose is to be taken, except that John said both, whether one at one time and the other at another, or in one connected statement? For he could say thus: "whose sandal strap I am not worthy to loose, nor to carry his sandals," so that one of the evangelists took up one thing from this, the others another, yet all narrated the truth.

But if John intended nothing, when he spoke of the Lord's sandals, except his excellence and his own humility, whichever of these he said—whether about loosing the strap of the sandals or about carrying the sandals—he nevertheless held the same meaning; whoever, even with his own words, through the mention of the sandals, expressed the same signification of humility, did not thereby stray from the same intention. A useful manner, then, and most of all to be committed to memory, when we speak of the agreement of the evangelists: that there is no falsehood when each, even saying something else which the one of whom he narrates something did not say, nevertheless sets forth that intention of his which the one who mentions his words also sets forth. For thus we wholesomely learn that nothing else is to be sought than what he who speaks means.

On the baptism of Jesus.

**13. 30.** Matthew, then, goes on and says: *Then Jesus came from Galilee to the Jordan, to John, to be baptized by him. But John tried to prevent him, saying: 'I ought to be baptized by you, and you come to me?' But Jesus, answering, said to him: 'Allow it for now; for thus it is fitting for us to fulfill all justice.' Then he allowed him*[^25]. The rest too attest that Jesus came to John. And three mention that he was baptized[^26], but they are silent about what Matthew says—that John said it to the Lord, or that the Lord answered John.
