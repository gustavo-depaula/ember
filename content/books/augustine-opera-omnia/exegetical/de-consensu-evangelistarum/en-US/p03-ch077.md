# Where he sends the disciples to prepare for him to eat the Passover.

**80.** 157. Matthew continues: *then one of the twelve, who is called Judas Iscariot, went to the chief priests and said to them: 'What are you willing to give me, and I will hand him over to you?' And they fixed for him thirty pieces of silver*[^1], and so on down to where he says: *and the disciples did as Jesus had appointed them, and they prepared the Passover*[^2].

Nothing in this chapter can be thought contradictory to Mark and Luke, who narrate this same thing in like manner[^3]; for what Matthew says: *go into the city to a certain man and say to him: 'The Teacher says: My time is near; at your house I keep the Passover with my disciples*[^4],' he signifies the one whom Mark and Luke call the householder or master of the house, in which the upper room was shown to them, where they should prepare the Passover.

What Matthew therefore interposed, *to a certain man,* as it were in his own person, for the sake of brevity, he wished to convey that man by a shorthand. For if he were to say that the Lord said, 'Go into the city and say to it: The Teacher says, My time is near; at your house I keep the Passover,' it would be taken as though it were to be said to the city. And on this account he interposed, not in the person of the Lord, whose command he was narrating, but in his own, that the Lord ordered them to go to a certain man, lest he need to say the whole, since this seemed to him sufficient to convey the meaning of the one commanding.

For who does not know that no one speaks so as to say 'go to a certain man'? For if he were to say 'go to whomever,' or 'to anyone you please,' the expression could be complete, but the man to whom they were sent would not be definite, whereas Mark and Luke show that he was definite, although his name is not stated[^5]. For the Lord knew to whom he was sending them. And so that those whom he was sending might be able to find him, he forewarned them what sign they should follow, about a man carrying a jar or jug of water, so that, following him, they might come to the house he wished.

Since therefore it could not be said here 'go to anyone you please,' which the completeness of the expression indeed admits, but which the truth of this matter that was being conveyed does not admit in this place, how much less could it be said here 'go to a certain man,' which can never at all be said by correct speech! But it is plainly clear that the disciples were sent by the Lord not to anyone whatever, but to a certain man, that is, to some definite person. Which the evangelist could rightly narrate to us in his own person, so as to say that he sent them to a certain man, that they should say to him, 'At your house I keep the Passover.' He could also have done it thus: he sent them to a certain man, saying, 'Go, say to him: At your house I keep the Passover.'

And on this account, when he had set down the words of the Lord saying, *go into the city,* he himself interposed *to a certain man,* not because the Lord said this, but that he himself might convey to us that there was, with his name unstated, a certain man in the city to whom the Lord's disciples were sent to prepare the Passover. And so, after this interposition of two words in his own person, he follows the order of the words of the Lord saying: *and say to him: 'The Teacher says.'*

For now if you ask to whom, it is rightly answered: to that certain man to whom the evangelist conveyed that he sent them, when in his own person he had interposed *to a certain man.* A less usual expression, indeed, but yet, understood thus, it is perfectly complete;

or, if the Hebrew language, in which Matthew is reported to have written, has some property such that even the whole, said in the person of the Lord, does not lack completeness of expression, let those see to it who know.

Surely it could be said so even in Latin, if one were to say: 'Go into the city to a certain man, whom the man who meets you carrying a jar of water will point out to you.' In this way the one commanding could be obeyed without ambiguity, just as also if he were to say: 'Go into the city to a certain man who lives in such and such a place, in such and such a house'; with the expression of the place and the designation of the house it could be understood, it could be done.

But with these and other such signs left unsaid, one who says 'go to a certain man, and say to him' cannot for this reason be heeded, because he wishes a definite person to be understood when he says 'to a certain man,' and does not express how he is to be recognized. But if we take that interposed phrase as said in the person of the evangelist, the expression will indeed be somewhat obscure by the necessity of brevity, but yet complete. As for the fact that Mark says a jar which Luke calls a jug, the one signified the kind of vessel, the other the measure, and each preserved the truth of the meaning.

**80.** 158. Matthew continues: *now when evening had come, he reclined with the twelve disciples, and as they were eating he said: 'Truly I say to you that one of you will hand me over.' And being greatly grieved, they began each one to say: 'Is it I, Lord?'*[^6]. And so on down to where he says: *but Judas, who handed him over, answered and said: 'Is it I, Rabbi?' He said to him: 'You have said it*[^7].' In these things which we have now proposed for consideration the other evangelists too, who record such things, raise no question[^8].
