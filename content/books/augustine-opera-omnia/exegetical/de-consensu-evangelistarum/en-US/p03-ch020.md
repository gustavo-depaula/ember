# How Matthew says that the centurion came to him on behalf of his servant, when Luke says that he sent friends to him.

**20. 48.** After these things Matthew goes on and says: *but when he had entered Capernaum, a centurion came to him, beseeching him and saying: 'Lord, my servant lies at home paralyzed and is grievously tormented*[^1],' and the rest, up to where he says: *and the servant was healed from that hour*[^2]. This about the centurion's servant Luke too mentions—not, like this one, after the cleansed leper, whom he afterward, recalling, mentioned,

but after the end of that lengthier discourse, which he joins thus: *but when he had completed all his words in the ears of the people, he entered Capernaum. Now a certain centurion's servant, being ill, was about to die, who was precious to him*[^3], and the rest, up to the place where he was healed[^4]. Here it is to be understood that, when he had indeed completed all his words in the ears of the people, Christ entered Capernaum—that is, that he did not enter before he had concluded these words, but that it is not expressed after how great an interval of time, when he had concluded these discourses, he entered Capernaum. For in that very interval that leper was cleansed, whom Matthew interposes in his own place, but this one afterward recalls.

**20. 49.** Now then let us see whether Matthew and Luke agree among themselves about this servant of the centurion. For Matthew says: *a centurion came to him, beseeching him and saying: 'My servant lies at home paralyzed*[^5].' To which seems to be opposed what Luke says: *and when he had heard about Jesus, he sent to him the elders of the Jews, beseeching him to come and save his servant. But when they had come to Jesus, they besought him earnestly, saying to him: 'He is worthy that you should grant him this; for he loves our nation, and he himself built us our synagogue.' And Jesus was going with them. And when he was now not far from the house, the centurion sent friends to him, saying: 'Lord, do not be troubled; for I am not worthy that you should enter under my roof, for which reason I did not even judge myself worthy to come to you; but say the word, and my servant shall be healed*[^6].' For if this was done thus, how will what Matthew narrates be true—*a certain centurion came to him*—when he himself did not come, but sent friends,

unless, attending diligently, we understand that Matthew did not in every way forsake the customary manner of speaking? For we are accustomed to say that something has approached even before it arrives where it is said to approach—whence we also say, "it has approached a little," or, "it has approached much," toward that which it seeks to reach—but we also for the most part say that the very arrival, for the sake of attaining which one approaches, has been made, even if the one to whom he arrives does not see the one who arrives, when through a friend he reaches someone whose favor is necessary to him. Which custom has so held that now even commonly those are called "reachers" who, by the interposition of suitable persons, attain by the art of solicitation the as-it-were-inaccessible minds of any powerful persons. If, then, the arrival itself is customarily said to be made through others, how much more can an approach be made through others, which for the most part remains short of arrival, since one could indeed approach very much, but yet could not arrive! Not absurdly, then, did Matthew wish to say in brief—what even commonly can be understood, the approach of the centurion to the Lord having been made through others—*a centurion came to him*.

**20. 50.** Nevertheless, the depth of the holy evangelist's mystical expression must not be carelessly regarded, according to which it is written in the *Psalm*: *Come to him and be enlightened*[^7]. Accordingly, because the Lord himself so praised the faith of the centurion, by which one truly comes to Jesus, that he said, *I have not found such faith in Israel*[^8], the prudent Evangelist wished to say that the centurion himself rather came to Christ, than those through whom he had sent his words. But Luke, for this reason, disclosed the whole, how it was done, so that from this we might be compelled to understand in what way the other, who could not lie, said that he came. For thus also that woman who suffered a flow of blood, although she touched the hem of his garment, nevertheless touched the Lord more than those crowds by whom he was pressed[^9]. For as she, the more she believed, the more she touched the Lord, so the centurion too, the more he believed, the more he approached the Lord. The rest in this chapter, which one says and another passes over, are gone over superfluously, since, by that rule first commended, they are found to have nothing contrary.
