# On the two whom their father commanded to go into the vineyard, and on the vineyard that was let out to other husbandmen.

**70.** 133. Matthew continues: *but what do you think? A man had two sons, and going to the first he said: 'Son, go, work today in my vineyard.' But he answered and said: 'I will not'; yet afterward, moved by repentance, he went. And going to the other he said the same; but he answered and said: 'I go, sir'; and he did not go*[^1], and so on down to where he says: *and whoever falls on this stone will be broken to pieces; but on whomever it falls, it will crush him*[^2]. Mark and Luke do not record this matter of the two sons who were commanded to go and work in the vineyard, but what Matthew next narrates about the vineyard let out to husbandmen, who persecuted the servants sent to them and afterward killed the beloved son and cast him out of the vineyard[^3], those two also do not pass over in silence, keeping the same order, that is, after the Jews, questioned about the baptism of John, said they did not know, and he answered them: *neither will I tell you by what authority I do these things*[^4].

**70.** 134. No question of any contradiction therefore arises here, except that Matthew, when he had said that the Lord asked the Jews: *when the lord of the vineyard comes, what will he do to those husbandmen?* subjoins that they answered and said: *he will wretchedly destroy those wretches and will let out the vineyard to other husbandmen, who will render him the fruit in their seasons*[^5]. Mark records this as answered not by them, but that the Lord, speaking consequently after his question, in some way answered himself. For he says thus: *what then will the lord of the vineyard do? He will come and destroy the tenants and give the vineyard to others*[^6]. But it can easily be understood either that their utterance was subjoined in such a way that there was not interposed 'they said' or 'they answered,' yet it was understood, or else that this answer was attributed rather to the Lord because, when they said what was true, he too answered this concerning them, he who is the truth[^7].

**70.** 135. But this is more of a stumbling block, that Luke not only does not say they answered this — attributing these words too, like Mark, to the Lord — but even relates that they returned the contrary answer, saying: *God forbid.* For he narrates thus: *what then will the lord of the vineyard do to them? He will come and destroy those tenants and give the vineyard to others. And hearing this, they said: 'God forbid.' But he looked at them and said: 'What then is this that is written: 'The stone which the builders rejected has become the head of the corner'?'*[^8].

How, then, according to Matthew, did those to whom he was speaking these things say: *he will wretchedly destroy those wretches and will let out the vineyard to other husbandmen, who will render him the fruit in their seasons*[^9], when according to Luke they contradicted such words, saying: *God forbid?* And indeed what the Lord said next, about the stone rejected by the builders and made the head of the corner, was brought in so that by this testimony those who contradicted the parable might be confuted. For Matthew too records this as said to those who were contradicting, when he says: *have you never read in the Scriptures: 'The stone which the builders rejected has become the head of the corner'?*[^10] For what is *have you never read* except that they had answered something that was contrary? Mark too signifies this, who relates these very words thus: *and have you not read this Scripture: 'The stone which the builders rejected has become the head of the corner'?*[^11] Which meaning, according to Luke, appears more clearly said in its place after their contradiction, by which they said: *God forbid.* For it amounts to the same thing, as he too sets it down: *what then is this that is written: 'The stone which the builders rejected has become the head of the corner'?*[^12] For this is the intention of the meaning that he conveys, whether by *have you never read,* or *have you not read this,* or *what then is this* that is written.

**70.** 136. It remains, therefore, that we understand that, among the people who were listening, some answered what Matthew records, saying: *they say to him: 'He will wretchedly destroy those wretches and will let out his vineyard to other husbandmen'*[^13], but others answered what Luke did not pass over in silence, that is: *God forbid.* To these, then, who had given that answer to the Lord, those others answered: *God forbid;* but it was the answer of those to whom these said: *God forbid.*

For this reason it was attributed to the Lord both by Mark and by Luke, because, as I have said, through them the truth itself spoke, whether through those who did not know, if they were evil — as through Caiaphas, who, not knowing what he said, prophesied because he was high priest[^14] — or through those who knew and now understood and believed.

For there too was that multitude through whom the prophecy had already been fulfilled, when, meeting him with great festivity as he came, they cried out: *blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord*[^15].

**70.** 137. Nor let it be a stumbling block that the same Matthew said the chief priests and the elders of the people came to the Lord and asked by what authority he was doing these things and who had given him this authority, when he in turn asked them about the baptism of John, where it was from, from heaven or from men[^16]; and when they answered that they did not know, he said: *neither will I tell you by what authority I do these things*[^17].

For from there he continued, speaking without break, and said: *but what do you think? A man had two sons*[^18], and the rest, and according to Matthew the discourse is woven together without the interposition of any thing or person down to where the vineyard let out to husbandmen is recorded. For it can be supposed that he said all these things to the chief priests and elders of the people, by whom he had been questioned about his authority.

And if these had questioned him as testers and enemies, the answer cannot be understood as coming from those who had believed and had borne to the Lord that clear testimony from the prophet, who even now could have answered, not in ignorance but in belief: *he will wretchedly destroy those wretches and will let out the vineyard to other husbandmen*[^19]. This ought not at all to disturb us, so that we should suppose on that account that there were no believers in that multitude which was then listening to the parables of the Lord.

For the same Matthew, for the sake of brevity, passed over what Luke does not pass over — namely, that this parable was spoken not to those alone who had asked about his authority, but to the people. For he says thus: *and he began to speak this parable to the people: 'A man planted a vineyard*[^20],' and the rest. In this people, then, it is to be understood that there could also have been those who listened to him as those did to whom they had said: *blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord*[^21], and that they, or some of them, were the ones who answered: *he will wretchedly destroy those wretches and will let out the vineyard to other husbandmen*[^22].

Mark and Luke attributed their answer to the Lord not only because he himself had said this, since he is the truth[^23] — which often speaks even through the evil and the ignorant, by a certain hidden prompting moving a person's mind, not by the merit of his holiness but by the right of his own power — but also because they could have been such that they were now reckoned, as it were, members in the very body of the Lord, so that their utterance was rightly attributed to him whose members they were, since he had already baptized more than John and had crowds of disciples[^24], as the evangelists themselves often attest, and from whom were also those five hundred brethren to whom the apostle Paul records that he appeared after the resurrection[^25]; especially since, according to the same Matthew, it was not said: *they say to him: 'He will wretchedly destroy those wretches,'* in such a way that in the word *illi* the plural number is to be taken, as though that answer were of those who had craftily questioned him about his authority, but *they say to him* was said, that is, 'they to the Lord himself,' with a singular pronoun, not a plural, which appears in the Greek manuscripts without any scruple of ambiguity.

**70.** 138. There is a certain discourse of the Lord in the evangelist John, where what I am saying can more easily be understood. *Jesus therefore said,* he says, *to those Jews who had believed in him: 'If you continue in my word, you will truly be my disciples, and you will know the truth, and the truth will set you free.' And they answered him: 'We are the seed of Abraham, and we have never been slaves to anyone. How do you say: 'You will be free'?' Jesus answered them: 'Truly, truly, I say to you, everyone who commits sin is a slave of sin; and the slave does not remain in the house forever. But the son remains forever; if therefore the Son sets you free, you will be truly free. I know that you are sons of Abraham, but you seek to kill me, because my word finds no place in you*[^26].'

He surely would not have said to those who had already believed in him, to whom he had said: *if you continue in my word, you will truly be my disciples,* the words: *you seek to kill me;* but because he had said this to those who had already believed in him, while that multitude was present which had more enemies,

even though the evangelist does not state who those were who answered, from this very fact — that they answered, and that they then deserved to hear from him what they did — it sufficiently appears which words are to be attributed to which persons. As, therefore, in this multitude according to John there were those who had already believed in Jesus, and there were also those who were seeking to kill him, so in that multitude of which we are now speaking there were those who had craftily questioned the Lord by what authority he did those things[^27], and there were also those who had cried out, not craftily but faithfully: *blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord*[^28].

And on this account there were those who would say: *he will destroy them and will let out his vineyard to others*[^29]. Which utterance is rightly understood to have been the Lord's own as well, whether on account of the truth or on account of the unity of his members with their head. There were also those who would say to those answering such things: *God forbid*[^30], because they understood that the parable was spoken against themselves.
