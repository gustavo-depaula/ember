# Where, having pity on the crowds, he sent his disciples, giving them power to grant healings, and gave them many commands, ordering how they should live.

**30. 70.** Hereafter, indeed, the order of the things done does not appear. For after these two deeds about the blind men and the mute demon, he goes on thus: *and Jesus went about all the cities and villages, teaching in their synagogues and preaching the Gospel of the kingdom and curing every illness and every infirmity. But seeing the crowds, he had pity on them, because they were harassed and lying down like sheep having no shepherd. Then he says to his disciples: 'The harvest indeed is great, but the laborers are few; ask, therefore, the Lord of the harvest to send out laborers into his harvest.' And calling together his twelve disciples, he gave them power over unclean spirits*, and the rest[^1], up to where he says, *amen I say to you, he shall not lose his reward*[^2].

In this whole passage which we have now mentioned, he gave his disciples many admonitions; but whether Matthew subjoined them in order, or whether his recollection was the order of his narrating, as has been said, does not appear. This passage Mark seems to have touched on briefly, and he entered upon it thus, saying: *and he went about the villages round about, teaching. And he called together the twelve and began to send them out two by two, and gave them power over unclean spirits*, and the rest[^3], up to where he says, *shake off the dust from your feet in testimony against them*[^4].

But before Mark narrated this, he first, after the raised daughter of the ruler of the synagogue, narrated that in which they marveled at the Lord in his own country, whence he had such great wisdom and powers, when they knew his kindred[^5]—which Matthew mentions after this admonition of the disciples and after many other things[^6]. And so it is uncertain whether Matthew recalled this, which takes place in his own country, having omitted it, or whether Mark anticipated it, recalling it; which of them kept the order of the thing done and which the order of his recollection. But Luke immediately, after the resurrection of the daughter of Jairus, subjoins this passage about the power and admonition of the disciples[^7], as briefly indeed as Mark, nor he in such a way that this appears to follow even in the order of the things done. In the names, then, of the disciples, Luke, who names them by another name when they are first chosen on the mountain, does not differ from Matthew except in the name of Judas the son of James, whom Matthew calls Thaddeus[^8], but some codices have Lebbaeus. But who would ever forbid that one man be called by two or three names?

**30. 71.** Likewise it is wont to be asked how Matthew and Luke mention that the Lord said to the disciples that they should not even carry a staff[^9], when Mark says: *and he charged them that they should take nothing on the way except a staff only*, and he too goes on: *no bag, no bread, nor money in the belt*[^10], so that he shows that his narration is engaged in the same passage in which that of those too who said that not even a staff should be carried.

This is resolved so that we understand that the staff which, according to Mark, is to be carried was said under one signification, and that which, according to Matthew and Luke, is not to be carried, under another; just as *temptation* is understood under one signification, of which it is said, *God tempts no one*[^11], and under another, of which it is said, *the Lord your God tempts you, that he may know whether you love him*[^12]. The former is of seduction, the latter of testing; just as *judgment* is taken in one way, of which it is said, *those who have done good, to the resurrection of life; those who have done evil, to the resurrection of judgment*[^13], and in another, of which it is said, *Judge me, O God, and distinguish my cause from a nation not holy*[^14]. For that *judgment* is of condemnation, this of discernment.

**30. 72.** And there are many other words which do not have one signification, but, fittingly placed in different places, are understood in different ways, and are sometimes said with an explanation, as is that: *do not become children in your senses, but in malice be little ones, that in your senses you may be perfect*[^15]. For this could be said briefly in a closed sentence: "do not be children, but be children." And that: *if anyone among you thinks himself wise in this world, let him become a fool, that he may be wise*[^16].

For what else did he say than: "let him not be wise, that he may be wise"? But sometimes they are said so closed that they exercise the inquirer, as is what he says to the Galatians: *bear one another's burdens, and so you shall fulfill the law of Christ. For if anyone thinks himself to be something, when he is nothing, he deceives himself. But let each one prove his own work, and then he shall have glory in himself and not in another. For each one shall bear his own burden*[^17].

Unless you take the word *burden* under different significations, you will without doubt think that he is contrary to himself in speaking, and this in one sentence with the words placed so close together—he who, when he had said a little before, *bear one another's burdens*, afterward said, *each one shall bear his own burden*. But the burdens of sharing infirmity are one thing, those of rendering an account to God of our acts another: the former, to be sustained, are shared with the brethren; the latter, as one's own, are borne by each one. Thus also *staff* is understood spiritually, of which the Apostle said, *shall I come to you with a staff?*[^18] and corporeally, which we use whether for a horse or for whatever else there may be need—to forbear, for the moment, the other figurative significations of this word.

**30. 73.** Both, then, must be taken as said by the Lord to the apostles: both that they should not carry a staff and that they should carry only a staff. For when, according to Matthew, he said to them, *Do not possess gold nor silver nor money in your belts, no bag for the way nor two tunics nor sandals nor a staff*, he immediately subjoined: *for the laborer is worthy of his food*[^19].

Whence he sufficiently shows why he wished them not to possess and carry these things—not that they are not necessary for the sustenance of this life, but because he was so sending them that he showed these things were owed to them by those very ones to whom, believing, they would announce the Gospel: as wages to those serving as soldiers, as the fruit of the vineyard to those who plant it, as the milk of the flock to those who shepherd it.

Whence Paul says: *who ever serves as a soldier at his own expense? Who plants a vineyard and does not eat of its fruit? Who shepherds a flock and does not partake of the milk of the flock?*[^20] For here he was speaking of the things that are necessary for the preachers of the Gospel. Whence a little after he says: *if we have sown spiritual things for you, is it a great thing if we reap your carnal things? If others partake of this power over you, do we not much more? But we have not used this power*[^21].

Whence it appears that the Lord did not so command these things as though the evangelists ought to live from no other source than from those providing for them, to whom they announce the Gospel—otherwise the same Apostle acted against this command, who got his living from the labors of his own hands, lest he be a burden to anyone[^22]—but that he gave a power, in which they might know that these things were owed to them. But when something is commanded by the Lord, unless it is done, it is the fault of disobedience.

But when a power is given, it is lawful for anyone not to use it, and, as it were, to yield his own right. Speaking these things to the disciples, then, the Lord was doing that which the Apostle himself a little after more openly sets forth thus: *do you not know that those who work in the temple eat of the things of the temple? That those who serve the altar share with the altar? So too the Lord ordained for those who announce the Gospel to live from the Gospel. But I have used none of these things*[^23].

And so, when he says that the Lord thus ordained, but that he himself did not use it, he surely shows that a power of using was given, not a necessity of serving imposed.

**30. 74.** Ordaining this, then—what the Apostle says he ordained, that those who announce the Gospel should live from the Gospel—the Lord spoke those things to the apostles, that, secure, they should neither possess nor carry the things necessary for this life, neither great nor small. Therefore he set down *not even a staff*, showing that all things are owed to his ministers by his faithful, who require nothing superfluous. And for this reason, by adding *for the laborer is worthy of his food*[^24], he altogether opened and illuminated whence and why he spoke all these things.

This power, then, he signified by the name of the staff, when he said: *that they should take nothing on the way except a staff only*[^25]. For it could even be said thus briefly: "carry with you none of the necessary things, not even a staff, except a staff only," so that what was said, *not even a staff*, is understood, "not even the smallest things"; but what was added, *except a staff only*, is understood, because, through the power received from the Lord, which was signified by the name of the staff, even the things that are not carried will not be lacking. Both, then, the Lord said; but because not both are thought to have been mentioned by one evangelist, the one who said that the staff, set under one signification, was to be taken away, is thought to be contrary to the one who, again, said that the staff, signifying something else, was not to be taken away; but, the reason now rendered, let him not be so thought.

**30. 75.** So too, when Matthew says that sandals are not to be carried on the way[^26], he forbids the care by which they are thought to be carried lest they be lacking. This is to be understood also of the two tunics, lest any of them should think that, besides the one he was wearing, another was to be carried, anxious lest there be need, when from that power he could receive it.

Accordingly Mark, by saying that they were shod with sandals or soles, admonishes that this footwear has some mystical signification, so that the foot be neither covered nor bare to the ground—that is, that the Gospel be neither hidden nor lean upon earthly advantages. And as for the fact that he forbids two tunics to be carried or had, but more expressly to be worn, saying, *and that they should not be clothed with two tunics*[^27], what does he admonish them but to walk not doubly, but simply?

**30. 76.** Thus it is in no way to be doubted that the Lord said all these things, partly properly, partly figuratively; but that the evangelists inserted into their writings, one these, another those, and that certain of the same things either two or three of them, whichever, or all four, set down—nor yet that all things were so written which were either said or done by him. But whoever thinks the Lord could not, in one discourse, set down some sayings figuratively, some properly, let him inspect the rest of his discourses; he will see how rashly and unlearnedly he thinks this. For this one can—to say one thing that for the moment occurs to my mind—since he admonishes that the left hand should not know what the right hand does[^28], think that the very almsgiving, and whatever else he there enjoins, is to be taken figuratively.

**30. 77.** I admonish again, indeed—what the reader ought to remember, lest he very often need such admonition—that in various places of his discourses the Lord already repeated many things which he had said elsewhere, lest perhaps, when the order of the very passages does not agree with that of another evangelist, anyone think there is here something contrary to one of them, when he ought to understand that what was already said elsewhere is said again elsewhere; and that this must be observed not only of sayings, but also of deeds. For nothing forbids us to believe this same thing was done again. But to calumniate the Gospel, while one does not believe that what no one proves could not be done again was done again, is of sacrilegious vanity.
