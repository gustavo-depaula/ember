# Why forty generations, apart from Christ himself, are found in Matthew, when he makes fourteen threefold.

**4. 8.** But that which is next to be intimated truly required a most attentive and diligent reader, that it might be noticed and seen. For it has been acutely observed that Matthew, who had set out to intimate the royal person in Christ, named, apart from Christ himself, forty men in the series of generations[^1]. Now this number signifies that time in which, in this world and on this earth, we must be ruled by Christ according to the laborious discipline by which, as it is written, *God scourges every son whom he receives*[^2], of which the Apostle says that we must enter the kingdom of God through tribulation[^3]. This is signified also by that iron rod of which we read in the *Psalm*: *you shall rule them with an iron rod*[^4], when he had said above: *but I have been set up by him as king over Zion, his holy mountain*[^5]. For even the good are ruled with an iron rod, of whom it is said: *it is time that judgment begin at the house of the Lord; and if it begins with us, what will be the end of those who do not believe the Gospel of God? And if the just man is scarcely saved, where shall the sinner and the impious appear*[^6]? To whom pertains what follows: *you shall shatter them like a potter's vessel*[^7]. For the good are ruled through this discipline, but the evil are broken in pieces—who are mentioned as though the same persons, on account of the one and the same sacraments which the evil have in common with the good.

**4. 9.** Since, then, this number is a sacrament of this laborious time, in which, under the discipline of Christ the king, we contend against the devil, it also declares this: that the fast of forty days, that is, the humbling of the soul, was consecrated both by the Law and the prophets through Moses and Elijah, who fasted forty days each[^8], and by the Gospel through the Lord's own fast, during which forty days he was also tempted by the devil[^9]—prefiguring what else than temptation, throughout all the time of this world, in his own flesh, which he deigned to take on from our mortality? After the resurrection too he willed to be with his disciples on this earth no more than forty days[^10], still mingled with this life of theirs by human conversation and taking with them the foods of mortals, although now not about to die, that through those forty days he might signify, by his hidden presence, that he would fulfill what he had promised when he said: *behold, I am with you even to the consummation of the world*[^11]. But why this number signifies this temporal and earthly life—one cause, indeed, occurs close at hand, although there is perhaps another more hidden—is that the seasons of the years run in fourfold turns, and the world itself is bounded by four parts, which Scripture sometimes mentions under the name of the winds, from the East and the West, from the North and the South[^12]. And forty contains ten four times. Moreover, ten itself is completed by the number proceeding from one up to four.

**4. 10.** Receiving, therefore, Christ the king as coming to this world and to this earthly and mortal life of men, to rule us laboring in temptation, Matthew began from Abraham and enumerated forty men. For from the very nation of the Hebrews, that it might be distinguished from the other nations, God separated Abraham from his land and from his kindred[^13]. Christ came in the flesh, so that this too should pertain especially to prophesying and foretelling him more distinctly: from which nation it was promised that he would come. For although he had distinguished the four-times-ten generations by three sections, saying that from Abraham to David there are fourteen generations, and from David to the transmigration of Babylon another fourteen, and as many others to the nativity of Christ[^14], he did not nevertheless add them into a sum so as to say: there are forty-two in all. For one in those forefathers is counted twice, namely Jechoniah, at whom there occurred a certain bending toward foreign nations, when there was the transmigration into Babylon. But where an order is bent from its straight course and, that it may go in a different direction, makes as it were an angle, that which is at the angle is counted twice, namely at the end of the prior order and at the head of the bending itself. And this very thing already also prefigured Christ as about to migrate, as it were, from the circumcision to the uncircumcision[^15]—as though from Jerusalem to Babylon—and to be, for those believing in him from this side and from that, as it were a cornerstone[^16]. These things God then prepared in figure for things to come in truth. For Jechoniah himself too, where this angle was prefigured, is interpreted "the preparation of God." Thus, then, there are now not forty-two, which three times fourteen make, but, on account of the one counted twice, forty-one generations, if we also reckon in Christ himself, who royally presides over this temporal and earthly life of ours, to be ruled, as it were by the number forty.

**4. 11.** Because Matthew wished to signify him as descending to share this mortality with us, he therefore recorded the generations themselves, from Abraham down to Joseph and to the nativity of Christ himself, by descending, from the beginning of his Gospel[^17]. But Luke narrates the generations not from the beginning, but from the baptism of Christ[^18], and not by descending but by ascending, signifying him rather as a priest in expiating sins, where the voice from heaven declared him, where John bore testimony to him, saying: *behold him who takes away the sins of the world*[^19]. And by ascending he passes beyond Abraham too and comes to God, to whom, cleansed and expiated, we are reconciled[^20]. Rightly, too, did he take up the origin of adoption, because through adoption we are made sons of God by believing in the Son of God. But through carnal generation the Son of God was rather made, for our sake, the Son of man. And he sufficiently showed that he did not call Joseph the son of Eli because he was begotten of him, but because he had been adopted by him, since he also called Adam himself the son of God, though he was made by God—but, through the grace which he afterward lost by sinning, was set up in paradise as a son.

**4. 12.** Wherefore in the generations of Matthew is signified the taking up of our sins by the Lord Christ, but in the generations of Luke is signified the abolition of our sins by the Lord Christ. Therefore the one narrates them descending, the other ascending. For what the Apostle says—*God sent his Son in the likeness of sinful flesh*—this is the taking up of sins; but what he adds—*that from sin he might condemn sin in the flesh*[^21]—this is the expiation of sins. Accordingly Matthew descends from David himself through Solomon, in whose mother David sinned; but Luke ascends to David himself through Nathan, the prophet through whom God expiated David's sin[^22]. The very number too, which Luke follows out, indicates most certainly the abolition of sins. For because Christ had no iniquity, which he did not have, it is surely not joined to the iniquities of men which he took up in his flesh; therefore the number in Matthew, apart from Christ, is forty. But because he joins us, expiated and purified from all sin, to his own justice and the Father's, that there may be what the Apostle says—*but he who cleaves to the Lord is one spirit*[^23]—therefore in that number which is in Luke, both Christ himself, from whom the enumeration begins, and God, to whom it comes, are counted in, and the number seventy-seven is made, by which is signified the remission and abolition of absolutely all sins. Which the Lord himself too evidently expressed through the mystery of this number, saying that it must be forgiven to one who sins not only seven times, but even seventy-seven times[^24].

**4. 13.** Nor is it in vain that this number pertains to the cleansing of all sins, if it is inquired into more diligently. For the number ten, as it were the number of justice, is shown in the ten precepts of the Law. Now sin is the transgression of the Law, and surely the transgression of the number ten is fittingly figured by the number eleven; whence the curtains of haircloth in the tabernacle too are commanded to be made eleven[^25]. But who would doubt that haircloth pertains to the signification of sin? And for this reason, because all time revolves in the sevenfold number of days, fittingly, by eleven multiplied seven times, all sins come to the number seventy-seven. In which number there also occurs the full remission of sins, with the flesh of our priest expiating us—from whom this number now begins—and reconciling us to God, to whom this number now comes, through the Holy Spirit, who appeared in the form of a dove at this baptism[^26], where this number is mentioned.
