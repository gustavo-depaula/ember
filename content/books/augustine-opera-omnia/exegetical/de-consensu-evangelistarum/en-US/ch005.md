# Book IV

## Prologue.

**1.** 1. Now, since by considering Matthew's narrative continuously and comparing the other three with it to the end we have shown that they nowhere contradict either themselves or one another,

let us look at Mark in the same way, so that—apart from those things he said in common with Matthew, on which we have already settled what seemed to need discussion—the rest, examined and compared, may be shown to contradict none of them, up to the Lord's Supper. For from that point on we have already treated, considered to the end, all the matter of all four and how they agree with one another.

**1.** 2. Mark, then, begins thus: *the beginning of the Gospel of Jesus Christ, the Son of God. As it is written in Isaiah the prophet*[^1], and so on up to where he says: *and they enter Capernaum, and immediately on the Sabbath, having entered the synagogue, he taught them*[^2]. In this whole connected passage all the earlier matter has been considered together with Matthew. But that Mark, having entered Capernaum, taught them in their synagogue on the Sabbaths, he said in common with Luke[^3], yet this raises no difficulty.

On the man from whom an unclean spirit was cast out.

**2.** 3. Mark continues and says: *and they were astonished at his teaching; for he was teaching them as one having authority, and not as their scribes*. *And there was in their synagogue a man with an unclean spirit, and he cried out, saying: 'what have we to do with you, Jesus of Nazareth? Have you come to destroy us*[^4]?' and so on up to the place where he says: *and he was preaching in their synagogues and in all Galilee and casting out demons*[^5].

And in this whole passage, although there are some things he said in common with Luke alone[^6], they have nonetheless already been treated when we were following Matthew's continuous narrative, because they fell into that very sequence in such a way that I judged they should not be passed over.

But Luke says of the unclean spirit that it went out from the man in such a way as to do him no harm. Mark, however, says: *and the unclean spirit, tearing him and crying out with a loud voice, went out of him*[^7]. This can seem contradictory: for how does it tear him, or, as some manuscripts have it, convulse him, when according to Luke it did him no harm? But Luke himself says: *and when the demon had thrown him down in the midst, it went out of him and did him no harm*[^8]. From this it is understood that what Mark said by *convulsing him* is what Luke said by *when it had thrown him down in the midst*, so that what follows in Luke, *and did him no harm*, is to be understood in this way: that this tossing and convulsing of his limbs did not weaken him, in the way demons usually go out, dislocating even certain limbs by the convulsion they cause.

The name given to Peter.

**3.** 4. The same Mark continues: *and a leper came to him, beseeching him, and kneeling down said: 'if you will, you can make me clean*[^9],' and so on up to where he says: *and they cried out, saying: 'you are the Son of God.' And he sternly charged them not to make him known*[^10]. To this last item that we have set down Luke too says something similar[^11], but without any question of contradiction. Mark continues: *and going up on the mountain he called to himself those whom he willed, and they came to him. And he appointed twelve to be with him, and to send them out to preach. And he gave them power to heal infirmities and to cast out demons. And he imposed on Simon the name Peter*[^12], and so on up to where he says: *and he went away and began to preach in the Decapolis how much Jesus had done for him, and all marveled*[^13].

I know I have already spoken before about the names of the disciples, when I was following Matthew's order, and here I give the reminder again, lest anyone suppose that Simon now received the name by which he was to be called Peter, and that there is a contradiction with John, who records that it had been said to him long before: *you will be called Cephas, which is interpreted Peter*[^14].

For he recorded the very words of the Lord by which he imposed the name on him; Mark, however, recorded it in this place by way of recapitulation, when he said: *and he imposed on Simon the name Peter*[^15]. For, wishing to enumerate the names of the twelve apostles and having to name Peter, he wanted to indicate briefly that this was not what he had been called before, but that the Lord had imposed this name on him—not then, but when John set down the very words of the Lord. The rest contradicts no one and has already been dealt with before.

Those bidden to be silent who were the more inclined to preach.

**4.** 5. Mark continues: *and when Jesus had crossed again in the boat to the other side, a great crowd gathered to him, and he was beside the sea*[^16], and so on up to where he says: *and the apostles, gathering to Jesus, reported to him all that they had done and taught*[^17]. This last he said in common with Luke, in no way disagreeing[^18]; the rest has already been treated before. Mark continues: *and he said to them: 'come apart into a desert place and rest a little*[^19],' and so on up to where he says: *but the more he charged them, the more they preached, and the more abundantly they marveled, saying: 'he has done all things well; he has made both the deaf hear and the mute speak*[^20].'

In these things there is nothing in which Mark seems to contradict Luke, and we have already considered all the earlier matter when we were comparing them with Matthew. But we must take care that no one suppose that this passage which I have set down last from the Gospel of Mark contradicts all those passages of his that show, by many other deeds and sayings of his, that he knew what was happening in people—that is, that their thoughts and wills could not be hidden from him, as John says most plainly: *but Jesus himself did not trust himself to them, because he knew them all, and because he had no need that anyone should bear witness about man; for he himself knew what was in man*[^21].

But what wonder if he saw the present wills of men, who even foretold to Peter the future one which he certainly did not then have, when he was presuming himself ready to die for him or with him[^22]? Since this is so, how is it not contrary to this so great knowledge and foreknowledge of his that Mark says: *he charged them to tell no one; but the more he charged them, the more abundantly they preached*[^23]? For if he knew them, as one who held men's wills both present and future as marked, to be the more inclined to preach the more he charged them not to preach, why did he charge this, except that he wished to show the sluggish how much more zealously and how much more fervently those to whom he commands that they preach ought to preach, when those who were forbidden could not keep silent?

Two sayings of the Lord reconciled with each other.

**5.** 6. Mark continues: *in those days, when again there was a great crowd and they had nothing to eat*, and so on up to where he says: *John answered him, saying: 'Teacher, we saw someone casting out demons in your name, who does not follow with us, and we forbade him.' But Jesus said: 'do not forbid him; for there is no one who works a miracle in my name and can quickly speak ill of me; for he who is not against you is for you*[^24].' Luke narrates this similarly, except that he himself does not say: *for there is no one who works a miracle in my name and can quickly speak ill of me*[^25]. There is therefore no question of any contradiction between them. But we must see that this not be thought contrary to that saying of the Lord where he says: *he who is not with me is against me; and he who does not gather with me scatters*[^26].

For how was this man, who was not with him, not against him—the man about whom John reported that he did not follow him along with them—if whoever is not with him is against him? Or if he was against him, how does he say to the disciples: *do not forbid him, for he who is not against you is for you*[^27]? Will someone say that the difference is this, that here he says to the disciples, *he who is not against you is for you*[^28], but there he spoke of himself: *he who is not with me is against me*?

As if indeed one could fail to be with him who is joined to his disciples as to his members. Otherwise, how will it be true: *he who receives you receives me*[^29], and: *when you did it to one of my least ones, you did it to me*[^30]? Or can one even fail to be against him who has been against his disciples? For where will that saying stand: *he who scorns you scorns me*[^31], and: *when you did not do it to one of my least ones, neither did you do it to me*[^32], and: *Saul, Saul, why do you persecute me?*[^33] when he was persecuting his disciples?

But surely he wants this to be understood: that someone is not with him to the extent that he is against him, and is not against him to the extent that he is with him. For example, as this very man who worked miracles in the name of Christ and was not in the fellowship of Christ's disciples: insofar as he worked miracles in his name, to that extent he was with them and was not against them; but insofar as he did not cling to their fellowship, to that extent he was not with them and was against them. But because they had forbidden him to do the thing in which he was with them, the Lord said to them: *do not forbid him*.

For they ought to have forbidden that which was outside their fellowship, so as to urge upon him the unity of the Church—not that in which he was with them, namely his commending the name of their Master and Lord in the expulsion of demons. So the Catholic Church acts when it does not disapprove of the sacraments held in common among heretics; for in these things they are with us and not against us. But it disapproves and forbids division and separation and any view contrary to peace and truth; for in this they are against us, because in this they are not with us, and do not gather with us, and therefore scatter.

Things said by Christ on the occasion of the man casting out demons in his name.

**6.** 7. Mark continues and says: *for whoever gives you a cup of water to drink in my name, because you are Christ's, amen I say to you, he will not lose his reward. And whoever scandalizes one of these little ones who believe in me, it would be better for him that a millstone be hung around his neck and he be cast into the sea. And if your hand scandalizes you, cut it off; it is better for you to enter into life maimed than, having two hands, to go into hell, into the unquenchable fire, where their worm does not die and the fire is not quenched*[^34], and so on up to where he says: *have salt in yourselves and have peace among yourselves*[^35].

These things Mark records the Lord to have said, after he had forbidden the man who was casting out demons in his name and not following him with the disciples to be hindered. He records them connectedly, setting down some things that no other evangelist set down, some things that Matthew too set down[^36], and likewise some that both Matthew and Luke set down[^37]—but they set them down on other occasions and in another order of events, not in this place, where the man was mentioned who did not follow Christ with the disciples and was casting out demons in his name.

Hence it seems to me that, even in this place, the Lord, according to Mark's account, said these things which he said in other places too, because they pertained well enough to this very saying of his by which he forbade that miracles done in his name be hindered, even by the one who did not follow him with the disciples. For he connects it thus: *for he who is not against you is for you. For whoever gives you a cup of water to drink in my name, because you are Christ's, amen I say to you, he will not lose his reward*[^38].

By this he shows that even the man about whom John had reported, and from whom this discourse of his arose, was not so separated from the fellowship of the disciples as to disapprove of it like a heretic; rather, as men commonly do who do not yet dare to receive the sacraments of Christ and yet favor the Christian name, so that they even welcome Christians and show them kindness for no other reason than that they are Christians—of such he says that they do not lose their reward,

not because they ought now to consider themselves safe and secure on the strength of this goodwill which they have toward Christians, even if they are not washed by the baptism of Christ nor incorporated into his unity, but because they are now so governed by the mercy of God that they may also attain to these things and so depart secure from this world. Such persons assuredly, even before they are joined to the number of Christians, are more useful than those who, though they are already called Christians and have been steeped in the Christian sacraments, urge such things as drag those whom they persuade into eternal punishment with them.

These persons, under the name of bodily members—as a hand or an eye that scandalizes—he commands to be plucked out from the body[^39], that is, from the very fellowship of unity, so that one may come to life rather without them than go into hell with them.

And by this very thing they are separated from those from whom they are separated: that when they urge evils—that is, scandalize—they are not consented to.

And if indeed they become known for this perversity to all the good with whom they have fellowship, they are utterly separated from the fellowship of all and from the very participation in the divine sacraments; but if they are known to certain people, while their perversity is unknown to the many, they are to be tolerated in the way that, before the winnowing, chaff is tolerated on the threshing floor, so that neither is anyone consented to in fellowship with iniquity, nor on their account is the fellowship of the good abandoned. This those do who have salt in themselves and have peace among themselves.

That from here up to the Lord's Supper no question concerning Mark is treated.

**7.** 8. Mark continues: *and rising up from there he came into the region of Judea beyond the Jordan, and crowds gathered to him again, and, as he was accustomed, he again taught them*[^40], and so on up to where he says: *for all gave out of their abundance, but she out of her want gave all she had, her whole living*[^41]. In this whole connected passage all the earlier matter has been considered, lest they should seem to have anything contradictory, when we were comparing the others with Matthew's order. But this about the poor widow who put two small coins into the treasury only two relate, Mark and Luke[^42], yet they agree without any question. From here on, up to the Lord's Supper[^43], from which point we have treated all the matter of all of them as considered, Mark too says nothing that compels comparison with anyone for inquiry, lest anything should seem contradictory.

On the Gospel of Luke, how its beginning agrees with the beginning of the book of the Acts of the Apostles.

**8.** 9. Now, then, let us next treat the Gospel of Luke in order, apart from those things he has in common with Matthew and Mark, since all those have already been treated. Luke, then, begins thus: *since indeed many have undertaken to set in order a narrative of the things that have been accomplished among us, just as those handed them down to us who from the beginning themselves saw and were ministers of the word, it seemed good to me also, having followed all things diligently from the start, to write to you in order, most excellent Theophilus, that you may know the truth of those words in which you have been instructed*[^44].

This beginning does not yet pertain to the Gospel narrative. But it advises us to know that the same Luke also wrote that book which is called the *Acts of the Apostles*—not only because the name of Theophilus is found there too (for it could be that there was some other Theophilus, and, even if it were the same one, that something might be written to him by someone else, as the Gospel was by Luke),

but because there too he began in such a way as to say: *the first account, O Theophilus, I made of all the things that Jesus began to do and to teach, until the day on which, giving commandment through the Holy Spirit to the apostles whom he had chosen, he bade them preach the Gospel*[^45]; he gave us to understand that he had already written one of the four books of the Gospel, which have sublime authority in the Church. Nor, because he said that he had made an account of all the things that Jesus began to do and to teach until the day on which he gave commandment to the apostles, ought it to be supposed that he wrote in his Gospel everything that Jesus did and said while he dwelt with the apostles on earth,

lest this be contrary to John, who says that Jesus did many other things which, if they were written, the whole world could not contain those books[^46]—since it is also established that not a few things are narrated by the other evangelists which Luke himself did not touch in his narration.

He therefore made an account of all things by selecting from all the things he judged fit and suitable to suffice for the office of his stewardship. And when he says that many had undertaken to *set in order a narrative of the things that have been accomplished among us*, he seems to signify some who could not fulfill this task they had taken up;

and for this reason he says it *seemed* good to him to *write in order diligently, since many have undertaken*; but we must take these to be those whose authority does not stand in the Church, because they were utterly unable to fulfill what they had undertaken.

This man, however, not only carried his narrative through to the resurrection and the assumption of the Lord, so as to hold a place worthy of his labor among the four authors of the Gospel Scripture, but also wrote thereafter what was done through the apostles, what he believed sufficient to build up the faith of readers or hearers—and he wrote it in such a way that his book alone is held worthy of faith in the Church among those narrating the *Acts of the Apostles*, all being rejected who dared to write the deeds and sayings of the apostles not with the faith that was required.

For Mark and Luke wrote at a time when they could be approved not only by the Church of Christ but also by the apostles themselves, who were then still remaining in the flesh.

On the catch of fish.

**9.** 10. Luke, then, begins to narrate the Gospel thus: *there was in the days of Herod, king of Judea, a certain priest named Zechariah, of the division of Abijah, and he had a wife of the daughters of Aaron, and her name was Elizabeth*[^47], and so on up to the place where he says: *and when he had ceased speaking, he said to Simon: 'put out into the deep and let down your nets for a catch*[^48].' This whole passage has no question of contradiction. John, indeed, seems to say something similar, but that is far different, being what happened after the Lord's resurrection at the Sea of Tiberias[^49].

For there not only is the time itself very different, but the event itself differs greatly. For there the nets, cast to the right side, caught a hundred and fifty-three fish, large ones indeed; but it was relevant for the evangelist to say that, though they were so large, the nets were not torn—looking, that is, to this event which Luke records, where the nets were breaking from the multitude of fish[^50]. As for the rest of what Luke said in common with John, he said it only around the Lord's passion and resurrection. That whole passage, from his supper to the end, we have so treated that, with the testimonies of all compared, we showed that they in no way disagree.

On the evangelist John, how he differs from the other three.

**10.** 11. John remains, and there is now no one left with whom he may be compared. For whatever the individual evangelists said that was not said by the others, it is unlikely that they should have any question of contradiction. And by this it is clearly established that these three—namely Matthew, Mark, and Luke—were occupied chiefly with the humanity of our Lord Jesus Christ, according to which he is both king and priest. And therefore Mark, who in that mystery of the four living creatures seems to represent the person of the man[^51],

either seems to be more Matthew's companion, because with him he says more things on account of the royal person, which is not wont to go unaccompanied—as I mentioned in the first book—

or, as is more probably understood, walks along with both. For although he agrees with Matthew in more places, yet in some others he agrees rather with Luke, so that by this very fact it may be shown that what Christ is as man pertains to the lion and to the calf—that is, both to the royal person, which Matthew suggests, and to the priestly person, which Luke suggests—of which figure Mark bears, pertaining to both.

But the divinity of Christ, by which he is equal to the Father—according to which he is the Word and God with God, and the Word was made flesh that he might dwell among us[^52], according to which he and the Father are one[^53]—John undertook chiefly to commend, who like an eagle dwells in those things that Christ spoke more sublimely, and in a manner descends to earth only rarely. In short, although he plainly attests that he knows the mother of Christ, yet he says nothing about his nativity along with Matthew and Luke,

nor does he record his baptism along with the three, but only there, commending the testimony of John deeply and sublimely[^54]; leaving these matters, he goes on with him to the wedding at Cana of Galilee, where, although the evangelist himself records that his mother was present[^55], he nonetheless says: *what have I to do with you, woman?*[^56]—not repelling her from whom he took flesh, but then most of all suggesting his divinity, as he was about to turn the water into wine—the divinity that had made that woman too, and was not made in her.

**10.** 12. Then, after a few days spent in Capernaum, he returns to the temple, where he records that Jesus said concerning the temple of his body: *destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up*[^57]. Here he most of all suggests not only that the Word made flesh was God in the temple, but also that he himself raised up that same flesh—and that, of course, only according to what is one with the Father and works inseparably; whereas in perhaps all other places Scripture says only that God raised him, nor is it anywhere so expressly stated that, when God raised Christ, he also raised himself, because he is one God with the Father—as in this place, where he says: *destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up*[^58].

**10.** 13. Then, with that Nicodemus, how great, how divine the things he spoke! Then again he goes on to the testimony of John and commends that the friend of the bridegroom rejoices only on account of the voice of the bridegroom[^59].

Here he admonishes that the human soul does not shine to itself from itself, nor is made blessed except by participation in the unchangeable Wisdom. Then to the Samaritan woman, where there is mention of water, of which whoever drinks will not thirst forever[^60].

Then again to Cana of Galilee, where he had made wine from water[^61]. Here he records that Jesus said to the official whose son was sick: *unless you see signs and wonders, you do not believe*[^62], so much does he wish to lift the believer's mind above all changeable things, that he does not want even the miracles themselves—which, though they are done by divine power upon the changeableness of bodies—to be sought by the faithful.

**10.** 14. Then he returns to Jerusalem; the man sick for thirty-eight years is made well[^63]. On this occasion what things are said! How sublime the things that are said! There it is said: *the Jews sought to kill him, because he not only broke the Sabbath, but also called God his own Father, making himself equal to God*[^64]. Here it is shown well enough how it was not in the ordinary way—as holy men are accustomed to speak—that he called God his own Father, but suggesting that he is equal to him;

for a little before he had said to them, as they slandered him about the Sabbath: *my Father works until now, and I work*[^65]. There they blazed up, not because he called God his own Father, but because he wished to be understood as equal to him by saying, *my Father works until now, and I work*, showing it to follow that, since the Father works, the Son also works, because the Father does not work without the Son. For there, a little before, he had already said to them, as they were angry on this account: *for whatever things he does, these the Son also does likewise*[^66].

**10.** 15. Then at last John descends to those three, walking with the same Lord on earth, so that five thousand may be fed with five loaves—where, however, he alone records that, when they wished to make him king, he fled alone into the mountain[^67]. In this matter nothing else seems to me that he wished to admonish the rational soul, except that he reigns over our mind and reason in such a way that he is in the heights with no fellowship of nature with men, alone, because he is the Only-begotten of the Father.

But this mystery flees from carnal men creeping below, because it is exceedingly sublime; hence he himself also fled into the mountain from those who sought his kingdom with an earthly mind—whence elsewhere too he says: *my kingdom is not of this world*[^68]. Nor does anyone but John himself record even this, soaring above the lands with a kind of ethereal flight and rejoicing in the light of the Sun of righteousness.

Likewise from that mountain, after the miracle of the five loaves, having tarried a little with those same three until they had crossed the sea—when he walked upon the waters—he at once lifts himself again into the word of the Lord, how great, how lengthy, how long sublime and lofty, arising from the occasion of bread[^69], when he had said to the crowds: *amen, amen I say to you, you seek me not because you saw signs, but because you ate of the loaves and were filled: work not for the food that perishes, but for that which endures unto eternal life*[^70]:

and from there onward such things at very great length and very loftily. Then from this loftiness of the word those fell away who thereafter did not walk after him,

to whom indeed those clung who were able to understand: *it is the Spirit that gives life, but the flesh profits nothing*[^71], because surely the Spirit profits even through the flesh, and the Spirit alone profits, but the flesh without the Spirit profits nothing.

**10.** 16. Then, when his brothers—that is, the kinsmen of his flesh—suggested that he go up to the feast where he might become known to the multitude[^72], with what loftiness he answered: *my time has not yet come, but your time is always ready. The world cannot hate you; but it hates me, because I bear witness about it, that its works are evil!*[^73] This, then, is the meaning: "*your time is always ready*, because you desire this day, of which the Prophet says: '*but I have not labored, following you, O Lord, and I have not desired the day of men; you know*'[^74]." This is to fly to the light of the Word and to desire that day which Abraham desired to see and *saw and was glad*[^75]. Then, when he had gone up to the feast in the temple, what wonderful, what divine, what lofty things does John record that he spoke![^76] That they could not come where he was going, that they both knew him and knew whence he was, and that he who sent him was true, whom they did not know—as if he said: "you both know whence I am, and you do not know whence I am." What else did he wish to be understood by this except that he could be known to them according to the flesh, by nation and homeland, but according to his divinity was unknown? There too, speaking of the gift of the Holy Spirit, he showed who he was, since he could give so lofty a gift.

**10.** 17. Again, as he returned there from the Mount of Olives[^77], what and how great things does he narrate that he spoke after pardon was granted to that adulteress, who had been brought to him by his tempters as one to be stoned,

when he wrote with his finger on the earth, as if to signify that such people were to be written on the earth and not in heaven,

where he admonished the disciples to rejoice that they were written[^78];

or, by humbling himself—which he showed by bowing his head—he was making signs on the earth; or because it was now the time that his law should be written on earth that would yield fruit, not on barren stone as before!

Then, after these things, he said that he was the light of the world, and that whoever followed him would not walk in darkness but would have the light of life[^79]. He also said that he was the beginning, which was why he spoke to them[^80]. By which name he distinguished himself from that light which he made, as the light through which all things were made,

so that the fact that he had said he was the light of the world should not be taken in the way he says to the disciples: *you are the light of the world*[^81], as a lamp that is not to be put under a bushel but on a lampstand[^82], as also of John the Baptist: *he was*, he says, *a burning and shining lamp*[^83]—but rather as the beginning, of which it is said: *we have all received of his fullness*[^84]. There he said that he, the Son, was the truth, which, unless it sets free, no one will be free[^85].

**10.** 18. Then, after he enlightened the man blind from birth[^86], from that very occasion John dwells in a lengthy discourse of his, about the sheep and the shepherd and the door, and about the power of laying down his life and taking it up again[^87], in which he shows the most excellent power of his divinity. Then, when the Feast of Dedication was being held in Jerusalem[^88], he records that the Jews said to him: *how long will you take away our soul? If you are the Christ, tell us plainly*[^89]. And then, taking the opportunity for discourse, he narrates what sublime things he also said[^90]. There he said: *I and the Father are one*[^91]. Then he proclaims Lazarus, raised by him[^92], where he said: *I am the resurrection and the life; whoever believes in me, even if he dies, will live; and everyone who lives and believes in me will not die forever*[^93]. In which words what do we recognize but the loftiness of his divinity, by participation in which we shall live forever? Then again John meets Matthew and Mark in Bethany, where that event with the precious ointment took place, with which his feet were anointed by Mary and his head poured over; and from here onward, up to the passion and resurrection of the Lord, John walks with the three evangelists, though his narration dwells on the same places[^94].

**10.** 19. Moreover, as for the discourses of the Lord, he does not cease to lift himself up into those things which the Lord from this point on also spoke sublimely and at length. For even when the gentiles wished to see him through Philip and Andrew, he had a lofty discourse which none of the other evangelists inserted; there again he records splendid things about the light that enlightens and makes sons of light[^95]. Then at the supper itself, about which none of the evangelists was silent, how many and how lofty words of his does John record[^96] which the others passed over in silence!

Not only concerning the commendation of humility, when he washed the disciples' feet, but, after his betrayer—pointed out by the morsel—had gone out, the eleven remaining with him, the same John dwelt upon his discourse, marvelously astonishing and exceedingly long, where he said: *he who has seen me has also seen the Father*[^97].

There he said many things about the Holy Spirit, the Paraclete, whom he was about to send to them[^98], and about his own glorification, which he had with the Father before the world existed, and that he would make us one in himself, as he and the Father are one—not so that he and the Father and we should be one, but that we should be one as they are one,

and many other things, and wonderfully sublime—to discuss which as is fitting, even if we were now qualified, who does not perceive that we have not undertaken to do in this work?

This is perhaps to be rendered elsewhere; here it is certainly not to be sought. For we wished to commend to the lovers of the Word of God and to those zealous for holy truth that, although John was the announcer and preacher in his Gospel of that same Christ who is true and truthful—of whom the other three who wrote the Gospel, and the other apostles also,

who did not indeed undertake to write the narrative itself but nonetheless fulfilled the duty of their office in that preaching, were announcers and preachers—yet this man, borne up to the higher things of Christ, was from the very beginning of his book rarely with the others: that is, first around the Jordan on account of the testimony of John the Baptist[^99], then across the Sea of Tiberias, when he fed the crowds with five loaves and walked upon the waters[^100], third in Bethany, where he was anointed with precious ointment by the devotion of the faithful woman[^101];

until from there he met them at the time of the passion, which he was of necessity going to narrate together with them—where, however, he set forth the Lord's Supper itself, about which none of them was silent, much more richly, as it were from the storeroom of the Lord's breast, on which he used to recline[^102]. Then he strikes Pilate himself with loftier words, saying that his kingdom is not of this world and that he was born a king and had come into this world for this, that he might bear witness to the truth[^103]. Visiting Mary also after the resurrection with a mystical loftiness, he says: *do not touch me; for I have not yet ascended to the Father*[^104]. To the disciples too, by breathing on them, he gave the Holy Spirit, lest that same Spirit, who is consubstantial and coeternal with the Trinity, be thought to be the Spirit only of the Father and not also of the Son[^105].

**10.** 20. Finally, commending his sheep to Peter, who loved him and confessed that love three times, he says that this same John wished so to remain until he comes[^106]. Here too it seems to me that he taught, by a deep mystery, that this very evangelical stewardship of John's—by which he is borne sublimely into the most limpid light of the Word, where the equality and unchangeableness of the Trinity can be seen—and by what property especially the man differs from the rest, by whose taking up the Word was made flesh, cannot be clearly discerned and known until the Lord himself comes. Therefore he will so remain until he comes. But now he will remain in the faith of believers; then, however, he will be contemplated face to face[^107], when our life shall appear and we too shall appear with him in glory[^108].

But whoever thinks that it can befall a man still leading this mortal life that, with every cloud of bodily and carnal images removed and dispelled, he may possess the most serene light of unchangeable truth and, with his mind utterly estranged from the habit of this life, cling to it constantly and unswervingly, understands neither what he seeks nor who is seeking.

Let him therefore rather believe the sublime and in no way deceptive authority: that as long as we are in the body we are away from the Lord and walk by faith, not yet by sight[^109]; and so, perseveringly holding and guarding faith, hope, and charity, let him strain toward sight from the pledge we have received of the Holy Spirit, who will teach us all truth[^110], when God, who raised Jesus Christ from the dead, will also give life to our mortal bodies through his Spirit dwelling in us[^111]. But before that which is dead because of sin is given life, it is beyond doubt corruptible and weighs down the soul[^112]; and if at any time, when helped, it rises above this mist by which the whole earth is covered[^113]—that is, this carnal darkness by which the whole earthly life is covered—it is grazed as if by a swift flash and returns into its own weakness, with desire still living by which it may again be raised up, but with insufficient cleanness by which it may be fixed there.

And the more each one is able to do this, the greater he is; but the less, the lesser. If, however, the mind of a man—in which Christ nonetheless dwells through faith—has not yet experienced anything of this kind, he ought to apply himself to diminishing and ending the desires of this world by the exercise of moral virtue, walking as it were in the company of those three evangelists with Christ the mediator. Let him faithfully hold, with the gladness of great hope, him who is ever the Son of God, made for our sake the Son of man, so that his everlasting power and divinity, tempered to our weakness and mortality, might make for us, out of what is ours, a way in himself and to himself.

Lest he sin, let him be ruled by Christ the king; if perchance he has sinned, let him be expiated by the same Christ the priest[^114]; and thus, nourished in the practice of good conduct and life, borne up from the earth on the feathers of a twofold love as on two strong wings, let him be enlightened by that same Christ the Word—the Word that was in the beginning, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God[^115]—even if through a glass and in a riddle[^116], yet far more sublimely than by any bodily likeness. Wherefore, although in those three the gifts of active virtue, but in the Gospel of John the gifts of contemplative virtue shine forth to those who are qualified to discern these things, nevertheless this of John's too, since it is in part, will so remain until that which is perfect comes[^117].

And to one indeed is given through the Spirit the utterance of wisdom, to another the utterance of knowledge according to the same Spirit[^118]; one observes the day for the Lord[^119], another drinks something more limpid from the Lord's breast, another, raised up to the third heaven, hears ineffable words[^120]; yet all, as long as they are in the body, are away from the Lord[^121], and for all the faithful of good hope who are written in the book of life[^122] there is reserved what was said: *and I will love him and will show myself to him*[^123].

Nevertheless, in this pilgrimage, the more each one has advanced in the understanding or knowledge of this matter, the more let him beware of the diabolical vices of pride and envy. Let him remember here that this very *Gospel of John*, by however much more it raises us to the contemplation of truth, by just so much more does it enjoin upon us the sweetness of love.

And because that precept is most true and most wholesome: *the greater you are, the more humble yourself in all things*[^124], the evangelist who commends Christ far more loftily than the rest is the one in whom Christ washes the disciples' feet[^125].
