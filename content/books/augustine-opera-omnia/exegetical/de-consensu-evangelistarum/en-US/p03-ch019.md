# On that lengthy discourse which, according to Matthew, he held on the mountain.

**19. 43.** Now let us see, concerning that lengthy discourse which, according to Matthew, the Lord held on the mountain[^1], whether the other evangelists seem to contradict it in nothing. For Mark did not mention it at all, nor said anything like it, except certain sentences not connectedly, but scattered, which the Lord repeated in other places. Yet he left a place in the text of his narrative where we may understand that this discourse was spoken, but passed over by him: *and he was*, he says, *preaching in their synagogues and in all Galilee and casting out demons*[^2]. In this preaching, which he says he held in all Galilee, that discourse too is understood to have been held on the mountain, which Matthew mentions. For the same Mark goes on thus: *and there came to him a leper, beseeching him, and kneeling he said: 'If you will, you can cleanse me*[^3],' and the rest. About this cleansed leper he connects such things that he is understood to be the same one whom Matthew mentions as cleansed then, when, after that discourse, the Lord came down from the mountain. For Matthew says thus: *and when he had come down from the mountain, great crowds followed him. And behold, a leper, coming, adored him, saying: 'Lord, if you will, you can cleanse me*[^4],' and the rest.

**19. 44.** Luke too mentions this leper, not indeed in this order[^5], but as they are wont to recall things passed over or to anticipate things done later, just as those things were divinely suggested which, known before, they afterward wrote down by recalling. Nevertheless the same Luke himself too narrated a lengthy discourse of the Lord, where he too began as Matthew did in this one.

For the one said, *Blessed are the poor in spirit, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven*[^6], and the other, *Blessed are the poor, for yours is the kingdom of God*[^7]. Then many of the things that follow are similar in Luke's narration too. And at the end of the discourse the very conclusion is found altogether the same, about the prudent man who builds upon the rock, and about the foolish one who builds upon the sand, except that there he says only the river dashed against the house, not also the rain and the winds, as Matthew does[^8]. It could, then, very easily be believed that he too interposed the same discourse of the Lord, but passed over some sentences which Matthew set down, likewise set down others which this one did not say, and even set forth some, not in the same words, the integrity of the truth being nevertheless preserved, in a similar way.

**19. 45.** This could, as I said, very easily be believed, were it not troubling that Matthew says this discourse was held by the Lord sitting, on the mountain[^9], but Luke, by the Lord standing, in a level place[^10]. This diversity, therefore, makes it seem that the one was a different discourse, the other another. For what would prevent Christ from repeating elsewhere certain things which he had already said before, or from doing again certain things which he had already done before?

These two discourses, of which Matthew inserted one, Luke the other, are not indeed probably believed to be separated by a long distance of time, for this reason: that both before and afterward both narrated certain similar or the same things, so that it is not absurdly thought that their narrations, interposing these things, were engaged with the same places and days. For Matthew says it thus: *and great crowds followed him from Galilee and the Decapolis and Jerusalem and Judea and from beyond the Jordan. And seeing the crowds, he went up onto the mountain, and when he had sat down, his disciples came to him. And opening his mouth, he taught them, saying: 'Blessed are the poor in spirit, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven*[^11],' and the rest.

Here it can seem that he wished to avoid the many crowds, and for this reason went up onto the mountain, as though withdrawing from the crowds, that he might speak to his disciples alone.

To which thing Luke too seems to attest, narrating thus: *now it came to pass in those days, he went out onto the mountain to pray, and he was spending the night in prayer to God. And when day came, he called his disciples and chose twelve of them, whom he also named apostles: Simon, whom he surnamed Peter, and Andrew his brother, James and John, Philip and Bartholomew, Matthew and Thomas, James the son of Alphaeus and Simon who is called the Zealot, Judas the son of James and Judas Iscariot, who was the traitor. And coming down with them, he stood in a level place, and a crowd of his disciples and a copious multitude of the people from all Judea and Jerusalem and the coast of Tyre and Sidon, who had come to hear him and to be healed of their illnesses. And those who were troubled by unclean spirits were cured. And the whole crowd sought to touch him, because power went out from him and healed all. And he himself, lifting up his eyes upon his disciples, said: 'Blessed are the poor, for yours is the kingdom of God'*[^12], and the rest.

Here it can be understood that, when on the mountain he chose twelve disciples out of the many, whom he named apostles—which Matthew passed over—he then held that discourse which Matthew interposed and Luke was silent about; that is, that on the mountain, and then, when he had come down, in a level place, he held another similar one, about which Matthew is silent and Luke is not silent, and that both discourses were concluded in the same way[^13].

**19. 46.** But as for the fact that Matthew, when that discourse is ended, goes on and says, *and it came to pass, when Jesus had finished these words, the crowds marveled at his teaching*[^14], it can seem that he meant the crowds of disciples, out of whom he had chosen those twelve[^15]. But what he soon says—*and when he had come down from the mountain, great crowds followed him, and behold, a leper, coming, adored him*[^16]—can be understood to have happened after both discourses, not only the one which Matthew, but also the one which Luke interposes. For it does not appear how much time was interposed after the coming down from the mountain, but Matthew wished only to signify this: that after that coming down there were many crowds with the Lord when he cleansed the leper, not how much time intervened—especially since Luke says that the same leper was cleansed when the Lord was already placed in a city[^17], which Matthew did not care to say.

**19. 47.** Although this too can occur: that, in some higher part of the mountain, the Lord was first with his disciples alone, when he chose those twelve out of them; then that he came down with them, not from the mountain, but from the very height of the mountain into a level place, that is, into some flat area which was on the side of the mountain and could hold many crowds; and that he stood there until the crowds gathered to him, and afterward, when he had sat down, his disciples came nearer, and thus, with them and the rest of the crowds present, he held one discourse,

which Matthew and Luke narrated, in a different manner of narrating, but with the same truth of the things and the sentences which both said. For we have already given warning—what, even with no one warning, each one had spontaneously to see—that if one passes over something which another says, it is not contrary, nor if one says something in a different way than another, provided the same truth of the things and sentences is set forth; so that what Matthew says, *and when he had come down from the mountain*[^18], may at the same time be understood also of that level place which could be on the side of the mountain. Then Matthew narrates about the cleansed leper, which Mark and Luke too do similarly[^19].
