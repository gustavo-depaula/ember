# On the banquet where it was objected to him that he ate with sinners, and that his disciples did not fast—which seems to be that one says different persons made the objection from those another says—and on their words and the Lord's answers.

**27. 60.** Matthew, accordingly, goes on and says: *and it came to pass, as he reclined at table in the house, behold, many publicans and sinners came and reclined with Jesus and his disciples*, and the rest, up to where he says, *but they put new wine into new wineskins, and both are preserved*[^1].

Here Matthew did not express in whose house Jesus reclined with the publicans and sinners. Whence it could seem that he subjoined this not in order, but interposed it, recalling what was done at another time, were it not that Mark and Luke, who narrate this altogether similarly[^2], make it manifest that Jesus reclined in the house of Levi, that is, of Matthew, and that all those things which follow were said there.

For Mark says this same thing thus, keeping the same order: *and it came to pass, as he reclined at table in his house, many publicans and sinners reclined together with Jesus*[^3]. When, then, he says *in his house*, he surely expresses the one of whom he was speaking above, that is, Levi. So too Luke, when he had said, *he said to him: 'Follow me,' and leaving all and rising he followed him*, immediately subjoined: *and Levi made him a great banquet in his house, and there was a great crowd of publicans and of others who were reclining at table with them*[^4]. It is therefore manifest in whose house these things were done.

**27. 61.** Now let us see the very words which all these three evangelists set down—either what was said to the Lord or what was answered by him. Matthew: *and seeing this*, he says, *the Pharisees said to his disciples: 'Why does your master eat with publicans and sinners?*[^5]' In almost the same words Mark too says this: *Why does your master eat and drink with publicans and sinners?*[^6]

There was passed over by Matthew, then, what this one added, *and drink*. But what does it matter to the matter, since the meaning is full, intimating that they feasted together? But Luke seems to have mentioned this somewhat differently. *And the Pharisees and their scribes murmured*, he says, *saying to his disciples: 'Why do you eat and drink with publicans and sinners?'*[^7]

Not, surely, wishing their master to be understood as not there, but intimating that this was objected to all together, both to him and to his disciples; yet not said to him, but to them, so that it might be taken both of him and of them. For surely Luke too says that the Lord answered thus: *'I have not come to call the just, but sinners to repentance*[^8].'

Which he would not have answered them, unless what they had said—*you eat and drink*—pertained especially to himself. Therefore Matthew and Mark too narrated that this was objected concerning him to his disciples, because even when it was said about the disciples, it was rather objected to the master, whom they imitated by following.

There is, then, one meaning, and it is so much the better intimated, the more, in certain words, the truth remaining, it is varied. Likewise, what Matthew reports the Lord answered: *The healthy have no need of a physician, but those who are ill. But go and learn what this is: 'I desire mercy and not sacrifice*[^9].' *For I have not come to call the just, but sinners*[^10]. Mark too and Luke kept the same meaning in almost the same words[^11], except that both do not interpose that testimony from the prophet, *I desire mercy rather than sacrifice*[^12]. But Luke, when he had said, *I have not come to call the just, but sinners*, added *to repentance*[^13], which serves to explain the meaning, lest anyone suppose that sinners are loved by Christ for this very thing, that they are sinners—since that similitude too about the sick well intimates what God wills by calling sinners, as a physician the sick, namely, that from iniquity, as from sickness, they may be made well, which is done through repentance.

**27. 62.** Likewise, what Matthew says: *then the disciples of John came to him, saying: 'Why do we and the Pharisees fast frequently?*[^14]' Mark brought it in similarly, saying: *and the disciples of John and the Pharisees were fasting, and they come and say to him: 'Why do the disciples of John and of the Pharisees fast?*[^15]' Except that this one can be thought to have added that the Pharisees said this together with the disciples of John, when Matthew reports that only the disciples of John said this. But the very words which it is read in Mark that they said indicate rather that others said this about others—that is, that the guests who were present came to Jesus, because the disciples of John and the Pharisees were fasting, and said this to him about them; so that what he says, *they come*, he did not say of those very ones about whom he had interposed, *and the disciples of John and the Pharisees were fasting*, but, while these were fasting, those came whom this moved, and they say to him: *Why do the disciples of John and of the Pharisees fast, but yours do not fast?* Which Luke expressed more evidently, intimating this same thing thus, when he had said what the Lord answered them about the calling of sinners as of the sick: *but they*, he says, *said to him: 'Why do the disciples of John fast frequently and make supplications, and likewise those of the Pharisees, but yours eat and drink?*[^16]' So here too, like Mark, he narrated that others said this about others. Whence, then, does Matthew say: *then the disciples of John came to him, saying: 'Why do we and the Pharisees fast?*[^17]' Except that they too were present, and all in turn, as each one could, objected this, whose meaning, in a different manner of speaking, but yet not alien from the truth, was intimated by the three evangelists.

**27. 63.** Likewise that about the sons of the bridegroom, that they will not fast as long as the bridegroom is with them[^18], Matthew and Mark interposed similarly, except that Mark called *sons of the wedding* those whom the other called of the bridegroom, which makes no difference to the matter. For by *sons of the wedding* we understand not only those of the bridegroom, but also those of the bride.

There is, then, the same open meaning, not another, opposed one. But Luke did not say, "Can the sons of the bridegroom fast?" but said: *Can you make the sons of the bridegroom fast, as long as the bridegroom is with them?*[^19] In which he too, to intimate something else, elegantly opened the same meaning.

For thus it is understood that those very ones who were speaking were about to bring it about that the sons of the bridegroom, mourning, should fast, since they themselves were about to kill the bridegroom. But what Matthew said, *mourn*, Mark and Luke said *fast*, because the other too afterward says *then they will fast*, not, "then they will mourn." But by that word he signified that the Lord spoke of such a fast as pertains to the humility of tribulation, so that the other one, which pertains to the joy of a mind suspended toward spiritual things and on this account in some way withdrawn from corporeal foods, the Lord is understood to have signified by the later similitudes about the new cloth and the new wine[^20], showing this: that this kind of fasting does not suit those who are animal and carnal, occupied about the body, and on this account still drawing the old sense. Which similitudes the other two also set forth similarly. For it is now sufficiently at hand that there is nothing contrary, if one says something, whether of word or of thing, which another passes over, provided either that the same meaning is not departed from, or that what is perhaps otherwise set down is not opposed to the other.
