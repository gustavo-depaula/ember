# Book IV

## How the six days are to be taken.

**1. 1.** *And heaven and earth were finished, and all their adornment. And God finished on the sixth day his works which he had made; and God rested on the seventh day from all his works which he had made. And God blessed the seventh day, and sanctified it; because in it he rested from all his works which God had begun to make*[^1]*.* It is indeed arduous and most difficult, for the powers of our attention, to penetrate by the liveliness of the mind the will of the writer in these six days: whether those days passed, and, the seventh being added, are now repeated through the volumes of the times, not in reality but in name. For in the whole of time many days come like those past, but no same day returns.

Whether, then, those days passed; or whether, while these days which are reckoned by their names and number run by daily in the order of times, those days remain in the very foundings of things—so that not only in those three before the luminaries were made, but also in the remaining three likewise, we may understand the name of *day* in the appearance of the thing that was created, and its *night* in the privation or defect, or by whatever other name it is better signified, when the appearance is lost, some change declining and verging from form toward formlessness.

Which change is present in every creature either in possibility, even if it be lacking in effect, as in the higher heavenly things; or, for filling up temporal beauty in the lowest things, is carried out through the ordered turns of the yieldings and successions of changeable things, as is manifest in earthly and mortal things: but *evening* in all things is, as it were, a certain term of the completed founding: but *morning* the beginning of what is beginning; for every created nature is contained by its own fixed beginnings and ends.

It is difficult to track down. But whether this, or that, or even some third thing more probable can be found to be said—which will perhaps appear in the progress of the disputation—as to how in those days both *night* and *evening* and *morning* are understood: it is not, however, beside the point that we consider the perfection of the number six in the very inner nature of numbers; gazing at which with the mind, we number the things that lie also before the senses of the body, and dispose them numerically.

## On the perfection of the number six.

**2. 2.** We find, then, that the number six is the first perfect number, by this reason: that it is completed by its parts. For there are also other numbers perfect by other causes and reasons. Accordingly we said that this six is perfect by this reason, that it is completed by its parts—by such parts, that is, as, multiplied, can make up the number whose parts they are. For such a part of a number can be said by what fraction it is. For the number three can be called a part not only of six, of which it is the half, but of all numbers larger than three itself. For three is the larger part of four and of five: for four can be divided into three and one, and five into three and two. And three is a part of seven and of eight and of nine, and of whatever numbers are beyond, not a larger part nor the half, but a smaller. For seven too can be divided into three and four, and eight into three and five, and nine into three and six: but of none of these can three be said by what fraction it is a part, except only of nine, of which it is the third part, as it is the half of six. And so of all these which I have mentioned, none is made up of some number of threes, except six and nine. For the former consists of two threes, but the latter of three.

## The number six is the first of the perfect numbers.

**2. 3.** The number six, then, as I had begun to say, with its parts counted together and brought into a sum, is itself completed. For there are some numbers whose parts taken together make a smaller sum, but others a larger: and by fixed reasons of intervals fewer are found which are completed by their parts, whose sum neither stops short nor grows beyond, but meets exactly as much as is the number itself whose parts they are. Of these the first is six. For of one, in numbers, there are no parts. For "one" is so spoken of, among the numbers by which we count, that it has neither a half nor any part, but is truly and purely and simply one. But of two the part is one, and that the half, nor is there any other. But three has two parts: one which can be said by what fraction it is, which is one, for it is the third of it; and another larger which cannot be said by what fraction it is, that is, two: nor, then, can those parts of it be reckoned which we are dealing with, that is, those which can be said by what fraction they are. Further, four has two such: for one is the fourth of it; two, the half; but both brought into a sum, that is, one and two together, make three, not four. Therefore its parts do not complete it, because they stop at a smaller sum. Five has no such part but one, that is, one itself, which is the fifth part of it: for though two be a smaller part of it and three a larger, yet neither of them can be said by what fraction it is a part of it. But six has three such parts: a sixth, a third, a half: its sixth is one; its third, two; its half, three. And these parts brought into a sum, that is, one and two and three, together make up and perfect the same six.

## Augustine examines the other numbers.

**2. 4.** Now seven has no such part but a seventh, which is one. Eight has three: an eighth, a fourth, a half, that is, one, two, and four: but these brought together make seven, which stops short, and therefore they do not fill out the same eight. Nine has two: a ninth, which is one; and a third, which is three: but these together make four, far smaller than nine. Ten has three such parts: one a tenth, two a fifth, five a half; which numbers brought together reach eight, not ten. Eleven has only an eleventh, as seven has only a seventh, and five only a fifth, and three only a third, and two only a half, which in all is one.

But twelve, by its such parts brought together, is not itself completed but exceeded; for they make a number larger than twelve: for they reach as far as sixteen. For it has five of them: a twelfth, a sixth, a fourth, a third, a half: for its twelfth is one; its sixth, two; its fourth, three; its third, four; its half, six: but one, and two, and three, and four, and six, brought into a sum, make sixteen.

## The different account of perfect, imperfect, and more-than-perfect numbers.

**2. 5.** And not to delay with many things, in the infinite series of numbers more numbers are found which either have such parts only singly, like three, and five, and the rest of that kind; or have such parts so many that, brought into one and reduced to a sum, they either stop short, as is the case with eight, and nine, and very many others, or exceed beyond, like twelve and eighteen, and many such. There are found, then, more of any of these than of those which are called perfect, in that they are completed by their such parts brought together. For after six, twenty-eight is found, which similarly consists of its such parts: for it has five of them: a twenty-eighth, a fourteenth, a seventh, a fourth, a half; that is, one, and two, and four, and seven, and fourteen; which brought together complete the same twenty-eight: for they make twenty-eight. And the further the order of numbers proceeds, the more these numbers are found at intervals, proportionally more extended, which, by their such parts reduced to a sum, meet themselves, and are called perfect. For those numbers whose such parts brought together do not fill up the same numbers whose parts they are, are named imperfect: but those whose parts even exceed are called more than perfect.

## The order of creation in relation to the account of numbers.

**2. 6.** With the number of days, then, being perfect, that is, six, God perfected his works which he had made. For so it is written: *and God finished on the sixth day his works which he had made*[^2]*.* But I become more intent upon this number when I consider also the order of the works themselves. For just as that same number rises step by step by its parts into a triangle—for one, two, three so follow that no other can be interposed, which singly are the parts of six of which it consists: one, the sixth; two, the third; three, the half—so in one day the light was made, but in the two following the fabric of this world; in one the upper part, that is, the firmament, in the other the lower, that is, the sea and the earth.

But the upper part he filled with no kinds of corporeal foods, because he was not going to place there bodies needing such refreshment; but the lower, which he was going to adorn with animals suited to it, he first enriched with the foods necessary for their need. In the remaining three days, then, were created the things which, within the world—that is, within this visible whole made of all the elements—would be driven, as visible things, by their own fitting motions: first the luminaries in the firmament, because the firmament had been made first; then the living things in the lower part, as the order itself demanded, on one day those of the waters, on another those of the lands.

Nor is anyone so mad as to dare to say that God could not have made all things in one day, if he had willed; or, if he had willed, in two days, on one day the spiritual creature, and on another the corporeal; or on one day the heaven with all that belongs to it, and on another the earth with all that is in it; and altogether when he willed, and as long as he willed, and how he willed: who is there who would say that anything could have resisted his will?

## On Wisdom 11:21: You have ordered all things in measure and number and weight.

**3. 7.** Wherefore, when we read that he perfected all things in six days, and, considering the number six, find it to be perfect, and that the order of creatures so runs that there appears even, as it were, a graded distinction of the very parts by which this number is perfected; let this too come to mind, which is said to him in another place of the Scriptures, *you have ordered all things in measure, and number, and weight*[^3]; and let the soul thus consider—the one who can, God being invoked to aid, and imparting and inspiring its powers—whether these three, measure, number, weight, in which it is written that God ordered all things, were somewhere before the whole of nature was created, or whether they too were created; and if they were before, where they were.

For before the creature there was nothing but the Creator. In him, then, they were. But how? For we read that even these things which were created are in him[^4]: were those things like himself, but these like things in him by whom they are ruled and governed?

And how were those things himself? For God is not measure, or number, or weight, or all these things. Or, according to that by which we know measure in the things we measure, and number in the things we number, and weight in the things we weigh, God is not these things: but according to that by which measure prefixes a limit to every thing, and number affords a form to every thing, and weight draws every thing to rest and stability, he is, primally and truly and singularly, these things, who terminates all things, and forms all things, and orders all things; and nothing else is understood to have been said—in the way it could be, through the human heart and tongue—*you have ordered all things in measure, and number, and weight,* than: "you have ordered all things in yourself"?

**3. 8.** It is a great thing, and granted to few, to exceed all things that can be measured, so that there is seen measure without measure; to exceed all things that can be weighed, so that there is seen number without number; to exceed all things that can be weighed, so that there is seen weight without weight.

## Measure, number, and weight are not in visible things alone but in the affections of the mind and the affairs of the mind; number without number.

**4. 8.** For measure and number and weight can be observed and thought of not only in stones and wood and masses of that kind, and in corporeal things of any sort, whether earthly or heavenly. But there is a measure of doing something, lest the progress be unrecallable and immoderate; and there is a number both of the affections of the mind and of the virtues, by which it is gathered from the deformity of folly to the form and beauty of wisdom; and there is a weight of the will and of love, where it appears at how much each thing is weighed in desiring, fleeing, preferring, and putting after: but these measure, number, and weight of minds are themselves restrained by another measure, and formed by another number, and drawn by another weight.

But measure without measure is that to which is equaled what is from it, nor is it itself from anything else: number without number is that by which all things are formed, nor is it itself formed: weight without weight is that by which things are referred so that they may rest, whose rest is pure joy, nor is it now referred to anything else.

## How the words measure, and so on, are to be taken.

**4. 9.** But whoever knows the names of *measure* and *number* and *weight* only visibly, knows them slavishly. Let him, then, transcend all that he so knows, or, if he cannot yet, let him not cling to the very names of which he cannot think except basely. For these things are dear to each in the higher realm by as much as he himself is less flesh in the lower.

But if someone does not wish to transfer those terms, which he learned in the lowest and most abject things, to those sublime things, in gazing at which he tries to make the mind serene, he is not to be pressed to do so.

For provided that this be understood which is to be understood, it is not greatly to be cared about what it is called. Yet one must know what sort of likeness there is of the lower to the higher. For not otherwise does reason rightly tend and strive from here to there.

## Wisdom 11:21 is explained.

**4. 10.** But now if anyone says that measure and number and weight were created, in which Scripture testifies that God ordered all things; if he ordered all things in them, where did he order these same things? If in others, how then are all things in them, when they themselves are in others? It is therefore not to be doubted that those are outside the things that are ordered, in which all things are ordered.

## That in God himself is the account of measure, number, and weight, according to which all things were ordered.

**5. 11.** Or shall we perhaps think it was so said, *you have ordered all things in measure, and number, and weight*[^5]*,* as if it were said, "you have so ordered all things that they should have measure, and number, and weight"? Because even if it were said, "you have ordered all bodies in colors," it would not be a consequence that the very Wisdom of God, *through which all things were made,* should be understood to have had colors first in herself, in which she might make the bodies.

But it would be taken thus, "you have ordered all bodies in colors," as if it were said, "you have so ordered all bodies that they should have colors." As though indeed bodies ordered in colors by God the Creator, that is, so ordered that they should be colored, could be understood otherwise than that some account of distributing colors to the several kinds of bodies was not lacking in the very wisdom of the orderer, even if color is not there called by name. For this is what I said: provided the matter be granted, one need not labor about the terms.

## A probable explanation of the verse Wisdom 11:21.

**5. 12.** Let us grant, then, that it was so said, *you have ordered all things in measure, and number, and weight*[^6]*,* as though it had been said, so ordered that they should have their own proper measures, and their own numbers, and their own weight, which in them would be changed according to the mutability of each one's kind, by increases and diminutions, by multitude and fewness, by lightness and heaviness, according to the disposition of God: do we then, just as these things are changed, say that the very counsel of God, in which he ordered them, is changeable? May he himself avert so great madness.

**6. 12.** When, therefore, these things were so ordered that they should have their own measures, and numbers, and weights, where did the orderer himself discern them? For not outside himself, as we discern bodies with the eyes; which surely did not yet exist, when they were being ordered that they should come to be. Nor did he discern these things within himself in the way we discern in the mind the images of bodies which are not present to the eyes, but think, by imagining, of those which we have seen, or out of those which we have seen. In what way, then, did he discern these things, so as to order them thus? In what, except in that in which he alone can?

**6. 13.** Nevertheless we mortals and sinners, whose *corruptible bodies weigh down the soul, and whose earthly dwelling presses down the sense thinking many things*[^7]; even if we bore the most pure hearts and the simplest minds, and *were already equal to the holy angels*, the divine substance would certainly not be so known to us as it is to itself.

## How we discern the perfection of the number six.

**7. 13.** Yet this perfection of the number six we discern neither outside ourselves, as bodies with the eyes, nor within ourselves, as the images of bodies and the likenesses of visible things, but in a certain other, far different way.

For although certain images, as it were, of little bodies obtrude themselves upon the gaze of the mind, when the composition or order or partition of the number six is thought of: yet reason, stronger and more powerful from above, does not assent to them, but inwardly contemplates the power of number; through which contemplation it confidently says that what is called "one" in numbers can be divided into no parts; but that no bodies can be divided except into innumerable parts; and that heaven and earth, which were fashioned according to the number six, would more easily pass away than it could be brought about that the number six should not be completed by its parts.

Let the human mind, therefore, always give thanks to the Creator, by whom it was so created that it can see this, which no bird, no beast can—which nevertheless see with us both heaven, and earth, and the luminaries, and the sea, and the dry land, and all the things that are in them.

## That God perfected his works in six days because the number six is perfect.

**7. 14.** Wherefore we cannot say that the number six is perfect because God perfected all his works in six days; but rather that God perfected his works in six days because the number six is perfect. And so, even if these works did not exist, that number would be perfect; but unless that number were perfect, these works would not be made perfect according to it.

## How the rest of God on the seventh day is to be understood.

**8. 15.** Now, as for what is written, that God rested on the seventh day from all his works which he had made; and therefore blessed and sanctified that same day, because in it he rested from all his works: so that we may try, as we can, as far as we are aided by him, to attain it by understanding, let us first drive from our minds the carnal suspicions of men about this. For is it lawful to say or to believe that God labored in working, when he founded the things written above, when *he spoke, and they came to be*? For not even a man labors, if something to be done, as soon as he has spoken, comes to be.

For although human words, supported by sounds, are uttered in such a way that a long discourse wearies: yet when they are as few as the few we read in what is written, when God said, *let there be light: let there be a firmament*[^8]*,* and the rest down to the end of the works which he completed on the sixth day; it is the raving of an exceedingly absurd delirium to think this a labor even of a man, much less of God.

## A figurative interpretation.

**8. 16.** Or will someone perhaps say that, in saying that they should come to be, which were at once made, God did not labor, but perhaps in thinking what ought to come to be; from which care, as though freed by the perfection of things, he rested, and for that reason willed to bless and sanctify the day on which he was first made free from this intention of mind? But if to think these things is to be very foolish (for both the capacity and the ease of founding things is, with God, incomparable and ineffable).

## By what kind of expression it is to be understood that God rested, whom it is impious to believe to have labored.

**9. 16.** What remains for us to understand, except perhaps that to the rational creature, in which he also created man, he afforded rest in himself, after its perfection, through the gift of the Holy Spirit, *through whom love is poured out in our hearts*[^9], so that we may be borne thither by the longing of desire, that, when we have come, we may rest, that is, seek nothing further? For just as God is rightly said to do whatever we do by him working in us; so God is rightly said to rest, when by his gift we shall rest.

**9. 17.** This indeed we rightly understand; because it is both true, and does not need great attention, to see that God is said to rest, when he makes them rest, just as he is said to know, when he brings it about that we know. For God does not in time know what before he did not know; and yet he says to Abraham, *now I know that you fear God*[^10]: where what else do we take it to mean than, "now I have made you to know"?

By these modes of expression, when we speak of things which do not happen to God as though they happened to him, we recognize that he brings it about that they happen to us; those, at least, which are praiseworthy: and these as far as the usage of the Scriptures admits. For we ought not rashly to say any such thing about God which we do not read in his Scripture.

## Other passages of holy Scripture expressed by the words of the same kind of expression.

**9. 18.** By this mode of expression I think that was said by the Apostle, *do not grieve the Holy Spirit of God, in whom you were sealed unto the day of redemption*[^11]. For the very substance of the Holy Spirit, by which it is whatever it itself is, cannot be grieved; since it has eternal and unchangeable blessedness, and is rather the very eternal and unchangeable blessedness. But because it so dwells in the saints that it fills them with love, by which men must in time rejoice at the advancement of the faithful and at good works; and therefore must also be grieved at the fall or sins of those at whose faith and piety they had rejoiced; which grief is praiseworthy, because it comes from the love which the Holy Spirit pours in: therefore the Spirit itself is said to be grieved by those who so act that the saints are grieved by their deeds, for no other reason than that they have the Holy Spirit; by which gift they are so good that the evil sadden them, especially those whom they have either known or believed to have been good. Which grief, surely, is not only not to be blamed, but even especially to be praised and proclaimed.

## The same kind of expression of the Apostle is explained.

**9. 19.** By this kind of expression the same Apostle again wonderfully made use, where he says, *but now, knowing God, or rather being known by God*[^12]. For God had not then come to know them, foreknown, that is, *before the foundation of the world*[^13]; but because they themselves had then come to know him by his gift, not by their own merit or capacity, he preferred to speak figuratively, so as to say that they were then known by him, when he afforded himself to them to be known, and to correct the word, as though he had said less rightly what he had properly said, rather than to allow them to arrogate to themselves that they had been able to do what he had granted them to be able to do.

## It is asked whether God himself could properly rest.

**10. 20.** This understanding, then, in that which is set down, that God rested from all his works which he made very good, because he himself makes us rest when we have done good works, will perhaps suffice for some: but we, having undertaken the consideration of this sentence of the Scriptures, are pressed to inquire whether he himself too could in some way rest; although, his rest having been intimated to us, he has admonished us to hope that our rest will be in him.

For just as he himself made heaven and earth, and the things that are in them, and completed all things on the sixth day; nor can it be said that we created anything in them, he granting that we should create, and therefore that it was said, *God finished on the sixth day his works which he made*[^14]*,* because he himself granted that they should be finished by us.

So too that which was said, *God rested on the seventh day from all his works which he made*[^15]*,* we ought to understand not, indeed, of our rest, which we shall attain with his granting, but primarily of *his own*, by which he rested on the seventh day, the works being completed; so that first all the things that are written may be shown to have been made, and then, if there is need, they may be taught to have signified something as well.

For it is rightly said that, just as God rested after his good works, so we too shall rest after our good works. But for this reason it is also rightly demanded that, just as it was disputed concerning the works of God, which sufficiently appear to be his own; so concerning the rest of God let it be sufficiently discussed, which is shown to be properly his own.

## It is asked how both can be true—that God rested on the seventh day and that he is still at work.

**11. 21.** For this reason we are compelled by the most just reasoning to investigate, if we are able, and to explain how both are true: both what is written here, that on the seventh day God rested from all his works that he had made, and what he says himself in the Gospel, he through whom all things were made: *My Father is working still, and I am working*[^16].

For he gave this answer to those who complained that, on account of God's rest commended of old by the authority of this Scripture, the Sabbath was not observed by him. And it can indeed be said with probability that the Sabbath was commanded to be observed by the Jews as a shadow of what was to come, foreshadowing the spiritual rest that God, by the example of this rest of his, promised with a hidden meaning to the faithful who do good works.

And the Lord Christ too, who suffered only when he willed, confirmed the mystery of this rest even by his burial. For on the very day of the Sabbath he rested in the tomb, and he kept that whole day as a kind of holy repose, after on the sixth day—that is, the Day of Preparation, which they call the sixth day of the week—he had completed all his works, when the things written about him were fulfilled on the gibbet of the cross itself. For he used this very word when he said, *It is finished; and he bowed his head and gave up his spirit*[^17].

What wonder, then, if God, wishing to foretell in this way too the day on which Christ would rest in burial, rested from his works on one day, thereafter to carry on the order of the ages, so that this too might truly be said: *My Father is working still?*

## Another way of reconciling Scripture concerning God's rest and his continual working.

**12. 22.** It can also be understood that God rested from founding the kinds of creation, because he founded no further new kinds; but that thereafter, up to now and beyond, he works at the administration of those same kinds that were then established—not such that, even on the seventh day itself, his power would cease from the governance of heaven and earth and all the things he had founded, for otherwise they would at once dissolve.

For the power of the Creator, and the might of him who is Almighty and All-sustaining, is the cause of subsisting for every creature; and if this might should at any time cease to govern the things that have been created, their form would likewise cease, and all their nature would collapse. For it is not as with the builder of a house, who departs once he has built it, and his work stands while he ceases and departs; the world will not be able to stand even for the blink of an eye if God withdraws his governance from it.

## The same matter continued.

**12. 23.** Accordingly, what the Lord says, *My Father is working still*[^18]*,* shows a kind of continuation of his work, by which he holds together and administers the whole of creation. For it could be understood one way if he said, "and now he is working," where there would be no need for us to take it as a continuation of his work; but it compels us to understand it another way when he says *still,* that is, from the time when he did his work in founding all things.

And what is written about his Wisdom, that *she reaches mightily from one end to the other, and orders all things sweetly*[^19], of whom it is likewise written that *her movement is more agile and swifter than all motions*[^20], makes it sufficiently clear to those who see rightly that this very incomparable and ineffable, and—if it can be understood—stable, movement of hers is what she affords to things to be ordered sweetly; and that if this were withdrawn, if she ceased from this working, they would at once perish.

And what the Apostle said when he preached God to the Athenians, *In him we live and move and have our being*[^21], considered as clearly as the human mind is able, supports this view, by which we believe and say that God works unceasingly in the things he has created. For we are not in him as though his substance were our being, in the way it is said that *he has life in himself*[^22]; rather, since we are something other than he is, we are in him for no other reason than that he does this, and this is his work by which he holds together all things, and by which his *Wisdom reaches mightily from one end to the other, and orders all things sweetly*; through which ordering *in him we live and move and have our being.* From which it is gathered that if he withdrew this work of his from things, we would neither live nor move nor be.

It is clear, therefore, that not even for a single day did God cease from the work of governing the things he created, lest they should at once lose those natural movements by which they are driven and quickened, so as to be natures at all and to remain in what they are, each according to its kind, and should altogether cease to be anything, if that movement of the Wisdom of God by which he orders all things sweetly were withdrawn from them. For this reason we understand that God rested from all his works that he had made in such a way that he would found no further new nature, not such that he ceased to hold together and govern the things he had founded. Hence both this is true, that *on the seventh day God rested*[^23], and this, that *he is working still*[^24]*.*

## On the observance of the Sabbath and on the Christian Sabbath.

**13. 24.** And we see indeed that his works are good; but his rest after our good works we shall see. To signify this he commanded the Hebrew people to observe one day[^25]—which they did so carnally that, seeing our Lord and Savior work on that day, they made it a charge against him; and so he most rightly answered them concerning the work of the Father, with whom he too was working equally, not only the administration of the whole of creation, but also our very salvation.

But now, in the time of revealed grace, that observance of the Sabbath, which was figured by the rest of one day, has been taken away from the observance of the faithful.

For in that grace he already observes a perpetual Sabbath who, in hope of future rest, does whatever good he does, and does not glory in his very good works as though he had a good that he had not received; for thus, receiving and understanding the sacrament of Baptism as the day of the Sabbath—that is, of the Lord's repose in the tomb—he rests from his former works, so that now, walking in newness of life[^26], he may recognize that God works in him, who at once both works and rests, both affording fitting governance to creation and keeping eternal tranquility with himself.

## Why God sanctified only the day of rest.

**14. 25.** Finally, he himself was neither wearied when he created nor refreshed when he ceased; but he willed through his Scripture to exhort us to a desire for rest, intimating to us that he sanctified that day on which he rested from all his works. For nowhere in any of the six days on which all things were created do we read that he sanctified anything, nor before those six days, where it is written, *in the beginning God made heaven and earth*[^27], is it added, *And he sanctified it*; but this day, on which he rested from all his works that he had made, he willed to sanctify, as though even with him, who toils at nothing in his work, rest is worth more than working.

This indeed the Gospel intimates to us in the case of human beings, where our Savior says that the better part was Mary's, who, sitting at his feet, was resting in his word, than Martha's, although she was occupied about many things on account of the service by which she ministered to him, and although she was doing a good work[^28].

But how this is or is to be understood in God is hard to say, even if it can be touched a little by thought—why God sanctified the day of his rest, who sanctified none of the day of his work, not even the sixth itself, on which he both made man and completed all things. And first of all, what is the nature of God's very resting, which the keen edge of the human mind can attain? Yet unless this were so, this Scripture would not set it down at all.

I will say plainly what I think, prefacing these two things as beyond doubt: that God was not delighted by some temporal rest, as though after labor or after the desired end of his business; and that these Writings, deservedly preeminent in such great authority, did not say in vain and falsely that God on the seventh day rested from all his works that he had made, and that for this reason he sanctified that same day.

## The question posed above is resolved.

**15. 26.** Without doubt, then, because it is a fault and a weakness of the soul to be so delighted by its own works that it rests in them rather than rests from them in itself—since beyond doubt there is something better in the soul, by which they were made, than the very things that were made—God is intimated to us through this Scripture, in which he is said to have rested from all his works that he had made, as delighted by no work of his as though he had needed to make it, or would have been lesser had he not made it, or happier once he had made it.

For since whatever is from him is from him in such a way that it owes to him what it is, while he himself owes to nothing that is from himself the fact that he is blessed, he set himself before the things he made by loving them—not sanctifying the day on which he began to make them, nor the one on which he completed them, lest his joy should seem to be increased by their being made or having been made, but the one on which he rested from them in himself.

And he himself indeed was never without this rest, but he showed it to us through the seventh day; signifying by this too that his rest is not perceived except by the perfect, since to intimate it he assigned no day but the one that followed the perfection of all things. For he who is always at rest then rested for us, when he showed that he had rested.

## That God does not need the works he has made.

**16. 27.** This too must be attended to: that God's rest, by which he is blessed in himself, had to be intimated to us, so that we might understand how he is also said to rest in us—which is not said except when he grants rest in himself to us as well. God's rest, therefore, to those who understand rightly, is that by which he needs the good of no one; and so it is sure for us too in him, because we too are made blessed by the good that he is, not he by the good that we are. For we too are some good thing from him, who made all things very good, among which he made us also. Moreover, there is no other good thing besides himself that he did not make; and thus he needs no other good besides himself, who does not need the good that he made. This is his rest from all his works that he had made.

But of what goods would he laudably have no need, if he had made none? For it might be said in this way too that he needs no goods—not by resting in himself from things made, but by making none at all. But if he could not make good things, there would be no power; while if he could and yet did not make them, there would be great grudging.

Because, then, he is almighty and good, he made all things very good; but because he is perfectly blessed by himself as his own good, he rested in himself from all the things he made—with that rest, namely, from which he never departed. But if he were said to have rested from making things, nothing else would be understood than that he had not made them. And unless he were said to have rested from things made, his not needing the things he made would be less commended.

## Why God rested after the sixth day.

**16. 28.** On what day, then, was this fitting to be commended, if not the seventh? Anyone understands this who recalls the perfection of the number six, of which we spoke above, as fittingly applied to the perfection of creation. For if creation had to be perfected in the number six, as it was perfected, and that rest of God had to be commended to us, by which it would be shown that he is not made blessed even by perfect creatures, then without doubt the day to be sanctified in this commendation was the one that follows the sixth, so that we might be raised up to long for this rest, that we too might rest in him.

## Our rest in God.

**17. 29.** For the likeness is not a holy one, if we should wish to be like God in such a way that we too rest from our works in ourselves, as he rested in himself from his works. For we ought to rest in a certain unchangeable good, which he is to us who made us. This, then, will be our supreme and least proud and truly holy rest: that, just as he rested from all his works—because not his works, but he himself is the good to himself by which he is blessed—so we too may hope that we shall rest from all works, not only our own but also his own, in him alone; and may desire this after our good works, which we acknowledge in ourselves to be his rather than ours, so that even thus he may rest after his good works, when, after the good works that we, justified by him, have done, he grants us rest in himself.

For it is a great thing for us to have come into being from him, but it will be a greater thing to have rested in him. Just as he is not blessed because he made these things, but because, not needing even the things made, he rested in himself rather than in them. Hence he sanctified not the day of work, but of rest; because he intimated himself blessed not by doing these things, but by not needing the things he had made.

## Conclusion: God always rests in himself because he is blessed in himself alone.

**17. 30.** What, then, is so humble and easy to utter, and what so sublime and hard to think, as God resting from all his works that he had made? And resting where, if not in himself, since he is blessed by nothing but himself? When, if not always? But in the days in which the completion of the things he founded is narrated, and the order of God's rest is distinguished from them, when, if not on the seventh day, which follows their perfection? For he rests from the perfect, who has no need even of the perfect, by which he might be made happier.

## Why God's rest lacks morning and evening.

**18. 31.** And as for the fact that with him indeed his rest has neither morning nor evening—because it is neither opened by a beginning nor closed by an end—yet in relation to his perfected works it has a morning and does not have an evening; for the perfected creature has a certain beginning of its turning toward the rest of its Creator, but that rest has no end as a kind of limit of its perfection, like the things that have been made. And thus God's rest begins not for God himself, but for the perfection of the things founded by him, so that what is perfected by him may begin to rest in him, and to have its morning in him; for in its own kind it was brought to an end as though at evening, but in God it can no longer have an evening, because there will be nothing more perfect than that perfection.

## That the end of the day is the evening, namely night, and the morning is the beginning of another day, so that in the natural order the day is recognized to begin from light.

**18. 32.** For in those days on which all things were being created, we took the evening as the limit of the creature founded, and the morning as the beginning of another to be founded. And thus the evening of the fifth day is the limit of the creature founded on the fifth day; while the morning that was made after that evening is the beginning of the creature to be founded on the sixth day, and once it was founded, the evening was made as its limit.

And because nothing else remained to be founded, the morning was made after that evening in such a way that it was not the beginning of another creature to be founded, but the beginning of the rest of all creation in the repose of the Creator.

For heaven and earth and all the things that are in them—that is, the whole of creation, spiritual and corporeal—does not remain in itself, but assuredly in him of whom it is said, *for in him we live and move and have our being*[^29]. For although each part can be in the whole of which it is a part, yet the whole itself is only in him by whom it was founded. And therefore it is not absurdly understood that, the sixth day being completed, the morning was made after its evening, not so as to signify the beginning of another creature to be founded, as in the rest, but so as to signify the beginning of the remaining and resting of all that was founded, in the repose of him who founded it. This rest has for God neither beginning nor limit; but for creation it has a beginning, though it does not have a limit; and therefore the seventh day began for that same creation from a morning, but is ended by no evening.

## How the days of creation differ from those of our week.

**18. 33.** For if on the other days evening and morning signify the alternations of such times as are now carried out through these daily spans, I do not see what would have prevented closing the seventh day too with an evening, and its morning with a night, so that it would similarly be said, "and evening came, and morning came, the seventh day"—since indeed it too is one of the days, which are all seven, by whose repetition months and years and ages are carried out; so that the morning that would be set after the evening of the seventh would be the beginning of the eighth day, about which thereafter silence was to be kept, because it is the same as the first, to which there is a return, from which again the series of the week takes its order.

Hence it is more probable that these seven days, by their names and number, succeed one another again and again as they run on, carrying out temporal spans; but that those first six days were unfolded in a kind of form unexperienced and unfamiliar to us, in the very foundings of things, in which both evening and morning, like light and darkness themselves—that is, day and night—did not afford that alternation which these days afford through the circuits of the sun: which we are certainly compelled to admit concerning those three days that were recorded and numbered before the lights were founded.

## How God's rest and creation's rest are to be understood.

**18. 34.** And thus, whatever sort of evening and morning there may have been in them, in no way is it to be supposed that God's rest took its beginning from that morning which was made after the evening of the sixth day, lest with rash vanity we should suspect that some temporal good befell that eternity and unchangeableness; but rather that God's rest, by which he rests in himself, and is blessed by that good which he himself is to himself, has for him neither beginning nor limit; whereas the same rest of God has a beginning for the completed creature.

For the perfection of each thing is established—so that it may rest, that is, may hold the order of its proper measure—not so much in the universe of which it is a part as in him from whom it is, in whom the universe itself also is, according to the measure of its kind.

And thus the very totality of creation, which was completed in six days, has one thing in its own nature, another in the order by which it is in God—not as God is, yet still in such a way that for it there is no rest of its proper stability except in the repose of him who seeks nothing besides himself, and rests when he has attained it.

And therefore, while he himself remains in himself, he turns back to himself whatever is from him, so that every creature has in itself the limit of its own nature, by which it is not what he is, but in him has the place of rest, by which it preserves what it itself is.

I know that I have not said "place" in the proper sense; for it is properly said of the spaces occupied by bodies. But because even bodies themselves do not remain in a place except the one they reach by the appetite, as it were, of their own weight, so that, having found it, they rest there—therefore the word is not unfittingly transferred from corporeal to spiritual things, so that "place" is said, even though the thing itself is very far removed.

## Why the evening does not follow the morning of the seventh day.

**18. 35.** The beginning of creation in the rest of the Creator, then, I think is signified by that morning which was made after the evening of the sixth day; for it could not rest in him unless it were perfected. Accordingly, on the sixth day, when all things were completed and the evening was made, the morning too was made, on which the completed creature would begin to rest in him by whom it was made.

At this beginning it found God resting in himself, where it too could rest—the more stably and firmly inasmuch as it needed him, not he it, for its rest. But since whatever the whole of creation will be, amid whatever changes of its own, it will certainly not be nothing, therefore the whole of creation will always remain in its Creator; and thus after that morning there was thereafter no evening.

**18. 36.** We have said this to explain why the seventh day, on which God rested from all his works, had a morning after the evening of the sixth, but did not have an evening.

## Why the seventh day was without evening: another explanation.

**19. 36.** There is another thing on this matter that can, in my estimation, be understood more properly and better, but is somewhat harder to explain: namely, that God's rest on the seventh day had a morning without evening—that is, a beginning without limit—not for creation, but for himself as well. For if it were said merely, "God rested on the seventh day," and there were not added, "from all his works that he had made," we would seek the beginning of this rest in vain. For God does not begin to rest, whose rest is everlasting, without beginning, without limit.

But since he rested "from all his works that he had made," by not needing them, it is understood that God's rest indeed was neither begun nor ended, while his rest from all his works that he had made began from the moment when he perfected them.

For he would not have rested by not needing his works before they existed, the works that he did not need even once they were perfected; and because he never needed them at all, nor will this blessedness by which he does not need them be perfected as though by progress, therefore no evening was added to the seventh day.

## It is asked whether the seventh day was created.

**20. 37.** But plainly it can be asked—and it moves us with a consideration worthy of attention—how it is to be understood that God rested in himself from all his works that he had made, when it is written, *and God rested on the seventh day*[^30]*.* For it was not said "in himself," but *on the seventh day.* What, then, is the seventh day? Is it some creature, or only a span of time? But even a span of time is "co-created" with the temporal creature; and thus it too is without doubt a creature. For there neither are nor could nor will be any times of which God is not the Creator; and thus, who created this seventh day too, if it is time, but the Creator of all times? But with which creatures, or in which creatures, those six days were created, the earlier discourse of Holy Scripture indicates. Therefore, among these seven days, whose form is known to us—which indeed pass away in themselves, but in a manner hand on their names to others that succeed them, so that those six days are named—we know when the first of them was created; but when God created the seventh day, which is called by the name of the Sabbath, we do not see. For on that very day he did not make anything; rather, from the things he had made in six days he rested on that same seventh. How, then, did he rest on a day he did not create? Or how did he create it immediately after the six days, when on the sixth day he completed all the things he created, and created nothing on the seventh day, but rather rested on it from all the things he had created?

Or did God create only one day, so that by its repetition the many that are called days would pass and run by, and there was no need for him to create the seventh day, because the seventh repetition of the one he had created assuredly made it? For the light of which it is written, *and God said, "Let there be light"; and there was light*[^31], he separated from the darkness, and called it day, and called the darkness night. Then, therefore, God made the day whose repetition Scripture calls the second day, then the third, and on to the sixth, on which God completed his works; and from there the seventh repetition of that first-created day received the name of the seventh day, on which God rested. And thus the seventh day is no creature except that same one returning seven times, which was founded when God called the light day and called the darkness night.

## On the light that existed before the lights, for the alternation of day and night.

**21. 38.** Again, then, we slip back into that question from which we seemed to have escaped in the first book, so that we again ask how light could have gone round to exhibit the alternation of day and night, not only before the lights of heaven, but before heaven itself, which was called the firmament, was made—before, finally, there was any form of earth or sea that might admit the going round of the light, with the night following, by which it had passed on.

Driven by the difficulty of this question, we ventured to limit our discussion as it were to this view, saying that the light first made was the forming of the spiritual creature, while the night was the matter, still to be formed in the remaining works of things, which had been established when in the beginning God made heaven and earth, before he made the day by his word.

But now, admonished by the consideration of the seventh day, it is easier for us to confess that we do not know—since it is removed from our senses—in what way that light which was called day carried out the alternations of day and night, whether by its going round, or by its contraction and emission if it is corporeal; or, if it is spiritual, whether it was made present at the founding of all creatures, and by its own presence made the day, by its absence the night, by the beginning of its absence the evening, by the beginning of its presence the morning.

—than that, in so plain a matter, we should attempt something against the words of Divine Scripture, by saying that the seventh day is something other than the seventh repetition of that day which God made.

Otherwise either God did not create the seventh day, or he created something after those six days, namely the seventh day itself; and it would be false that it is written that on the sixth day he completed all his works, and on the seventh rested from all his works. Since this assuredly cannot be false, it remains that the presence of that light, which God made the day, was repeated through all his works, as often as a day was named, even on the seventh itself, on which he rested from his works.

## Morning and evening suggest the varying knowledge of the spiritual creature.

**22. 39.** But since we do not find by what going round, or by what advance and withdrawal, corporeal light, before the heaven called the firmament was made—in which the lights too were made—could have exhibited the alternations of day and night, we ought not to leave this question without bringing forward some view of our own: namely, that if that light which was first created is not corporeal but spiritual, then just as it was made after the darkness, where it is understood to have been turned from a certain formlessness of its own toward the Creator and formed, so too after the evening let the morning be made, when, after the knowledge of its own proper nature, by which it is not what God is, it refers itself to praising the light that God himself is, by the contemplation of whom it is formed. And because the other creatures, which are made beneath it, are not made without its knowledge, therefore no doubt the same day is everywhere repeated, so that by its repetition there are made as many days as there are distinct kinds of created things, to be brought to a close by the perfection of the number six.

So that the evening of the first day is also its knowledge that it is not what God is; while the morning after this evening, by which the one day is concluded and the second begun, is its turning, by which it refers what it was created to the praise of the Creator, and receives from the Word of God the knowledge of the creature that is made after it—that is, of the firmament: which is made first in its knowledge, when it is said, *and so it was made*; then in the nature of the firmament itself, which is founded, when it is added afterward, after it was already said, *and so it was made: and God made the firmament*[^32]*.* Then the evening of that light is made, when it comes to know the firmament itself, not in the Word of God as before, but in its own nature: which knowledge, because it is less, is rightly signified by the name of "evening."

After which the morning is made, by which the second day is concluded and the third begins; on which morning there is likewise the turning of this light—that is, of this day—to praising God for having wrought the firmament, and to receiving from his Word the knowledge of the creature that is to be founded after the firmament.

And thus, when God says, *let the water which is under heaven be gathered into one collection, and let the dry land appear*[^33]*,* that light knows this in the Word of God by which it is said; and therefore there follows, *and so it was made,* that is, in its knowledge from the Word of God; then, when it is added, *and the water was gathered together*[^34]*,* and the rest, after it had already been said, *and so it was made,* the creature itself is made in its own kind; and when this same creature, made in its own kind, is known by that light which had already known in the Word of God that it was to be made, the evening is made a third time. And thence in this way the rest, up to the morning after the evening of the sixth day.

## That there is a great difference between the knowledge of things in the Word of God and the knowledge of them in themselves.

**23. 40.** For there is a great difference between the knowledge of each thing in the Word of God and the knowledge of it in its own nature, so that the former rightly belongs to the day, the latter to the evening. For in comparison with that light which is beheld in the Word of God, all knowledge by which we know any creature in itself can not undeservedly be called night; which knowledge again differs so much from the error or ignorance of those who do not even know the creature itself, that in comparison with it, it is not unfittingly called day.

Just as the very life of the faithful, which is led in this flesh and in this age, is not unreasonably called light and day in comparison with the unbelieving and impious life, the Apostle saying, *you were once darkness, but now you are light in the Lord*[^35]; and this, *let us cast off the works of darkness, and put on the armor of light; let us walk becomingly as in the day*[^36]: which day, however, would itself again be night unless compared with that day on which, made equal to the angels, we shall see God as he is; here we would not need the lamp of prophecy, of which the Apostle Peter says, *we have the prophetic word made more sure, to which you do well to attend as to a lamp shining in a dark place, until the day dawns and the morning star rises in your hearts*[^37].

## On the knowledge of the angels.

**24. 41.** Therefore, since the holy angels, to whom we shall be made equal after the resurrection[^38], if we hold to the end the way that Christ has become for us, always see the face of God and enjoy his only-begotten Son, the Word, who is equal to the Father, in whom wisdom was created first of all—without doubt they first knew the whole of creation, in which they themselves are principally founded, in the very Word of God, in whom are the eternal reasons of all things, even of those made in time, as in him *through whom all things were made*; and then in the creature itself, which they knew as it were by looking down upon it from above, and by referring it to the praise of him in whose unchangeable Truth they principally see the reasons according to which it was made.

There, then, it is as it were through day—whence too their most harmonious unity, by the sharing of the same truth, is the day first created; here, however, it is as it were through evening; but the morning immediately comes (which can be observed in all six days), because the angelic knowledge does not remain in what is created without immediately referring it to the praise and love of him in whom it is known not to have been made, but to have been to be made; by standing in which Truth it is day. For if the angelic nature were to turn even to itself, and delight in itself more than in him by sharing in whom it is blessed, swelling up with pride it would fall, like the devil—of whom we must speak in its own place, when the discourse owed concerning the serpent who seduced man is demanded.

## Why night is not added to the six days.

**25. 42.** Because, then, the angels know the creature in that creature itself in such a way that, by choice and love, they set before that knowledge the fact that they know it in the Truth through which all things were made, being made sharers in it—therefore, throughout all six days, night is not named, but "after evening and morning, one day"; likewise "after evening and morning, a second day"; then "after evening and morning, a third day"; and so on up to the morning of the sixth day, whence the seventh, of God's rest, begins—although with their nights, yet days, not nights, are narrated.

For then night belongs to day, not day to night, when the lofty and holy angels refer what they have known of the creature in the creature itself to the honor and love of him in whom they contemplate the eternal reasons by which it was created; and by that most harmonious contemplation they are one *day, which the Lord has made,* to which the Church will be joined, freed from this pilgrimage, so that *we too may exult and rejoice in it*[^39].

## How the number of the days of creation is to be understood.

**26. 43.** By the sixth repetition, then, of this day—whose evening and morning can be understood according to the reasoning given above—the whole of creation was completed; and the morning was made by which the sixth day would be finished, and from which the seventh, not destined to have an evening, would begin, because God's rest is not a creature.

And this creation, while it was being founded through the other days, was known otherwise as made in itself than in him in whose Truth it was seen as to be made; and the as it were colorless form of that knowledge made the evening.

And so the form of the work itself ought not now to be understood as the day, and its limit as the evening, and the beginning of another work as the morning, in this narrative of the things founded; lest we be compelled to say, against Scripture, either that the creature of the seventh day was founded besides the six days, or that the seventh day itself is no creature.

Rather, that *day which God made* is itself repeated through his works, not by a corporeal going round, but by spiritual knowledge, when that blessed society of angels both first contemplates in the Word of God, by which God says, *let it be made*; and so it is made first in their knowledge, when it is said, *and so it was made*; and afterward it knows the thing itself, made, in that same thing, which is signified by the evening being made.

And then it refers that knowledge of the thing made to the praise of that Truth in which it had seen the reason for making it, which is signified by the morning being made. And so through all those days there is one day, to be understood not by the custom of these days, which we see determined and numbered by the going round of the sun, but in some other way, from which not even those three days, recorded before the founding of these lights, can be foreign.

For that mode was carried not only to the fourth day, so that from there we should think of these usual days, but to the sixth and seventh; so that the day and night between which two *God divided* are to be understood far otherwise than this day and night between which he said the lights he created should divide, when he said, *and let them divide the day from the night*[^40]. For then he founded this day, when he founded the sun, by whose presence it exhibits the same day; but that day first founded had already passed through three days when these lights were created on the fourth repetition of that day.

## The usual days of the week are far unequal to the seven days of Genesis.

**27. 44.** For this reason, since we cannot experience and perceive that day, or those days that were numbered by its repetition, in this earthly mortality of ours, and even if we can attempt anything toward understanding them, we ought not to rush headlong into a rash judgment, as though nothing more fitting and more probable could be thought about these matters.

Let us believe that these seven days, which act in place of those as a week—by whose course and recourse times are swept along, in which one day is the going round from the sun's rising to its rising—exhibit a certain likeness of those, in such a way that we in no way doubt that they are not like them, but very unequal.

## The foregoing interpretation of the spiritual light and day is by no means to be thought improper or figurative.

**28. 45.** And let no one think that what I have said about the spiritual light, and about the day founded in the spiritual and angelic creature, and about the contemplation it has in the Word of God, and about the knowledge by which the creature is known in itself, and about its referral to the praise of the unchangeable Truth, where the reason for the thing to be made was seen first, which was known once made—let no one think that this applies to the understanding of day and evening and morning not properly now, but as it were figuratively and allegorically; rather it is indeed otherwise than in this custom of this daily and corporeal light, yet not as though here it were proper and there figurative.

For where there is a better and more certain light, there too is a truer day; why then not also a truer evening and a truer morning? For if in these days the light has a certain decline of its own toward setting, which we call by the name of "evening," and again a return toward rising, which we call morning—why should we not also speak there of an evening, when the creature is looked down upon away from the contemplation of the Creator, and of a morning, when from the knowledge of the creature one rises to the praise of the Creator? For Christ is not called light[^41] in the way he is called a stone[^42]; rather the former properly, the latter assuredly figuratively.

Whoever, then, seeks in the enumeration of those days not the view which we, according to our measure, have been able to investigate or suppose, but another, which can be understood not figuratively in prophecy but properly and better in this founding of creatures—let him seek, and with divine help find. For it may be that even I will perhaps find another more fitting to these words of Divine Scripture. For I do not so affirm this view that I would contend that another, to be preferred, cannot be found—as I do affirm that Holy Scripture did not wish to intimate to us the rest of God as though after weariness or the trouble of care.

## That in angelic knowledge day, evening, and morning are simultaneous.

**29. 46.** For this reason someone may perhaps contend with me in argument, saying that the angels of the lofty heavens do not behold these things alternately—first the reasons of creatures unchangeably in the unchangeable Truth of the Word of God, and then the creatures themselves, and third their knowledge even in themselves, referring it to the praise of the Creator—but that their mind can do all these things at once with marvelous ease.

Yet will he say—or, if anyone says it, is he to be heeded—that that heavenly city in its thousands of angels either does not contemplate the eternity of the Creator, or is ignorant of the changeableness of the creature, or does not, from a certain lower knowledge of it as well, praise the Creator? Let them be able to do all this at once, let them do all this at once; they can, nonetheless, and they do. At once, therefore, they have both day, and evening, and morning.

## How in the heavenly homeland there is always day, evening, and morning in the knowledge of God and of the creature.

**30. 47.** For there is no need to fear lest perhaps one who is now competent to perceive those things should suppose that this cannot happen there because, in these days that are carried out by the going round of this sun, it cannot happen. And this indeed cannot happen for the same parts of the earth; but who would not see, if he is willing to attend, that the whole world has at once both day, where the sun is, and night, where it is not, and evening, from which it departs, and morning, to which it approaches?

But we plainly cannot have all these things at once on earth; nor for that reason, however, ought we to make this earthly condition and the temporal and local going round of corporeal light equal to that spiritual homeland, where there is always day in the contemplation of the unchangeable Truth, always evening in the knowledge of the creature in itself, always morning in the praise of the Creator even from this knowledge.

Because there the evening is made not by the withdrawal of the higher light, but by the distinction of the lower knowledge; nor does morning knowledge succeed as it were to a night of ignorance, but it is that it lifts up even the evening knowledge into the glory of the Founder. Finally, that other one too, with night not named, says, *at evening and morning and noonday I will tell and proclaim; and you will hear my voice*[^43]: here perhaps through the alternations of times, but yet, as I think, signifying what was being done without alternations of times in that homeland for which his pilgrimage sighed.

## How, at the beginning of creation, day, evening, and morning in angelic knowledge were not simultaneous.

**31. 48.** But if that angelic society and unity of the day which God founded first now carries on and has all these things at once, did it also have them at once then, when these things were being founded? Did it not, throughout all six days, when the things that it pleased God to found through each day were being founded, receive them first in the Word of God, so that they were made first in its knowledge, when it was said, *and so it was made*; then, when they had been made in their own proper nature by which they exist, and had pleased God because they are good, then again know them by a certain other, lower knowledge, which was signified by the name of *evening*; *and then, the evening being made, the morning came,* when God was praised for his work, and the knowledge of another creature, which was thereafter to be made, was received from the Word of God before it was made? Not, then, were all things at once then—both day and evening and morning—but one by one, in the order that Scripture records.

## Whether all those things in the knowledge of the angels were at least not without some order.

**32. 49.** Or were all things even then at once, since it was not according to delays of time, as these days are made when the sun rises and sets and returns to its place to rise again, but according to the spiritual power of the angelic mind, with its knowledge comprehending most easily at once all the things it willed? Yet not for that reason without the order by which the connection of preceding and following causes appears. For knowledge cannot come to be unless the things to be known precede; which are likewise prior in the *Word, through which all things were made,* than in all the things that were made.

And so the human mind first experiences these things that have been made through the senses of the body, and grasps their knowledge according to the measure of human weakness; and then it seeks their causes, in case it can in some way arrive at them as abiding principally and unchangeably in the Word of God, and thus *perceive his invisible things, understood through the things that have been made*[^44]. With how great slowness and difficulty it does this, and with how great a delay of time on account of *the corruptible body that weighs down the soul*[^45], even the mind that is swept on by the most fervent zeal to do this insistently and perseveringly—who does not know?

But the angelic mind, clinging to the Word of God with pure love, after it was created in that order so as to precede the rest, saw those things first in the Word of God as to be made, before they were made; and thus they were made first in its knowledge, when God said "that they be made," than in their own proper nature: which things, likewise made, it also knew in them themselves, by a lesser knowledge, of course, which was called evening.

Which knowledge, indeed, the things that were being made preceded; because whatever can be known precedes the knowledge of it. For unless what is to be known first exists, it cannot be known.

After this, if it should so please itself as to delight more in itself than in its Creator, the morning would not come—that is, it would not rise up from its knowledge to the praise of the Creator. But when the morning came, another thing was to be made and to be known, with God saying, *let it be made*; so that likewise it would be made first in the knowledge of the angelic mind, and it could again be said, *and so it was made*; and then in its proper nature, where, with the evening following, it would be known.

## On the creating light and the created light.

**32. 50.** And thus, even if there are here no intervals of temporal delays, yet the reason for founding the creature preceded in the Word of God, when he said, *let there be light*; and there followed the very light by which the angelic mind was formed, and was made in its own nature—but it did not follow elsewhere that it be made: and therefore it was not first said, *and so it was made,* and afterward said, "and God made the light"; but immediately after the Word of God the light was made, and the created light clung to the creating light, seeing it and itself in it—that is, the reason by which it was made. It also saw itself in itself—that is, with what was made distinct from him who made it.

And therefore, when the thing made had pleased God, who saw that it is good, and the light had been divided from the darkness, and the light called day, and the darkness night, the evening too was made: because that knowledge too was necessary, by which the creature would be distinguished from the Creator, known otherwise in itself than in him; and thence the morning, and another thing to be foreknown, which was to be made by the Word of God, first in the knowledge of the angelic mind, then in the nature of the firmament itself.

And therefore God said, *let there be a firmament; and so it was made*[^46]*,* in the knowledge of the spiritual creature foreknowing this in itself before it was made. Then *God made the firmament*; now indeed the very nature of the firmament, of which the knowledge would be lesser, as it were evening: and so on up to the end of all the works, and up to the rest of God, which has no evening, because it was not made like a creature, so that the knowledge of it too could be doubled, as it were prior and greater in the Word of God as in day, and later and lesser in itself as in evening.

## It is asked whether all things were founded at once, or through intervals of days.

**33. 51.** But if the angelic mind can do at once all the things that the narrative distinguishes one by one through the order of connected causes, were even the things that were being made—such as the firmament itself, such as the gathering of the waters, and the form of the lands laid bare, such as the budding of shrubs and trees, the forming of the lights and stars, the water and land animals—all made at once? Or rather through intervals of times according to the predetermined days? Or perhaps ought we not to think of them as they were when first established in the way we now experience them according to their natural movements, but according to the marvelous and ineffable power of the Wisdom of God, which *reaches mightily from one end to the other, and orders all things sweetly*[^47]? For neither does she herself reach by steps, or arrive as though by paces.

For this reason, as her most efficacious movement is easy for her, so easily did God found all things; since they were founded through her: so that the fact that we now see them moved through temporal intervals to carry out what befits each in its own kind comes from those implanted reasons which God scattered, as it were seminally, in the stroke of founding, when *he spoke, and they were made; he commanded, and they were created*[^48].

## What absurdities would follow unless it is held that all things were founded at once.

**33. 52.** Things were not, then, established slowly so that the things which are slow might be slow; nor were the ages founded with that delay by which they run their course. For times carry out these numbers, which, when they were created, they did not receive temporally.

Otherwise, if we think of the natural motions of things, and the usual spans of these days that we know, when these things were first made by the Word of God, there would have been need not of one day but of several, so that the things which sprout from roots and clothe the earth might first germinate underneath; then, after a certain number of days, each according to its kind, break out into the open air—even if this were done up to the point that Scripture narrated to have been done concerning their created nature on one day, that is, the third.

Then, how many days would have been needed for the birds to fly, if, existing from their first beginnings, they came to feathers and wings through the numbers of their nature? Or perhaps only eggs had been created, when on the fifth day it was said that the waters brought forth every winged flying thing according to its kind?

Or if this could rightly be said for the reason that in that moisture of the eggs were already all the things that, through certain numbers of days, coalesce and unfold in a manner, because the very numerical reasons were already present, incorporeally woven into corporeal things—why could not the same thing rightly have been said even before the eggs, when those same reasons were already coming to be in the moist element, by which winged creatures, through the temporal delays each of its own kind, could arise and be perfected?

For concerning the Creator about whom this Scripture narrated that he completed his works in six days, it is elsewhere written about him, not at all discordantly, *that he created all things at once*[^49].

And thus these six or seven days too—or rather the one day repeated six or seven times—he made at once, who made all things at once. Why, then, was there need that the six days be narrated so distinctly and in order? Because, of course, those who cannot see what is said, *he created all things at once*[^50]*,* except when the discourse proceeds with them more slowly, cannot arrive at that to which it leads them.

## All things at once, yet made through six days.

**34. 53.** How, then, do we say that the presence of that light was repeated six times through angelic knowledge from evening to morning, when those three at once—that is, both day and evening and morning—would have sufficed for it to have once: when it would at once contemplate the whole of creation, as it was made at once, both in the primal and unchangeable reasons through which it was founded, on account of the day, and know it in its own nature, on account of the evening, and praise the Creator from that lower knowledge as well, on account of the morning?

Or how did the morning precede, so that in the Word it knew what was afterward to be made by God, and was consequently going to know that same thing in the evening, if nothing was made earlier or later, because all things were made at once? Indeed rather, both "earlier and later" were made through the six days that have been recorded, and all things were made at once: because both this Scripture, which narrates the works of God through the said days, and that which says he made all things at once, is truthful; and both are one, because it was written down by the inspiration of one *Spirit of truth.*

## That in created things there is both "earlier" and "later," and that, on account of the usual course of times, it cannot be wholly expressed.

**34. 54.** But in these things, in which intervals of times do not show what is earlier or later, although both can be said—that is, "at once" and "earlier and later"—yet what is called "at once" is more easily understood than "earlier and later": as when we behold the rising sun, it is certainly evident that our gaze could not reach it unless it crossed the whole space of air and sky that is between us and it; but of what great distance this is, who is able to estimate? Nor would that same gaze, or ray of our eyes, reach to crossing the air that is over the sea, unless it had first crossed the air that is over the land, in whatever inland region we may be, from the place where we are to the seashore. Then, if along the same line of our gaze lands still lie beyond the sea, our gaze cannot cross the air over those lands beyond the sea either, unless the space of that air which extends over the sea first met is first traversed. Let us now suppose that beyond those lands across the sea nothing remains but the Ocean; can our gaze cross even the air spread over the Ocean, unless it has first crossed all the air there is on this side of the Ocean, above the land? But the magnitude of the Ocean is said to be incomparable; yet however great it is, the rays of our eyes must first cross the air that is above it, and afterward whatever is beyond, and then at last reach the sun, which we see.

Is it, then, because we have so often here said "earlier" and "afterward," that our gaze does not cross all things at once in one stroke? For if, with eyes closed, we set our face opposite to see the sun, will we not, as soon as we open them, think that we have rather found our gaze there than led it thither, so that the eyes themselves do not even seem to have been opened before our gaze had reached that to which it was directed? And surely this is a ray of corporeal light, flashing out from our eyes and touching things set so far off with such great speed that it cannot be estimated or compared. Surely it is here evident both that all those so vast and immense spaces are crossed at once in one stroke, and it is nonetheless certain what is crossed earlier and later.

## That all things were made at once according to a preestablished order.

**34. 55.** Rightly, when the Apostle wished to express the speed of our resurrection, he said it happens *in the twinkling of an eye*[^51]. For nothing can be found swifter among the movements or strokes of corporeal things. But if the gaze of fleshly eyes is capable of so much by speed, what of the gaze of the mind, even the human mind? How much more of the angelic? What now is to be said of the speed of the supreme Wisdom of God herself, which reaches everywhere on account of her purity, and into which nothing defiled enters[^52]? In these things, then, that were made at once, no one sees what ought to have been made earlier or later, except in that Wisdom through which all things were made, in order, at once.

## Conclusion concerning the six days: that earlier and later are in the connection of creatures, not in the efficacy of the Creator.

**35. 56.** That day, then, which God made first, if it is a spiritual and rational creature—that is, of the angels of the heights of heaven and of the virtues—was made present to all the works of God by this order of presence, which is the order of knowledge by which it both foreknew them in the Word of God as to be made, and knew them in the creature once made, not through the delays of temporal intervals, but having earlier and later in the connection of creatures, while in the efficacy of the Creator all things are at once. For he so made the things that were to be that he did not make temporal things temporally, but times ran from him once they were made.

And thus these seven days, which the light of the heavenly body unfolds and folds up again by its going round, by a certain shadow of meaning admonish us to seek those days in which the created spiritual light could have been made present to all the works of God through the perfection of the number six; and thence have a morning, but not an evening, in the seventh, the rest of God.

So that God's having rested on the seventh day does not mean that he had need of the seventh day itself for his rest; but that in the sight of his angels he rested from all his works that he had made—surely in none but himself, who was not made; that is, so that his angelic creature, which was made present, like a day with its evening, for the knowing of all his works in himself and in them, should know nothing better, after all his very good works, than that he rests from all of them in himself, needing none of them to be more blessed.
