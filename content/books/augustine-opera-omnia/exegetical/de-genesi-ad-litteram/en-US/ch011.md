# Book X

## Was the woman's soul made from the man's soul?

**1.** 1. Now indeed the very order seems to demand that we discuss the sin of the first man; but because Scripture narrated about the woman's flesh, how it was made, but was silent about her soul, it has made us much more attentive, that we inquire more diligently into this: in what way those can be refuted, or cannot be, who believe that the soul comes from the soul of man, just as flesh from flesh, the seeds of both being transfused from parents into children. For from this they are first moved to say that God made one soul, which he breathed into the face of the man whom he had formed from dust, so that from it the other souls of men might now be created, just as from his flesh all the flesh of men too. Since Adam was first formed, then Eve: and of him indeed it was said whence he had a body, whence a soul—the body, namely, the dust of the earth, but the soul, the breath of God; but since she is said to have been made from his side, it is not said that God likewise animated her by breathing, as though both were drawn from him who had already been animated.

For either it ought to have been kept silent, they say, about the man's soul too, so that we might either understand, or at least believe, as we could, that it was given divinely; or, if Scripture did not keep silent about this, lest we suppose the soul too, like the flesh, was made from earth, it ought also not to have kept silent about the woman's soul, lest it be supposed to be from transmission, if this is not true. Therefore, they say, it was for this reason kept silent that God breathed into her face, because this is true: that the soul too was propagated from the man.

## The foregoing suspicion is met.

**1.** 2. This suspicion is easily met. For if they think the woman's soul was made from the man's soul because it is not written that God breathed into the woman's face, why do they believe the woman was animated from the man, when not even that is written? Whence, if God makes all the souls of men being born, like the first, Scripture was for this reason silent about the others, because what is recorded to have been done in one could prudently be understood of the rest as well.

And so, if we had to be admonished of something about this matter through this Scripture, rather, if something else were being done in the woman that had not been done in the man—so that her soul was drawn from animated flesh, not, as her husband's, the body from one source and the soul from another—this very thing, that it was being done in another mode, Scripture rather ought not to have kept silent, lest we suppose that to have been done in the same way which we had already learned about him.

Accordingly, because it did not say that the woman's soul was made from the man's soul, it is more fittingly believed that by that very fact it wished to admonish us to suppose here nothing other than what we had known about the man's soul—that is, that it was likewise given to the woman: especially since there was a place of most evident occasion, so that, if not then when she was formed, at least afterward it would be said, where Adam says: *this now is bone of my bones, and flesh of my flesh*[^1]. For how much more dearly and lovingly would he say, "and soul of my soul"? Yet not on this account is so great a question now so solved that we hold one of these manifest and certain.

## On the origin of the woman's soul according to the foregoing inquiries.

**2.** 3. Therefore we must first see whether the holy Scripture of this book, treated from its beginning, permits us to be in doubt here: then perhaps we shall rightly inquire either which opinion is rather to be chosen, or what mode we ought to hold in the uncertainty of this matter. For certainly on the sixth day *God made man to his image*; where it was also said: *male and female he made them*[^2]. Of which the former, where the image of God is mentioned, we took according to the soul; but the latter, where the difference of sex is, according to the flesh.

And since so many and so great testimonies, which were considered and treated there, did not allow us to take it that on that same sixth day the man too was formed from the mire and the woman from his side, but that this was done afterward, after those first works of God in which he created all things at once, we inquired what we should believe about the man's soul; and, all the parts of our debate being discussed, it seemed more credible or more tolerable to say that the soul of man itself was made among those works, but the reason of his body in the corporeal world as though in a seed: lest we be compelled, against the words of Scripture, either to say that the whole was made on the sixth day—that is, both the man from the mire and the woman from his side; or that in those six days' works man was in no way made; or that only the causal reason of the human body was made, but none of the soul, when rather it is according to it that man is to the image of God.

Or at least, even if not openly against the words of Scripture, yet we would be saying something harsh and intolerable: either that in that spiritual creation, which had been created for this purpose only, the reason of the human soul was made, when the very creation in which that reason was said to be made is not mentioned among the works of God; or that the reason of the soul was made in some creation which is mentioned among those works, just as in men who now exist the reason of children to be begotten lies hidden; and thus we would believe it to be either the daughter of angels, or—what is more intolerable—of some corporeal element.

## Three opinions on the origin of souls.

**3.** 4. But now, if on this account the woman is asserted to have received a soul made by God not from the man, but, like him, because God makes them one by one for each, then the woman's soul was not made among those first works; or if a general reason of all souls had been made, as in men the reason of begetting, we return to that harsh and troublesome thing, that we should say the souls of men are daughters either of angels, or—what is most unworthy—of the corporeal heaven, or even of some lower element.

And by this it must be seen, even if what is true lies hidden, what at least can be more tolerably said: whether this which I have just said, or that among those first works of God one soul of the first man was made, from whose propagation all the souls of men would be created; or that new souls come into being from time to time, of which no reason, even, preceded as having been made in those first six days' works of God. But of these three, the first two are not repugnant to those first establishments, where all things were created at once. For whether in some creation, as in a parent, the reason of the soul was made, so that all souls are generated from it, but are created by God when they are given to individual men, as bodies are given by human parents; or whether not the reason of the soul as the reason of offspring in a parent, but the soul itself altogether was made when the day was made, as the day itself, as heaven and earth, and the lights of heaven—fittingly it was said: *God made man to his image*[^3]*.*

## How the third opinion can agree with the creation of all things made at once.

**3.** 5. But how this third does not conflict with that opinion by which man is taken both to have been made on the sixth day to the image of God, and to have been visibly created after the seventh day, cannot so easily be seen. For if we say that new souls come into being, which neither themselves nor their reason, as offspring in a parent, were made on that sixth day with those works, from which, completed and begun, God rested on the seventh day, we must beware lest in vain Scripture so diligently commend that God completed in six days all his works, which he made very good, if he was still going to create some natures which neither themselves nor their reasons he there made causally.

Unless it be understood that he has in himself the reason of souls to be made one by one, to be given to each one being born, and did not establish it in any creation; but that, because the soul is of no other kind of creation than that according to which on the sixth day man was made to the image of God, God is not rightly said to make now that which he did not then complete. For he had already then made a soul, such as he makes now too; and therefore he does not now make any new kind of creation which he did not then create among his completed works; nor is this working of his against those causal reasons of future things which he then imparted to the universe, but rather according to them; since to human bodies, whose propagation is extended from those first works by continued succession, it is fitting that such souls be inserted as he now makes and inserts.

## That the question is to be treated more diligently.

**3.** 6. Therefore, now fearing nothing—lest we seem to hold, against the words of this book which were written about that first establishment of the six days, that the probability of any of these three opinions has prevailed—let us undertake a more diligent treatment of this question, as much as God helps: lest perhaps it may come about that, if not a clear opinion about which there ought to be no further doubt, we may at least obtain so acceptable an opinion about this matter that it would not be absurd to hold it until something certain shines forth. But if we cannot do even this, the weights of the documents wavering on all sides and equally, at least our doubt will not seem to have shunned the labor of seeking, but the rashness of affirming: so that, if anyone is now rightly certain, he may deign to teach me; but if anyone has been made certain not by the authority of divine speech, nor of clear reason, but by his own presumption, he may not disdain to doubt along with me.

**4.** 7. And first let us hold this most firmly: that the nature of the soul is neither changed into the nature of a body, so that what was already soul becomes a body; nor into the nature of an irrational soul, so that what was the soul of a man becomes that of cattle; nor into the nature of God, so that what was soul becomes what God is: and so, conversely, neither a body, nor an irrational soul, nor the substance that is God, is changed and becomes a human soul. This too ought to be no less certain: that the soul is nothing but a creature of God.

Therefore, if God made the soul of man neither from a body, nor from an irrational soul, nor from himself, it remains that he either makes it from nothing, or from some spiritual yet rational creature. But to wish to show that something is made from nothing after the works were completed, by which he created all things at once, is forced; and whether it can be maintained by clear documents, I do not know. Nor is it to be demanded of us what a man either cannot comprehend, or, if he now can, it is a wonder if he can persuade anyone, except such a one who, even with no man attempting to teach, can himself understand some such thing. It is safer, then, in matters of this kind, to deal not with human conjectures, but to examine the divine testimonies.

## It is denied that souls are made from angels, or from elements, or from the substance of God.

**5.** 8. That God creates souls from angels, as from parents, no authority from the canonical Books occurs to me. Much less, then, from the corporeal elements of the world: unless perhaps that moves someone which is in the prophet Ezekiel, when the resurrection of the dead is shown, the bodies being restored, the spirit is called from the four winds of heaven, by whose blowing they are made alive, so that they rise. For so it is written: *and the Lord said to me, Prophesy over the spirit; prophesy, son of man, and say to the spirit: Thus says the Lord: From the four parts of the winds of heaven come, and breathe into these dead, and let them live. And I prophesied, as the Lord commanded me, and the spirit of life entered into them, and they revived, and stood upon their feet, an exceedingly great gathering*[^4]. Where it seems to me to be prophetically signified that men would rise not only from that field where the thing itself was shown, but from the whole world, and that this was figured by the blowing of the four parts of the world.

For neither was that breath from the Lord's body the substance of the Holy Spirit, when he breathed and said: *receive the Holy Spirit*[^5]; but surely it was signified that the Holy Spirit so proceeds from him too as that breath proceeded from his body. But because the world is not coupled to God in unity of person as that flesh was to his Word, the only-begotten Son, we cannot say that the soul is from the substance of God in the way that that breath was made from the four winds of the nature of the world: but yet I think it was one thing and signified another; which can be rightly understood by the example of the breath proceeding from the Lord's body—even if the prophet Ezekiel in that place foresaw, by a figured revelation, not the resurrection of the flesh, such as it will properly be, but the unhoped-for restoration of a despairing people through the Spirit of the Lord, *who has filled the whole world*[^6].

## The second and third opinions on the soul to be weighed against the testimonies of Scripture: Isaiah 57:16.

**6.** 9. Let us now see, then, which opinion the divine testimonies rather support: the one that says God made one soul, and gave it to the first man, from which he would make the rest, as from his body the other bodies of men; or the one that says he makes each soul for each, as he made one for him, not the rest from it. For that which he says through Isaiah—*every breath I have made*[^7]—since what follows sufficiently shows that he says this of the soul, can be taken for both. For whether from the one soul of the first man, or from some secret of his own, he without doubt himself makes all souls.

## Another passage from holy Scripture: Psalm 33:15.

**6.** 10. And that which is written: *who formed their hearts one by one*[^8], if by the name of hearts we wish to understand souls, neither does this conflict with either of the two about which we are now in doubt. For whether from that one which he breathed into the face of the first man, he surely forms each one, as he does the bodies too; or whether he both forms each one and sends it, or forms them in those very ones to whom he has sent them: although this does not seem to me to be said, except from that by which our souls are formed by grace through the renewal to the image of God. Whence the Apostle says: *for by grace you have been saved through faith, and this not of yourselves, but it is the gift of God; not of works, lest perhaps anyone be lifted up. For we are his handiwork, created in Christ Jesus in good works*[^9]. For we cannot understand our bodies to be created or formed through this grace of faith, but, as it was said in the Psalm: *create in me a clean heart, O God*[^10].

## The passage Zechariah 12:1 is explained.

**6.** 11. From this too I think is that: *who formed the spirit of man within him*[^11]; as though it were one thing to send a soul already made, another to make it in the man himself—that is, to remake and renew it. But even if we understand this not of the grace by which we are renewed, but of the nature in which we are born, it can be drawn to either opinion: because either, from that one soul of the first man, as it were a seed of soul drawn off, he himself forms it in the man, to give life to the body; or, the spirit of life being infused into the body not from that propagation but from elsewhere, he likewise forms it through the mortal senses of the flesh, so that the man becomes a living soul.

## The passage Wisdom 8:19–20 is weighed.

**7.** 12. That passage indeed from the book of Wisdom, where it says: *I was allotted a good soul, and being more good, I came to an undefiled body*[^12], demands more diligent consideration. For it seems rather to attest to the opinion by which souls are believed to come to bodies not propagated from one, but from above. Nevertheless, what is: *I was allotted a good soul?* As though either in that fount of souls, if there is any, some souls are good, others not good, which by a kind of lot go forth, which is to be assigned to which man; or God makes some good at the hour of the conceived or born, others not good, of which each one has by lot what has befallen him.

It would be a wonder if this even helps those who believe that souls, made elsewhere, are sent one by one by God to each body of men, and not rather those who say that souls are sent into bodies according to the merits of the works which they did before the body. For according to what could some be thought to come good, others not good, to bodies, except according to their works? For not according to nature, in which they are made by him who makes all natures good. But far be it that we should contradict the Apostle, who says that those not yet born had done nothing good or evil, whence he confirms that it could be said not from works but from him who calls: *the elder will serve the younger*[^13]; when he was dealing with twins still in Rebecca's womb. Let us therefore set aside for a little this testimony from the book of Wisdom; for those are not to be neglected, whether they err or are truly wise, who think this was said specially and singly of that soul of the mediator of God and men, the man Christ Jesus. Which, if it be necessary, what sort it is we shall afterward consider, so that, if it could not suit Christ, we may ask how we ought to take it, lest we come against the apostolic faith, supposing souls to have some merits of their works before they begin to live in bodies.

## What Psalm 104:29–30 means.

**8.** 13. Now let us see how this was said: *you will take away their spirit, and they will fail, and will be turned into their dust. You will send forth your spirit, and they will be created; and you will renew the face of the earth*[^14]. For to those who think that souls are created from parents as bodies are, this seems to sound, when it is so understood that he said "their spirit" because men received it from men: which, when they are dead, cannot be returned to them by men, that they may rise; because it is not again, as when they were born, drawn from parents, but God will return it, who raises the dead[^15].

And by this he said it was their spirit when they die, but God's when they rise. Which those who assert that souls come not from parents but by God sending them can, for their opinion, so understand: that he said it was their spirit when they die, because it was in them and goes out from them; but God's when they rise, because it is sent by him, is returned by him. Accordingly this testimony too is opposed to neither of them.

## Psalm 104:29–30 is explained allegorically.

**8.** 14. But I think this is better understood of the grace of God, by which we are inwardly renewed. For of all the proud who live according to the earthly man and presume on their own vanity, their own spirit is in a way taken away, when they put off the old man and are made weak, that they may be perfected, pride being expelled, saying to the Lord by humble confession: *remember that we are dust*[^16]; to whom it had been said: *why is earth and ashes proud?*[^17] For, beholding through the eye of faith the justice of God, so that they do not wish to establish their own[^18], they despise themselves, as Job says[^19], and waste away, and reckon themselves earth and ashes: for this is, "and they will be turned into their dust." But, the Spirit of God received, they say: *I live, yet now not I, but Christ lives in me*[^20]. So the face of the earth is renewed, through the grace of the New Testament, by the multitude of the saints. The testimony from the book of Ecclesiastes (12:7) is called into the debate.

**9.** 15. That too which is written in Ecclesiastes: *and let the dust return into the earth, as it was, and the spirit return to God who gave it*[^21], supports neither opinion against the other, but stands between both. For when these say that from this it is proved that the soul is given not by parents but by God—because, the dust being turned into its own earth, that is, the flesh, which was made from dust, the spirit will return to God who gave it—those answer: *surely it is so.* For the spirit returns to God, who gave it to the first man, when he breathed into his face[^22], the dust—that is, the human body—being turned into the earth from which it was first made[^23]. For the spirit was not going to return to the parents, although it is created from there, from that one which was given to the first man; just as the flesh itself does not, after death, return to the parents from whom it is certainly agreed to be propagated. As, therefore, the flesh does not return to the men from whom it was created, but to the earth from which it was formed for the first man; so too the spirit does not return to the men from whom it was transfused, but to God by whom it was given to that first flesh.

**9.** 16. By which testimony indeed we are sufficiently admonished that God made from nothing the soul which he gave to the first man, not from some creation already made, as the body from earth: and therefore, when it returns, it has nowhere to return except to the author who gave it; not to that creation from which it was made, as the body to the earth. For there is no creation from which it was made, because it was made from nothing; and by this it returns to the maker, that which returns, by whom it was made from nothing. For not all return, since there are those of whom it is said: *a spirit going and not returning*[^24].

## That the question of the origin of the soul is difficult to solve from the Scriptures.

**10.** 17. It is therefore difficult to gather all the testimonies of the holy Scriptures about this matter; which, even if it could be done, so that they were not only mentioned but also treated, would run to a great length of discourse: but yet, unless something be brought forward as certain as the certain things are brought forward by which it is shown that God made the soul, or that he gave it to the first man, I do not know how this question is to be solved through the testimony of divine speech. For if it were written that God likewise breathed into the face of the formed woman, and that she became a living soul, much light would indeed be added, by which we would believe that to each formed flesh of man the soul is given not from parents: yet still it would be awaited what was properly held in offspring, which is the customary mode for us of man from man. But the first woman was made otherwise, and therefore it could still be said that the soul was for this reason not given to Eve divinely from Adam, because she did not arise from him as offspring. But if Scripture recorded of the man who was first born of them that the soul was not drawn from parents but given from above, that then ought to be understood of the rest as well, even with Scripture silent.

## Whether Romans 5:12, 18–19 can be accommodated to either opinion.

**11.** 18. Now, then, let us consider this too: whether it confirms neither opinion, but can be accommodated to either, what is written: *through one man sin entered into this world, and through sin death, and so it passed to all men, in whom all sinned*[^25]; and a little after: *as through the trespass of one upon all men to condemnation, so also through the justification of one upon all men to justification of life. For as through the disobedience of one man many were made sinners, so also through the obedience of one many will be made just*[^26].

For from these words of the Apostle those who defend the propagation of souls strive to build up their opinion thus: "if," they say, "sin or sinner can be understood according to the flesh alone, we are not compelled in these words to believe the soul comes from parents; but if, although through the enticement of the flesh, yet only the soul sins, how is it to be taken what was said, in whom all sinned, if the soul too, like the flesh, was not propagated from Adam? Or how were they made sinners through his disobedience, if they were in him only according to the flesh, not also according to the soul?"

## An argument from the baptism of infants.

**11.** 19. For we must beware lest either God seem to be the author of sin, if he gives a soul to flesh in which it must necessarily sin; or there could be a soul, apart from Christ's own, for whose freeing from sin Christian grace is not necessary, because it did not sin in Adam, if it was said that all sinned in him only according to the flesh which was created from him, not also according to the soul: which is so contrary to the faith of the Church that parents run to receive the grace of holy Baptism even with little ones and infants; in whom, if that bond of sin is loosed which is only of the flesh, not also of the soul, it is rightly asked what it would harm them, if at that age they went out of the body without Baptism.

For if through this Sacrament their body is provided for, not also their soul, the dead too ought to be baptized: but since we see the Church universally hold this, that one runs with the living, and the living are helped, lest, when they are dead, nothing can be done that profits; we do not see what else can be understood, except that each little one is nothing but Adam both in body and in soul, and therefore the grace of Christ is necessary for him. For that age has done nothing in itself either good or evil; accordingly the soul there is most innocent, if it was not propagated from Adam: whence, how it could justly go into condemnation, if it went out of the body without Baptism, let whoever, holding this opinion of the soul, can demonstrate—he is to be wondered at.

## That the cause of concupiscence is both in the body and in the soul.

**12.** 20. For it is most truly and most truthfully written: *the flesh lusts against the spirit, and the spirit against the flesh*[^27]: but yet that flesh without the soul can lust at all, I think no one, learned or unlearned, doubts. And by this the cause of carnal concupiscence itself is not in the soul alone, but much less is it in the flesh alone. For it comes from both: from the soul, namely, because without it no delight is felt; but from the flesh, because without it carnal delight is not felt. The flesh, therefore, lusting against the spirit, the Apostle without doubt calls carnal delight, which the spirit has from the flesh and with the flesh, against the delight which it has alone. For it alone has, unless I am mistaken, that desire, not mingled with the pleasure of the flesh or the cupidity of carnal things, by which the soul desires and faints for the courts of the Lord[^28]. It alone has also that of which it is said to it: you have desired wisdom; keep the commandment, and the Lord will furnish it to you[^29].

For when the spirit commands the members of the body to serve this desire by which it alone is kindled—as when a book is taken up, when something is written, read, debated, heard, when finally bread is broken for the hungry, and the other duties of humanity and mercy are rendered—the flesh exhibits obedience, it does not move concupiscence. When something that delights the same soul according to the flesh opposes these and such good desires by which the soul alone lusts, then the flesh is said to lust against the spirit, and the spirit against the flesh.

## The soul lusts as long as sin is in the body of death.

**12.** 21. For "flesh" was so spoken of, in that which the soul does according to it, when he says: *the flesh lusts*[^30]; as it is said, "the ear hears, and the eye sees." For who does not know that it is rather the soul that both hears through the ear and sees through the eye? So we speak too when we say, "your hand helped a man," when something is given by an outstretched hand by which someone is helped. But if it was said even of the eye of faith itself, to which it belongs to believe the things that are not seen through the flesh, *all flesh will see the salvation of God*[^31]; surely it was said only of the soul, by which the flesh lives, since even to see Christ devoutly through our flesh itself—that is, the form with which he was clothed for our sake—belongs not to concupiscence but to the ministry of the flesh, lest perhaps someone wish so to take what was said: *all flesh will see the salvation of God*: how much more fittingly is the flesh said to lust, when the soul not only furnishes animal life to the flesh, but even lusts for something according to the flesh; which it is not in its power not to lust for, as long as sin is in the members—that is, a certain violent enticement of the flesh in the body of this death, coming from the punishment of that sin from which we draw our propagation, according to which all before grace are children of wrath[^32].

Against which sin those established under grace do battle, not that it should not be in their body, as long as it is so mortal that it is even rightly called dead, but that it should not reign. But it does not reign when its desires—that is, those things which are lusted for according to the flesh against the spirit—are not obeyed. Accordingly, did the Apostle say, "let there be no sin in your mortal body" (for he knew the delight of sin to be present, which he calls sin, nature being depraved from the first transgression)? But: *let not sin,* he says, *reign in your mortal body so as to obey its desires; nor present your members as weapons of iniquity to sin*[^33].

## How well suited this opinion is for avoiding two errors.

**13.** 22. According to this opinion, we neither say a thing most absurd, that the flesh lusts without the soul; nor do we agree with the Manichees, who, since they saw that the flesh could not lust without the soul, supposed the flesh to have a certain soul of its own, from another nature contrary to God, whence it lusts against the spirit. Nor are we compelled to say that the grace of Christ is not necessary for some soul, when it is said to us: "what did the soul of an infant deserve, that it should be ruinous for it to go out of the body without receiving the sacrament of Christian Baptism, if it neither committed any sin of its own, nor is from that one which first sinned in Adam?"

## The baptism of infants is discussed again.

**13.** 23. For we are not dealing with older boys, to whom indeed some are unwilling to attribute their own sin except from the point of the fourteenth year, when they have begun to reach puberty. Which we would rightly believe, if there were no sins except those committed by the genital members; but who would dare to affirm that thefts, lies, and perjuries are not sins, except one who wishes to commit such things with impunity? But the age of boyhood is full of these, although in them they do not seem to be punished as in their elders, because it is hoped that, with the years advancing, in which reason grows strong, they may be able to understand the saving precepts better, and to obey them more willingly. But now we are not dealing with boys, whose carnal and childish pleasure, whether of body or of mind, if truth and equity assail it, they resist with what powers they can of words and deeds; for what do they fight, except for the falsehood and iniquity which will seem to favor them, either for obtaining the things that allure them, or for avoiding the things that offend them?

We are speaking of infants—not because they are often born of adulterers (for in depraved morals the gifts of nature are not to be blamed; nor for that reason ought the grain not to have germinated, because the hand of a thief sowed it; nor indeed will their own iniquity harm the parents themselves, if by turning to God they correct themselves; how much less the children, if they live rightly?).

## That infant baptism favors traducianism.

**14.** 23. But that age moves a vehement question: whose soul, since it has no sin of its own from the freedom of the will, it is asked how it can be justified through the obedience of that one man, if it is not guilty through the disobedience of that other one man. This is the voice of those who would have the souls of men created from human parents—not, indeed, except by God the creator, but as the bodies too. For neither do parents create these, and not he who says: *before I formed you in the womb, I knew you*[^34].

## Here it is answered in the person of those who think that souls are given one by one to each.

**14.** 24. To whom it is answered that God gives new souls one by one to the bodies of men, to this end: that in the flesh of sin, coming from original sin, by living rightly and subduing carnal concupiscences under the grace of God, they may acquire merit, by which with the body itself they may be transferred to a better state at the time of resurrection, and live in Christ forever with the angels. But that it is necessary that, when they are fitted in a marvelous way to earthly and mortal members, and especially ones propagated from the flesh of sin, so that they can first give them life, and afterward, with the increase of age, govern them, they be weighed down, as it were, with forgetfulness.

Which, if it were in a way indigestible, would be attributed to the Creator; but since gradually the soul, recovering from the torpor of this forgetfulness, can turn to its own God, and merit his mercy and truth—first by the very devotion of conversion, then by perseverance in keeping his precept—what harm does it do it to be immersed a little while in that, as it were, sleep, from which, gradually waking into the light of understanding, for the sake of which the rational soul was made, it can, by a good will, choose a good life? which indeed it will not be able to do, unless the grace of God through the Mediator helps it. If man neglects this, he will be Adam not only according to the flesh, but also according to the spirit; but if he takes care, he will be Adam only according to the flesh; but according to the spirit, living rightly, he will deserve to receive even that which was drawn culpably from Adam, cleansed from the stain of sin, by that change which the resurrection promises to the saints.

## Baptism is necessary for infants to loose the punishment of original sin and to tame concupiscence.

**14.** 25. But before, through age, he can live according to the spirit, he has need of the Sacrament of the Mediator, so that what he cannot yet do through his own faith may be done through the faith of those who love him. For by his Sacrament the original punishment of sin is loosed even in the age of infancy; unless helped by which, even as a young man he will not tame carnal concupiscence; nor, that being subjugated, will he lay hold of the merit of eternal life, except by the gift of him whom he strives to merit. Therefore even an infant ought to be baptized while alive, lest the fellowship of the flesh of sin harm the soul, which, being shared, brings it about that the soul of the infant can be wise in nothing according to the spirit. For that very affection weighs down even the soul stripped of the body, unless, while it is in the body, it is expiated by the one sacrifice of the true priest.

## The same argument is weighed more deeply.

**15.** 26. What then, someone says, if his own people do not take care of this, whether through unbelief or negligence? This indeed can be said of grown people too. For they can either suddenly die, or fall sick among people where no one comes to their aid to be baptized. But those, he says, have also their own sins, of which they need the remission, which if they are not forgiven, no one will rightly say that they are punished undeservedly for the things which in their life they committed by their own will: but that soul, to which a certain contagion has been drawn from the flesh of sin, if it was not created from that first sinning soul, can in no way be imputed (for it was given to the flesh by no sin, but by the nature in which it was so made, and with God giving); why will it be alienated from eternal life, if no one comes to the aid of the infant to be baptized? Or will it perhaps do no harm? What, then, does it profit the one who is helped, if it does no harm to the one who is not helped?

## What the advocates of "creationism" answer.

**15.** 27. What those can answer here for their cause who, according to the holy Scriptures—either by what is found in them, or by what is not opposed to them—try to assert that new souls, not drawn from parents, are given to bodies, I confess I have not yet heard, nor anywhere read. Yet the business of those absent is not to be deserted, if anything occur to me by which they may seem to be helped. For they can still say that God, foreknowing how each soul would live, if it lived longer in the body, procures for it the ministration of the saving bath, whose future devotion he foresees, when it should come to the years capable of faith, if it did not need, for some hidden cause, to be forestalled by death.

It is therefore hidden, and most remote from human—or at least from my—wit, why an infant is born who will die either at once or soon: but this is so hidden that it helps neither of those whose opinions we are now debating. For, that opinion being exploded by which souls are thought to be thrust into bodies for the merits of a life lived before, so that that one would seem to have deserved to be sooner released which had not sinned much—lest we contradict the Apostle, testifying that those not yet born had done nothing good or evil[^35]—neither can those who affirm the transmission of the soul show for themselves why the death of some is hastened, of others retarded, nor those who would have new souls given one by one to each. This cause, then, is hidden, and equally to both, as I judge, it neither favors nor opposes.

## On Baptism and the premature death of infants.

**16.** 28. Accordingly, those who were pressed about the deaths of infants—why the Sacrament of Baptism is necessary for all, whose souls were not drawn from that one by whose disobedience many were made sinners[^36]—when they answer that all are indeed made sinners according to the flesh, but according to the soul only those who at the time lived badly when they could also have lived well; but that all souls, that is, even those of infants, therefore have need of the Sacrament of Baptism, without which it is not expedient to emigrate from this life even at that age, because the contagion of sin from the flesh of sin, by which the soul is overwhelmed when it is inserted into these members, will harm it after death, unless, while it is still in the flesh itself, it is expiated by the Sacrament of the Mediator; and that this help is divinely procured for the one whom God foreknew would have lived devoutly, if he had lived here up to the years suited to faith, whom for something which he himself knows he both willed to be born in a body and quickly drew out of the body.

When, then, they answer these things, what can be objected to them, except that we become uncertain of the salvation of those who, this life having been well lived, have died in the peace of the Church; if each is to be judged not only according to what he lived, but also according to what he would have lived, if he could have lived longer? Since with God evil merits avail, not only of past, but even of future trespasses, from the guilt of which neither does death free, if it come before they have been committed; nor was anything bestowed on him who was snatched away lest malice change his understanding[^37]. For God, foreknowing that future malice, why will he not rather judge him according to it, if he judged that the dying soul of the infant should be helped through Baptism, lest the filth shared from the body of sin harm it, because he foreknew that it would, if it lived, have lived devoutly and faithfully?

## That all men are subject to original sin.

**16.** 29. Or can this invention rather be refuted because it is mine? But those who affirm that they are certain of this opinion perhaps bring forward other testimonies of the Scriptures, or documents of reasonings, by which they may take away this ambiguity, or at least show that it is not against what they hold, that saying of the Apostle by which, commending with great intent the grace by which we are made saved, he says: *as in Adam all die, so also in Christ all will be made alive*[^38]; and: *as through the disobedience of one man many were made sinners, so through the obedience of one man many will be made just*[^39]; and wishing those same many sinners to be understood not with some excepted, but all, he says above of Adam: *in whom all sinned*[^40]: whence surely the souls of infants cannot be set apart, both by what was said, all, and by the fact that they are helped through Baptism—not absurdly do those believe who are wise about souls from the transmission of one, unless they are refuted by some manifest and clear reason, which is not repugnant to the holy Scriptures, or by the authority of those very Scriptures.

## The testimony from the book of Wisdom is treated on both sides.

**17.** 30. Now, then, let us see, as much as the necessity of this undertaken work allows, what sort that too is which a little before we deferred. For it is written in the book of Wisdom: *but I was a child of good wit, and I was allotted a good soul; and being more good, I came to an undefiled body*[^41]. For although this testimony seems to help those who say that souls are not created from parents, but come to the body, or descend, with God sending them; yet again it hinders their opinion, that it says: *I was allotted a good soul*: since without doubt they believe that the souls which God sends into bodies either flow in a way like rivulets from one fount, or are made of equal nature; not, however, some good or more good, and others not good or less good.

*For whence are souls good or more good, or not good or less good, except either by morals according to the freedom of the will, or by the difference of the constitution of bodies, while some are more, others less, weighed down by the body, which is corrupted and weighs down the soul*[^42]? But there was no action of any individual souls by which their morals would be distinguished, before they came to bodies; nor could he say he received a good soul from a body weighing it down less, he who says: I was allotted a good soul; and being more good, I came to an undefiled body. For he said there was added to the goodness by which he was good—being allotted, namely, a good soul—that he came also to an undefiled body. From elsewhere, then, was he good before he came to the body: but surely not by a difference of morals, because there was previously no merit of a life lived; not by a difference of body, because he was good before he came to the body. Whence, then?

## The explanation of Wisdom 8:19–20 is continued.

**17.** 31. But to those who assert that souls are created from the transmission of that prevaricating soul, although what was said—*I came to a body*—does not seem to sound for them, yet in the rest it is not unfittingly fitted; so that, when he had said, *but I was a child of good wit,* explaining this very thing, by what causes he was of good wit, he immediately added: *and I was allotted a good soul, namely from a paternal wit or corporeal constitution*. Then: *being,* he says, *more good, I came to an undefiled body*: which, if it be understood as maternal, not even this which was said—*I came to a body*—will gainsay this opinion, since it is taken that from a soul and a paternal body he came to a maternal body undefiled, namely either from menstrual blood (for from this wits are said to be weighed down), or from adulterous contamination. So these words too of this book either rather favor those who speak of the transmission of souls; or, if these too can interpret them for themselves, they alternate between both.

## Can that testimony suit Christ?

**18.** 32. But if we wish to take these things of the Lord, according to the human creature which was assumed by the Word, there are indeed, in the same context of the reading, things that do not suit that excellence; especially this, that the same one who speaks these things in the same book, somewhat above, when he said the very words about which we are now dealing, *confessed himself to have been coagulated in blood from the seed of a man*[^43]: from which mode of being born the birth of the Virgin is surely alien, who, that she did not conceive the flesh of Christ from the seed of a man, no Christian doubts. But because there are things in the Psalms—where he says: *they pierced my hands and feet, they numbered all my bones; but they themselves considered and gazed upon me: they divided my garments among themselves, and over my vesture they cast lots*[^44]—which properly suit that one alone; there too he said: *God, my God, look upon me, why have you forsaken me? Far from my salvation are the words of my sins*[^45], which again do not suit him, except as he transfigures into himself the body of our lowliness, since we are members of his body.

And because in the Gospel itself the child increased in age and wisdom[^46]: if these things too which are read around this opinion in the book of Wisdom can, on account of the humble form of a servant and the unity of the body of the Church with its head, be fitted to the same Lord; *what is more of good wit than that child, whose wisdom the elders marveled at in his twelfth year*[^47]? And what soul better than that one, which, even if those who affirm the transmission of souls should win—not by contending but by proving—it will not follow that it too is to be believed to have come from the transmission of that prevaricator; lest through the disobedience of that man he too be made a sinner, through whose one obedience the many, freed from that guilt, are made just?

And what more undefiled than that womb of the Virgin, whose flesh, even if it came from the propagation of sin, yet did not conceive from the propagation of sin; so that not even the body of Christ itself was sown in the womb of Mary by that law which, placed in the members of the body of death, wars against the law of the mind? which the holy fathers, married, restraining, indeed relaxed into intercourse only as far as was lawful; nor yet did they bear its impulse only as far as was lawful. Accordingly the body of Christ, although it was assumed from the flesh of a woman who had been conceived from that propagation of the flesh of sin, yet, because it was not conceived in her in the way that she had been conceived, was not itself the flesh of sin, but the likeness of the flesh of sin.

For he did not take from there the guilt of dying, which appears in the involuntary motion of the flesh, though to be overcome by the will, *against which the spirit lusts*[^48]: but he took from there what would suffice not for the contagion of prevarication, but for the discharging of an undue death, and for the showing of the promised resurrection; of which the one would avail us for not fearing, the other for hoping.

## Whence Christ's soul.

**18.** 33. Finally, if it be asked of me whence Jesus Christ received his soul, I would indeed prefer to hear about this from better and more learned men; but yet, according to my capacity, I would more willingly answer, whence Adam, than from Adam. For if dust taken from the earth, in which no man had worked, deserved to be divinely animated, how much more did a body assumed from flesh, in which likewise no man had worked, obtain a good soul by lot—when there the one to fall was being raised up, here the one to lift up was coming down? And perhaps for this reason he says: *I was allotted a good soul*[^49] (if indeed this is to be understood of him), because things that are given by lot are wont to be given divinely: or, what is to be said confidently, lest even that soul be thought to have been carried up to so great a summit by some preceding works, so that with it *the Word became flesh, and dwelt among us*[^50], the name of lot was added to remove the suspicion of preceding merits.

## That Christ's soul was not in the loins of Abraham, and therefore not from transmission.

**19.** 34. There is in the Epistle inscribed to the Hebrews a certain passage most worthy of diligent consideration. For when, through Melchizedek, in whom a figure of this future thing had preceded, he distinguished the priesthood of Christ from the priesthood of Levi: *see, then,* he says, *how great this man is, to whom even the patriarch Abraham gave a tenth part of the firstfruits*. And those indeed of the sons of Levi, receiving the priesthood, have a commandment to take tithes from the people according to the Law, that is, from their brethren, though they too came out of the loins of Abraham; but he who is not of their kindred took a tithe of Abraham, and blessed him who had the promise. But without any contradiction, the lesser is blessed by the greater: and here indeed dying men receive tithes; but there one who is testified to live, and, as one ought to say, on account of Abraham, even Levi, who receives tithes, was tithed: *for he was still in the loins of his father*[^51].

If, then, this too avails for that distinction—how far the priesthood of Christ excels the Levitical priesthood, because Christ was prefigured as priest through that one who tithed Abraham, in whom Levi too was tithed—surely Christ was not tithed by him. But if Levi was therefore tithed because he was in the loins of Abraham, therefore Christ was not tithed because he was not in the loins of Abraham. Furthermore, if we take Levi to have been in Abraham not according to soul, but only according to flesh; there too Christ was, because Christ too is according to the flesh from the seed of Abraham; and so he too was tithed. What, then, is brought forward for the great difference of the priesthood of Christ from the priesthood of Levi—that Levi was tithed by Melchizedek, since he was in the loins of Abraham, where Christ too was, whence they were equally tithed—unless because it is necessary that we understand that, in some mode, Christ was not there? But who would deny that he was there according to the flesh? Therefore according to the soul he was not there. The soul of Christ, then, is not from the transmission of Adam's prevarication; otherwise it too would have been there.

## To the argument now brought forward, what is to be answered by those defending the transmission of souls?

**20.** 35. Here those arise who defend the transmission of souls, and say that their opinion is confirmed, if it is agreed that Levi was in the loins of Abraham, where Melchizedek tithed him, even according to the soul, so that Christ can be distinguished from him in this tithing: who, since he was not tithed, and yet was in the loins of Abraham according to the flesh, it remains that he was not there according to the soul, and so it is consequent that Levi was there according to the soul.

This does not much concern me, who am still more ready to hear the comparison of both sides than now to confirm the opinion of either. Meanwhile, I wished, through this testimony, to set apart Christ's soul from the origin of this transmission. They will find what perhaps to answer for the rest, and will say—what moves me too not a little—that, although no man's soul is in the loins of his father, yet Levi, established according to the flesh in the loins of Abraham, was tithed, and Christ, established there according to the flesh, was not tithed. For according to that seminal reason Levi was there, by which reason he was going to come, through intercourse, into his mother; according to which reason Christ's flesh was not there, although according to it Mary's flesh was there. Wherefore neither Levi nor Christ was in the loins of Abraham according to the soul; but according to the flesh both Levi and Christ; yet Levi according to carnal concupiscence, but Christ according to the corporeal substance alone.

For since there is in the seed both a visible corpulence and an invisible reason, both ran from Abraham, or even from Adam himself, all the way to the body of Mary; because that too was conceived and arose in that mode: but Christ took the visible substance of his flesh from the flesh of the Virgin; whereas the reason of his conception came not from male seed, but far otherwise and from above. Accordingly, according to that which he took from his mother, he was also in the loins of Abraham.

## An argument for the first opinion.

**20.** 36. He, therefore, was tithed in Abraham, who, although only according to the flesh, yet was in his loins in the way that Abraham himself too was in his own father's; that is, who was so born of his father Abraham in the way that Abraham was born of his own father, namely *through the law in the members warring against the law of the mind*[^52] and the invisible concupiscence, although the chaste and good laws of marriage do not let it avail except as far as, through it, they can provide for the substituting of offspring.

But that one was not tithed there, whose flesh drew thence not the heat of the wound, but the material of the remedy. For since the tithing itself pertained to prefiguring the medicine, that was tithed in the flesh of Abraham which was being cured, not that whence it was cured. For the same flesh, not only of Abraham but of that first and earthly man himself, had at once both the wound of prevarication and the remedy of the wound: the wound of prevarication in the law of the members warring against the law of the mind, which through all the flesh propagated from there is, as it were, transcribed by the seminal reason; but the remedy of the wound in that which thence, without concupiscent work, in corporeal material alone, through the divine reason of conception and formation, was assumed from the Virgin—for the sake of a fellowship with death without iniquity, and an example of resurrection without falsehood.

Wherefore that the soul of Christ is not from the transmission of that first prevaricating soul, I think even those who defend the transmission of souls agree; for they would have the seed of the soul too transfused through the seed of the father in intercourse, from which kind of conception Christ is alien: and that, if he had been in Abraham according to the soul, he too would have been tithed; but that he was not tithed, Scripture testifies, which from this too distinguishes his priesthood from the Levitical priesthood.

## Augustine in doubt between both opinions.

**21.** 37. Or will they perhaps say: "as he could be there according to the flesh and not be tithed, why could he not also be there according to the soul without tithing"? Here it is answered: "because surely that the simple substance of the soul is increased by corporeal increments, not even those thought who consider it a body, in whose party are especially those who think it created from parents." Accordingly, in the seed of the body there can be an invisible force which incorporeally accomplishes numbers, to be distinguished not by the eyes but by the understanding from that corpulence which is sensed by sight and touch: and the very quantity of the human body, which surely exceeds the measure of the seed incomparably, sufficiently shows that something can be taken thence which does not have that seminal force, but only the corporeal substance, which divinely, not from the propagation of those in intercourse, was assumed and formed into the flesh of Christ. But who could affirm this of the soul, that it has both—both the manifest material of seed, and the hidden reason of seed?

But why should I labor in a matter which perhaps can be persuaded to no one by words, unless his wit be so great and such that it can fly ahead of the speaker's effort, and not await the whole from speech? Briefly, then, I will sum up: if it could come about even from the soul (which, when we said it of the flesh, was perhaps understood), the soul of Christ is so from transmission that it did not draw with it the stain of prevarication; but if it could not be thence without that guilt, it is not thence.

Now concerning the coming of the other souls—whether they are from parents or from above—let those win who can: I still waver between both, and am moved, sometimes thus, sometimes thus, with this alone preserved: that I will not believe the soul to be a body, or any corporeal quality or fitting-together, if it is to be so called, which the Greeks call harmonia—nor, with anyone whatever chattering this, do I trust that I shall believe it, with God helping my mind.

## The testimony John 3:6 is explained.

**22.** 38. There is another testimony not to be neglected which those can bring forward for themselves who believe that souls come from above, the Lord himself saying: *that which is born of the flesh is flesh; and that which is born of the spirit is spirit*[^53]. What, they say, is more determinate than this statement, that the soul cannot be born of the flesh? For what else is the soul, than the spirit of life, created surely, not creator?

Against whom those others say: "what else do we hold, who say flesh from flesh, soul from soul?" For man consists of both, from which we hold that both come—the flesh from the flesh of the one working, the spirit from the spirit of the one lusting: so that, for the moment, it be passed over that the Lord said that not of carnal generation, but of spiritual regeneration.

## It is concluded: the testimonies brought forward support neither opinion.

**23.** 39. These things, then, being treated as far as we could for the time, I would pronounce the weights of reasonings and testimonies on both sides equal, or nearly equal, were it not that the opinion of those who think that souls are created from parents preponderates on account of the baptism of infants. As to which, what can be answered to them does not yet for the moment occur to me: if perhaps God should later grant anything, if he should grant also some faculty of writing to those zealous for such things, I will not be reluctant. Now, however, I declare beforehand that the testimony of the infants is not to be despised, so that it should be neglected as though refuted, if the truth is against it.

For either nothing is to be inquired about this matter, so that it suffices for our faith to know whither we are going to come by living devoutly, even if we do not know whence we have come: *or, if the rational soul not shamelessly burns to know this too about itself, let there be no obstinacy of contending, but diligence of inquiring, humility of asking, perseverance of knocking*[^54]; so that, *if he knows this to be expedient for us, who surely knows better than we what is expedient for us, he may grant this too, who gives good gifts to his children*[^55]. Yet the custom of mother Church in baptizing infants is by no means to be despised, nor in any way to be reckoned superfluous, nor at all to be believed unless it were an apostolic tradition. For that little age too has a great weight of testimony, which first deserved to shed blood for Christ.

## What those who defend that souls are from transmission must beware.

**24.** 40. I admonish indeed, as much as I am able, if this opinion has preoccupied any, that they believe souls to be propagated from parents, that they consider themselves as much as they can, and meanwhile be wise that their bodies are not their souls. For there is no nearer nature, by which, diligently inspected, even God, who remains immutable above his whole creation, can be incorporeally thought, than that which was made to his image: and there is nothing nearer, or perhaps nothing so consequent, than that, when it is believed that the soul is a body, God too be believed to be a body.

For on this account, accustomed and affected by corporeal senses, they are unwilling to believe the soul to be anything other than a body, lest, if it were not a body, it should be nothing: and by this they fear all the more to believe even of God that he is not a body, the more they fear to believe God to be nothing. For they are so carried into the phantasies or phantasms of images, which thought turns over about bodies, that, these being withdrawn, they dread that they must perish, as it were, through a void. So it is necessary that they paint, as it were, both justice and wisdom in their hearts with the forms and colors which they cannot think incorporeal: nor yet do they say, when they are moved by justice or wisdom, either to praise them or to do something according to them, what color, what stature, what lineaments, or what forms they have beheld.

But of these things we have already said much elsewhere, and, if God will, where the matter shall seem to require, we will say more. Now, what we had begun to say: if certain people either do not doubt about the transmission of souls from parents that it is so, or doubt whether it is so, let them yet not dare to believe or say that the soul is a body; chiefly on account of what I said, lest they suppose God himself too to be nothing other than a body—even if a most excellent one, even if of a certain nature surpassing the rest, yet a body.

## Tertullian's error about the soul.

**25.** 41. Finally Tertullian, because he believed the soul to be a body, for no other reason than that he could not think it incorporeal, and therefore feared lest it should be nothing if it were not a body, was not able to be wise about God otherwise either: who indeed, since he is acute, is sometimes, the truth being seen, overcome against his own opinion. For what truer could he say than that which he says in a certain place: *everything corporeal is capable of suffering*[^56]? He ought, then, to have changed the opinion by which a little above he had said that God too is a body. For I do not think he was so foolish as even to believe the nature of God capable of suffering, so that now Christ would be believed to be capable of suffering and mutable not only in the flesh, nor in flesh and soul, but in the very Word through whom all things were made: which be far from a Christian heart.

Likewise, when *he gave the soul also an airy and luminous color*[^57], he came to the senses by which he tried to furnish it, member by member, as if it were a body, and he says: *this will be the inner man, the other the outer, doubly one, having, that one too, his own eyes and ears, with which the people ought to have heard and seen the Lord; having too the other limbs, by which he uses them both in thoughts and exercises them in dreams*[^58].

## How inept and self-contradictory the things Tertullian says about the soul.

**25.** 42. Behold with what ears and what eyes the people ought to have heard and seen God, with which the soul exercises itself in dreams: whereas, if someone saw Tertullian himself in dreams, he would never say that he had been seen by him, and had spoken with him whom he in turn had not seen. Finally, if the soul sees itself in dreams—when, the members of its body lying surely in one place, it itself wanders about through the various images which it sees—who ever saw it in dreams of an airy and luminous color, except perhaps as the other things which it likewise falsely sees? For it can see this too: but far be it that, when it has awakened, it should believe it such; otherwise, when it has seen itself otherwise—which is more frequent—either its soul will have been changed, or not even then is the substance of the soul seen, but the incorporeal image of a body, which in a marvelous way is formed as in thought. For what Ethiopian has not almost always seen himself black in dreams; or, if he has seen himself in another color, has he not wondered the more, if he had memory with him? Yet of an airy and luminous color I do not know whether he would ever have seen himself, if he had never read or heard of this man.

## That God is not like the images in which he has appeared to his saints.

**25.** 43. What of the fact that men are led by such visions, and from the Scriptures wish to prescribe to us that something such is, not the soul, but God himself, of the sort he was shown figuratively to the spirits of the saints, of the sort he is set down also in allegorical speech? For those visions are similar to such sayings. And so they err, setting up in their heart the simulacra of vain opinion, and not understanding that the saints judged of their such visions just as they would judge if they read or heard such things said divinely in figures: as *the seven ears and seven oxen are seven years*[^59]; as the linen bound by four corners, like a dish full of various animals, *is the world with all its nations*[^60]: so all the rest, and much more those things which are signified about incorporeal things not by corporeal things, but by images.

## What Tertullian thought about the increments of the soul.

**26.** 44. Tertullian, however, was unwilling that the soul should grow in substance like the body; bringing forward too the cause of his fear: *lest it be said also to decrease in substance,* he says, *and so be believed even destined to fail*[^61]. And yet, because he extends it locally through the body, he finds no way out for its increments, which he wishes to be equaled from a tiny seed to the quantity of the body; and he says: *but its force, in which the natural properties are implanted and retained, with the measure of substance preserved with which it was breathed in from the beginning, is gradually brought forth along with the flesh*[^62]. This perhaps we would not understand, unless, a similitude being applied, he made plain what he was saying.

Set, he says, a fixed weight of gold or silver, as yet a rough mass: it has a collected condition, and for the moment less than its future state, yet containing within the line of its measure all that is the nature of gold or silver: thereafter, when the mass is spread out into a sheet, it is made greater than its beginning, by the dilatation of the fixed weight, not by addition, while it is extended, not while it is increased; though even thus it is increased, while it is extended. For it is allowed to be increased in condition, when it is not allowed in state. Then too the very splendor of the gold or silver is brought forth, which before had indeed been even in the mass, but more obscure, yet not none: then too other and other conditions are added, according to the pliability of the material, as he who works it has led it, conferring nothing on the measure except a shape. So too *the increments of the soul are to be reckoned not substantive, but evocative*[^63].

## Many absurd things said by Tertullian about the soul.

**26.** 45. Who would believe that this man, with such a heart, could have been so eloquent? But these things are to be dreaded, not laughed at. For would he be driven to this, if he could think anything that both is and is not a body? But what is more absurd than to think that the mass of some metal can grow in some part while it is hammered, unless it decreases in another; or be increased in breadth, unless it be diminished in thickness? or that there is any body which, the quantity of its nature remaining, grows on all sides, unless it becomes rarer?

How, then, will the soul fill from that drop of seed the magnitude of the body which it animates, if it too is a body, whose substance grows by no accession? How, I say, will it fill the flesh which it gives life to, unless it is the rarer the larger that which it has animated? He feared, namely, lest it fail even by diminishing, if it grew; and he did not fear lest it fail by becoming rarer, when it grew.

But why should I linger longer, when both the discourse proceeds to a more prolix measure than the necessity of ending demands, and my opinion is now sufficiently known—both what I hold as certain, and whence I still doubt, and why I doubt? Accordingly let this volume too now be concluded, that we may see the things which follow thereafter.
