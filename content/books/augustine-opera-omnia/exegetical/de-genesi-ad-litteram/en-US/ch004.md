# Book III

## The bringing-forth of animals from water and earth; what the relation of water to air is, and of air to heaven.

**1. 1.** *And God said, 'let the waters bring forth the creeping things of living souls, and the flying things over the earth, beneath the firmament of heaven.' And so it was made. And God made the great sea-creatures and every animal of the creeping things, which the waters brought forth according to their kind; and every winged flying thing according to its kind. And God saw that they were good, and God blessed them, saying, 'increase and multiply, and fill the waters in the sea, and let the flying things be multiplied over the earth.' And there was evening, and there was morning, the fifth day*[^1]*.* Now, in the lower part of the world, those things which are moved by the breath of life come to be, and first those of the waters, which is the element nearest to the quality of air; because to this heaven, in which are the luminaries, the air is so near that it too has received the name of *heaven*; but I do not know whether it can also be called *firmament.*

But *heavens* are spoken of in the plural in one and the same thing, which is called one heaven. For although in this book heaven is spoken of in the singular number, which divides between the waters that are above and those that are below; yet in that *Psalm* it says, *and let the waters that are above the heavens praise the name of the Lord*[^2]*.* And if we rightly understand the *heavens of heavens* to be the starry ones of the airy ones, as the higher of the lower, we take these too in the same *Psalm*, where it was said, *praise him, you heavens of heavens*[^3]. It sufficiently appears that this air is called not only heaven but also heavens: just as lands too are spoken of, and nothing else is signified than that which in the singular is called earth, when we say the orb of lands and the orb of the earth.

## How it is to be understood that the heavens perished by the flood.

**2. 2.** That these airy heavens too once perished by the flood we read in a certain one of the Epistles which are called canonical[^4]. For that moist nature, which had so grown together that it surpassed by fifteen cubits the peaks of the highest mountains[^5], could not reach the stars. But because it had filled either all or nearly all the spaces of this moister air, in which the birds fly, the heavens that had been are written in that Epistle to have perished.

Which I do not know how it can be understood, unless by the quality of this denser air being turned into the nature of waters: otherwise these heavens did not then perish, but were raised higher, when the water occupied their place. And so we more easily believe, according to the authority of that Epistle, that they perished, and others, as is there written, were set in their place[^6]—the exhalations, namely, being extended—than that they were so raised that the nature of the higher heaven gave way from its place to them.

## That the nature of water and of air is closely akin.

**2. 3.** It was fitting, therefore, that in creating the dwellers of this lower part of the world, which is often commemorated wholly under the name of *earth*, the animals should first be produced from the waters, but afterward from the earth: because water is so like air that it is proved to grow dense by its exhalations, so that it both makes *the breath of the storm*, that is, wind, and gathers clouds, and can sustain the flight of birds.

Wherefore, even if a certain one of the secular poets said truly, *Olympus rises above the clouds,* and, *the heights hold peace*[^7]—because it is reported that on the peak of Olympus the air is so thin that it is neither shaded by mists, nor disturbed by wind, nor can sustain birds, nor nourish, by the breath of a denser air, the very men who perhaps have ascended, as they are wont to be nourished in this air—yet it too is air, whence it is spread out with a quality akin to waters, and therefore it too is believed to have been turned into a moist nature at the time of the flood. For it is not to be thought that the water usurped anything of the spaces of the starry heaven, when it had surpassed even all the highest mountains.

## The opinion of some that the elements can be changed into one another by alternating turns.

**3. 4.** Although concerning the conversion of the elements, even among those who have scrutinized these things most subtly with this idle care, there is no small question. For some say that all things can be changed and converted into all things; but others report that there is something altogether proper to the several elements, which is in no way turned into the quality of another element.

Whence perhaps, in its own place, if the Lord wills, we shall discuss it more diligently: but now, as far as it pertains to the present discourse, I have judged that these things should be mentioned, so that we may understand that the order of things was preserved by which it was fitting first to narrate the animals of the waters created before those of the lands.

## On the four elements.

**3. 5.** And it is in no way to be thought that any element of this world was passed over in this Scripture, since it is held as persuaded that it consists of four most well-known elements, because here heaven, and water, and earth seem to be mentioned, but the air is passed over in silence. For it is the custom of our Scriptures either to call the world by the name of heaven and earth, or sometimes to add also the sea. And so the air is understood either to belong to heaven, if there are in its upper parts most tranquil and most peaceful spaces; or to the earth, on account of this turbulent and dark place, which grows dense with moist exhalation, although it too is more often called by the name of heaven: and on this account it was not written, "let the waters bring forth the creeping things of living souls, and let the air bring forth the flying things flying over the earth"; but each of these kinds of living things is narrated to have been produced from the waters. Whatever, then, of the waters is either slippingly billowing and fluid, or thinned and suspended as vapor—so that the former appears assigned to the creeping things of living souls, the latter to the flying things—each is yet reckoned to the moist nature.

## That the five senses of men are, according to some, distinguished by the four elements of the world.

**4. 6.** And so there are even those who, by a most subtle consideration, so distinguish these five most manifest senses of the body according to the four usual elements that they say the eyes belong to fire, the ears to air. But the sense of smelling and tasting they attribute to the moist nature: smell indeed to these moist exhalations, by which this space is thickened in which the birds fly; but taste to these flowing and corpulent fluids. For whatever things have flavor in the mouth are mingled with the moisture of the mouth itself in order to have flavor, even if they seemed to have been dry when they were received.

Fire, nevertheless, penetrates all things, so as to make motion in them. For moisture congeals by the privation of heat; and although the other elements can grow hot, fire cannot grow cold: for it is more easily extinguished, so as not to be fire, than that it should remain cold or become lukewarm by the contact of something cold.

But touch, which is the fifth among the senses, agrees rather with the earthen element: accordingly, through the whole body of the living thing, which is most of all from earth, whatever things are touched are perceived.

They say also that nothing can be seen without fire, nor touched without earth. And on this account that all the elements are present in all things; but each of them has received its name from that which it has in greater abundance. And that for this reason, by the privation of heat, when the body grows excessively cold, the sense is dulled, because the motion grows sluggish which is in the body from heat, while fire affects the air, and air the moist things, and moisture the earthen things—the finer, namely, penetrating the coarser.

**4. 7.** But the finer each thing is in corporeal nature, the nearer it is to the spiritual nature; although by a far different kind, since the one is a body, the other is not.

## How the power of perceiving in the five senses stands variously in relation to the four elements.

**5. 7.** And on this account, since to perceive belongs not to the body but to the soul through the body, although it may be subtly discussed that the senses of the body are distributed according to the diversity of the corporeal elements, yet the soul, in which the power of perceiving is present, since it is not corporeal, drives the vigor of perceiving through the finer body. And so it begins motion in all the senses from the subtlety of fire, but does not in all reach the same point.

For in sight it reaches, the heat being restrained, as far as its light. In hearing the heat of fire penetrates as far as the more liquid air. But in smell it passes through the pure air, and reaches the moist exhalation, whence this coarser breath subsists. In taste it passes through this too, and reaches as far as the more corpulent fluid: which also being penetrated and crossed, when it reaches the earthen heaviness, it performs the last sense, that of touching.

## How the air stands in relation to heaven and the waters.

**6. 8.** He, then, was not ignorant of the natures of the elements and their order, who, when he introduced the founding of the visible things which are moved within the world in the elements by nature, narrated first the heavenly luminaries, then the animals of the waters, but the animals of the lands last.

Not that he passed over the air, but that the spaces, if there are any, of the purest and most quiet air, where birds are said not to be able to fly, are joined to the upper heaven, and are understood, by the naming of *heaven* in the Scriptures, to belong to the upper part of the world: so that by the name of *earth* this whole is universally signified, from which it begins downward, *fire, hail, snow, ice, the breath of the tempest, and all the abysses*[^8]*,* until one arrives at the dry land, which by its proper name is called earth.

That upper air, then, whether because it belongs to the heavenly part of the world, or because it has no visible dweller, of which kind the narrator's speech now is, was neither passed over when heaven was named, nor numbered among the animals to be created: but this lower one, which receives the moist exhalations of the sea and the earth, and is in a way thickened to sustain the birds, receives its animals only from the waters. For that which is moist in it carries the bodies of the winged ones, which strive thus by flying with their wings, as fish, swimming, with certain wings of their own.

## Why the book of Genesis says that birds were born from water.

**7. 9.** Accordingly, knowingly, as the Spirit of God, who was present to the writer, he says that the flying things were produced from the waters. Their nature has been allotted a twofold place: the lower, namely, in the slipping wave, but the upper in the blowing breeze: the former assigned to swimmers, the latter to flyers. Just as we see two senses, fitting for this element, also given to animals: smell for examining vapors, taste for examining liquids. For that we perceive both waters and winds by touch too, this is because the solid of earth is mingled with all the elements; but in these coarser things it is perceived more, so that they can even be handled by touching.

And therefore these too, in the two greatest parts of the world, are embraced under the general name of *earth*: as that psalm shows, enumerating all the higher things from that beginning, *praise the Lord from the heavens*[^9]; and all the lower things from another beginning, *praise the Lord from the earth*[^10]; where both the breath of the tempest and all the abysses are named, and this fire which burns the one who touches it, because it so arises from earthen and moist motions that it is again and again turned into another element. And although by striving upward it indicates the appetite of its nature, yet it cannot escape into the heavenly upper tranquillity; because, overcome by much air and turned into it, it is extinguished: and on this account, in this more corruptible and more sluggish part of things, it is stirred by turbulent motions, to temper its rigor, and for the uses and terrors of mortals.

## Why holy Scripture calls birds the flying things of heaven.

**7. 10.** Since, then, by touch too, which properly belongs to the earth, both the waves of the waters and the blowings of the breezes can be perceived, therefore the very bodies of the water-animals also feed on earthen things, and most of all the birds; they also rest on the earth, and propagate their offspring there: for the part of the moisture which is exhaled as vapor extends even over the lands. And therefore, when Scripture had said, *let the waters bring forth the creeping things of living souls,* and it said, *and the flying things over the lands*; it certainly added, saying, *beneath the firmament of heaven*[^11]*,* in which that can appear somewhat more manifestly which before seemed obscure.

For it does not say, "in the firmament of heaven," as of the luminaries; but, it says, *the flying things over the earth, beneath the firmament of heaven,* that is, next to the firmament of heaven: because, namely, this dark and moist space, in which the birds fly, is contiguous to that space where they cannot fly, which already, by the merit of its tranquillity and quiet, belongs to the firmament of heaven. In heaven, then, the birds fly, but in this heaven which that psalm includes also under the name of *earth*; on account of which they are called in many places the *flying things of heaven*: yet not in the firmament, but beneath the firmament.

## Why the creeping things of living souls are called fish. The first opinion.

**8. 11.** Some think that, on account of the slowness of their sense, they were called not "a living soul" but *the creeping things of living souls.* But if they were called so for this reason, the name "living soul" would be given to the birds. But since the flying things too are called, like those, creeping things, so that "of living souls" is to be understood, it must be confessed, as I think, that it was so said, as if it were said, "those things which among living souls are creeping or flying things": just as it could be said, "the ignoble among men," so that we might understand, "whoever among men are ignoble." For although there are also land animals which creep upon the earth, yet by a much greater number they move on feet, and perhaps as few creep on the lands as few walk in the waters.

## The other opinion.

**8. 12.** But some have thought that for this reason fish were called not "a living soul" but *the creeping things of living souls*, because they have no memory, nor any life that is, as it were, nearer to reason. But too little experience deceives them. For some have written many marvelous things which they were able to observe in fish-ponds. But even if perhaps they wrote false things, yet it is most certain that fish have memory. Which I myself have experienced, and let those experience it who can and will. For a certain great spring of Bulla Regia is nearly full of fish. And men gazing down from above are wont to throw them something, which the converging fish either snatch for themselves, or, contending among themselves, tear apart. Accustomed to this feeding, when men walk along the edge of the spring, they too go and return with them, swimming in a shoal, waiting for them to throw something, whose presence they perceive.

And so it does not seem to me in vain that the animals of the waters were called creeping things, as the birds flying things: for if either no memory, or a slower sense, had repelled the name of "living soul" from the fish, it would certainly be applied to the flying things, whose life is, before our eyes, both mindful and chattering, and most skillful in building nests and rearing offspring.

## That by certain philosophers its own animals are assigned to each element.

**9. 13.** Nor am I ignorant that certain philosophers have so distributed the animals of each element that they say earthen ones are not only those which creep and walk on the earth, but also birds, because these too rest on the earth, wearied by flying: but that the airy animals are demons; the heavenly ones, gods; of which we call some luminaries, some angels. These same men, however, attribute to the waters the fish and the monsters of their kind, so that no element may be empty of its own animals; as though indeed there were not earth beneath the waters, or they could prove that fish do not rest on it and restore their strength for swimming, as birds for flying; and if perhaps fish do this more rarely, because the wave is stronger than the breeze for carrying bodies, so that it also carries earthen animals that swim, whether taught by practice, like men, or by nature, like quadrupeds or serpents.

Or if they think otherwise because feet are not present in fish [nor in seals]: yet neither are the seals of the waters, nor the snakes and snails of the lands; for those have feet, and these kinds, without any feet, do not, I say, rest on the earth, but scarcely or never depart from it. But dragons, without feet, are reported both to rest in caves and to be lifted up into the air: which, although they are not easily known, yet the writings, not only ours but also those of the Gentiles, have by no means kept silent about the kind of these living things.

## What the "place" of the demons is.

**10. 14.** Wherefore, even if demons are airy animals, since they thrive by the nature of airy bodies; and therefore are not dissolved by death; because in them prevails the element which is fitter for acting than for being acted upon; two being set beneath, namely water and earth, but one set above, that is, starry fire: for the elements are distributed two for being acted upon, moisture and soil; but for acting other two, air and fire.

If, then, these things are so, this distinction does not hinder our Scripture, which indicates that the flying things were produced not from the air, but from the waters; because to the flying things was given the place, indeed, of a finer moisture, exhaled and stretched into the breezes, but moisture nonetheless.

But the air reaches from the border of the luminous heaven as far as the fluid of the waters and the bare lands. Yet the moist exhalations do not darken its whole space, but as far as that boundary, whence it begins to be called also earth, according to that *Psalm* where it is said, *praise the Lord from the earth*[^12]. But the upper part of the air, on account of its pure tranquillity, is joined by a common peace to the heaven on which it borders, and is named with its name.

In which part perhaps, if before their transgression the transgressing angels were, with their prince, now the devil, then an archangel (for some of our people do not think them to have been heavenly or supercelestial angels), it is no wonder if after their sin they were thrust down into this darkness; where, nevertheless, there is also air, and it is woven with a thin moisture, which, stirred, makes winds, and more vehemently stirred makes even fires and thunders, and gathered makes clouds, and thickened makes rain, and, the clouds congealing, snow, and, the denser clouds more turbulently congealing, hail, and, stretched out, makes clear weather—by the hidden commands and work of God, administering from the highest to the lowest all things that he created. Whence in that *Psalm*, when there had been mentioned *fire, hail, snow, ice, the breath of the tempest*[^13]; lest such things be thought to come to be and to be moved without divine providence, it immediately added, *which do his word*[^14].

## On the airy body of the demons.

**10. 15.** But if those transgressors, before they transgressed, bore heavenly bodies, neither is it strange if they were turned, as a punishment, into an airy quality, so that they could now suffer something from fire, that is, from the element of a higher nature: nor were they permitted to hold even the upper and purer spaces of the air, but these dark ones, which is for them, after its kind, a sort of prison, until the time of judgment.

And if anything about these transgressing angels is to be more diligently inquired into, there will be another place more suited to Scripture. Accordingly, what is now enough: if these turbid and stormy spaces, on account of the nature of the air extended as far as the waves and the lands, can sustain airy bodies; they can sustain also the birds, which were produced from the waters, on account of the thin breaths of the waters: which, namely, are inserted as vapor into this same air, poured around next to the waves and the lands, and on this account assigned to the lowest and earthen part, and they weave the breezes, which, weighed down by nocturnal chills, distill in serene dew also; but if the cold is more vehement, they whiten with a whiter frost.

## On the various kinds of living things created from the earth.

**11. 16.** *And God said, 'let the earth bring forth the living soul according to its kind: quadrupeds, and creeping things, and beasts of the earth according to their kind, and cattle according to their kind.' And so it was made. And God made the beasts of the earth according to their kind, and the cattle according to their kind, and all the creeping things of the earth according to their kind. And God saw that they were good*[^15]*.* Now it was consequent that he should adorn with its own animals the other part, which is properly called earth, of this lowest place—which whole, with all the abysses and the misty air, Scripture elsewhere universally embraces under the name of *earth.* And the kinds of animals which the earth produced at the word of God are indeed manifest. But because often by the name of *cattle* or by the name of *beasts* all animals devoid of reason are wont to be understood, it is rightly asked which he now properly calls *beasts,* and which *cattle.* And as for the things that crawl, or the earthen *creeping things,* it is not to be doubted that he wished all serpents to be understood; although they can also be called beasts: but the name *cattle* does not usually befit serpents. Again, to lions, and leopards, and tigers, and wolves, and foxes, dogs too, and apes, and the rest of that kind, the term *beasts* usually befits. But the name *cattle* is wont to be more fittingly applied to those animals which are in the use of men: whether for aiding labors, like oxen and horses, and any such; or for wool, or for eating, like sheep and swine.

## On the quadrupeds.

**11. 17.** What, then, are the quadrupeds? For although all these, except certain creeping things, walk on four feet, yet unless he wished certain ones to be properly understood by this name, he would certainly not here also name quadrupeds, although in the repetition he kept silent about them. Or were stags, and deer, and wild asses, and boars (because they cannot be joined to those beasts where the lions are, for they are like those cattle, yet are not under human care) properly called quadrupeds; as though these were the remaining animals, to which this appellation, general indeed with many on account of the number of feet, was yet given by a special signification?

Or because he said three times, *according to its kind*[^16]*,* does he invite us to attend to three kinds? First, *according to its kind,* quadrupeds and creeping things; where I think it is signified which ones he called quadrupeds, namely those which are in the kind of creeping things, such as lizards, geckos, and anything of this sort. And therefore in the repetition he did not repeat the name *quadrupeds,* because he perhaps embraced them under the term *creeping things*: whence there he does not simply say, *creeping things*; but added, *all the creeping things of the earth*[^17]*.* Therefore *of the earth,* because there are also those of the waters; and therefore *all,* so that there may be understood those which strive on four feet too, which above were properly signified by the name *quadrupeds.*

But beasts, of which likewise he says, *according to their kind,* are whatever rages with mouth or also with claws, except the serpents. But cattle, of which thirdly he says, *according to their kind,* are those which tear with neither force, but either with horns, or not even with this.

For I have already said that the name of quadrupeds, how widely it extends, is easily recognized by the very number of feet, and that by the name of cattle or beasts every irrational animal is sometimes comprehended. But the term "wild beasts" too is wont in the Latin tongue to be of just as much force: yet not on that account was the consideration to be neglected, how these names, which were not set down in vain in this place of the Scriptures, can be distinguished even by a special distinction, which in everyday speech can easily be noticed.

## The repeated words "according to its kind" and the eternal reasons.

**12. 18.** Not in vain too does it move the reader whether *according to its kind* is said at random and as it were by chance, or by some reason, as though they had existed before, since they are narrated to be created for the first time: or whether "their kind" is to be understood in the higher reasons, spiritual of course, according to which they are created below.

But if it were so, this would be said of the light, of the heaven, of the waters and lands, of the luminaries of heaven. For which of them is there, whose eternal and unchangeable reason does not thrive in the very Wisdom of God, which *reaches mightily from end to end, and orders all things sweetly*[^18]?

But this begins to be said of the herbs and trees, down to these earthen living things. For even in those which were created from the waters, although in the first mention it was not said, yet in that repetition it was said: *and God made the great sea-creatures and every soul of the creeping animals, which the waters brought forth according to their kind; and every winged flying thing according to its kind*[^19]*.*

## What other explanations can be given.

**12. 19.** Or is it because these arose in such a way that from them others should be born, and they should preserve the form of their origin by succession, that they are said to be *according to their kind,* on account of the propagation of offspring, by which they were created to be lasting? But why is it said of the herbs and trees not only *according to their kind,* but also *according to their likeness,* when the animals too, whether of the waters or of the lands, generate according to their likeness?

Or, because likeness follows kind, did he not wish to repeat it everywhere? For seed too he did not repeat everywhere; although it is present in herbs and trees as well as in animals, even if not in all. For it has been observed that some things are so born from the waters or earth that they have no sex; and therefore their seed is not in them, but in the elements from which they arise.

This, then, is *according to its kind,* where both the power of seeds and the likeness of those succeeding to those passing away is understood; because none of them was so created as to exist once for all, either to remain, or to pass away with none succeeding.

## Why holy Scripture does not at all use those words in the creation of man.

**12. 20.** Why, then, was it not said of man too, "let us make man to our image and likeness, according to his kind," since the propagation of man too is manifest? Is it because God had not made man so as to die, if he had wished to keep the commandment, and therefore a successor was not necessary to one passing away: but after sin *he was compared to the senseless cattle, and was made like to them*[^20]; so that now *the sons of this age* generate and are generated, by which the race of mortals can subsist with succession preserved? What, then, does that blessing mean, after man was made: *increase and multiply, and fill the earth*; which surely could come to pass by generating?

Or is nothing to be rashly said here, until we come to that place of Scripture where these things are to be more diligently inquired into and treated? For now it can perhaps suffice that *according to its kind* was not said of man, because one was being made, from whom the woman too was made. For there are not many kinds of men, as of herbs, trees, fish, flying things, serpents, cattle, beasts: so that we take *according to its kind* to have been said as if it were said "by kinds," so that things like one another and belonging to one origin of seed might be distinguished from the rest.

## Why the blessing was imparted to the water-creatures alone, as to man.

**13. 21.** Likewise it is asked what the animals of the waters so deserved of the Creator that they alone were blessed, as men. For God blessed them too, saying, *increase and multiply, and fill the waters of the sea, and let the flying things be multiplied over the earth*[^21]*.* Or had it to be said in one kind of creature, so that in the rest it might consequently be understood, namely those which grow by generations? Then it would be said first in that which was first created of such a kind, namely in the herb and tree.

Or did he perhaps judge those which had no affection for propagating offspring, and generated without any sense, unworthy of those words of blessing, *increase and multiply*: but where such an affection was present, there he first said this, so that in the earthen animals it might be understood even though not said? But it was necessary to repeat this in man, lest anyone should say that there is any sin in the office of begetting children, as there is in lust, whether of fornicating, or of abusing marriage itself immoderately.

## A question about the creation of insects.

**14. 22.** There is also some question about certain very tiny animals, whether they were created in the first foundings of things, or from the consequent corruptions of mortal things? For very many of them are born either from the defects of living bodies, or from refuse, or from exhalations, or from the rotting of corpses; some too from the corruption of trees and herbs, some from the corruptions of fruits: of all which we cannot rightly say that God is not the Creator.

For there is present in all things a certain beauty of nature proper to their kind, such that in these there is greater wonder in one who considers well, and richer praise of the omnipotent craftsman.

He who made all things in Wisdom[^22], which, reaching from end to end, and ordering all things sweetly[^23], does not leave even those last things of all, which are dissolved according to the order of their kind, and whose dissolution we dread because of the penalty of our mortality, unformed.

But he creates living things, least in body, keen in sense; so that with greater attention we are astonished at the agility of a flying fly than at the size of a walking beast of burden, and marvel more at the works of little ants than at the burdens of camels.

## When insects were created, and what is to be said of the other things that are born from the bodies of animals.

**14. 23.** But whether, in the first foundings of things, as I said, which are narrated to have been created in this order of six days, we are to believe that these least things too were established, or afterward by the consequent dissolutions of corruptible bodies, this is asked. And it can indeed be said that those very tiny things which arise from the waters or lands were then created: among which not absurdly are understood also those which are born from the things that arose by the earth sprouting; and because they had preceded the founding not only of animals but also of luminaries; and because they are continuous with the lands through the connection of roots, whence on that day on which the dry land appeared they arose, so that they might be understood to belong rather to the supplement of the dwelling than to the number of the dwellers.

But the others, which are born from the bodies of animals, and most of all of dead ones, it is most absurd to say were then created, when the animals themselves were created: except that there was already present in all animate bodies a certain natural power, and, as it were, fore-sown and in a way threaded beginnings of future animals, which were going to arise from the corruptions of such bodies, each according to its kind and differences, through the ineffable administration of the Creator moving all things unchangeably.

## Why venomous and harmful animals were created.

**15. 24.** Concerning the venomous and harmful kinds of animals too it is commonly asked whether they were created after the sin of man for vengeance; or rather whether, having been already created harmless, they only afterward began to harm sinners.

Nor is this strange, since even in the times of this laborious and toilsome life, when no one is so just that he dares to call himself perfect, the Apostle faithfully attesting and saying, *not that I have already received, or am already perfect*[^24].

And since, for virtue still to be exercised and to be perfected in weakness, temptations and bodily troubles are necessary, the same Apostle again making it manifest, who says that, lest he be exalted by the greatness of the revelations, there was given to him *a thorn of the flesh, an angel of Satan, to buffet him*; and that, having asked the Lord three times that it might depart from him, the answer was made to him, *my grace is sufficient for you; for strength is made perfect in weakness*[^25].

Yet holy Daniel too lived safe and unafraid among the lions[^26], who, surely not lying to God in prayer, confesses not only the sins of his people but also his own[^27]; and on the hand of the Apostle himself a deadly viper clung, and did not harm him[^28].

These things, then, could even when created harm nothing, if there had not arisen a cause either for terrifying and punishing vices, or for proving and perfecting virtue: because both examples of patience are to be shown, for the advancement of others; and man himself becomes more certainly known to himself in temptations; and justly that perpetual salvation, which was basely lost through pleasure, is strongly recovered through pain.

## Why were the beasts created that harm one another?

**16. 25.** Someone will say: "Why, then, do the beasts harm one another, for whom there are neither any sins, so that this should be called vengeance, nor do they receive any virtue from such exercise?"

No doubt for this reason, namely because some are the food of others. Nor can we rightly say, "there should not be others on which others might feed." For all things, as long as they exist, have their measures, numbers, and orders; all which, rightly considered, are praised, and are not changed without a hidden moderation, proper to their kind, of temporal beauty, even by passing from one thing into another. Which, even if it lies hidden from the foolish, shines dimly upon those advancing, and is clear to the perfect.

And certainly by all such motions of the lower creation healthful admonitions are offered to man, so that he may see how much he must be busy for his spiritual and everlasting salvation, by which he surpasses all irrational living things; when he sees those things, from the greatest elephants to the smallest little worms, do whatever they can for the bodily and temporal salvation which they have been allotted, according to the lower ordering of their kind, whether by resisting or by avoiding.

Which does not appear except when some seek the refreshment of their own body from the bodies of others; others protect themselves either by the strength of fighting back, or by the safeguard of flight, or by the defense of hiding-places.

For the very pain of the body in any living thing is a great and wonderful power of the soul, which holds that framework together vitally by an ineffable commingling, and reduces it to a certain unity of its own measure, when it suffers it to be corrupted and dissolved not indifferently but, so to speak, with indignation.

## A scruple about the bodies of the dead being devoured.

**17. 26.** This too may perhaps move someone: if harmful animals either injure living men as a penalty, or healthfully exercise them, or usefully prove them, or unknowingly teach them, why do they tear apart even the bodies of dead men for their food? As though indeed it mattered anything to our advantage by what passages this now lifeless flesh goes into the deep secret places of nature, whence by the wonderful omnipotence of the Creator it is to be drawn forth again to be reformed.

Although from this too there comes a certain admonition to the prudent, that they so commend themselves to the faithful Creator, administering all things great and small by a hidden nod, to whom even the hairs of our head are numbered[^29], that they do not, on account of empty cares for their lifeless bodies, dread any kinds of death, but do not hesitate to prepare the sinews of pious fortitude for all things.

## A question about thorns and thistles.

**18. 27.** Such a question too is wont to arise about thorns and thistles, and about certain unfruitful trees, why or when they were created, since God said, *let the earth bring forth the herb of fodder sowing seed, and the fruit-bearing tree making fruit*[^30]*.* But those who are so moved do not understand, at least from the usual formulas of human law, how a usufruct is named. For a certain usefulness to those enjoying is considered in the name of fruit. But how great are the uses, whether manifest or hidden, of all the things that the earth, generating, nourishes from the root, let them both behold some themselves, and inquire the rest from those who have experience.

## A more diligent answer to the foregoing question.

**18. 28.** And concerning thorns and thistles indeed the answer can be more complete, because after sin it was said to man, of the earth, *it shall bring forth thorns and thistles for you*[^31]. Yet it is not easily to be said that these then began to arise from the earth. For perhaps, since in those very kinds of seeds many uses are found, they could have had their place without any penalty of man. But that in the fields in which he now labored as a penalty, these too should be born, this can be believed to avail for the heaping up of the penalty, since they could be born elsewhere, either for the pastures of birds and cattle, or for some uses of men themselves.

Although that sense too does not clash with these words, by which *it shall bring forth thorns and thistles for you* is so understood that the earth, bringing these forth even before, did not yet bring them forth for man unto labor, but as fitting food for animals of every sort; for there are those which feed conveniently and pleasantly on these kinds, both softer and drier: but then it began to bring these forth for man unto toilsome business, when after sin he began to labor on the earth.

Not that these were born before in other places, and afterward in the fields which man cultivated to take crops; but both before and afterward in the same places: before, however, not for man, but afterward for man; so that this may be signified which was added, *for you*: because it was not said, "it shall bring forth thorns and thistles," but, *it shall bring forth for you*; that is, "so that these may now begin to be born for you unto labor, which before were born only for the pasture of other animals."

## Why in the creation of man alone it was said: Let us make.

**19. 29.** *And God said, 'let us make man to our image and likeness; and let him have dominion over the fish of the sea and the flying things of heaven, and all the cattle, and all the earth, and all the creeping things that creep upon the earth.' And God made man; to the image of God he made him: male and female he made them. And God blessed them, saying, 'increase, and multiply, and fill the earth, and have dominion over it, and rule the fish of the sea, and the flying things of heaven, and all the cattle, and all the earth, and all the creeping things that creep upon the earth.' And God said, 'behold, I have given you every seed-bearing herb, sowing seed, which is over all the earth, and every tree which has in itself the fruit of seed-bearing seed; it shall be for you for food, and for all the beasts of the earth, and for all the flying things of heaven, and for every creeping thing that creeps upon the earth, which has in itself the breath of life; and every green herb for food.' And so it was made. And God saw all the things that he had made, and behold, they were very good. And there was evening, and there was morning, the sixth day*[^32]*.*

Again and again there will later be a richer place for considering and discussing more diligently the nature of man. Now, however, so that our inquiry and treatment of the works of the six days may conclude, we say this first briefly: that it is not to be taken indifferently that in the other works it is said, *God said, 'let it be'*; but here, *God said, 'let us make man to our image and likeness'*: namely to intimate, so to speak, a plurality of persons on account of the Father, and the Son, and the Holy Spirit. Which unity of deity, however, he at once admonishes us to understand, saying, *and God made man to the image of God*; not as if the Father to the image of the Son, or the Son to the image of the Father; otherwise it was not truly said, *to our image,* if man was made to the image of the Father alone, or of the Son alone: but it was so said, *God made him to the image of God,* as though it were said, "God made him to his own image."

But when it is now said, *to the image of God,* whereas above it was said, *to our image*; it is signified that that plurality of persons does not bring it about that we should say, or believe, or understand several gods; but that we should take the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit, on account of which Trinity it was said *to our image,* as one God, on account of which it was said, *to the image of God.*

## In what part man was made to the image of God.

**20. 30.** Here too this is not to be passed over: that when he had said, *to our image*; he at once subjoined, *and let him have power over the fish of the sea and the flying things of heaven*[^33]*,* and the rest of the animals devoid of reason: so that we may, of course, understand man to have been made to the image of God in that in which he surpasses the irrational living things. And that is reason itself, or mind, or understanding, or whatever else it is more conveniently called. Whence the Apostle too says, *be renewed in the spirit of your mind, and put on the new man*[^34], *who is renewed unto the knowledge of God, according to the image of him who created him*[^35]; sufficiently showing where man was created to the image of God, namely not in the lineaments of the body, but in a certain intelligible form of the illumined mind.

## In the creation of man, why it was not said: And so it was made.

**20. 31.** And on this account, just as in that first light—if by that name is rightly understood the intellectual light made, a partaker of the eternal and unchangeable Wisdom of God—it was not said, "and so it was made," so that then it might be repeated, *and God made*: because, as we have already discussed as far as we could, no knowledge of the Word of God came to be in the first creature, so that after that knowledge there might be created below what was being created in that Word; but the light itself was first created, in which there might come to be the knowledge of the Word of God, through which it was being created, and that very knowledge was for it the turning, from its formlessness, toward the forming God, and being created and formed.

But afterward, in the other creatures, it is said, *and so it was made*; where it is signified that in that light, that is, in the intellectual creature, the knowledge of the Word was first made; and then, when it is said, *and God made,* the coming-to-be of the kind of creature itself is shown, which in the Word of God had been said to be made.

This too is preserved in the founding of man. For God said, *let us make man to our image and likeness*[^36]*,* and the rest. And then it is not said, *and so it was made*; but at once it is brought in, *and God made man to the image of God*: because that nature too is, namely, intellectual, like that light, and therefore for it to come to be is the same as to know the Word of God through which it comes to be.

## What is meant by: And so it was made.

**20. 32.** For if it were said, *and so it was made,* and afterward brought in, *and God made*; it would be understood as made, as it were, first in the knowledge of the rational creature, and then in some creature which was not rational: but because this is a rational creature too, and it is itself perfected by that same knowledge. For just as after the fall of sin man *is renewed unto the knowledge of God, according to the image of him who created him*; so in that very knowledge he was created, before he grew old by his fault, whence again in the same knowledge he might be renewed.

But the things which were not created in that knowledge—because either bodies or irrational souls were being created—the knowledge of them was first made in the intellectual creature, from the Word by which it was said that they should come to be: on account of which knowledge it was first said, *and so it was made*: so that it might be shown that the knowledge itself was made in that nature which could know this beforehand in the Word of God; and then the corporeal and irrational creatures themselves came to be, on account of which it was thereupon added, *and God made.*

## A difficulty about the immortality of man, from the food provided for him.

**21. 33.** But how man was made immortal, and yet received for food, with the other animals, "the herb of fodder bearing seed, and the fruit-bearing tree, and the green herb," is difficult to say. For if he was made mortal by sin, surely before sin he did not need such foods. For that body could not be corrupted by hunger.

For that which was said, *increase and multiply, and fill the earth*[^37]*,* although it seems unable to come to pass except through the intercourse of male and female—whence here too there arises an indication of mortal bodies: yet it can be said that there could have been another mode in immortal bodies, so that children might be born by the affection of pious love alone, with no desire of corruption, neither to succeed parents who had died, nor themselves to die; until the earth was filled with immortal men, and so, a just and holy people being established, such as we believe will be after the resurrection, a mode of being born would also come to be: it can indeed be said, and how it is to be said is another consideration; but neither will anyone dare to say this, that a need of foods by which they may be refreshed can belong to any but mortal bodies.

## The opinion of some: that the creation of the soul is signified by the words "And he made," but that of the body by these, "And he formed."

**22. 34.** But some have even suspected that then the inner man was made, but the body of man afterward, when Scripture says, *and God formed man of the slime of the earth*[^38]: so that what was said, *he made,* belongs to the spirit; but what, *he formed,* to the body. Nor do they attend that male and female could not have been made except according to the body.

For although it may be most subtly discussed that the very mind of man, in which he was made to the image of God, namely a certain rational life, is distributed into the truth of eternal contemplation and into the administration of temporal things; and that thus it is made, as it were, male and female, that part counseling, this one obeying: yet in this distribution it is not rightly called the image of God, except that which cleaves to the unchangeable Truth to be contemplated. As a figure of which the Apostle Paul says that the man only is the image and glory of God: *but the woman,* he says, *is the glory of the man*[^39].

And so, although this was figured outwardly, according to the body, in two human beings of different sex, which is also understood within in the single mind of one man; yet the woman too, because she is a woman in body, is herself also renewed in the spirit of her mind unto the knowledge of God according to the image of him who created her, where there is neither male nor female. For just as women are not separated from this grace of renewal and from the reforming of the image of God, although in the sex of their body something else is figured, according to which the man alone is said to be "the image and glory of God"; so too in the very first founding of man, according to that by which the woman too was "man," she certainly had her own mind, and that rational, according to which she too was made to the image of God.

But on account of the unity of the conjunction: *God made,* it says, *man to the image of God*[^40]*.* And lest anyone should think that the spirit of man alone was made, although according to the spirit alone he was made to the image of God: *he made him,* it says, *male and female he made them*[^41]*,* so that now the body too may be understood to have been made. Again, lest anyone should suppose that he was so made that in a single human being each sex was expressed, as sometimes those are born whom they call androgynous; it shows that it set down the singular number on account of the unity of conjunction, and that the woman was made from the man, as is afterward made manifest, when what was here briefly said begins to be more diligently explained: and therefore it immediately subjoined the plural number, saying, *he made them, and he blessed them*[^42]*.* But, as I have already said, we shall inquire more diligently in the following Scripture about the creation of man.

## To what verse 30 refers: And so it was made.

**23. 35.** Now it is to be noticed that, after he said, *and so it was made*, he at once subjoined, *and God saw all the things that he had made, and behold, they were very good*[^43]: where is understood the power and faculty itself given to human nature of taking for food the fodder of the field and the fruit of the tree. For to this he referred, *and so it was made,* which he had begun from that place where he says, *and God said, 'behold, I have given you seed-bearing fodder*[^44]*,'* and the rest. For if we refer to all the things said above what he says, *and so it was made*; it will follow that we must confess that they had also already increased, and, multiplied, had filled the earth on that same sixth day; which we find, that same Scripture testifying, to have come to pass after many years.

Wherefore, when this faculty of eating had been given, and man had known it, God saying so, it is said, *and so it was made*; in this, namely, that, God saying so, man knew. For if he had even then done it, that is, had taken those things that were given even by eating them, that custom of Scripture would be preserved, that after it is said, *and so it was made,* which belongs to expressing the preceding knowledge, then the operation itself too would be brought in, and it would be said, *and they took and ate.* For it could be so said, even if God were not named again. As in that place, after it is said, *let the water that is under the heaven be gathered into one gathering, and let the dry land appear*[^45]; it is brought in, *and so it was made*: and then it is not said, *and God made*; but yet it is thus repeated, *and the water was gathered into its gatherings,* and so on.

## Why of man it was not said singly, as of the rest: God saw that it was good.

**24. 36.** But that he did not say singly, as in the rest, of the human creature too, *and God saw that it was good*: but after man was made, and the power either of ruling or of eating given to him, he brought in concerning all things, *and God saw all the things that he had made, and behold, they were very good,* can rightly be asked.

For it could first have been rendered to man singly, what was rendered singly to the rest which were made before; then at last it could be said of all the things that God made, *behold, they were very good.* Or is it perhaps because on the sixth day all things are completed, that therefore it had to be said of all things, *God saw all the things that he had made, and behold, they were very good*[^46]; not singly of those which were made on that very day? Why, then, was it said of the cattle and beasts and creeping things of the earth, which belong to the same sixth day? Unless perhaps those both singly in their kind, and with the rest universally, deserved to be called good; but man made to the image of God deserved to have this said only with the rest. Or is it because he was not yet perfect, since he was not yet established in Paradise? As though indeed, after he was established there, what was here passed over had been said.

## Even if some natures become deformed by sinning, the whole nevertheless remains beautiful with them.

**24. 37.** What then shall we say? Is it because God foreknew that man would sin, and would not remain in the perfection of his image, that he wished to call him good not singly, but with the rest, as it were intimating what was to come? Because, when the things that were made remain, in that they were made, as far as they received, like either those which did not sin, or those which cannot sin, they are both good singly, and in the whole very good. For not in vain was *very* added; because the members of a body too, even if singly they are beautiful, are yet all far more beautiful in the framework of the whole body. For the eye, for example, pleasing and praised, yet if we saw it separated from the body, we would not call it so beautiful as in that connection of members, when, set in its place, it was discerned in the whole body.

But those things which by sinning lose their proper beauty in no way bring it about that they too, rightly ordered, are not good with the whole and the universe. Man, therefore, before sin was good even in his own kind; but Scripture passed over saying this, so that it might rather say that which would foretell something to come. For nothing false was said of him. For he who is good singly is surely more good with all. But when he is good with all, it does not follow that he is also good singly. It was therefore measured that that should be said which would be true in the present, and would signify the foreknowledge of the future.

For God is the best Founder of natures, but the most just orderer of sinners; so that even if some things become deformed singly by sinning, yet the whole is always beautiful with them. But let us now treat the things that follow in the following volume.
