# Book XI: The temptation and fall of man according to Genesis 2:25–3:24.

**1.** 1. *And they were both naked, Adam and his wife, and they were not ashamed. But the serpent was the most prudent of all the beasts that are upon the earth, which the Lord God made. And the serpent said to the woman: Why is it that God said, You shall not eat of every tree of paradise? And the woman said to the serpent: Of the fruit of the tree which is in paradise we shall eat; but of the fruit of the tree which is in the midst of paradise, God said, You shall not eat of it, neither shall you touch it, lest you die. And the serpent said to the woman: You shall not die the death; for God knew that on the day you eat of it, your eyes will be opened, and you will be like gods, knowing good and evil.*

*And the woman saw that the tree was good for food, and that it is pleasing to the eyes to see, and beautiful to know. And taking of its fruit, she ate, and gave to her husband with her, and they ate. And the eyes of both were opened, and they recognized that they were naked, and they sewed fig leaves, and made for themselves aprons. And they heard the voice of the Lord God walking in paradise toward evening, and Adam and his wife hid themselves from the face of the Lord God, in the midst of the tree of paradise.*

*And the Lord God called Adam, and said to him: Where are you? And he said to him: I heard your voice walking in paradise, and I was afraid, because I am naked, and I hid myself. And he said to him: Who told you that you are naked, unless you have eaten of the tree of which alone I had commanded you not to eat? And Adam said: The woman whom you gave to be with me, she gave me of the tree, and I ate. And the Lord God said to the woman: Why have you done this? And the woman said: The serpent deceived me, and I ate.*

*And the Lord God said to the serpent: Because you have done this, cursed are you above all cattle, and above all the beasts that are upon the earth. Upon your breast and your belly you will go, and earth you will eat all the days of your life. And I will put enmities between you and the woman, and between your seed and her seed: she will watch for your head, and you will watch for her heel.*

*And to the woman he said: Multiplying I will multiply your sorrows and your groaning. In sorrows you will bear children, and your turning will be to your husband, and he will have dominion over you. And to Adam he said: Because you have heard the voice of your wife, and have eaten of the tree, of which alone I had commanded you not to eat, cursed is the earth in your works: in sorrows you will eat of it all the days of your life; thorns and thistles it will bring forth for you, and you will eat the hay of the field. In the sweat of your face you will eat your bread, until you return into the earth from which you were taken; for earth you are, and into earth you will go*[^1]*.*

*And Adam called the name of his wife Life*, *because she is the mother of all the living. And the Lord God made for Adam and his wife garments of skins, and clothed them. And the Lord God said: Behold, Adam has become like one of us in knowing good and evil. And now, lest perhaps he stretch out his hand and take of the tree of life, and eat, and live forever. And the Lord God sent him out of the paradise of delight to till the earth from which he was taken. And he cast out Adam, and placed him over against the paradise of delight; and he set the Cherubim, and a flaming sword that turns, to keep the way of the tree of life*[^2]*.*

## That what Scripture narrates as done is to be defended according to the propriety of the letter.

**1.** 2. Before we treat the text of this set Scripture in order, I think we must be admonished—what I remember having already premised elsewhere in this work—that this is to be demanded of us: that what the very writer narrates as done be defended according to the propriety of the letter. But if, in the words of God, or of any person assumed into the prophetic office, something is said which cannot be understood literally except absurdly, it ought without doubt to be taken as said figuratively for the sake of some signification; yet that it was said it is not lawful to doubt: for this is demanded by the faithfulness of the narrator and the promise of the expositor.

## Why the first-formed were not ashamed of their nakedness.

**1.** 3. *They were,* then, *both naked*[^3]: it is true, the bodies of the two human beings dwelling in paradise were altogether naked. *And they were not ashamed*[^4]: for what would shame them, when they had felt no law in their members warring against the law of their mind[^5]? which, as a punishment of sin, followed them after the perpetration of the prevarication, disobedience usurping the forbidden thing, and justice punishing the committed deed. Before this happened, they were naked, as was said, and were not confounded: there was no motion in the body to which shame would be owed; they thought nothing was to be veiled, because they had felt nothing was to be restrained. How they would have propagated children has already been discussed: yet it is not to be believed in the way they propagated after the foretold vengeance followed the committing of the crime; when, before they died, in the body of the now disobedient human beings, death conceived was, by a most just reciprocation of the disobedient members, stirring up tumult. Not yet were Adam and Eve such, when they were both naked and were not confounded.

## What the cunning of the serpent was like, and whence it came.

**2.** 4. *But the serpent was* there *the most prudent indeed of all the beasts that were upon the earth, which the Lord God had made*[^6]*.* For by a transferred word it was said *most prudent,* or, as several Latin manuscripts have it, *most wise,* not in the proper sense in which wisdom is wont to be taken for good—whether of God, or of angels, or of the rational soul—as if we should call bees and ants wise too, on account of works as if imitating wisdom. Although this serpent could be called most wise of all the beasts not by its own irrational soul, but by an alien spirit already, that is, a diabolic one. For however much the prevaricating angels were cast down from the supernal seats by the desert of their perversity and pride, yet by nature they are more excellent than all the beasts on account of the eminence of reason. What wonder, then, if the devil, now filling the serpent by his own instinct, and mingling his spirit with it, in the manner in which the soothsayers of demons are wont to be filled, had rendered it most wise of all the beasts according to the living and irrational soul of living things?

For by a misuse of the name, "wisdom" is so spoken of in an evil sense, as "cunning" is in a good one; whereas properly and more usually, at least in the Latin language, the wise are praiseworthily so called, but the cunning are understood as ill-disposed in heart. Whence some, as we find in very many manuscripts, conforming to the usage of Latin speech, transferring not the word but rather the sense, preferred to call this serpent more cunning than all the beasts, rather than more wise. But what the Hebrew propriety has—whether there the wise can be called and understood in an evil sense not by misuse but properly—let those see who know it well. Yet we plainly read in another place of the holy Scriptures the wise unto evil, not unto good[^7]; and the Lord says that the children of this age are wiser than the children of light, in providing for themselves for the future, though by fraud, not by right[^8].

## The devil was permitted to tempt only through the serpent.

**3.** 5. Nor indeed ought we to suppose that the devil chose for himself the serpent through which to tempt and persuade to sin; but since there was in him, on account of a perverse and envious will, a cupidity of deceiving, he could do it only through that animal through which he was permitted to be able. For the will to harm can be even from one's own depraved mind; but the power is only from God[^9], and this by a hidden and deep justice, since there is no iniquity with God.

## Why the temptation of man was permitted.

**4.** 6. If, then, it is asked why God permitted man to be tempted, whom he foreknew would consent to the tempter, I cannot indeed penetrate the depth of his counsel, and I confess this to be far above my powers. There is, then, some perhaps more hidden cause, reserved for the better and holier, by his grace rather than by their merits: but yet, as far as he either grants me to be wise or allows me to say, it does not seem to me that man would have been going to be of great praise, if he could live well for this reason, that no one persuaded him to live badly; since he had it both in his nature to be able, and in his power to will, not to consent to the persuader—with the help, however, of him who *resists the proud, but gives grace to the humble*[^10].

Why, then, would he not allow him to be tempted, whom he foreknew would consent, since he was going to do it by his own will through fault, and was to be ordered by his equity through punishment: so that even thus he might show to the proud soul, for the instruction of the future saints, how rightly he himself would use the wills of souls, even evil ones, when they used good natures perversely?

## Man was cast down by the tempter because he was proud.

**5.** 7. Nor is it to be supposed that this tempter would have cast man down, unless there had preceded in the soul of man a certain elation to be checked, so that through the humiliation of sin, which he had falsely presumed about himself, he might learn. For it was most truly said: *before ruin the heart is exalted, and before glory it is humbled*[^11]. And this is perhaps the voice of this man in the Psalm: *I said in my abundance: I shall not be moved forever*[^12]. Then, having now experienced what evil the proud presumption of one's own power has, and what good the help of the grace of God: *Lord,* he says, *by your will you gave strength to my beauty; but you turned away your face, and I was troubled*[^13]. But whether that was said of this man, or of another, yet to a soul exalting itself and trusting too much, as it were, in its own power, it had to be demonstrated even by the experience of punishment how ill a made nature fares, if it withdraws from its maker.

For from this above all it is commended what a good God is, when it is well with no one who withdraws from him: because both those who rejoice in deadly pleasures cannot be without the fear of pains; and those who, by a greater stupor of pride, do not feel at all the evil of their desertion, appear utterly more wretched to others who have known how to discern these things; so that, if they are unwilling to receive the medicine of avoiding such things, they may avail as an example by which such things can be avoided.

For as the Apostle James says: *each one is tempted by his own concupiscence, being drawn away and enticed; then concupiscence, when it has conceived, brings forth sin; but sin, when it has been completed, generates death*[^14]. Whence, the swelling of pride being healed, one rises again, if the will which was lacking before the experience, that one might remain with God, is at least present after the experience, that one may return to God.

## What good temptation brings.

**6.** 8. But certain people are moved about this temptation of the first man, that God permitted it to happen, as though they did not now see the whole human race tempted without ceasing by the snares of the devil. Why does God permit this too? Is it not because virtue is tested and exercised, and the palm is more glorious for having, when tempted, not consented, than for not having been able to be tempted: since even those who, forsaking the Creator, go after the tempter, tempt more and more those who remain in the word of God, and offer them an example against the cupidity of withdrawing, and strike into them a devout fear against pride? Whence the Apostle says: *looking to yourself, lest you too be tempted*[^15].

For it is a wonder how greatly that humility, by which we are subject to the Creator, lest we presume of our own powers as though not needing his help, is commended with continual care through all the divine Scriptures. Since, then, even through the unjust the just, and through the impious the devout, make progress, it is said in vain: "God would not create those whom he foreknew would be evil." For why would he not create those whom he foreknew would profit the good, so that they might be born both useful for exercising and admonishing the good wills of the others, and justly punished for their own evil will?

## Why man was not created such that he would never wish to sin.

**7.** 9. He would, they say, make a man such that he would not wish to sin at all. Behold, we concede that that nature is better which would not wish to sin at all; let them too concede that the nature is not evil which was so made that it could not sin if it would not, and that the sentence is just by which that one was punished which sinned by will, not by necessity. As, then, true reason teaches that that nature is better which nothing unlawful delights at all; so true reason likewise teaches that that one too is good which has it in its power so to restrain unlawful delight, if it should arise, that it rejoices not only over the other lawful and rightly done things, but even over the restraining of that depraved delight itself.

Since, then, this nature is good, that one better, why would God make that one alone, and not rather both? And by this, those who were ready to praise God for that one alone ought to praise him more abundantly for both. For that one is in the holy angels, this one in holy men. But those who chose for themselves the parts of iniquity, and depraved a praiseworthy nature by a culpable will—not because they were foreknown ought they therefore by no means to have been created: for they too have their own place, which they fill among things for the profit of the saints. For God needs neither the justice of any righteous man; how much less the iniquity of a perverse one?

## Why those were created who were foreknown to be future evil.

**8.** 10. But who with sober consideration would say: It is better not to create one whom he foreknew could be corrected by another's iniquity, than to create even one whom he foreknew would have to be damned for his own iniquity? For this is to say that it is better that he not exist who would by using another's evil well be mercifully crowned, than that even an evil one exist who would for his own desert be justly punished. For when sure reason demonstrates two goods, not equal, but one higher, the other lower, the slow of heart do not understand that, when they say *let both be such,* they are saying nothing other than *let that one alone exist.* And so, when they wish to equalize the kinds of goods, they diminish the number; and by immoderately increasing one kind, they take away the other. But who would listen to these men, if they said: "since the sense of seeing is more excellent than that of hearing, let there be four eyes, and let there be no ears"?

So, if that rational creature is more excellent which, without comparison to any punishment, without any pride, is subject to God; but some creature among men is so created that it cannot recognize the benefit of God in itself except by seeing the punishment of another, so that it may not be high-minded, but fear[^16]—that is, not presume about itself, but trust in God—who, understanding rightly, would say, "let this one be such as that one"; and not see that he is saying nothing other than, "let this one not exist, but let that one alone exist"?

But if this is said unlearnedly and unwisely, why, then, would God not create even those whom he foreknew would be evil, willing to show his wrath and to demonstrate his power, and for this enduring *with much patience the vessels of wrath which are fitted for destruction, that he might make known the riches of his glory upon the vessels of mercy, which he prepared for glory*[^17]? For so *he who glories*, let him *glory only in the Lord*[^18], when he recognizes that it is not his own but his that it is well with him not only that he exists, but also that it is well with him only from him, from whom he has that he exists.

## On the same matter.

**8.** 11. It is, then, very inopportunely said: "there would not be those to whom God grants so great a beneficence of his mercy, if they could not exist otherwise unless there were also those in whom he might demonstrate the justice of vengeance."

**9.** 11. For why should not both rather exist, when in both the goodness of God and his equity are by right proclaimed?

## God's foreknowledge and man's freedom.

**9.** 12. But indeed, if God willed, these too would be good. How much better did God will this: that they should be what they willed; but that the good should not be unfruitful, nor the evil unpunished, and that in this very thing they should be useful to others? But he foreknew that their will would be evil. He foreknew indeed, and because his foreknowledge cannot be deceived, therefore the evil will is not his but theirs. Why, then, did he create those whom he foreknew would be such? Because, as he foresaw what evil they would do, so he also foresaw what good he himself would do out of their evil deeds. For he so made them that he left them whence they too might do something, so that whatever even culpably they chose, they would find him working laudably out of them. For from themselves they have the evil will, but from him both a good nature and a just punishment; for themselves a due place, for others a support of exercise and an example of fear.

## God can convert the will of the evil into good.

**10.** 13. But he could, one says, convert even their will into good, since he is almighty. He could plainly. Why, then, did he not do it? Because he was unwilling. Why he was unwilling rests with him. For we ought not to be wiser than we ought to be wise[^19]. Yet I think we have shown sufficiently a little before that the rational creature is no small good, even this one which by comparison with the evil guards against evil: which kind of good creature surely would not exist, if God had converted the evil wills of all into good, and inflicted on no iniquity the due punishment; and so there would be only that kind which would profit by no comparison of the evil, whether of sin or of punishment. Thus, the numerousness of the more excellent kind being, as it were, increased, the number of the kinds of goods themselves would be diminished.

## That by the punishments of the evil God provides for the salvation of the good.

**11.** 14. "Therefore," they say, "is there something in the works of God which needs another's evil, in order to profit toward good?" Have men become so deaf and blind, by I know not what zeal of contention, that they neither hear nor see how very many are corrected, when certain ones are punished? For what pagan, what Jew, what heretic does not prove this daily in his own house? But when it comes to the debate and inquiry of truth, men are unwilling to attend to their own senses, from which work of divine providence there comes upon them the prompting of discipline to be imposed; so that, if those who are punished are not corrected, the rest may yet fear their example, and the just ruin of some may avail for the salvation of others. For is God the author of their malice or wickedness, by whose just punishment he provides for those for whom he has determined provision is to be made in this way? Surely not.

But although he foreknew that they would be evil by their own vices, he yet did not cease to create them; reckoning it to the profit of those whom he created in this kind, that they could not profit toward good except by comparison with the evil. For if they did not exist, they would surely profit nothing. Is it a small good that it has been brought about that they exist, who are certainly useful to that kind? Which kind, whoever does not wish to exist, does nothing other than that he himself not be in it.

## God's foreknowledge and providence.

**11.** 15. *Great are the works of the Lord; sought out in all his wills*[^20]: he foresees the future good, and creates; he foresees the future evil, and creates: offering himself for the good to enjoy, lavishing many of his gifts even on the evil, mercifully pardoning, justly avenging; and likewise mercifully avenging, justly pardoning: fearing nothing from anyone's malice, needing nothing from anyone's justice; taking counsel for nothing for himself either from the works of the good, and taking counsel for the good even from the punishments of the evil.

Why, then, would he not permit man to be tempted—man who was to be exposed, convicted, and punished by that temptation, when the proud concupiscence of his own power would bring forth what it had conceived, and be confounded by its own offspring[^21], and by a just punishment would deter from the evil of pride and disobedience the posterity for whom these things were being prepared to be written down and announced?

## Why the temptation was permitted to be done through the serpent.

**12.** 16. But if it is asked why the devil was permitted to tempt especially through the serpent—that this was done for the sake of signifying something, whom would Scripture of so great authority not admonish, dealing in prophecy with so great documents of divinity, with as many fulfillments as the world is now filled with? Not that the devil wished to signify something for our instruction; but since he could not approach to tempt unless permitted, surely he could not do it through anything else than that through which he was permitted to approach. Whatever, then, that serpent signified, it is to be attributed to that providence under which the devil too has indeed his cupidity of harming, but the faculty only such as is given, either for subverting and destroying the vessels of wrath, or for humbling or testing the vessels of mercy.

Whence the nature of the serpent comes, then, we know: for the earth, at the word of God, produced all the cattle, and beasts, and serpents; which whole creation, having in itself a living irrational soul, is subject, by the law of the divine order, to every rational creature, whether of good or of evil will[^22]. What wonder, then, if the devil was permitted to do something through the serpent, when Christ himself permitted demons to enter into swine[^23]?

## That the nature of the devil is good, because it was created by God.

**13.** 17. It is wont to be inquired more scrupulously about the very nature of the devil, which certain heretics, offended by the trouble of his evil will, try wholly to alienate from the creation of the highest and true God, and to give him another principle, which is against God. For they cannot understand that everything that is, insofar as it is some substance, is both good, and cannot be except from that true God, from whom is every good; but that the evil will is moved inordinately, by setting lower goods before higher ones; and that so it came about that the spirit of the rational creature, delighting in its own power on account of excellence, swelled with pride, through which it would fall from the blessedness of the spiritual paradise, and waste away with envy.

In which, nevertheless, this very thing is good, that it lives and gives life to a body—whether airy, like the spirit of the devil himself or of the demons, or earthly, like the soul of man, even of any malignant and perverse one. Thus, while they are unwilling that anything which God made should sin by its own will, they say that the substance of God himself was corrupted and perverted, first by necessity, and afterward inexpiably by will. But of the most demented error of these men we have already said much elsewhere.

## That pride was the cause of the angelic ruin.

**14.** 18. But in this work it must be inquired, according to holy Scripture, what is to be said about the devil. First, whether from the very beginning of the world, delighting in his own power, he stood apart from that fellowship and love by which the angels who enjoy God are blessed; or whether for some time he was in the holy company of angels, himself too equally just, and equally blessed. For some say that this was his fall from the supernal seats, that he envied man made to the image of God. But envy follows pride, it does not precede it: for envy is not the cause of being proud, but pride is the cause of envying.

Since, then, pride is love of one's own excellence, but envy is hatred of another's felicity, what is born from what is sufficiently at hand. For by loving his own excellence, one envies either equals, that they be made equal to him; or inferiors, lest they be made equal to him; or superiors, because he is not made equal to them. Therefore by being proud one is envious, not by envying is one proud.

## That pride and perverse self-love are the fountains of all evils.

**15.** 19. Rightly Scripture defined pride as the beginning of all sin, saying: *the beginning of all sin is pride*[^24]. To which testimony there is not unfittingly fitted also that which the Apostle says: *the root of all evils is avarice*[^25]; if we understand a general avarice, by which one desires something more than he ought, on account of his own excellence and a certain love of his own thing: to which the Latin language wisely gave a name, when it called it "private," which clearly is said rather from loss than from increase. For all privation diminishes. Whence, therefore, pride wishes to be eminent, thence it is thrust into straits and want, when it is reduced from the common to the private by a love of self that damages it.

But that is a special avarice which is more usually called the love of money. By the name of which the Apostle, signifying the genus through the species, wished a universal avarice to be understood, by saying: *the root of all evils is avarice.* For by this the devil too fell, who surely did not love money, but his own power. Accordingly, perverse self-love deprives the swollen spirit of holy fellowship, and confines it in misery, now desiring to be sated through iniquity.

Hence in another place, when he had said: *for men will be lovers of themselves*; he immediately added, *lovers of money*[^26], descending from that general avarice whose head is pride, to this special one which is proper to men. For neither would men be lovers of money, unless they thought themselves the more excellent the richer they were. To which disease love is contrary, which *does not seek the things that are its own*[^27]—that is, does not rejoice in private excellence: rightly, then, it also *is not puffed up*[^28].

## Two loves, two cities.

**15.** 20. These two loves—of which the one is holy, the other unclean; the one social, the other private; the one taking counsel for the common profit on account of the supernal society, the other reducing even the common thing into its own power on account of arrogant domination; the one subject, the other a rival to God; the one tranquil, the other turbulent; the one peaceful, the other seditious; the one preferring truth to the praises of those who err, the other greedy of praise in any way whatever; the one friendly, the other envious; the one wishing for the neighbor what it wishes for itself, the other wishing to subject the neighbor to itself; the one ruling the neighbor for the neighbor's profit, the other for its own—these went before in the angels, the one in the good, the other in the evil; and they distinguished the two cities founded among the human race, under the admirable and ineffable providence of God, administering and ordering all the things that he creates: the one of the just, the other of the unjust. By a certain temporal commingling of these too the age is carried on, until at the last judgment they are separated, and the one, joined to the good angels in its king, attains eternal life, the other, joined to the evil angels, is sent into eternal fire with its king. Of which two cities we shall perhaps discuss more broadly in another place, if the Lord wills.

## When the devil fell.

**16.** 21. When, then, pride cast down the devil, so that he perverted his good nature by a depraved will, Scripture does not say; yet that it was done before, and that from this he envied man, manifest reason declares. For it is at hand to all who consider these things, that pride is not born from envy, but rather envy from pride. But it can not without reason be thought that from the beginning of time the devil fell by pride, and that there was no time before in which he lived peaceably and blessedly with the holy angels, but that from the very beginning of the creation he apostatized from his creator; so that what the Lord says—*he was a murderer from the beginning, and did not stand in the truth*[^29]—we understand both from the beginning, not only that he was a murderer, but also that he did not stand in the truth. And a murderer indeed from that beginning from which man could be killed; but he could not be killed before there was one to be killed. From the beginning, then, the devil was a murderer, because he himself killed the first man, before whom there was no man. But he did not stand in the truth, and this from the beginning from which he himself was created, who would have stood if he had wished to stand.

## It is asked whether the devil was blessed before he sinned.

**17.** 22. For how can he be believed to have led a blessed life among the blessed angels, who, if he was not foreknowing of his future sin and punishment—that is, of his desertion and of eternal fire—it is rightly asked why he was not. For the holy angels are not uncertain of their eternal life and blessedness. For how are they blessed, if uncertain? Or shall we say that God was unwilling to reveal this to the devil, while he was still a good angel—either what he would do, or what he would suffer; but revealed to the rest that they would remain forever in his truth? But if it is so, then he was for that reason not equally blessed—indeed, not even fully blessed—since the fully blessed are certain of their blessedness, so that no fear disturbs it. But by what evil was he so rightly distinguished from the rest, that God did not reveal to him even the future things which pertained to himself? Was that one an avenger before this one a sinner? Far be it: for God does not condemn the innocent.

Or was he perhaps of another kind of angels, to whom God did not give even foreknowledge of the future things about themselves? Who, in what way they can be blessed, I do not see, to whom their very blessedness is uncertain. For this too some have thought: that the devil was not in that sublime nature of angels which is supercelestial, but in that of those who were made somewhat lower in the world, and distributed through their offices. For such, perhaps, something even unlawful could delight: which delight, however, if they were unwilling to sin, they would restrain by free choice; like man, especially that first one, who did not yet have the punishment of sin in his members, since even that very thing is overcome by holy men subject to God, through his grace, by devotion.

## Was Adam truly blessed in paradise?

**18.** 23. Furthermore, this question about the blessed life—whether anyone is to be said already to have it, of which he is uncertain whether it will remain with him, or whether misery may at some time succeed it—can arise about the first man himself. For if he was conscious of his future sin and of the divine vengeance, whence could he be blessed? Therefore he was in paradise not blessed. But was he not foreknowing of his future sin? Then through this ignorance he was either uncertain of that same blessedness; and how was he then truly blessed? or certain by a false hope, not by knowledge; and how was he not foolish?

## How man could be blessed in paradise.

**18.** 24. But yet, of a man still constituted in an animal body, to whom, living obediently, the fellowship of the angels was still to be given, and the change of the body from animal into spiritual, we can understand a blessed life in a certain mode, even if he was not foreknowing of his future sin. For neither were those foreknowing to whom the Apostle says: *you who are spiritual, instruct such a one in the spirit of gentleness, looking to yourself, lest you too be tempted*[^30]; yet we say, not absurdly nor improperly, that they were already blessed in this very thing, that they were spiritual, not in body, but in the justice of faith, *rejoicing in hope, patient in tribulation*[^31].

How much more, then, and in a fuller mode, was man blessed in paradise before sin, although uncertain of his future fall—who so rejoiced in hope, on account of the reward of the future change, that there was no tribulation for the enduring of which patience did battle? For although he was certain of the uncertain not by a vain presumption, like a fool, but by a hope not unfaithful; before he laid hold of that life, where he would be most certain of his own eternal life, he could exult, as it is written, *with trembling*[^32]; and by this exultation be blessed in paradise much more abundantly than the saints on this earth—in a certain mode lower than in that eternal life of the saints and of the supercelestial angels, yet not in none.

## The condition of the angels before they sinned.

**19. 25.** But to say of certain angels that they could be blessed in some manner of their own while uncertain of their future wickedness and damnation, or at any rate uncertain of their perpetual salvation, with no hope underlying them that they too would one day come to be certain about this matter through some change for the better—this is a presumption scarcely to be borne. Unless perhaps this too be said: that these angels were so created, distributed to worldly ministries under others more sublime and more blessed, that for the duties they have rightly discharged in their offices they should receive that blessed and more sublime life of which they could be most certain, rejoicing in the hope of which they might not unfittingly be called already blessed.

If the devil fell from the number of these along with the partners of his iniquity, this is similar to the way in which even human beings fall from the righteousness of faith, having transgressed through a like pride, either deceiving themselves or consenting to that deceiver.

## That the devil may be thought to have fallen from the beginning of his creation.

**19. 26.** But let those who can assert these two kinds of good angels—one of the supercelestial angels, among whom there never was the one who by falling became the devil; the other of the worldly angels, among whom he was. For myself, I confess, what I might assert this on the authority of Scripture does not for the present occur to me. But, hard pressed by that question in which it is asked whether before he fell he had foreknowledge of his fall, I said—lest I should say that the angels are or ever were uncertain of their blessedness—that it can not without reason be thought that the devil fell from the very beginning of creation, that is, from the very beginning either of time or of his own condition, and never stood in the truth[^33].

## Was the devil created in malice?

**20. 27.** Hence some think that he was not deflected into this malice by the free choice of his will, but was created altogether in it—though by the Lord God, the supreme and true creator of all natures—and they bring forward a testimony from the book of *Job*, since it is written there, when the discourse was about him: *this is the beginning of the Lord's handiwork, which he made to be mocked by his angels*[^34]; and with this opinion there agrees what is written in a *Psalm*: *this dragon which you formed to mock him*[^35]—except that here he said *which you formed*, but not, as there, *this is the beginning of the Lord's handiwork*, as though he had so formed him at the beginning that he was evil, envious, a deceiver, in short the devil, not depraved by his will but so created.

## The foregoing opinion is refuted.

**21. 28.** Although they may try to show how this opinion is not contrary to what is written, *God made all things; and behold, they were very good*[^36]—where the devil is found not depraved by his own will but made very evil by the Lord God himself—it may also be asserted, not foolishly or without learning, that not only by the first condition but even now, with so many wills depraved, nevertheless in sum all the things that have been created, that is, the whole of creation altogether, are very good: not in the sense that the evil ones in it are good, but in the sense that by their malice they do not bring it about that under the command, power, and wisdom of God the administrator the beauty and order of the universe should in any part be defiled or disturbed; since to each of their wills, even the evil ones, there are assigned fixed and fitting limits of power and weights of merits, so that even when these too are suitably and justly ordered the universe is beautiful.

Yet because it occurs to anyone, and is true and manifest, that it is contrary to justice itself for God to condemn in anyone, with no preceding merit, the very thing that he himself created in him, and because the certain and evident damnation of the devil and his angels is recited from the Gospel, where the Lord foretold that he would say to those who are on the left, *go into the eternal fire, which has been prepared for the devil and his angels*[^37], we must believe that in no way is the nature which God created to be punished by the penalty of eternal fire, but his own evil will.

## Why God created the devil and the wicked.

**22. 29.** Nor is it his nature that was signified by what was said, *this is the beginning of the Lord's handiwork, which he made to be mocked by his angels*[^38]; but rather either the airy body which he fitted suitably to such a will, or the very ordering in which he made him useful to the good even against his will, or that, foreknowing him to be evil in the future by his own will, he nevertheless made him—not withholding his goodness in granting life and substance even to a will that would be harmful—at the same time foreseeing how great a good he would, by his wonderful goodness and power, bring about from him.

Now he was called *the beginning of the Lord's handiwork, which he made to be mocked by his angels*, not because he first created him, or created him evil in the beginning, but because, since he knew that he would be evil by his own will so as to harm the good, he created him to this end, that through him he himself might profit the good. For this is what *to be mocked by his angels* means: he is mocked in this way, when his temptations, by which he tries to deprave the saints, profit them, so that the malice in which he himself willed to be, against his will, is useful to the servants of God; for foreseeing this God formed him.

He is therefore the beginning unto mocking, because even evil human beings, the vessels of this same devil and, as it were, the body of which he is the head—whom God, no less foreseeing that they would be evil, nevertheless created for the benefit of the saints—are likewise mocked, since, even while they wish to harm the saints, the saints are granted, by comparison with them, caution, and devout humility under God, and an understanding of grace, and training in bearing with the wicked, and proof in loving their enemies. But he is the beginning of the handiwork that is thus mocked, because he precedes them both in length of time and in the headship of malice.

God does this to him through the holy angels, by that work of providence whereby he administers created natures, subjecting, namely, the evil angels to the good, so that the wickedness of the evil may have power not so far as it strives, but so far as it is permitted—and not only the power of the evil angels but also of evil human beings—until even this righteousness, by which we live from faith, which is now patiently exercised in human beings, is turned into judgment, so that they too may judge not only the twelve tribes of Israel but even the angels[^39].

## That the devil fell by his own will from what he was to have received.

**23. 30.** What is therefore thought, that the devil never stood in the truth[^40], never led a blessed life with the angels, but fell from the very beginning of his condition, is not to be taken in such a way that he should be thought not depraved by his own will but created evil by the good God; otherwise he would not be said to have fallen from the beginning, for he did not fall if he was made such. But once made, he immediately turned himself away from the light of truth, swollen with pride and corrupted by delight in his own power: whence he did not taste the sweetness of the blessed and angelic life, which he did not indeed disdain after receiving it, but by being unwilling to receive it forsook and lost.

Accordingly, he could not have had foreknowledge of his fall, since wisdom is the fruit of devotion. But he, immediately impious and consequently blind in mind, fell not from what he had received but from what he would have received, had he been willing to be subject to God: and because he assuredly was unwilling, he both fell from what he was to have received and did not escape the power of him under whom he was unwilling to be; and it was brought about in him, by the weight of his merits, that he can neither delight in the light of justice nor be freed from its sentence.

## That the impious and the deserters of Christ are the body of the devil.

**24. 31.** What is therefore said against him through the prophet Isaiah—*how has Lucifer fallen, who rose in the morning? He was crushed to the earth, he who sent to all the nations? But you said in your mind: I will ascend into heaven, above the stars of heaven I will set my throne, I will sit on the lofty mountain above the high mountains that are toward the north, I will ascend above the clouds, I will be like the Most High: but now you shall go down to the underworld*[^41]; and the rest, which are understood to be said against the devil under the figure of the king, as it were, of Babylon—several things fit his body, which he gathers also from the human race, and especially those who cleave to him through pride, apostatizing from the commandments of God. For just as he who was the devil was called a man, as in the Gospel, *an enemy man has done this*[^42], so he who was a man was called a devil, as again in the Gospel, *did I not choose you twelve, and one of you is a devil*[^43]?

And just as the body of Christ, which is the Church, is called Christ, as in that text, *you are the seed of Abraham*[^44], when a little earlier he had said, *the promises were made to Abraham and to his seed: he does not say, and to seeds, as of many; but as of one, and to your seed, which is Christ*[^45]; and again, *for as the body is one and has many members, yet all the members of the body, though they are many, are one body, so also is Christ*[^46]: in that same way the body of the devil too, of which the devil is the head—that is, the very multitude of the impious, and especially of those who fall away from Christ or from the Church as from heaven—is called the devil, and many things are said figuratively against that body which fit not so much the head as the body and the members. And so Lucifer, who rose in the morning and fell, can be understood as the company of apostates either from Christ or from the Church, which is so turned to the darkness, having lost the light it bore, just as those who are turned to God pass from darkness to light, that is, those who were darkness become light.

## That heretics too belong to the body of the devil.

**25. 32.** Likewise the things said against the devil under the figure of the prince of Tyre through the prophet Ezekiel are understood thus: *you are the seal of likeness and the crown of beauty; you were in the delights of the paradise of God, you were adorned with every precious stone*[^47]; and the rest, which fit not so much that prince, the spirit of wickedness himself, as his body. For paradise is called the Church, as we read in the Song of Songs: *a garden enclosed, a sealed fountain, a well of living water, a paradise with the fruit of orchards*[^48]. From it have fallen, either by an open and bodily separation, all heretics; or by a hidden and spiritual one, although they seem to be in it bodily, all who have returned to their own vomit, when after the remission of all sins they had walked a little while in the way of righteousness—in whom *the last state has become worse than the first, and for whom it would have been better not to know the way of righteousness than, knowing it, to turn back from the holy commandment delivered to them*[^49].

The Lord describes this most wicked generation when he says that an unclean spirit goes out from a man, and returns with seven others, and dwells in the house which it had now found cleansed, so that the last state of that man is worse than the first[^50]. For to such a kind of human beings, which has now become the body of the devil, these words can be fitted: *from the day that you were created with the cherub*—that is, with the seat of God, which is interpreted "manifold knowledge"—*and he set you on the holy mountain of God*, that is, in the Church; whence comes, *and he heard me from his holy mountain*[^51]. *You were in the midst of stones of fire*[^52], that is, of saints fervent in spirit, of living stones: *you walked without fault in your days, from the day you were created until iniquities were found in you*[^53]. These things can be treated more carefully, so that perhaps they may show not only that this meaning can be in these words, but that there can be no other at all.

## The discussion is concluded with a summary presenting four opinions.

**26. 33.** But because it is a long matter, and requires another discourse devoted to that question alone, for now let this summary suffice: that either the devil, from the beginning of his condition, fell by impious pride from the blessedness which, had he willed it, he was to have received; or that there are other angels of a lower ministry in this world, among whom, according to a certain blessedness of theirs that lacked foreknowledge, he had lived, and from whose society, together with his own subject angels, he fell as an archangel through proud impiety—if this can in any way be asserted, which would be remarkable if it can.

Or else a reason must be sought how all the holy angels, if the devil at some time lived among them equally blessed with his own angels, did not yet themselves have certain foreknowledge of their perpetual happiness, but received it after his fall; or by what merit, before his sin, the devil was set apart from the rest of the angels along with his companions, so that he himself was ignorant of his future fall, while they were certain of their abiding.

Provided, however, that we in no way doubt that the sinning angels were thrust down, as into a prison of this murky air about the earth, and, according to the apostolic faith, are kept to be punished at the judgment[^54]; and that in that heavenly blessedness of the holy angels the eternal life is not uncertain, nor will it be uncertain for us, according to God's mercy and grace and most faithful promise, when we have been joined to them after the resurrection and the change of these bodies. For it is to this hope that we live, and by the grace of that promise we are renewed. But whatever can also be said about the devil—why God created him, when he foreknew that he would be such, and why the Almighty does not turn his will into good—let it be understood, or believed, according to the things we discussed when we likewise inquired about evil human beings; or, if something better can be found, let it be found.

## How the devil tempted the human being through the serpent and the woman.

**27. 34.** By him, therefore, whose power over all things he created is supreme, through the holy angels by whom the devil is mocked, when even his ill will is made to serve the Church of God, the devil was not permitted to tempt the woman except through the serpent, nor the man except through the woman. But in the serpent he himself spoke, using it as an instrument and moving its nature in the manner in which he could move it and it could be moved, so as to produce the sounds of words and bodily signs by which the woman might understand the will of the one persuading her. But in the woman herself, because she was a rational creature who could use her own motion to make words, he himself did not speak, but rather his working and persuasion; although by a hidden prompting he aided within what he had done without through the serpent.

And if indeed he were to act more secretly by prompting alone, as he acted in Judas to betray Christ[^55], he could accomplish it in a soul clothed with a proud love of its own power: but, as I have already said, the devil has the will to tempt, but in his power he has neither whether he may do it nor in what manner he may do it. Because, then, he was permitted, he tempted; and in the manner he was permitted, so he tempted: but to which kind of human beings what he was doing would profit, he neither knew nor willed, and by that very fact he was mocked by the angels.

## How the serpent could speak to the woman.

**28. 35.** The serpent, then, did not understand the sounds of the words that were made through it to the woman. For its soul is not to be believed to have been changed into a rational nature; since not even human beings themselves, whose nature is rational, when a demon speaks in them by that affliction for which an exorcist is required, know what they are saying. How much less did that serpent understand the sounds of the words which the devil made through it and from it in that manner—a serpent which would not understand a man speaking, if it heard him free from the diabolical affliction? For as to the fact that serpents are thought to hear and understand the words of the Marsi, so that when these chant they often dart out of their hiding places, there too a diabolical power is at work, in order to make known everywhere the providence that subjects one thing to another in the natural order, and that also permits with most wise power what it permits to evil wills, so that this rather should be in use: that serpents are moved by the chants of men more than any other kind of living creature.

For this too is no small testimony, that human nature was at first seduced by the colloquy of a serpent. For the demons rejoice that this power is given them, that at the incantation of men they may move serpents, so that in whatever way they may deceive those whom they can. And this they are permitted in order to keep in remembrance the first deed, so that they may have a certain familiarity with this kind of creature. Moreover, that first deed itself was permitted to this end, that through the nature of the serpent there might be signified, to the human race, which had to be instructed by having these deeds recorded, the likeness of every diabolical temptation: which will appear when the divine sentence begins to be pronounced against the serpent.

## How the serpent was said to be "prudent," that is, "cunning."

**29. 36.** Accordingly the serpent was called *the most prudent of all the beasts*[^56], that is, the most cunning, on account of the cunning of the devil, who in it and through it was working deceit; just as a tongue is called prudent or cunning which a prudent or cunning person moves to persuade something prudently or cunningly. For this is not the power or virtue of the bodily member called the tongue, but of the mind that uses it. So too the pen of the scribes is called lying: for to be lying belongs only to one who is living and sentient; but the pen is called lying because through it the liar lyingly works; just as if this serpent too were called lying, because the devil used it lyingly as a pen.

## That the serpent spoke with the woman by the working of the devil.

**29. 37.** I thought this should be pointed out, lest anyone, supposing that animals devoid of reason have human understanding, or are suddenly changed into a rational animal, be seduced into that ridiculous and harmful opinion of the transmigration of the souls of human beings into beasts, or of beasts into human beings. The serpent, then, spoke to the man just as the she-ass on which Balaam sat spoke to the man[^57], except that the one was a diabolical work, the other an angelic one.

For good and evil angels have certain similar works, as did Moses and Pharaoh's magicians[^58]. But in these the good angels are also the more powerful, nor can the evil angels do any of even such works except what God has permitted through the good angels, so that each may be repaid according to his heart, or according to the grace of God; both justly and kindly, through the depth of the riches of the wisdom and knowledge of God[^59].

## The conversation of the serpent with the woman.

**30. 38.** *The serpent*, then, *said to the woman: why is it that God said, you shall not eat of every tree of paradise? And the woman said to the serpent: of the fruit of the tree which is in paradise we shall eat: but of the fruit of the tree which is in the midst of paradise, God said, you shall not eat of it, neither shall you touch it, lest you die*[^60]. The serpent first asked, and the woman answered this, so that the transgression might be inexcusable, and that it could in no way be said that the woman had forgotten what God had commanded. And yet forgetting the commandment, especially a single and so necessary a one, would belong to the greatest fault of damnable negligence: nevertheless her transgression is more evident when it is held in memory, and God, as it were standing by and present in that commandment, is despised. Hence it was necessary, when it was said in a *Psalm*, *and to those who keep his commandments in memory*, to add, *that they may do them*[^61]. For many keep them in memory only to despise them, with a greater sin of transgression, where there is no excuse of forgetfulness.

## By a lie the serpent persuades, to those wrongly credulous, an appetite for their own power.

**30. 39.** *The serpent*, then, *said to the woman: you shall not die the death. For God knew that on the day you eat of it, your eyes will be opened, and you will be like gods, knowing good and evil*[^62]. When would the woman have believed by these words that they had been divinely forbidden from a good and useful thing, unless there were already in her mind that love of her own power, and a certain proud presumption about herself, which through that temptation was to be convicted and humbled? Finally, not content with the words of the serpent, she considered the tree, *and saw that it was good for food and fair to the sight*[^63], and, not believing that she could die from it, I suppose she thought that God had said for the sake of some signification, *if you eat, you shall die the death*[^64]: and therefore she took of its fruit, and ate, and gave also to her husband with her; perhaps even with some persuasive word, which Scripture, keeping silent, leaves to be understood. Or perhaps there was now no need to persuade the man, when he saw that she had not died from that food.

## What is meant by the opened eyes of the first parents.

**31. 40.** Therefore *they ate, and the eyes of both were opened*[^65]. To what, if not to desiring one another, as a penalty for sin conceived in the very death of the flesh; so that the body was now not merely an animal one, which could, if they had kept obedience, be changed without death into a better and spiritual condition, but already a body of death, in which the law in the members fought against the law of the mind[^66]?

For they had not been made with closed eyes, and they did not wander about blind and groping in the paradise of delights, so that they touched the forbidden tree even without knowing it, and groping plucked the forbidden fruits in ignorance. But how were the animals and the birds brought to Adam, that he might see what he would call them, if he could not see? And how was the woman herself brought to the man, when she was made, so that of her, whom he did not see, he might say, *this now is bone of my bones, and flesh of my flesh*[^67], and the rest? Finally, how did the woman see that the tree was good for food, and that it was pleasing to the eyes to look upon, and fair to know, if their eyes were closed?

## Here "to open the eyes" means "to know."

**31. 41.** Yet not for that reason, on account of the transferred sense of a single word, must the whole be taken figuratively. Let one consider in what way the serpent said, *your eyes will be opened.* For the writer of the book related that he said this; but in what signification or sense he said it, he left to the reader to consider. But what is written, *and their eyes were opened, and they knew that they were naked*[^68], is written in the same way as all the things that are narrated as having been done; nor for that reason ought they to lead us into an allegorical narrative.

For neither did the evangelist, as narrator, insert anyone's figurative words in his own person, rather than narrate in his own person the things that had been done, when he said of those two, one of whom was Cleopas, that when the Lord had broken bread for them, *their eyes were opened, and they knew him*, whom they had not known along the way[^69]—not, of course, that they walked with closed eyes, but that they were unable to recognize him. Just as there, then, so neither in this place is the narrative figurative, although Scripture used a transferred word, so as to say that the eyes were opened which were already open before: open, that is, to looking upon and considering something which they had never before noticed.

For when, to transgressing the commandment, a bold curiosity was moved, eager to experience hidden things—what might follow if the forbidden thing were touched—and delighting in the harmful liberty of breaking the reins of the prohibition, judging it more probable that the death they had feared would not follow (for such a fruit, it must be believed, was in that tree, of a kind whose fruits they had already experienced as harmless in other trees), they believed rather that God could easily pardon those who sinned than that they could patiently endure not knowing what it might be, or why he had forbidden them to take food from it: as soon, then, as they transgressed the commandment, with grace wholly forsaking them within—the grace which they had offended by a certain conceit and a proud love of their own power—they cast their eyes upon their own members, and desired them with that motion which they had not known. To this, then, their eyes were opened, to which they were not open before, although they were open to other things.

## That death and concupiscence arose after sin.

**32. 42.** This death befell on the day on which was done what God had forbidden. For when that wonderful condition was lost—the condition by which the body itself was sustained, by a mystical power, even from the tree of life, through which they could neither be assailed by disease nor changed by age, so that there might already be signified in their flesh (though still animal and afterward to be changed for the better) that which comes about in the angels, through their participation in eternity, by that very spiritual nourishment of wisdom whose sacrament that tree was, namely, that they are not changed for the worse—when, then, this condition was lost, their body took on a diseased and death-bearing quality, which is also in the flesh of beasts, and through this also the same motion by which there arises in beasts the appetite for coupling, so that those being born may succeed those dying.

But yet even in this very penalty the rational soul, as a mark of its own nobility, blushed at the bestial motion in the members of its flesh, and it struck shame into it, not only because it now felt this where it had never before felt anything of the kind, but also because that shameful motion came from the transgression of the commandment. For there it perceived by what grace it had formerly been clothed, when in its nakedness it suffered nothing unseemly. There was fulfilled, *Lord, in your will you gave strength to my beauty; but you turned away your face, and I became troubled*[^70].

Finally, in that troubling they ran to fig leaves, sewed aprons, and because they had abandoned what was to be gloried in, they covered what was to be ashamed of. Nor do I think that they considered anything in those leaves as fit to cover such members now itching; but by a hidden prompting they were driven by that troubling to this, so that even a signification of such a penalty of theirs might be made by those who did not know it—a signification which, once made, would convict the sinner, and which, once written, would teach the reader.

## How God spoke to the first-formed parents.

**33. 43.** *And they heard the voice of the Lord God walking in paradise toward evening*[^71]. It was fitting that at that hour they should now be visited, who had fallen away from the light of truth. For perhaps God formerly spoke with them inwardly in other ways, whether expressible or inexpressible, just as he also speaks with the angels, illumining their minds by that very unchangeable truth, where it belongs to the understanding to know at once even those things that do not come to pass at once through time. Perhaps, I say, he spoke with them in this way—even if not with so great a participation in divine wisdom as the angels grasp, yet according to the human measure, however much less, but by the same kind of visitation and speech; perhaps also by that which comes about through a creature, whether in an ecstasy of the spirit by bodily images, or by some form presented to the bodily senses themselves, either for seeing or for hearing, just as God is wont to be seen in his angels or to make sound through a cloud.

Now, however, the fact that they heard the voice of God walking in paradise toward evening was done visibly only through a creature, lest that invisible substance, wholly everywhere, which is of the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit, be believed to have appeared to their bodily senses by a local and temporal motion.

## What the first parents did, moved by shame.

**33. 44.** *And Adam and his wife hid themselves from the face of the Lord in the midst of the tree which is in paradise*[^72]. When God turns away his face inwardly, and a person becomes troubled, let us not wonder that these things are done which are like madness, through excessive shame and fear; that hidden prompting also not resting, so that they did unknowingly the things that would signify something to their descendants, who would one day know it, and for whose sake these things were written.

## God called Adam with the voice of one rebuking, not of one ignorant.

**34. 45.** *And the Lord God called Adam, and said to him: where are you?*[^73] It is the voice of one rebuking, not of one ignorant. And this surely belongs to some signification, that just as the commandment was given to the man, through whom it would reach the woman, so the man is questioned first. For the commandment came from the Lord through the man to the woman; but the sin came from the devil through the woman to the man. These things are full of mystical significations, not in those in whom they were done, but in the most powerful wisdom of God who is at work in them. But we do not now unfold what is signified; we defend what was done.

## Adam's answer is weighed.

**34. 46.** Adam therefore answered: *I heard your voice in paradise, and I was afraid, because I am naked, and I hid myself*[^74]. It is probable enough that God was wont to appear in human form, through a creature suited to such an action, to those first human beings: whom, however, he never permitted to notice their nakedness, lifting their attention to higher things, until after sin they felt the shameful motion in their members by the penal law of the members. Thus, then, they were affected as human beings are wont to be affected under the eyes of human beings; and such an affect came from the penalty of sin—to wish to hide from him from whom nothing can hide, and to conceal the flesh from him who is the inspector of the heart.

But what wonder if, proudly wishing to be like gods, they became vain in their thoughts, and their foolish heart was darkened? For they said that they were wise in their abundance, and, with him turning away his face, they were made fools[^75]. For what now shamed them with regard to themselves, whence they had made themselves aprons, they feared much more vehemently to be seen, even so girded, by him who, as if by a familiar accommodation, brought, through a visible creature, as it were human eyes to see them. For if he appeared in this way so that human beings might speak with him as with a man, as Abraham did at the oak of Mamre[^76], that very near friendliness which gave confidence before sin burdened them with shame after sin; nor did they now dare to show to such eyes that nakedness which displeased even their own.

## How insolent Adam's excuse was.

**35. 47.** The Lord, then, willing now to punish the sinners, questioned in the manner of justice, more than was that penalty over which they were now forced to blush, said: *who told you that you are naked, unless you have eaten from the tree of which alone I had commanded you not to eat?*[^77] For from this death, conceived on account of the sentence of God who had so threatened, it came about that the members were noticed concupiscently, where the eyes are said to have been opened, and there followed what would cause shame. *And Adam said: the woman whom you gave to be with me, she gave me from the tree, and I ate*[^78]. O pride! Did he say, "I have sinned"? It has the deformity of confusion, and does not have the humility of confession.

These things were written to this end, since the very questionings themselves were surely made to this end, that they might be written both truthfully and usefully; for if falsely, then certainly not usefully: namely, that we might notice with what disease of pride human beings labor today, trying to refer to the Creator anything evil they have done, while they wish anything good they have done to be attributed to themselves. *The woman*, he says, *whom you gave to be with me*—that is, whom you gave that she might be with me—*she gave me from the tree, and I ate*: as though she had been given for this, that she should not rather herself obey the man, and both obey God.

## The woman is rebuked by God, but neither does she confess her sin.

**35. 48.** *And the Lord God said to the woman: why have you done this? And the woman said: the serpent deceived me, and I ate*[^79]. Neither does she confess her sin, but refers it to another—unequal in sex, equal in disdain. Yet from these was born, and did not imitate them, but, fully exercised by many evils, the one who said, and will say until the end of the age, *I said: Lord, have mercy on me; heal my soul, for I have sinned against you*[^80]. How much better would these too have done so?

But the Lord had not yet cut down the necks of sinners[^81]. There remained labors, sorrows, deaths, and every affliction of the age, and the grace of God by which he comes to the aid of human beings at the opportune time, teaching them, when afflicted, that they ought not to presume on themselves. *The serpent*, she says, *deceived me, and I ate*: as though anyone's persuasion ought to be preferred to the commandment of God.

## The serpent is neither questioned nor rebuked, but cursed.

**36. 49.** *And the Lord God said to the serpent: because you have done this, you are cursed among all the cattle and among all the beasts that are upon the earth. Upon your breast and your belly you shall go, and you shall eat earth all the days of your life. And I will put enmities between you and the woman, and between your seed and her seed. She shall watch your head, and you shall watch her heel*[^82]. This whole sentence is figurative, and the trustworthiness of the writer and the truth of the narrative owe it nothing else but that we not doubt that it was spoken. For what is set down, *and the Lord God said to the serpent*, are the words of the one writing; these are to be required in their proper sense. This, then, is true, that it was said to the serpent. The rest are now the words of God, which are left to the free understanding of the reader, whether they ought to be taken properly or figuratively, as we said at the outset of this volume of ours.

Accordingly, the fact that the serpent was not asked why it had done this can be seen to mean that it surely had not done it of its own nature and will; but the devil had worked from it and through it and in it, the devil who, by the sin of his impiety and pride, had already been destined for everlasting fire. Now, then, what is said to the serpent, and is surely referred to him who worked through the serpent, is beyond doubt figurative: for in these words that tempter is described, of what sort he would be toward the human race; which human race then began to be propagated, when this sentence was pronounced against the devil as against the serpent. And so in what way these words are to be taken, with the figures explained, we have discussed as far as we could in those two books *on Genesis* published *against the Manichees*[^83], and if we can do anything more carefully and more fittingly elsewhere, God will be present that we may accomplish it: now, however, our attention is not to be turned to anything other than what we have undertaken, when nothing requires it.

## That it was the woman's fault, not her nature, that deserved to have her husband as lord.

**37. 50.** *And to the woman he said: multiplying I will multiply your sorrows and your groaning: in sorrows you shall bear children, and your turning shall be toward your husband, and he shall rule over you*[^84]. These words of God against the woman too are much more conveniently understood figuratively and prophetically: nevertheless, because the woman had not yet given birth, and the pain and groaning of one giving birth come only from the body of death, which was conceived by that transgression of the commandment—the members being indeed at that time still animal, but, had the human being not sinned, surely not destined to die, and destined to live in a certain other and happier condition, until, after a life well led, they should deserve to be changed for the better, as we have already intimated above in several places—this penalty is referred also to the proper sense of the letter; except that what is said, *and your turning shall be toward your husband, and he shall rule over you*, must be considered how it can be taken properly. For it is not fitting to believe that even before sin the woman was made otherwise than that the man should rule over her, and she should turn to him by serving.

But it can rightly be taken that there is signified that servitude which is of a certain condition rather than of love, so that even such servitude itself, by which human beings afterward began to be servants to human beings, is found to have arisen from the penalty of sin. The Apostle indeed said, *through love serve one another*[^85]; but he would by no means say, "rule over one another." Spouses can therefore through love serve one another; but the Apostle does not permit the woman to rule over the man[^86]. For the sentence of God conferred this rather upon the man, and it was not nature but fault that deserved that the woman should have her husband as lord: yet unless this is observed, nature will be further depraved, and the fault increased.

## With what penalty Adam was punished, and why he called his wife "Life."

**38. 51.** He said also to her husband: *because you have heard the voice of your wife, and have eaten of the tree of which alone I had commanded you not to eat; cursed is the earth in your works: in sorrows you shall eat of it all the days of your life; thorns and thistles it shall bring forth for you, and you shall eat the hay of the field. In the sweat of your face you shall eat your bread, until you return to the earth from which you were taken; for earth you are, and into earth you shall go*[^87]. Who does not know that these are the labors of the human race on earth? And that they would not exist, if the happiness which had been in paradise were retained, is surely not to be doubted; and on this account let it not be irksome to take these words also properly in the first place. Yet the signification of the prophecy is to be observed and awaited, which the intention of God speaking here especially looks to.

For not in vain did Adam himself too, by a certain wonderful prompting, then call his wife's name *Life*; adding also, *because she is the mother of all the living*[^88]. For these also are to be understood as the words not of the writer narrating or affirming, but of the first human being himself, so that he said, *because she is the mother of all the living*, as if giving the reason for the name imposed by him, why he called her *Life*.

## What the garments of skin signify.

**39. 52.** *And the Lord God made for Adam and his wife garments of skin, and clothed them*[^89]. This too was done for the sake of signification, but yet it was done; just as those things that were said for the sake of signification were yet said. For this, which I have often said, and am not too often loath to say, is to be required of a narrator of things properly done, that he narrate as done the things that were done, and as said the things that were said. But just as in deeds it is asked what was done and what it signifies, so in words it is asked both what was said and what it signifies. For whether what is narrated to have been said was said figuratively or properly, it ought not to be thought that it was not said.

## The words of Genesis 3:22 spoken in condemnation of pride.

**39. 53.** *And God said: behold, Adam has become like one of us in knowing good and evil*[^90]. Since this, by whatever means and in whatever way it was said, God nevertheless said, what he says, *one of us*, is not to be understood otherwise than that the plural number is taken on account of the Trinity; just as it had been said, *let us make man*[^91]; just as also the Lord, of himself and the Father, *we will come to him, and make our dwelling with him*[^92]. It was therefore turned back upon the head of the proud one, with what outcome he had desired what was suggested by the serpent, *you shall be like gods*[^93]: *behold*, he says, *Adam has become like one of us*.

For these are the words of God, not so much insulting this man as deterring the rest from being so proud, for whose sake these things were written. *He has become*, he says, *like one of us in knowing good and evil*. What else is to be understood, except that an example for striking fear is set forth, namely, that not only did he not become such as he wished to become, but he did not even preserve what he had been made?

## Adam cast out of paradise.

**40. 54.** *And now*, God says, *lest perhaps he stretch out his hand, and take of the tree of life, and eat, and live forever. And the Lord God sent him out of the paradise of delight to till the earth from which he was taken*[^94]. The earlier words are God's; but this deed followed on account of those very words. For, alienated from the life—not only from the life which he had been about to receive with the angels, had he kept the commandment, but also from that which he was leading in paradise, in a certain happy condition of body—he surely had to be separated from the tree of life: whether because from it that very happy condition of his body was sustained, from a visible thing, by an invisible power, or because in it there was also a visible sacrament of invisible wisdom; he surely had to be alienated from it, either as one now about to die, or even as one excommunicated: just as also in this paradise, that is, the Church, human beings are wont to be removed by ecclesiastical discipline from the visible sacraments of the altar.

## The earthly paradise a figure of the spiritual paradise.

**40. 55.** *And he cast Adam out, and placed him over against the paradise of delight*[^95]. This too was done for the sake of signifying, but yet it was done, that over against paradise, by which the blessed life was signified also spiritually, the sinner might dwell, surely in misery. *And he set the Cherubim, and a flaming sword which turns, to guard the way of the tree of life*[^96]. This is surely to be believed to have been done through the heavenly powers even in the visible paradise, so that through angelic ministry there was there a certain fiery guard: yet that it was not done in vain, but rather signifies something also about the spiritual paradise, is surely not to be doubted.

## Opinions on the nature of the first sin: a) An untimely haste to attain knowledge.

**41. 56.** But I am not unaware that it has seemed to some that those human beings anticipated, by haste, the appetite for the knowledge of good and evil, and wished to attain at an unripe time what was being kept for them, deferred, more opportunely; and that the tempter brought this about, so that by plucking beforehand what did not yet suit such persons, they might offend God, and be alienated, shut out and condemned, from the usefulness of that thing, which they could enjoyably partake of if they came to it at their proper time, as God willed. This, if perhaps they wish to take that tree not properly, as a true tree with its true fruits, but figuratively, may have some outcome probable to right faith and truth.

## b) The opinion that the first-formed parents stole their nuptials is ridiculous.

**41. 57.** It has seemed to some also that those two first human beings stole their nuptials, and mingled in intercourse before he who had created them had joined them; which thing, they say, was signified by the name of the tree from which they were forbidden, until at an opportune time they should be joined. As though indeed they are to be believed to have been made at such an age that the maturity of puberty had to be awaited; or as though that were not then lawful when it could first be done, but when it could not be done, surely was not done: unless perhaps the bride had to be handed over by her father, and the solemnity of vows had to be awaited, and the festivity of a banquet, and the assessment of a dowry, and the writing of documents. This is ridiculous; besides that it departs from the proper sense of the things done, which we have undertaken to assert, and have asserted as far as God has willed to grant.

## Eve the mediatrix of Adam's sin.

**42. 58.** This rather moves me: if Adam was already spiritual—though in mind, not in body—how he could have believed what was said through the serpent, that God had forbidden them to eat of the fruit of that tree because he knew that, if they did, they would become as gods through the discernment of good and evil, as though the Creator had begrudged this one good only to his creature: it is a wonder if a man endowed with a spiritual mind could have believed this. Or is it because he himself could not believe this that the woman was approached, who was of small understanding, and who perhaps still lived according to the sense of the flesh, not according to the spirit of the mind—and this is why the Apostle does not attribute to her the image of God? For he says thus: *a man indeed ought not to veil his head, since he is the image and glory of God; but the woman is the glory of the man*[^97].

Not that the mind of the woman cannot receive the same image, *since in that grace he says we are neither male nor female*[^98]; but because perhaps she had not yet attained that which comes about in the knowledge of God, and, with the man ruling and dispensing, was little by little to attain it. For not in vain is that also which the Apostle says: *for Adam was formed first, then Eve: and Adam was not deceived, but the woman, being deceived, fell into transgression*[^99]; that is, so that through her the man too might transgress. For he calls him too a transgressor, where he says, *after the likeness of the transgression of Adam, who is the figure of him who was to come*[^100]. Yet he denies that he was deceived. For, when questioned, he did not say, The woman whom you gave to be with me deceived me, and I ate; but, she gave me, he says, from the tree, and I ate. She, however, said, *the serpent deceived me*[^101].

## Solomon too fell into sins through the love of women.

**42. 59.** So Solomon, a man of such great wisdom—is it to be believed that he thought there was anything of use in the worship of idols? But he was unable to resist the love of women drawing him to this evil, doing what he knew ought not to be done, lest he sadden the deadly delights of those women, for whom he was pining and wasting away. So too Adam, after the woman, seduced, had eaten of the forbidden tree, and had given to him that they might eat together, was unwilling to sadden her, whom he believed could waste away without his consolation, if she were alienated from his mind, and could perish altogether in that discord. He was not indeed overcome by concupiscence of the flesh, which he had not yet felt in the law of the members resisting the law of his mind; but by a certain friendly goodwill, by which it often comes about that God is offended, lest a person become an enemy from a friend: which that he ought not to have done, the just outcome of the divine sentence indicated.

## Adam deceived in another way than the wife was seduced.

**42. 60.** In some other way, then, he too was deceived; but by that serpentine guile by which the woman was seduced, I judge that he could in no way have been seduced. And it is this seduction that the Apostle properly called that by which what was being urged, though it was false, was thought true; that is, that God had forbidden them to touch that tree because he knew that, if they touched it, they would become as gods, as though he begrudged divinity to those whom he had made human beings.

But even if some desire to make trial solicited the man, on account of some elation of mind which could not hide from God the searcher of inward things, when he saw that the woman, having taken that food, had not died—according to the things we treated above—nevertheless I do not judge that he, if he was already endowed with a spiritual mind, could in any way have believed that God had forbidden them that food of that tree out of envy. But why say more? That sin was urged upon them in such a way as it could be urged upon such persons: but it was written down in such a way as it behooved to be read by all, even if these things were understood by few as it behooved.
