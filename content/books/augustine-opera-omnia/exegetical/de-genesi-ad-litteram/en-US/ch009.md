# Book VIII

## Paradise in Eden, to be understood both literally and figuratively.

**1.** 1. *And God planted a paradise in Eden toward the east, and there he put the man whom he had formed*[^1]. I am not unaware that many have said many things about paradise; yet there are three more or less general opinions on this matter. One is of those who would have paradise understood only corporeally; another, of those who would have it understood only spiritually; a third, of those who take paradise in both ways, some things corporeally, others spiritually. To put it briefly, then, I confess that the third opinion pleases me. According to it I have now undertaken to speak of paradise, insofar as the Lord will deign to grant, so that man, made from the mire—which is surely the human body—may be understood to have been placed in a corporeal paradise: so that, just as Adam himself, even though he signifies something else, according to the fact that the Apostle said he was a figure of the one to come[^2], is nevertheless understood as a man expressed in his own proper nature, who lived a fixed number of years and, having propagated a numerous offspring, died as other men die, even if he was not born of parents as others are but, as was fitting at the first, was made from the earth—so too the paradise in which God placed him is understood to be nothing other than a certain place of the earth, namely where the earthly man might dwell.

## History and figure in the books of Scripture.

**1.** 2. For the narrative in these books is not of the genre of speech proper to figurative things, as in the Song of Songs, but altogether of things done, as in the books of Kings and others of the same sort. But because in those books the things spoken of are such as the most familiar usage of human life includes, they are at first taken literally without difficulty—indeed, most readily—so that thereafter there may be carved out from them what the very deeds done also signified concerning things to come; whereas here, because the things spoken of are such as do not occur to those observing the customary course of nature, certain people are unwilling to have them understood as said properly, but as said figuratively; and they would begin history—that is, the narrative of things properly done—from that point at which Adam and Eve, dismissed from paradise, came together and begot offspring. As though it were customary for us either that they lived so many years, or that Enoch was translated, or that a woman both aged and barren gave birth, and other things of this kind.

## A difficulty from the narrative of marvelous things and of created things.

**1.** 3. But the narrative of marvelous deeds is one thing, they say, and that of established creatures another. For in the former, those very unusual events show that there are, as it were, certain natural modes of things and others of miracles, which are called mighty works; but here the very establishment of natures is intimated.

To whom it is answered: but these things too are unusual for this very reason, because they are first. For what is so without precedent and without an equal deed in the constitution of worldly things as the world itself? Surely it is not to be believed that God did not make the world, because he no longer makes worlds; or that he did not make the sun, because he no longer makes suns?

And this indeed ought to have been the answer to those disturbed not about paradise but about man himself; but now, since they believe that he was so made by God as no other was made, why are they unwilling to believe that paradise was made just as they now see forests come to be?

## Things named corporeally are also to be understood corporeally in the first place.

**1.** 4. For I am speaking to those who follow the authority of these Writings; for some of them would have paradise understood not properly but figuratively. As for those who are altogether opposed to these writings, we have dealt with them elsewhere and otherwise—though even here, in this very work of ours, we defend these things literally, so far as we are able, in such a way that those who, moved not by reason but by an obstinate or dull mind, refuse to believe these things may nevertheless find no reason by which to prove them false.

But these our own people, who have faith in these divine Books and yet are unwilling to have paradise understood according to the propriety of the letter—namely, as a most pleasant place shaded by fruitful groves, and that a great one, made fertile by a great spring—since they see no human work make so many and so great green plantations grow into woodland by the hidden working of God, I marvel how they believe that man himself was made just as they have never seen. Or if he too is to be understood figuratively, who begot Cain, and Abel, and Seth? Or were these too only figurative, and not also men born of men?

Let them therefore consider closely where this presumption tends, and let them try, along with us, to take all those things that are narrated as done first in the expression of their proper sense. For who would not afterward favor them as they understand what these things also admonish by a figurative signification, whether of spiritual natures themselves or of their affections, or even of things to come?

To be sure, if the things here named corporeally could in no way, with the truth of faith preserved, be taken also corporeally, what else would remain but that we should rather understand them as said figuratively than impiously find fault with holy Scripture? But on the other hand, if these things, understood corporeally as well, not only do not hinder but even more solidly support the narrative of the divine utterance, no one, I think, will be so faithlessly stubborn that, when he has seen them properly expounded according to the rule of faith, he would prefer to remain in his former opinion, if it had perhaps seemed to him that they could be taken only figuratively.

## Why Augustine elsewhere expounded Genesis allegorically against the Manichees.

**2.** 5. For I too, against the Manichees—who err with regard to these writings of the Old Testament, not by taking them otherwise than they ought, but by not taking them at all and by blaspheming them with loathing—wrote two books shortly after the time of my conversion, wishing quickly either to refute their ravings or to raise up the intent toward seeking, in the writings they hate, the Christian and evangelical faith. And because at that time not everything occurred to me as to how it could be taken properly, and it seemed rather that it could not be taken so, or scarcely or with difficulty, I explained—lest I be delayed—with as much brevity and clarity as I could, what those things signified figuratively which I could not find literally, lest people, deterred either by lengthy reading or by the obscurity of the discussion, should not care to take them in hand. Yet, mindful of what I had especially wished and could not do—that all things might at first be understood not figuratively but properly—and not altogether despairing that they could be understood so, I set this very thing down in the first part of the second book thus.

*To be sure,* I said, *whoever wishes to take all that has been said according to the letter—that is, to understand it no otherwise than the letter sounds—and can avoid blasphemies and proclaim all things agreeable to the Catholic faith, is not only not to be envied, but is to be held an outstanding and highly praiseworthy interpreter. But if no way out is given for the things that are written to be understood piously and worthily of God except by believing them to be set forth figuratively and in riddles, then, having apostolic authority, by whom so many riddles from the books of the Old Testament are unraveled, let us hold to the manner we intend, with the help of him who exhorts us to ask, seek, and knock*[^3]: *that we may explain all these figures of things according to the Catholic faith, whether they pertain to history or to prophecy, not prejudging a better and more diligent treatment, whether by ourselves or by others, to whom the Lord deigns to reveal it*[^4].

This I said then. But now, because the Lord has willed that, looking at and considering these things more diligently, I should reckon—not in vain, I think—that it can be shown even by me that these things were written according to a proper, not an allegorical, manner of speaking, let us examine what follows concerning paradise, just as we wished to show the things above.

## On the planting of trees in paradise.

**3.** 6. *God* therefore *planted a paradise* in delights (for this is what *in Eden* means) *toward the east, and there he put the man whom he had formed*[^5]. For so it is written, because so it was done. Then he recapitulates, so that this very thing which he set down briefly he may show how it was done; that is, how God planted paradise and there set the man whom he had formed. For thus it follows: *and God brought forth besides from the earth every tree beautiful to behold and good for food*[^6]. He did not say, "and God brought forth from the earth another tree or the remaining trees"; but, *he brought forth,* he says, *besides from the earth every tree beautiful to behold and good for food.* Already, then, the earth had at that time produced every tree both beautiful to behold and good for food, that is, on the third day; for on the sixth day he had said: *behold, I have given you every seed-bearing herb sowing seed, which is upon all the earth; and every fruit-bearing tree which has in itself the fruit of seed for sowing, which shall be for you for food*[^7]. Did he, then, give them one thing then and wish to give another now? I think not; but since these trees established in paradise are of these kinds which the earth had already produced on the third day, he produced them again in their own time—because, namely, that the earth then produced them, as is written, had been done causally in the earth; that is, because it had then latently received the power of producing them, by which power it comes about that even now the earth brings forth such things openly and in their own time.

## How God spoke when he created the causal reasons of things.

**3.** 7. The words, then, of God saying on the sixth day, *behold, I have given you every seed-bearing herb sowing seed, which is upon all the earth*[^8], and the rest, are not words uttered by a sounding or temporal voice, but, as in his Word, there is the power of creating. But to tell men what God said without temporal sounds was not possible except through temporal sounds. For it was to come about that man, now formed from the mire and animated by his breath, and whatever of the human race should arise from him, would use for food those things that were to spring up upon the earth out of that power of generating which the earth had already received. Establishing in the creation the causal reasons of this future thing, he spoke, as though it had already come into being, by an interior and inmost truth, which neither eye has seen nor ear has heard[^9], but which his Spirit assuredly revealed to the writer.

## On the tree of life.

**4.** 8. That, however, which follows—*and the tree of life in the midst of paradise, and the tree of the knowledge of discerning good and evil*[^10]—must be considered more diligently, lest it force us into allegory, so that these were not trees but signified something else by the name of tree. For it was said of wisdom: *she is a tree of life to all who embrace her*[^11]. Nevertheless, although there is an eternal Jerusalem in the heavens, there was also founded on earth a city by which that one would be signified; and Sarah and Hagar, although they signified the two Testaments[^12], were nevertheless also two certain women; and although Christ waters us with a spiritual stream through the passion of the wood, there was nevertheless also a rock which, struck with wood, poured out water for the thirsting people[^13], of which it would be said: *and the rock was Christ*[^14]. They signified something other than what they all were, yet they too existed corporeally. And when they were mentioned by the narrator, it was not a figurative manner of speaking, but an express narrative of those things of which there had been a figurative foreshadowing.

There was, therefore, also a tree of life, just as the rock was Christ; nor did God will man to live in paradise without mysteries of spiritual things corporeally presented. There was for him, then, in the other trees nourishment, but in that one a sacrament; signifying what, if not wisdom, of which it was said thus: *she is a tree of life to those who embrace her*[^15]—just as it would be said of Christ, "he is a rock flowing for those who thirst for him"? For that is rightly named which went before to signify him. He himself is the lamb that is sacrificed at the Pasch; yet that too was figured not only by being said but also by being done. For that lamb was not no lamb; plainly it was a lamb, and it was slain and eaten[^16]; and yet by that true deed something else also was figured. Not as that fatted calf which was slaughtered for the feast when the younger son returned[^17]. For there the narrative itself is of figures, not of things done with a figurative signification.

For it was not the Evangelist, but the Lord himself, who narrated this; the Evangelist, indeed, narrated that the Lord narrated this. Accordingly, then, what the Evangelist narrated also happened—namely, that the Lord spoke such things; but the Lord's own narration was a parable, of which it is never demanded that the things uttered in speech be shown to have also been done literally. Christ is both the stone anointed by Jacob, and the stone rejected by the builders, which has become the head of the corner[^18]; but the former was done among things done, while the latter was only foretold in figures. For the former a narrator of past things wrote, the latter only a foreteller of things to come.

## That the tree of life was both a true thing and a figure.

**5.** 9. So too Wisdom, this same Christ, is the tree of life in the spiritual paradise, into which he sent the thief from the cross[^19]; but there was created, to signify it, a tree of life also in the corporeal paradise—because this is what that Scripture said, which, narrating things done in their own times, narrated that man was made corporeally and was set there living in the body.

But if anyone thinks that souls, when they have departed from the body, are contained in places corporeally visible, although they are without a body, let him assert his opinion: there will be no lack of those who so favor it as to contend that even that rich man, thirsting, was in a place that was surely corporeal, and who do not hesitate to declare that the soul itself is altogether corporeal, on account of the parched tongue and the drop of water longed for from Lazarus's finger[^20]—with whom I, on so great a question, contend with no rashness. For it is better to doubt about hidden things than to quarrel about uncertain ones. That the rich man is to be understood as in the burning of punishments, and that poor man in the refreshment of joys, I do not doubt. But how that flame of hell, that bosom of Abraham, that tongue of the rich man, that finger of the poor man, that thirst of torment, that drop of refreshment are to be understood, is scarcely perhaps found by those who inquire gently, and never by those who strive contentiously.

A brief answer must indeed be given, lest this deep question, needing much discourse, delay us: if souls are contained in corporeal places even when stripped of their bodies, that thief could have been brought into the paradise where the body of the first man had been; so that in a more fitting passage of the Scriptures, if any necessity should demand it, we may set forth in some way what we either inquire or judge about this matter as well.

## That the tree of life was truly a tree but also figured wisdom.

**5.** 10. Now, indeed, that wisdom is not a body, and therefore not a tree either, I neither doubt nor think doubted by anyone; but that wisdom could be signified through a tree—that is, through a corporeal creation, as by a kind of sacrament—in the corporeal paradise, that man does not think to be believed who either does not see, in the Scriptures, so many corporeal sacraments of spiritual things, or contends that the first man ought not to have lived with some such sacrament; whereas the Apostle says that even this, which was said of the woman, who, it is agreed, was made for him from his side—*therefore a man will leave father and mother, and will cleave to his wife; and the two will be in one flesh*[^21]—is *a great sacrament in Christ and in the Church*[^22].

It is strange, however, and scarcely tolerable, how men are willing to have paradise said figuratively and yet unwilling to have it also made figuratively. But if they grant—as in the case of Hagar and Sarah, as in the case of Ishmael and Isaac—that these things were both done and yet also figurative, I do not see why they do not also admit that the tree of life was both truly some tree and yet figured wisdom.

## Of what sort was the food that that tree supplied.

**5.** 11. This too I add: that, although that tree supplied corporeal food, it nevertheless supplied such food as would strengthen the body of man with stable health—not as from other food, but by some hidden inspiration of wholesomeness. For surely, although it was ordinary bread, it nevertheless had something more, by one small cake of which God preserved a man from the want of hunger for the space of forty days[^23]. Or shall we perhaps hesitate to believe that, through the food of some tree, for the sake of some higher signification, God provided for man that his body should not be changed for the worse by either weakness or age, or even sink into decline—God who provided for human food itself so marvelous a state that, in earthen vessels, he replenished the failing flour and oil and they did not fail[^24]? Now let there step forth here someone of the contentious sort, and say that God ought to have done such miracles in our lands, but ought not to have done them in paradise: as though, indeed, either from dust the man or from the man's side the woman, he did not there work a greater miracle than that by which here he raised the dead.

## The tree of the knowledge of good and evil was a true and harmless tree.

**6.** 12. It follows that we consider the tree of the knowledge of discerning good and evil. Assuredly this tree too was visible and corporeal, like the other trees. That it was a tree, then, is not to be doubted; but why it received this name must be inquired into. To me, however, as I consider it again and again, it cannot be said how much that opinion pleases me: that that tree was not harmful in its food—for he who had made all things very good[^25] had not established anything evil in paradise—but that the transgression of the precept was evil for man. Now it was fitting that man, placed under the Lord God, should be forbidden something from some quarter, so that obedience itself might be for him the virtue of meriting his Lord; which obedience I can most truly say is the sole virtue of every rational creature acting under the power of God; and that the first and greatest vice of pride, leading to ruin, is to wish to use one's own power—the name of which vice is disobedience.

There would therefore be nothing from which man might consider and perceive that he had the Lord, unless something were commanded him. And so that tree was not evil, but was called the tree of the knowledge of discerning good and evil, because, if after the prohibition man should eat of it, in it there would be the future transgression of the precept, in which man would learn, by the experience of punishment, what difference there is between the good of obedience and the evil of disobedience. Accordingly this too was said not in a figure, but is to be understood as a certain true tree; on which a name was imposed not from the fruit or apple that would be born from it, but from the very thing that was going to follow when it was touched against the prohibition.

## That the rivers of paradise were real.

**7.** 13. *And a river went out of Eden, which watered the paradise, and from there it was divided into four parts. The name of one of these is Phison; this is the one that encircles all the land of Havilah, where there is gold; and the gold of that land is good, and there is the carbuncle and the green stone. And the name of the second river is Gihon; this is the one that encircles all the land of Ethiopia. And the third river is the Tigris; this is the one that flows over against the Assyrians. And the fourth river is the Euphrates*[^26]. Concerning these rivers, why should I trouble further to establish that they are real rivers, and that things which do not exist are not figuratively spoken of, as though their very names signified only something, when they are most well known to the regions through which they flow and are renowned among almost all nations? Rather, from these—since it is agreed that they assuredly exist (for antiquity changed the names of two of them, just as the river now called the Tiber was earlier called the Albula; for Gihon is the very river now called the Nile, and Phison was the name of the one they now call the Ganges; while the other two, the Tigris and the Euphrates, have kept even their ancient names)—we ought to be admonished to take the rest too, in the first place, according to the propriety of the letter, not to suppose a figurative manner of speaking in them, but to hold that the very things thus narrated both exist and also figure something.

Not that a parabolic manner of speaking could not take up something from a thing which it was not agreed also existed properly, as the Lord speaks of that man who was going down from Jerusalem to Jericho and fell among robbers[^27]: for who would not perceive and plainly see that it is a parable, and that the whole manner of speaking is figurative? Yet the two cities named there are pointed out to this day in their proper places. But we would take these four rivers in this way too, if any necessity compelled us to take the rest of what is narrated about paradise not properly but figuratively; but now, since no reason forbids us to understand the very things properly in the first place, why do we not rather simply follow the authority of Scripture in the narrative of things done, first understanding the things as truly done, and only then examining whatever else they signify?

## On the source and course of those rivers.

**7.** 14. Or shall we be moved by what is said of these rivers, that the sources of some are known but those of others altogether unknown, and that therefore it cannot be taken literally that they are divided from one river of paradise? When rather it should be believed that, since the place of paradise itself is most remote from the knowledge of men, the four parts of waters are divided from there, as most faithful Scripture testifies; but that those rivers whose sources are said to be known went somewhere underground, and after the stretches of long regions burst out in other places, where they are reported to be known as though in their own sources. For who does not know that some waters are wont to do this? But it is known there where they do not run long underground. A river, then, went out of Eden—that is, from the place of delights—and watered paradise, that is, all the beautiful and fruitful trees that shaded all the land of that region.

## That man, placed in paradise, may be believed at first to have practiced agriculture without toil.

**8.** 15. *And God took the man whom he had made, and put him in paradise to till it and to keep it. And the Lord God commanded Adam, saying: Of every tree that is in paradise you shall eat for food; but of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil, you shall not eat of it. For on the day you eat of it, you shall die the death*[^28]. Since above he had briefly said that God planted paradise and set there the man whom he had formed, he recapitulated in order to narrate how paradise was established[^29]. Now, then, by way of recapitulation he has also mentioned how God put there the man whom he had made. Let us therefore see what it means that it was said, *to till it and to keep it.* What was he to *till,* and what to *keep?*

Did the Lord perhaps will the first man to work at agriculture? Or is it not incredible that he condemned him to toil before sin? We would indeed think so, were it not that we see certain people farm with such delight of mind that it is a great punishment for them to be called away from it to something else. Whatever delights, then, agriculture has, at that time it surely had far more, when nothing adverse befell, whether from the earth or from the sky. For there was not the affliction of toil, but the gladdening of the will, when the things God had created came forth more joyfully and more fruitfully with the help of human work; whereby the Creator himself would be more abundantly praised, who had given to the soul, set in an animal body, the reason and the capacity for working—as much as would suffice for a willing mind, not as much as the body's need would compel an unwilling one to do.

## That earth tilled by man bears fruit by the help of God, who governs all created things.

**8.** 16. For what is a greater and more marvelous spectacle, or where can human reason in some way more converse with the nature of things, than when, the seeds having been set, the slips planted, the saplings transplanted, the cuttings grafted, each power of root and shoot is questioned, as it were, as to what it can and cannot do, whence it can and whence it cannot, what avails in it the invisible and interior power of numbers, what the diligence applied from without—and in this very consideration to perceive *that neither he who plants is anything, nor he who waters, but God who gives the increase*[^30]; because even that part of the work which is applied from without is applied through the man whom God nonetheless created, and whom he invisibly governs and orders?

## On the twofold working of providence toward creatures.

**9.** 17. From this the eye of thought is now lifted up to the world itself, as to a kind of great tree of things; and in it too a twofold working of providence is found, partly natural, partly voluntary. The natural, indeed, is through the hidden administration of God, by which he gives increase even to trees and herbs; the voluntary, through the works of angels and of men. According to the first, the heavenly things are ordered above and the earthly below; the lights and stars shine, the alternations of day and night are carried on, the earth founded upon the waters is bathed within and around, the air is poured out higher above, shrubs and animals are conceived and born, grow and grow old and die, and whatever else is brought about in things by an interior and natural motion. But according to the second, signs are given, things are taught and learned, fields are tilled, societies are administered, the arts are practiced, and whatever other things are done either in the heavenly society or in this earthly and mortal one—in such a way that the good are provided for even through the ignorant wicked. And in man himself the same twofold power of providence is active: first, toward the body, the natural, namely by that motion by which he comes to be, grows, grows old; the voluntary, by which provision is made for his food, clothing, and care. Likewise toward the soul, it is acted naturally that it live, that it sense; but voluntarily that it learn, that it consent.

## On the work of agriculture allegorically: a comparison of the tree and man.

**9.** 18. Now, just as in a tree agriculture brings it about from without that what is carried on within may prosper, so in man, with respect to the body, medicine from without serves what nature does within; and likewise with respect to the soul, that nature may be made blessed within, teaching is supplied from without. But what neglect of cultivation is to the tree, that carelessness of healing is to the body, and that sluggishness of learning is to the soul; and what useless moisture is to the tree, that ruinous food is to the body, and that the persuasion of iniquity is to the soul.

God, therefore, above all things, who founded all things and governs all things, being good, creates all natures, and being just, orders all wills. What, then, is repugnant to the truth, if we believe that man was so placed in paradise as to work at agriculture, not with servile toil, but with honorable delight of mind? For what is more innocent than this work for those at leisure, and what fuller of great consideration for the prudent?

## What it means *to till it and to keep it*.

**10.** 19. But *to keep it*—what? Paradise itself? Against whom? Surely no neighboring invader was feared, no disturber of the boundary, no thief, no assailant. How, then, are we to understand that a corporeal paradise could be kept corporeally by man? But neither did Scripture say, "to till and keep paradise"; rather it said, *to till it and to keep it.* Although, if it is more diligently rendered word for word from the Greek, it is written thus: *and the Lord God took the man whom he had made, and put him in paradise to till it and to keep it*[^31]. But whether he put the man himself to till—for this is what the one who translated *to till it* meant—or to till that same paradise, that is, that the man should till paradise, sounds ambiguous; and the wording seems rather to require that it be said not "should till paradise" but *in paradise.*

## A first conjecture by which it can be taken figuratively.

**10.** 20. Nevertheless, lest perhaps it was said *to till* paradise just as it was said above, *and there was no man to till the earth*[^32] (for it is the same manner of speaking, to till the earth and to till paradise), let us treat the ambiguous statement in both ways. For if it is not necessary that we take it as keeping paradise, but as keeping *in paradise,* then what is there to keep in paradise? For what there is to till in paradise we have already discussed, as has been seen. Or is it that what he would till in the earth through agriculture, he should keep in himself through discipline—that is, that just as the field obeyed him as he tilled it, so he too should obey his Lord as he commanded, so that, having taken up the precept, he might render the fruit of obedience, not the thorns of disobedience? Finally, because, when subject, he was unwilling to keep in himself the likeness of the paradise tilled by him, when condemned he received a field like himself: *it will bring forth thorns,* he says, *and thistles for you*[^33].

## A second conjecture of the figurative sense.

**10.** 21. But if we understand even this, that he should till paradise and keep paradise, he could indeed till paradise, as we said above, through agriculture; but keep it not against wicked men or enemies, of whom there were none, but perhaps against beasts. How so? Or why? For were the beasts already raging against man, which would not happen except through sin? For he himself imposed names on all the beasts brought to him, as is afterward mentioned; he himself also, on the sixth day, by the law of the word of God, received food in common with them all. Or if there was already something to be feared in the beasts, in what way could one man fortify that paradise? For it was no small place that so great a spring watered. For he would have to keep it, if he could fence paradise round with so great and so strong a wall that the serpent could not enter it; but it would be a wonder if, before he fenced it, he could shut out from it all serpents.

## The meaning set before our eyes.

**10.** 22. Accordingly, why do we pass over the meaning that lies before our eyes? Man was indeed placed in paradise to till that same paradise, as was discussed above, through an agriculture that was not laborious but delightful, and that reminded the mind of the prudent of great and useful things; but to keep that same paradise for his own sake, lest he commit anything for which he might deserve to be expelled from it. Finally, he received also a precept, so that there might be that by which he keeps paradise for himself—that is, by which, if it is observed, he is not cast out of it. For anyone is rightly said not to have kept his own thing who acted so as to lose it, even if it is safe for another who either found it or deserved to receive it.

## It can be better understood that man himself is tilled and kept by God.

**10.** 23. There is another sense in these words which I think not undeservedly to be preferred: that God should till and keep man himself. For just as man tills the earth, not to make it be earth, but to make it tilled and fruitful, so God much more tills man—whom he himself created to be man—to make him be just, if man does not depart from him through pride; for this is to apostatize from God, which Scripture calls the beginning of pride: *the beginning,* it says, *of man's pride is to apostatize from God*[^34]. Because, then, God is the immutable good, but man, both with respect to soul and with respect to body, is a mutable thing, unless, having turned to the immutable good which is God, he abides there, he cannot be formed so as to be just and blessed. And by this, the same God who creates man to be man himself tills and keeps man so that he may also be good and blessed. Therefore, by the same manner of speaking by which man is said to till the earth, which was already earth, so that it may be adorned and fruitful, by that manner of speaking God is said to till man, who was already man, so that he may be devout and wise, and to keep him, because man cannot be safe by his own power within himself, delighting in himself, rather than by God's power above himself, and despising his dominion.

## Why the word "Lord" is added here.

**11.** 24. Accordingly, I think it in no way idle, but that something, and something great, is being brought to our attention: that from the very beginning of this divine book, from where it began thus, *in the beginning God made heaven and earth,* up to this place, nowhere is it set down "Lord God," but only *God;* yet now, where it has come to the point that he was to establish man in paradise and, through a precept, till and keep him, Scripture spoke thus: *and the Lord God took the man whom he had made, and put him in paradise to till it and to keep it*[^35]—not that he was not the Lord God of the creatures spoken of above, but because this was written not on account of the angels, nor on account of the other things that were created, but on account of man, to admonish him how much it profits him to have the Lord God—that is, to live obediently under his dominion—rather than licentiously to misuse his own power. He willed to set this down nowhere before, except where it came to placing man in paradise to till and keep him: so as not to say, as also of all the rest above, "and God took the man whom he had made," but to say, *and the Lord God took the man whom he had made, and put him in paradise to till it,* that he might be just, *and to keep it,* that he might be safe—by that very dominion of his, which is useful not to him but to us.

For he has no need of our service, but we have need of his dominion, that he may till and keep us; and therefore he alone is truly Lord, because we serve not for his profit but for our own profit and salvation. For if he had need of us, by that very fact he would not be the true Lord, since through us a need of his would be helped, under which he himself would also be a servant. Rightly that man in the Psalm says: *I said to the Lord, you are my God; for you have no need of my goods*[^36]. Nor is what we said to be so understood—that we serve him for our own profit and salvation—as though we expect from him something other than himself, who is our highest profit and salvation. For so we love him freely, according to that saying: *but for me it is good to cleave to God*[^37].

## That man can do nothing good without God.

**12.** 25. For man is not such a thing that, once made, with him who made him forsaking him, he could do anything well as though of himself; rather, his whole good action is to turn to him by whom he was made, and to be made by him always just, devout, wise, and blessed—not to be made and then withdraw, as one is healed by a physician of the body and then departs; for the physician of the body was a workman from without, serving the nature working within under God, who works all health by that twofold work of providence of which we spoke above. Man, therefore, ought not so to turn to God that, once made just by him, he should depart, but so that he may always be made by him. For by this very fact, when he does not depart from him, by his presence he is justified, and illuminated, and made blessed, with God tilling and keeping him, while he exercises dominion over one obedient and subject.

## How God tills man.

**12.** 26. For it is not, as we were saying—just as man tills the earth that it may be tilled and fruitful, who, when he has tilled it, departs, leaving it either plowed, or sown, or watered, or whatever else, the work that has been done remaining when the workman has departed—it is not thus that God tills the just man, that is, by justifying him, so that, if he should depart, what he made remains in the one departing; but rather, just as the air, with light present, has not been made bright but is being made bright—because if it had been made and were not being made, it would remain bright even in the absence of light—so man, with God present to him, is illuminated, but in his absence is immediately darkened; from whom one departs not by intervals of place, but by the turning away of the will.

## That man is made good through God, immutably good.

**12.** 27. Let him, therefore, till and keep man as good, who is immutably good. We ought always to be made by him and always perfected, clinging to him and remaining in that turning which is toward him, of whom it is said: *but for me it is good to cleave to God*[^38]; and to whom it is said: *I will keep my strength to you*[^39]. *For we are his handiwork*[^40], not only to this end, that we be men, but to this also, that we be good. For the Apostle too, when he commended to the faithful, turned from impiety, the grace by which we have been saved, says: *for by grace you have been saved through faith; and this not of yourselves, but it is the gift of God; not of works, lest perhaps anyone be lifted up. For we are his handiwork, created in Christ Jesus for good works which God prepared, that we should walk in them*[^41]. And elsewhere, when he had said, *with fear and trembling work out your own salvation,* lest they should think it to be attributed to themselves, as though they made themselves just and good, he immediately added: *for it is God who works in you*[^42]. *The Lord God,* therefore, *took the man whom he had made, and put him in paradise to till it,* that is, to work in him, *and to keep it,* that is, to keep him.

## Why man was forbidden the tree of the knowledge of good and evil.

**13.** 28. *And the Lord God commanded Adam, saying: Of every tree that is in paradise you shall eat for food; but of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil, you shall not eat of it. For on the day you eat of it, you shall die the death*[^43]. If that tree, from which God forbade man, were something evil, it would seem poisonous unto death by the very nature of that evil. But because he who made all things very good[^44] had planted in paradise all good trees, and there was no nature of evil there—because nowhere is there any nature of evil (which we shall discuss more diligently, if the Lord wills, when we begin to speak of that serpent)—man was forbidden from that tree, which was not evil, so that the very keeping of the precept might in itself be good for him, and the transgression evil.

## How great a good obedience is, how great an evil disobedience.

**13.** 29. Nor could it be better and more diligently shown how great an evil disobedience alone is, since man was made guilty of iniquity for this reason, that he touched, against the prohibition, a thing which, had he touched it without being forbidden, he would surely not have sinned. For one who says, for example, "Do not touch this herb," if perhaps it is poisonous and forewarns of death if he touches it, death will indeed follow the despiser of the precept; but even if no one had forbidden it, and he had touched it, he would nonetheless surely die. For that thing would not be suited to his health and life, whether he were forbidden it or not forbidden it.

Likewise, when someone forbids a thing to be touched which would harm not the one touching it, but the one who forbade it—as if someone had laid his hand on another's money, forbidden by him whose money it was—it would for this reason be a sin for the one forbidden, because it could be damaging to the one forbidding. But when that is touched which would neither harm the one touching it if it were not forbidden, nor anyone else whenever it were touched, why was it forbidden, except that the good of obedience in itself, and the evil of disobedience in itself, might be shown?

## That it is sin to set man's will before God's will.

**13.** 30. In short, nothing else was sought by the one sinning than not to be under the dominion of God, when that was committed in which, to avoid committing it, the bidding of the one in dominion alone ought to have been heeded. And if that alone were heeded, what else would be heeded but the will of God? What else would be loved but the will of God? What else would be set before the human will but the will of God? Why the Lord commanded, let him see to that; what he commanded must be done by the servant, and then perhaps why he commanded it may be seen by the one who deserves it. But yet—not to inquire any longer into the cause of this command—if this very thing is a great profit to man, that he serves God, then by commanding God makes useful whatever he wills to command, concerning whom there is no fear that he could command what is useless.

## That man, by despising God's precept, experienced the evil of disobedience.

**14.** 31. Nor can it come about that one's own will does not fall upon man with the great weight of ruin, if, exalting it, he sets it before the will of a superior. This man experienced, despising the precept of God; and by this experience he learned what difference there is between good and evil—namely, the good of obedience, but the evil of disobedience, that is, of pride and contumacy, of a perverse imitation of God and a harmful liberty. But the tree by which this could happen received its name, as was already said above, from the very thing. For we would not sense evil except by experience, because there would be none, if we had not done it. For evil is no nature, but the loss of good has received this name.

For the immutable good is God; but man, as far as concerns his nature, in which God founded him, is indeed good, but not immutable as God is. Now a mutable good, which comes after the immutable good, becomes a better good when it has clung to the immutable good, by loving and serving with a rational and proper will. For this very reason this too is a nature of great good, because it received even this, that it can cling to the nature of the highest good. But if it is unwilling, it deprives itself of the good, and this is an evil for it, whence by the justice of God torment also follows. For what is so unjust as that it should be well with the deserter of good? Nor can it in any way come about that it be so; but sometimes the evil of the higher good lost is not felt, when the lower good that was loved is possessed. But it is divine justice that he who by his will lost what he ought to have loved should lose with grief what he loved, while the creator of natures is everywhere praised. For it is still a good that grieves over the good lost; for unless some good had remained in the nature, there would be no grief over the good lost in the punishment.

## That evil is known through the prudence of good, and likewise good is known through the experience of evil.

**14.** 32. But he to whom the good pleases without the experience of evil—that is, who, before he feels the loss of the good, chooses to hold it lest he lose it—is to be proclaimed above all men. But unless this were a matter of some singular praise, it would not be attributed to that child who, made of the race of Israel, Emmanuel, God with us[^45], reconciled us to God, the man who is mediator of men and of God[^46], the Word with God, flesh with us[^47], the Word made flesh between God and us. For of him the prophet says: *before the child knows good or evil, he will despise wickedness, that he may choose the good*[^48].

How is it that he despises or chooses what he does not know, except because these two things are known in one way through the prudence of good, in another through the experience of evil? Through the prudence of good, evil is known, even if it is not felt. For the good is held, lest the loss of it be felt as evil. Likewise, through the experience of evil, good is known; since one for whom it has gone badly through the good lost feels what he has lost. Before the child knew, then, by experience, either the good he might lack or the evil he might feel through the loss of good, he despised evil that he might choose good—that is, he was unwilling to lose what he had, lest by losing it he should feel what he ought not to lose. A singular example of obedience; for he came not to do his own will, but the will of him by whom he was sent[^49]—not like that man who chose to do his own will, not the will of him by whom he was made. Rightly, then, *as by the disobedience of one many were made sinners, so by the obedience of one many are made just*[^50]; because *as in Adam all die, so also in Christ all will be made alive*[^51].

## Why the tree of the knowledge of good and evil was so named.

**15.** 33. But some have in vain been keenly dull, when they ask how the tree could have been called the tree of the discernment of good and evil before man had transgressed the precept in it and by the very experience discerned what difference there was between the good he lost and the evil he committed. For the tree received such a name so that, by not being touched according to the prohibition, what would be felt if it were touched against the prohibition might be guarded against. For it was not because they ate of it against the precept that that tree became the tree of the discernment of good and evil; rather, surely, even if they had been obedient and had taken nothing from it against the precept, it would rightly be called by that which would befall them there if they had taken it. Just as, if it were called the tree of satiety, because men could be filled from it—surely, if no one had approached it, would that name on that account be unfitting? Since, when they approached and were filled, then they would prove how rightly that tree was so called.

## That man, before the experience of evil, could understand what evil was.

**16.** 34. And how, they say, would man understand what was being called to him "the tree of the discernment of good and evil," when he did not at all know what evil itself was? Those who think so pay too little attention to how very many unknown things are understood from contrary known things, so that even the words of things that do not exist, when interposed in speech, leave no hearer in the dark. For that which altogether does not exist is called "nothing"; and there is no one who hears and speaks Latin who does not understand these two syllables. From where, except when the sense beholds that which is, and by its privation recognizes what also is not? So too, when "the empty" is spoken of, by beholding the fullness of a body, by its privation as though contrary, we understand what "the empty" means; just as, by the sense of hearing, we judge not only of sounds but also of silence.

So, from the life that was in man, he could be on guard against its contrary—that is, the privation of life which is called death; and the very cause by which he would lose what he loved—that is, any deed of his by which it would come about that he lost life, by whatever syllables it was named, as in Latin when "sin" or "evil" is spoken—he would understand as a sign of that which he discerned in his mind. For how do we understand when "resurrection" is spoken of, which we have never experienced? Is it not because we sense what it is to live, and call the privation of that thing death, whence we call the return to that which we sense "resurrection"? And if it is called by some other name in any language whatever, a sign is surely intimated to the mind in the voice of those speaking, so that, when it sounds, it may recognize what it would think even without the sign.

For it is a wonder how nature shuns the loss of the things it has, even a loss not experienced. For who taught the beasts to shun death, except the sense of life? Who taught a little child to cling to the one carrying him, if he threatened to drop him from a height? This begins from a certain time, but nonetheless before he has experienced anything of this kind.

## A difficulty: how the first parents could understand God speaking.

**16.** 35. So, then, to those first human beings life was already sweet, which they surely shunned losing; and they could understand God signifying this very thing by whatever modes or sounds; nor could sin be persuaded to them otherwise than if it were first persuaded to them that by that deed they would not die—that is, that they would not lose what they had and rejoiced to have; of which we must speak in its own place.

Let those, then, who are moved by this, consider how they could understand God naming or threatening things not experienced; and let them see that we recognize the names of all things not experienced, without any surge of doubt, only from contraries we already know, if they are of privations, or from things similar, if they are of forms. Unless perhaps someone is moved by how they could speak, or understand one speaking, who had not learned, either by growing up among speakers or by some instruction; as though it were a great thing for God to teach those to speak whom he had so made that they could learn this even from men, if there were any from whom to learn.

## It is asked whether the precept about that tree was given to Eve as well.

**17.** 36. It is rightly asked whether God gave this precept to the man only, or to the woman as well. But it has not yet been narrated how the woman was made. Or had she perhaps already been made? But how this was done, which had been done earlier, was narrated later by way of recapitulation. For the words of Scripture stand thus: *and the Lord God commanded Adam, saying*; he did not say, he commanded them; then it follows: *of every tree that is in paradise you shall eat for food*; he did not say, you (plural) shall eat. Then he adds: *but of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil, you shall not eat of it*[^52]: now here he speaks as if to both, in the plural, and ends the precept in the plural, saying: *for on the day you eat of it, you shall die the death*[^53]. Or did he, knowing that he was going to make the woman for him, so command in a most orderly way that the precept of the Lord might reach the woman through the man? This discipline the Apostle keeps in the Church, saying: *but if they wish to learn anything, let them ask their husbands at home*[^54].

## How God spoke to man.

**18.** 37. Likewise it can be asked how God now spoke to the man whom he had made, now certainly endowed with sense and mind, so that he was able to hear and understand one speaking. For he could not otherwise receive the precept, by transgressing which he would be guilty, unless he understood what he received. How, then, did God speak to him? Within, in the mind, according to the understanding—that is, so that he wisely understood the will and precept of God without any corporeal sounds or likenesses of corporeal things? But I do not think God spoke to the first man in this way. For Scripture narrates such things that we should rather believe that God spoke to man in paradise in the way that he also afterward spoke to the patriarchs, as to Abraham, as to Moses—that is, in some corporeal form. For this is why they heard his voice walking in paradise toward evening, and hid themselves[^55].

## On the twofold working of God in creatures.

**19.** 38. A great topic, then, not to be passed over, is set before us, that we may behold, as much as we can and as much as he himself deigns to help and grant, the twofold work of divine providence, which above, when we were speaking of agriculture, we touched on in a passing way, so that from there the reader's mind might already begin to accustom itself to consider this, which helps very much, lest we think anything unworthy of the very substance of God.

We say, then, that the highest, true, one, and only God, Father and Son and Holy Spirit—that is, God and his Word and the Spirit of both, the Trinity itself, neither confused nor separated—is God *who alone has immortality, and dwells in unapproachable light, whom no man has seen, nor can see*[^56], and is contained by no space of places, whether finite or infinite, nor varied by any volume of times, whether finite or infinite. For there is not in his substance, by which he is God, anything that is less in a part than in the whole, as must be the case with things that move in places about an unmoved pivot, as the whole palm moves from the joint; nor was there in his substance anything that now is not, or will be that is not yet, as in natures that can undergo the mutability of time.

## Corporeal creation is mutable in place and time, spiritual creation only in time, God in no way mutable.

**20.** 39. He, then, living in immutable eternity, created all things at once, out of which times would run and places be filled, and the ages be unrolled by the temporal and local motions of things. Among these things he established some spiritual, some corporeal, forming a matter which neither another nor no one, but he alone, established unformed and formable, so that it would precede its own formation not in time but in origin. But he set the spiritual creation before the corporeal, because the spiritual could be changed only through times, but the corporeal through times and places. For example, the mind is moved through time, either by remembering what it had forgotten, or by learning what it did not know, or by willing what it did not will; but the body is moved through places, either from earth to heaven, or from heaven to earth, or from east to west, or in some other similar way. Now everything that is moved through place cannot but be moved through time at the same time; but not everything that is moved through time must also be moved through place.

As, therefore, the substance that is moved through time and place is preceded by the substance that is moved only through time, so that substance is preceded by the one that is moved neither through place nor through time. And by this, just as the created spirit, itself moved only through time, moves the body through time and place, so the creator Spirit, moved neither through time nor through place, moves the created spirit through time: but the created spirit moves itself through time, and the body through time and place; whereas the creator Spirit moves himself without time and place, moves the created spirit through time without place, moves the body through time and place.

## How God, unmoved, moves creatures, grasped by the example of the soul.

**21.** 40. Therefore whoever tries to understand how the eternal—truly eternal and truly immortal and immutable God—himself moved neither through time nor through place, moves his creation temporally and locally, I do not think he can attain it unless he first understands how the soul, that is, the created spirit, moved not through place but only through time, moves the body through time and place. For if he cannot yet grasp what is done within himself, how much less that which is above?

## That in moving the body's members the soul is by no means moved through spaces.

**21.** 41. The soul, indeed, affected by the habit of the carnal senses, thinks that it too is moved together with the body through place, while it moves the body through place. But if it could diligently inspect, as it were, the pivots of its body's members—how they are arranged joint by joint, from which the beginnings of motions exert themselves—it would find that the things moved through spaces of places are moved only by those that are fixed in place. For the finger alone is not moved unless the hand is fixed, from whose joint, as from an unmoved pivot, it is moved. So the whole palm is moved from the joint of the elbow, the elbow from the joint of the shoulder, the shoulder from the shoulder blade, while the pivots on which the motion rests surely stand; that which is moved goes through space of place.

So in the sole of the foot there is a joint at the ankle, which, standing, it is moved by; so the leg's at the knee, and the whole foot's at the hip; and there is no motion of a member at all that the will moves, except from some joint-pivot, which the bidding of the same will first fixes, so that from that which is not moved by space of place, that which is moved may be enabled to act. Finally, even in walking the foot is not raised unless another, fixed, bears the whole body, until that one which has been moved rests, with the joint of its pivot unmoved, from the place from which it is borne to the place to which it is borne.

## The same explained more fully.

**21.** 42. Furthermore, if in the body the will moves no member through place except from that joint of the member which it does not move—since both that part of the body which is moved and that by which, fixed, it is enabled to be moved have their corporeal quantities by which they occupy the spaces of their places—how much more does the bidding of the soul itself, which the members serve so that what it pleases is fixed, from which what is to be moved may rest, since the soul is not a corporeal nature, nor fills a body by local space as water fills either a skin or a sponge, but by marvelous modes is mingled, by that very incorporeal bidding, in giving life to the body, by which it also commands the body, by a kind of intention, not by mass: how much more, I say, is the bidding of its will itself not moved through place, so as to move the body through place, when it moves the whole through parts, and moves none through place except through those which it does not move through place?

## How God moves creatures, how he moves souls.

**22.** 43. But if this is difficult to understand, let both be believed: both that the spiritual creation, not moved through place, moves the body through place, and that God, not moved through time, moves the spiritual creation through time. But if someone is unwilling to believe this about the soul—which indeed he would without doubt not only believe but even understand, if he could think it, as it is, incorporeal (for to whom would it not easily occur that what is not extended through spaces of place is not moved through place? but whatever is extended through spaces of place is a body; and by this it follows that the soul is not thought to be moved through place, if it is believed not to be a body)—but, as I had begun to say, if someone is unwilling to believe this about the soul, he is not to be pressed too hard. But unless he believes that the substance of God is moved neither through time nor through place, he does not yet believe it perfectly immutable.

## By what wisdom God administers all things.

**23.** 44. But because that nature of the Trinity is altogether immutable, and on this account so eternal that nothing can be coeternal with it, it, with itself and in itself without any time and place, nevertheless moves through time and place the creation subject to it, creating natures by goodness, ordering wills by power: so that among natures there is none that is not from him; but among wills there is none, good, that he does not profit by, none, evil, that he cannot use well. But because he did not give freedom of will to all natures, while those to which he gave it are more powerful and superior, those natures that do not have will must necessarily be subject to those that have it, and this by the ordination of the Creator, who never so punishes an evil will as to destroy the dignity of nature. Since, then, every body and every irrational soul does not have freedom of will, these are subject to those natures that are endowed with freedom of will; and not all to all, but as the justice of the Creator has distributed.

Therefore the providence of God, ruling and administering the whole creation—both natures and wills, natures that they may be, but wills so that the good are not unfruitful nor the evil unpunished—subjects first all things to itself, then the corporeal creation to the spiritual, the irrational to the rational, the earthly to the heavenly, the female to the male, the less strong to the stronger, the more needy to the more abundant. But among wills, the good ones to himself, the rest to those serving him; so that the evil will suffers what the good will, by God's bidding, has done, whether through itself or through an evil will, at least in those things which are by nature subject even to evil wills—that is, in bodies. For in themselves evil wills have their own interior punishment, that very iniquity of theirs.

## What creatures are subject to the holy angels.

**24.** 45. And by this, to the sublime angels, who submissively enjoy God and blessedly serve God, every corporeal nature is subject, every irrational life, every will whether weak or depraved; so that they may do concerning the subject things, or with the subject things, what the order of nature demands in all things, at the bidding of him to whom all things are subject. Accordingly they see in him the immutable truth, and according to it direct their wills. They become, then, partakers of his eternity, truth, and will always, without time and place. But they are moved by his command even temporally, while he is not moved temporally. Nor in such a way that they spring back or flow away from his contemplation; but at once they both contemplate him without place and time, and accomplish his commands in lower things, moving themselves through time, but the body through time and place, as much as suits their action. And therefore God, by the twofold work of his providence, presides over his whole creation: over natures, that they may come to be; but over wills, that without his bidding or permission they may do nothing.

## How corporeal creatures are administered by God.

**25.** 46. The nature of the corporeal universe, then, is not helped corporeally from without. For there is no body outside it; otherwise it is not the universe. But within it is helped incorporeally, with God bringing it about that it is a nature at all; *for from him and through him and in him are all things*[^57]. But the parts of the same universe are both helped incorporeally from within, or rather come to be, that they may be natures; and corporeally from without, that they may be in a better state—as by foods, agriculture, medicine, and whatever is also done for adornment, so that they may be not only safe and more fruitful, but even more comely.

## How spiritual creatures are administered by God.

**25.** 47. But the created spiritual nature, if it is perfect and blessed, as that of the holy angels is, as far as concerns itself, that it may be and may be wise, is helped only from within, incorporeally. For God speaks to it within in a marvelous and ineffable way—not through writing affixed to corporeal instruments, nor through voices sounding to corporeal ears, nor through likenesses of bodies, such as come to be imaginatively in the spirit, as in dreams or in some excess of spirit, which in Greek is called "ecstasy," and we already use that word for the Latin: because this kind of vision too, although it comes to be more inwardly than the things announced to the mind through the senses of the body, yet, because it is similar to them, so that when it occurs it can either not at all be distinguished from them, or only scarcely and most rarely, and because it is more outward than that which the rational and intellectual mind beholds in the immutable truth itself, and by that light judges of all these things—I judge it to be reckoned among those things that come to be from without.

The spiritual and intellectual creation, then, perfect and blessed, such as that of the angels, as I said, as far as concerns itself, that it may be and may be wise and blessed, is helped only from within by the eternity, truth, and love of the Creator. But if it is to be said to be helped from without, it is perhaps helped by this alone: that they see one another, and rejoice in their fellowship in God, and that, all the creatures being beheld in them too, it everywhere gives thanks and praises the Creator. But as concerns the action of the angelic creation, through which the providence of God provides for the kinds of all things, and especially for the human, that itself helps from without, both through those visions which are similar to corporeal things, and through bodies themselves which lie under the angelic power.

## That all things are governed by the immutable God.

**26.** 48. Since these things are so—since God, almighty and all-holding, by immutable eternity, truth, and will always the same, moved neither through time nor through place, moves the spiritual creation through time, and also moves the corporeal creation through time and place; so that by this motion the natures which he established from within he also administers from without, and through the wills subject to him, which he moves through time, and through bodies subject to him and to those wills, which he moves through time and place, at that time and place whose reason in God himself is life without time and place.

When, therefore, God does some such thing, we ought not to suppose his substance, by which he is God, to be mutable by times and places, or movable through times and places, but to recognize these things in the work of divine providence—not in that work by which he creates natures, but in that by which he administers from without the natures created within—while he himself is, by no interval or space of places, by immutable and excelling power, both interior to every thing, because in him are all things, and exterior to every thing, because he himself is above all things. Likewise, by no interval or space of times, by immutable eternity, he is both more ancient than all things, because he himself is before all things, and more new than all things, because the same he is after all things.

## How God speaks.

**27.** 49. Therefore, when we hear Scripture saying: *and the Lord God commanded Adam, saying: Of every tree that is in paradise you shall eat for food; but of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil, you shall not eat of it. For on the day you eat of it, you shall die the death*[^58]; if we ask the mode, how God spoke these things, the mode itself indeed cannot properly be comprehended by us; yet we ought most certainly to hold that God either speaks through his substance, or through a creation subject to him; but that through his substance he speaks only to create all natures, and to spiritual and intellectual ones not only to create but also to illuminate them, when they can now grasp his speech, such as it is in his Word, which *was in the beginning with God, and the Word was God, through whom all things were made*[^59]. But to those who cannot grasp it, when God speaks, he speaks only through a creation, either spiritual only, whether in dreams or in ecstasy in the likeness of corporeal things, or even through the corporeal creation itself, when to the senses of the body either some form appears or voices sound.

## How God spoke to Adam.

**27.** 50. If, then, Adam was such that he could grasp that speech of God which God offers to the angelic minds through his substance, it is not to be doubted that he moved his mind through time in a marvelous and ineffable way—not himself moved through time—and impressed upon him the useful and wholesome precept of truth, and showed him, in that very ineffable truth, what punishment would be owed to the transgressor: just as all good precepts are heard or seen in that immutable Wisdom which transfers itself into holy souls[^60] from some time, though there is no motion of it in time.

But if Adam was just only in that mode in which he still needed the authority of another, holier and wiser, creation, through which he might come to know the will and command of God—as the prophets do for us, as the angels do for them—why should we doubt that God spoke to him through some such creation, by such signs of voices as he could understand? For as for what was afterward written, that, when they had sinned, they heard the voice of the Lord God walking in paradise, because it was done not through the very substance of God, but through a creation subject to him, no one who is wise in the Catholic faith doubts in any way.

For I wished to discuss this matter somewhat more broadly for this reason: that some heretics think the substance of the Son of God to be by itself visible, with no body assumed, and therefore suppose that, before he received a body from the Virgin, he himself was seen by the patriarchs, as though it were said of God the Father alone: *whom no man has seen, nor can see*[^61]; because the Son was seen, even through his own substance, before he received the form of a servant[^62]: which impiety is to be driven far from Catholic minds.

But of this we shall discuss more fully elsewhere, if it please the Lord; now, this volume being ended, what follows—how the woman was created from the side of her husband—is to be hoped for in what comes next.
