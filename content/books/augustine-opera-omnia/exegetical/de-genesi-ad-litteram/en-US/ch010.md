# Book IX

## The text of Genesis 2:18–24 treated in this book.

**1.** 1. *And the Lord God said: It is not good for man to be alone; let us make him a helper like himself. And God further formed from the earth all the beasts of the field and all the birds of the heaven, and brought them to Adam, that he might see what he would call them. And whatever Adam called every living soul, that is its name. And Adam gave names to all the cattle, and to all the birds of the heaven, and to all the beasts of the field. But for Adam himself there was not found a helper like him. And God sent an ecstasy upon Adam, and he fell asleep. And he took one of his ribs, and filled up flesh in its place; and the Lord God built the rib which he took from Adam into a woman, and brought her to Adam. And Adam said: This now is bone of my bones, and flesh of my flesh; she shall be called woman, because she was taken from her husband. And for this reason a man will leave father and mother, and will be joined to his wife; and the two will be in one flesh*[^1].

If the things considered and written in the foregoing books help the reader at all, we ought not to linger longer on this, that *God further formed from the earth all the beasts of the field and all the birds of the heaven*: for why it was said *further,* that is, on account of the first establishment of creatures completed in six days, in which all things were at once both perfected causally and begun, so that thereafter the causes might be brought to their effects, we have already intimated as much as we could in what precedes. And if anyone thinks this is to be unraveled otherwise, let him only diligently attend to all those things which we attended to in order to think this; and if he can elicit a more probable opinion from them, not only ought we not to resist, but we ought even to rejoice.

## Why it was said: And God formed, etc.

**1.** 2. But if anyone is moved that he did not say, "God further formed from the earth all the beasts of the field, and from the waters all the birds of the heaven," but as though he formed both kinds from the earth: *and God,* he says, *further formed from the earth all the beasts of the field, and all the birds of the heaven*[^2]; let him see that it is to be understood in two ways: either that he was now silent about whence he formed the birds of the heaven, because even left unsaid it could occur to one that not both, but only the beasts of the field, were taken to have been formed by God from the earth, so that, even with Scripture silent, we understand whence he formed the birds of the heaven, as those who know that in the first establishment of the causal reasons they were produced from the waters; or that the earth was so named in a universal sense, together with the waters, as it is named in that Psalm where, the praises of the heavenly things being ended, the speech is turned to the earth, and it is said: *praise the Lord from the earth, you dragons and all deeps*[^3], and the rest, nor afterward is it said, "praise the Lord from the waters." For there are all the deeps, which nevertheless praise the Lord from the earth; there too the creeping things and winged birds, which nonetheless praise the Lord from the earth. According to this universal naming of the earth, according to which it is also said of the whole world, "God who made heaven and earth," whatever things are created, whether from the dry land or from the waters, are truly understood as created from the earth.

## Again it is asked in what ways God spoke, whether by temporal voices and syllables.

**2.** 3. Now let us see how we are to take what God said: *it is not good for man to be alone; let us make him a helper like himself*[^4]: whether God said this temporally, with voices and syllables uttered; or whether the very reason was recorded which was in the Word of God principally, that the woman should be made thus—which reason Scripture took up even then, when it said: *and God said: Let there be*[^5] this or that, when all things were first established. Or did God perhaps say this in the mind of the man himself, as he speaks to certain of his servants in his servants themselves? Of which kind of servants of his was also that man who said in the Psalm: *I will hear what the Lord God speaks in me*[^6]. Or was some revelation about this matter made to the man himself in the man himself through an angel, in likenesses of corporeal voices, although Scripture was silent whether in dreams or in ecstasy—for these things are wont to come about so—or in some other way, as it is revealed to the prophets, whence is that: *and the angel who spoke in me said to me*[^7]; or did the voice itself sound through a corporeal creation, as from the cloud: *this is my Son*[^8].

Which of all these took place, then, we cannot clearly comprehend; nevertheless let us most certainly hold both that God said this, and that, if he said it by a corporeal voice or by a temporally expressed likeness of a body, he said it not through his own substance, but through some creation subject to his command, as we treated in the preceding book.

## How God offers visions to man.

**2.** 4. For God was seen even afterward by holy men—at one time with a head white like wool, at another with the lower part of the body like fine brass[^9], at another in one way and another in another; yet that he offered those visions to men not through the substance by which he himself is, but through things subject to him which he created, and through likenesses of corporeal forms and voices showed and said what he willed, is most certain to those who either faithfully believe, or even excellently understand, that the substance of the Trinity is immutably eternal, is moved neither through time nor through place, and moves through time and place. Let us not, then, now ask how he said this, but rather understand what God said. For that a helper like himself was to be made for man, the eternal truth itself holds, through which all things were created; and in that truth he hears it who is able in it to recognize what was created and why.

## The woman was made as a helper for the sake of offspring.

**3.** 5. But if it is asked for what purpose this helper had to be made, nothing else probably occurs than for the sake of procreating children, just as the earth is a helper to the seed, so that a sprout is born from both: for this too had been said in the first establishment of things: *male and female he made them, and God blessed them, saying: Increase and multiply, and fill the earth, and have dominion over it*[^10]. This reason of the establishment and conjunction of male and female, and the blessing, did not fail even after the sin and punishment of man. For it is according to it that the earth is now full of men having dominion over it.

## What marriage in paradise would have been, if the first parents had not sinned.

**3.** 6. For although they are recorded to have come together and begotten offspring only after being sent out of paradise, yet I do not see what could have prevented their having honorable marriage and an undefiled bed even in paradise[^11]: God granting this to those living faithfully and justly, and serving him obediently and holily, so that without any restless ardor of desire, without any labor and pain of giving birth, offspring would be begotten from their seed; not so that children would succeed dying parents, but so that, with those who had begotten them remaining in some state of form, and taking corporeal vigor from the tree of life which was planted there, those who were begotten too would be brought to the same state, until, a fixed number being filled, if all lived justly and obediently, then that change would come about, so that the animal bodies, without any death, changed into another quality, because they would serve at every bidding the spirit ruling them, and would live by the life-giving spirit alone, without any corporeal supports of food, would be called spiritual. This could have happened, if the transgression of the precept had not deserved the punishment of death.

## The state of nature before sin.

**3.** 7. For those who do not believe this could have happened attend to nothing but the custom of nature now running thus after human sin and punishment; but we ought not to be among the kind who believe nothing but what they have been accustomed to see. For who would doubt that what we said could have been granted to a man living obediently and devoutly, who does not doubt that to the garments of the Israelites a certain state in their kind was imparted, so that for forty years they suffered no harm of age[^12]?

## Why the first parents did not come together in paradise.

**4.** 8. Why, then, did they not come together until they had gone out of paradise? It can be quickly answered: because, soon after the woman was created, before they came together, that transgression took place, for the desert of which, destined to death, they also went out of the place of that happiness. For Scripture did not express the time, how much there was between their being made and Cain being born from them. It can also be said that God had not yet commanded that they come together. For why should not the divine authority be awaited for this matter, where no concupiscence pressed like a goad of disobedient flesh? But God had not commanded this, because according to his foreknowledge he was disposing all things, in which he without doubt foreknew their fall too, whence the human race was now to be propagated as mortal.

## For what purpose the woman was made.

**5.** 9. Or if the woman was not made for the man as a helper for begetting children, then for what helping was she made? If as one to till the earth with him, there was not yet labor that he should need help, and if there were need, a male would be a better helper. This can be said also of comfort, if perhaps solitude was tedious. For how much more fittingly would two friends dwell together to live and converse than a man and a woman? But if it was fitting that they live together, one commanding, the other obeying, lest contrary wills disturb the peace of those dwelling together, neither for keeping this would order have been lacking, by which one would be first, the other later—especially if the later were created from the earlier, as the woman was created. Or will anyone say that God could have made from the man's rib only a female, not also a male, if he had wished this? Therefore I do not find for what helping the woman was made for the man, if the cause of bearing is withdrawn.

## What the succession of children would have been like, if Adam had not sinned.

**6.** 10. For if parents had to yield this life to their children, so that in this way the whole human race might be filled to a fixed number through departures and successions, men could also, their children begotten and the justice of human duty fulfilled, be transferred hence to better things, not through death, but through some change—either that highest one by which, their bodies received, the saints will become like the angels in heaven; or, if that is not to be given except to all together at the end of the age, then some lower one than that will be: which nevertheless would have a better state than either this body has, or even those that were first made—the man's from the mire of the earth, the woman's from the man's flesh.

## The first parents could have been transferred from the earth like Elijah.

**6.** 11. For we must not suppose that Elijah is either already such as the saints will be when, the day's work done, they will all alike receive the denarius; or such as the men are who have not yet emigrated from this life, from which he, however, migrated not by death but by translation. He has, then, already something better than he could have in this life; though he does not yet have what, from this life rightly lived, he will have in the end: for they provided better things for us, that they should not be made perfect without us[^13].

Or if anyone thinks that Elijah could not have merited this, if he had taken a wife and procreated children (for he is believed not to have had them, because Scripture does not say so, although it has also said nothing of his celibacy)—what will he answer about Enoch, who, having begotten children, pleasing God, was not put to death, but translated[^14]? Why, then, could not Adam and Eve too, if living justly they had chastely procreated children, yield to their successors by translation, not by death? For if Enoch and Elijah, having died in Adam and bearing the propagation of death in their flesh, are believed even to be going to return to this life so as to pay that debt, and, what has been so long deferred, to die, yet are now in another life, where, before the resurrection of the flesh, before the animal body is changed into a spiritual one, they fail neither by disease nor by old age: how much more justly and probably would it be granted to those first men, living without any sin of their own or of their parents, that, their children begotten, they should yield into some better state, whence, the age being ended, with all the posterity of the saints, they would be changed into an angelic form, not through the death of the flesh, but through the power of God, much more happily?

## Whence virginity and marriage are praiseworthy: the threefold good of matrimony.

**7.** 12. I do not, then, see for what other helping the woman was made for the man, if the cause of generating is withdrawn—though why it should be withdrawn I do not know. For whence does faithful and devout virginity have great merit and merit of great honor before God, except because in this present time of abstaining from embrace, since from all nations a most abundant supply is at hand for filling the number of the saints, the lust of perceiving sordid pleasure does not claim for itself what the necessity of supplying offspring no longer requires? Finally, the weakness of both sexes, inclining toward the ruin of disgrace, is rightly received by the honorableness of marriage, so that what could be a duty for the healthy may be a remedy for the sick.

For it is not because incontinence is an evil that marriage, by which the incontinent are joined, is therefore not a good; rather, this good is not blameworthy on account of that evil, but that evil is venial on account of this good; since that which marriage has as good, and by which marriage is good, can never be a sin. But this is threefold: faithfulness, offspring, sacrament. In faithfulness it is observed that there be no lying with another, man or woman, apart from the marriage bond; in offspring, that it be lovingly received, kindly nourished, religiously brought up; but in the sacrament, that the marriage not be separated, and that one dismissed, man or woman, not be joined to another even for the sake of offspring. This is, as it were, the rule of marriage, by which either the fruitfulness of nature is adorned, or the depravity of incontinence is ruled.

Therefore, because we discussed this sufficiently in the book *On the Good of Marriage* which we recently published, where we also distinguished widowed continence and virginal excellence according to the dignity of their grades, our pen need not be occupied here longer.

## That it is difficult for one perversely shunning vices not to run ruinously into their contraries.

**8.** 13. For now we are asking for what helping the woman was made for the man, if it was not permitted to them to be joined to each other in paradise for begetting children. For those who hold this perhaps think all intercourse to be sin. For it is difficult, while men perversely shun vices, not to run ruinously into their contraries. For just as one shuddering at avarice becomes lavish; or one shuddering at extravagance becomes avaricious; or one becomes restless whose sloth you have reproved; or sluggish, one whose restlessness you have reproved; or one who, being reproved, has begun to hate his own boldness, flees to timidity; or one who tries not to be timid, as though a chain were broken, becomes rash—while they measure faults not by reason but by opinion: so, while men do not know what is condemned in adulteries and fornications by divine law, they detest even conjugal intercourse for the sake of procreation.

## That the first parents could have procreated before sin.

**9.** 14. Those who do not do this, but yet think that the fruitfulness of the flesh was given divinely for the succession of mortality, themselves too think that those first men could not have come together unless, destined to die on account of the sin they had committed, they sought successors by begetting; nor do they attend to the fact that, if successors could rightly be sought for those who would die, much more rightly could companions be sought for those who would live. For when the earth was filled with the human race, rightly would no offspring be sought except such as would succeed the dying; but in order that the earth might be filled by two human beings, how would they fulfill the duty of fellowship, except by begetting?

Or is anyone so blind in mind as not to discern how great an adornment to the earth the human race is, even when by a few it is lived rightly and praiseworthily; and how much the order of the commonwealth avails, restraining even sinners within the bond of a certain earthly peace? For men are not so depraved that even such do not excel the cattle and the birds; the kinds of all of which, adorning this lowest part of the world according to the lot of its place, who would not delight to consider? But who would be so senseless as to think it could have been less adorned, if it were filled with the just not dying?

## The woman was created for begetting children, even if man had not had to die.

**9.** 15. For because the heavenly city of the angels is most numerous, they would not on that account rightly be joined in marriage unless they were to die. For the Lord, foreknowing that this perfect number would also, in the resurrection of the saints, be associated with the angels, says: *in the resurrection they will neither marry nor be given in marriage; for they will not begin to die, but will be equal to the angels of God*[^15]: but here, since the earth was to be filled with men, and it had to be filled from one, for the sake of the closer bond of kinship and the unity to be especially commended, for what else was a female sex sought as a helper like him, except that the womanly nature, like the fruitfulness of the earth, might help the man as he sowed the human race?

## The disease of lust comes from sin.

**10.** 16. Although it is more honorably and better believed that the body of those men, then constituted as animal in paradise, not yet condemned by the law of death, was so that they did not have the appetite for carnal pleasure which these bodies now have, which are already drawn from the propagation of death. For nothing was wrought in them when they ate of the forbidden tree: since God had said, not, "if you eat, you shall die the death," but, *on the day you eat, you shall die the death*[^16]: so that this very thing that day would work in them, which the Apostle laments, saying: *I delight in the law of God according to the inner man; but I see another law in my members, warring against the law of my mind, and taking me captive in the law of sin, which is in my members. Wretched man that I am, who will free me from the body of this death? The grace of God through Jesus Christ our Lord*[^17]. For it would not suffice him to say, "who will free me from this mortal body?" but he says, *from the body of this death.* So too that: *the body indeed,* he says, *is dead because of sin*[^18]; nor does he there say "mortal," but *dead*; although surely also mortal, because it will die.

We must not believe those bodies were thus, but, though animal, not yet spiritual, yet not dead—that is, such as it was necessary for them to die: which was done on the day they touched the tree against the prohibition.

## The bodies of the first parents, if they had not sinned, would have received an angelic form.

**10.** 17. Just as in our own bodies a certain health is spoken of in its own mode, which, if it is so disturbed that a lethal disease now feeds upon the vitals, so that, upon inspection, the physicians declare death to be imminent, the body is surely even then called mortal, but otherwise than when it was healthy, although it is at some time without doubt going to die: so those men, bearing indeed animal bodies, but not going to die unless they sinned, and going to receive an angelic form and a heavenly quality, as soon as they transgressed the precept, in their members, as it were a lethal sickness, death itself was conceived; and it changed that quality by which they so had dominion over the body that they did not say: *I see another law in my members, warring against the law of my mind*[^19]; because, although it was not yet a spiritual but an animal body, yet it was not yet of this death, in which and with which we are born.

For what else have we, I will not say born, but altogether conceived, except begun a certain sickness by which we are necessarily going to die? For it is not so necessary that one die of the very disease who has become dropsical, or consumptive, or leprous, as that one die who has begun to have this body, in which all men *are by nature children of wrath*[^20], because nothing made this except the punishment of sin.

## Before sin it would be sown without ardor and borne without pain.

**10.** 18. Since these things are so, why should we not believe that those men, before sin, could command the genital members for procreating children just as the others, which the soul moves in any work without any trouble, and as though by a tickling of pleasure? For if the almighty Creator, ineffably to be praised, who is great even in his least works, granted to bees that they so accomplish the generation of children as the form of wax and the liquid of honey; why should it seem incredible that he made the bodies of the first men such that, if they had not sinned and immediately conceived a certain disease by which they would die, they would command, by that bidding, the members from which offspring arises, with which the feet are commanded when one walks, so that there would be neither sowing with ardor nor bearing with pain?

But now, by transgressing the precept, they deserved to have in their members the motion of that law which wars against the law of the mind, of the death conceived; which marriage orders, which continence checks and restrains, so that, just as from sin punishment was made, so from punishment merit may be made.

## The matter concerning the female sex and conjugal intercourse is concluded.

**11.** 19. Whoever, then, would doubt that the woman was made for the man, from the man, in that sex, in that form and distinction of members by which females are marked—she who bore Cain and Abel, and all their brothers, from whom all men would be born, among whom she bore also Seth, through whom it came to Abraham[^21] and to the people of Israel, a nation now most well known to all nations, and through Noah's sons all nations—whoever, I say, would doubt this, makes all that we believe totter, and is to be driven far from the minds of the faithful. When, therefore, it is asked for what helping that sex was made for the man, to me, diligently considering all things as far as I am able, nothing occurs except the cause of offspring, that through their stock the earth might be filled; and not procreated in the way that men are now procreated, when the law of sin is in the members warring against the law of the mind, even if by the grace of God it is overcome by virtue: for this is to be believed could not have been except in the body of this death, which body is dead because of sin.

And what is more just than this punishment, that the body—that is, the soul's own servant—should not serve at every bidding, just as the soul itself refused to serve its own Lord; whether God creates both from the parents, body from body, soul from soul, or makes souls in some other way—surely not for an impossible work nor for a small reward: that when the soul, devoutly subject to God, has by grace itself overcome that law of sin which is in the members of the body of this death, which the first man received as a punishment, it may receive a heavenly reward with greater glory, showing how great is the praise of obedience, which by virtue could overcome the punishment of another's disobedience?

## What Adam prefigured by imposing names on the animals.

**12.** 20. But since for what helping the woman was made for the man has been sufficiently inquired into, as I judge, let us now see why it was done that all the beasts of the field and all the birds of the heaven were brought to Adam, that he might impose names on them; and thus a kind of necessity arose of creating a woman for him from his side, since among those animals a helper like him had not been found. For it seems to me to have been done for the sake of some prophetic signification, but nevertheless done, so that, the deed being confirmed, the interpretation of the figure may be left free.

For what is this very thing, that Adam imposed names on the winged and earthly living creatures, but not also on the fishes and all swimming things? For if human languages are consulted, all these are called as men, in speaking, imposed names on them. Not only these things that are in the waters and on the lands, but even the earth itself, and water, and heaven, and the things that are seen in heaven, and the things that are not seen but believed, are called by different names according to the diversity of the languages of the nations.

We have indeed learned that there was at first one language, before the pride of that tower built after the flood divided human society into the diverse sounds of signs[^22]. But whatever that language was, what does it matter to ask? That language Adam certainly then spoke, and in that language, if it still remains to this day, are those articulate words by which the first man imposed names on the earthly and winged animals. Is it then in any way credible that in the same language the names of fishes were instituted not by man, but divinely, which man would afterward learn by God's teaching? But if it had been done so, why it had been done so would without doubt resound with mystical signification. But it is to be believed that, the kinds of fishes being gradually known, names were imposed.

But at that time when the cattle, and the beasts, and the birds were brought to the man, that, gathered to him and distinguished by kinds, he might impose names on them—on which very ones he could impose names gradually, and much sooner than on the fishes, if this had not been done—what was the cause, except a reason of signifying something which would avail for the foretelling of things to come? To which thing the order of this narrative is especially attentive.

## Why the naming of the animals had a prophetic signification.

**12.** 21. Next, did God not know that he had created nothing of the kind among the natures of the animals which could be a helper like the man? Or was it necessary that the man himself too should know this, and hold his wife the more dear, because in all flesh created under heaven and living, as he himself, from this air, he found nothing like her? It would be a wonder if he could not know this except by all being brought to him and inspected. For if he believed God, God could tell him this in the way that he also gave the precept, in the way that he also questioned and judged the one who sinned. But if he did not believe, surely he could not know this either—whether the one whom he did not believe had brought all things to him; or whether perhaps in some more remote parts of the earth he had hidden some things like her which he had not shown. Therefore I think it is not to be doubted that this was done for the sake of some prophetic signification, but nonetheless done.

## The same matter is treated.

**12.** 22. Nor have we undertaken in this work to examine prophetic riddles, but to commend the faith of the things done according to the propriety of history, so that what can seem impossible to the vain and unbelieving, or to be repugnant by a kind of contrary testimony to the very authority of holy Scripture, I may, according to my powers, as much as God helps, by discussing, show to be neither impossible nor contrary.

But that which indeed appears possible, and has no appearance of repugnance, but yet can seem to some as if superfluous, or even foolish, this very thing I may show by discussing—namely, that it was therefore done not as though in the natural or customary order of things done, so that, the most faithful authority of the holy Scriptures being set before our hearts, because it cannot be foolish, it may be believed to be mystical—although its exposition or inquiry we have either already exhibited elsewhere, or defer to another time.

## What the manner in which the woman is narrated to have been made prefigured.

**13.** 23. What, then, does this also mean, that the woman was made from the man's side? But suppose we believe that, for the sake of commending the force of the conjunction itself, it had to be done thus; did the same reason or necessity demand that it be done to him as he slept, and that finally a bone be drawn out, in whose place flesh would be supplied? For could not the flesh itself have been drawn out, so that from it the woman, being the weaker sex, might more fittingly be formed? Or indeed, with so many additions, could God build a rib into a woman, and could he not build flesh or pulp—he who made the man himself from dust? Or if a rib had now to be drawn out, why was another rib not put back in its place? Why too was it not said, "he formed," or "he made," as in all the works above; but, *the Lord God built*, he says, *that rib*[^23], not as a human body, but as a house?

It is therefore not to be doubted that, since these things were done and cannot be foolish, they were done to signify something, with God, foreknowing, mercifully proclaiming, from the very first beginning of the human race, in his own works, the fruit of the age to come; so that, revealed and written down at a fixed time for his servants, whether through the successions of men, or through his Spirit, or by the ministry of angels, they might bear testimony both to things future to be promised and to things fulfilled to be recognized: which will appear more and more in what follows.

## How the animals were brought to Adam.

**14.** 24. Let us see, then—what we have undertaken in this work—not according to the prefiguration of things to come, but according to the not allegorical but proper signification of things done, how these can be taken: *and God,* he says, *further formed from the earth all the beasts of the field, and all the birds of the heaven*[^24]; whence we have already discussed what was seen, and as much as was seen. *And he brought all these to Adam, that he might see what he would call them*[^25]*.* How God brought these to Adam—lest we be wise carnally—what we treated in the preceding book about the twofold work of divine providence ought to help us. For we must not believe this was done in the way that hunters or fowlers track and drive to the nets whatever living creatures they catch; or that some voice of command was made to them from a cloud, in those words which rational souls are wont to hear, understand, and obey.

For the beasts or birds did not receive the power to do this: yet in their own kind they obey God; not by the rational freedom of will, but as he moves all things at the opportune times, himself not moved temporally, through angelic ministries, which grasp in his word what ought to be done and at what time: and while he is not moved temporally, they themselves are moved temporally, so that in the things subject to them they accomplish his commands.

## The account of the images by which we are urged to appetite with the beasts by a common emotion, but distinguished from them by judgment.

**14.** 25. For every living soul—not only the rational, as in men, but also the irrational, as in cattle, and birds, and fishes—is moved by things seen. But the rational soul, by the freedom of the will, either consents to the things seen or does not consent; the irrational, however, does not have this judgment, but, according to its own kind and nature, touched by some thing seen, is impelled. Nor is it in the power of any soul what things seen come to it, whether into the sense of the body or into the spirit itself within; by which things seen the appetite of any living creature is moved.

And thus, when those things seen are ministered from above through the obedience of angels, the command of God reaches not only men, nor only the birds and cattle, but even the things that lie hidden under the waters, as to the sea-creature that swallowed Jonah[^26]; nor only to these larger ones, but even to the little worm; for we read that this too was divinely commanded to gnaw the root of the gourd, under whose shade the prophet had rested[^27].

For if God granted to man, so establishing him, that even while bearing the flesh of sin he can not only subject to his own uses the cattle and beasts of burden, and not only domestic birds, but even freely flying ones, and any savage wild beasts, both catch and tame and marvelously command them by the power of reason, not of body—when, seizing on their appetites and pains, and gradually moderating them by enticing, pressing, and relaxing, he strips them of their wild habit and clothes them, as it were, with human manners.

How much more can the angels do this, who, at the command of God, having beheld in that immutable truth of his which they eternally contemplate, moving themselves through time, and the bodies subject to them through time and place, with marvelous agility are able to effect in every living soul both the things seen by which it is moved and the appetite of carnal need, so that it is led, unknowing, wherever it is needful for it to go?

## How the woman was created or built, and how the angels act in creating or reforming things.

**15.** 26. Now, then, let us see how the very formation of the woman, which is also mystically called a building, was done. For the nature of the woman was created, although from the male, which already was, not by any motion of natures already existing. But the angels can create no nature at all: for the one and only creator of any nature, whether great or least, is God—that is, the Trinity itself, Father and Son and Holy Spirit. It is asked, then, in another way, how Adam was put to sleep, and his rib drawn from the framework of his body without any sensation of pain. For these things perhaps may be said to have been able to be done through angels; but to form or build a rib, that it might be a woman, so far could be done by no one but God, by whom all nature subsists, that I would not believe even that supplement of flesh in the man's body, which came into the place of that rib, to have been made by angels, just as not the man himself from the dust of the earth: not that there is no work of angels in something being created, but they are not therefore creators; because we do not call farmers the creators of crops and trees either. *For neither he who plants is anything, nor he who waters, but God who gives the increase*[^28]. To this increase belongs also, in the human body, that, the bone being removed, the place was supplied with flesh—namely by that work of God by which he establishes natures that they may be, by which he created the angels themselves.

## What the first cause is, what the secondary causes are.

**15.** 27. It is the work of the farmer, then, to lead the water when he irrigates; but it is not his work that the water flows down the slopes, but his who disposed all things in measure, and number, and weight. Likewise it is the work of the farmer that a slip is torn from the tree and committed to the earth; but it is not his work that it imbibes sap and sends forth a shoot, that it fix one part in its soil, by which it stabilizes a root, and advance another into the air, by which it nourishes the trunk and spreads out the branches; but his who gives the increase.

The physician too applies nourishment to the sick body, and medicine to the wounded: the first not from things he created, but which, created by the work of the Creator, he found; then he was able to prepare and administer food or drink, to form a plaster and apply it smeared with medicine; but can he also, from the things he applies, work and create strength or flesh? Nature does this by an interior motion, most hidden to us. Yet if God should withdraw from it that inmost working by which he establishes and makes it, at once, as if extinguished, none will remain.

## On the twofold work of God by which he administers creatures both in natural and in voluntary motions.

**15.** 28. Therefore, since God administers his whole creation by a kind of twofold work of providence, of which we spoke in the preceding book, both in natural and in voluntary motions, an angel can as little create a nature as create itself. But the angelic will, obediently subject to God and executing his command, can, by natural motions, supply from subject things, as it were, the matter, so that, according to those principal reasons not created in the Word of God, or according to those reasons causally created in the first six days' works, something may be created in time, in the manner of farming or healing.

What ministry, then, the angels exhibited to God in that formation of the woman, who would dare to affirm? Yet most certainly I would say that that supplement of flesh in the place of the rib, and the very body and soul of the woman and the conformation of her members, all her vitals, all her senses, and whatever there was by which she was both a creature and a human being and a female, was made only in that work of God by which God—not through angels, but through himself—did not work and then dismiss, but so works continuously that the nature neither of any other things, nor of the angels themselves, subsists, if he does not work. The slowness of human wit does not attain the works of God.

**16.** 29. But since, as far as we have been able to experience the nature of things according to human capacity, we do not know animate and sentient flesh to be born except either from these, as it were, material elements—that is, water and earth—or from shrubs or the fruits of trees, or even from the flesh of animals, as the innumerable kinds of worms or reptiles, or at least from the intercourse of parents; but we know no flesh to have been born from the flesh of any living creature, so similar to it that it would be distinguished only by sex—we seek in the things of this creation a likeness by which the woman was made from the man's side, and we cannot find it: for no other reason than that we know how men work on this earth; but how the angels, in a certain way, farm in this world, we surely do not know.

For surely, if the course of nature wrought the kind of shrubs apart from human industry, we would know nothing but that trees and herbs are born from the earth, and from their seeds, falling likewise from them into the earth: would it become known to us what grafting could do, so that a tree of one kind, with its own root, would bear fruits of another, and, the unity growing together, now its own? These things we have learned through the works of farmers, though they were in no way creators of trees, but offered the course of nature a kind of office and ministry to God the Creator. For by no means would anything come into existence through their works, if the inmost reason of nature did not hold this in the work of God. What wonder, then, if we do not know how a human being was made from a human being's bone, when we are ignorant how angels serve God creating; we who could not even know how a tree is made from a tree's slip on alien stock, if we likewise did not know how farmers serve God creating?

## That it is difficult to distinguish a work of nature from a miracle.

**16.** 30. In no way, however, do we doubt that God alone is the creator both of men and of trees, and we faithfully believe that the woman was made from the man, with no intervening intercourse, even if perhaps the man's rib was ministered through angels in the work of the Creator: just as we faithfully believe that a man too was made from a woman, with no intervening intercourse, when the seed of Abraham was ordained *through angels in the hand of a mediator*[^29]. Both are incredible to unbelievers; but to the faithful, why should what was done concerning Christ seem credible according to the propriety of the deed, and only what is written of Eve according to a figurative signification?

Or indeed, could a man be made from a woman without anyone's intercourse, and could a woman not be made from a man? And did a virginal womb have the means from which a man might be made, while a male side did not have the means from which a woman might be made—when here the Lord was born of a handmaid, there a handmaid was formed from a servant? The Lord too could have created his flesh from a rib or from some member of the Virgin; but he who could show in his own body that he had done again what was done, more usefully showed in his mother's body that nothing is to be ashamed of which is chaste.

## The causal reason for forming the woman.

**17.** 31. But if it is asked how that causal establishment stands, in which God made the first man to his image and likeness (for there too this was said: *male and female he made them*[^30]): whether that reason which God created together with, and let grow up among, the first works of the world already had this, that according to it it was now necessary for the woman to be made from the man's side; or whether it had only this, that it could be done, but that it should be done so necessarily was not there already established, but was hidden in God—if this, then, is asked, I will say what seems to me, without rashness of affirming; which, however, when I have said it, perhaps those who consider these things prudently, whom the Christian faith has already imbued, even if they now learn these things for the first time, will judge not to be doubted.

## On the definitive force of natures and on the omnipotence of God.

**17.** 32. All this most customary course of nature has certain natural laws of its own, according to which even the spirit of life, which is a creature, has certain appetites of its own determined in a way, which even an evil will cannot exceed. And the elements of this corporeal world have their defined force and quality—what each can or cannot do, what can or cannot be made from what. From these, as the first beginnings of things, all things that are begotten take their rise and progress, each in its own time, and the ends and departures of each kind. Whence it comes about that from a grain of wheat a bean is not born, nor wheat from a bean, nor a man from cattle, nor cattle from a man.

But above this motion and course of natural things, the power of the Creator has within itself the ability to make from all these something other than what their, as it were, seminal reasons hold—yet not that which he did not put in them, so that it could be made from them either by himself. For he is almighty not by rash power, but by the virtue of wisdom; and he does of each thing, in its own time, that which he beforehand made in it that it could do.

There is, then, one mode of things by which that herb so sprouts, that one so; that age bears, that one does not bear; a man can speak, cattle cannot. The reasons of these and such modes are not only in God, but are also imparted to and ingrown in created things by him. But that a tree cut from the earth, dry, polished, without any root, without earth and water, should suddenly flower and bear fruit[^31]; that a woman, sterile in her youth, should bear in old age[^32]; that an ass should speak[^33], and whatever of this kind there is, he gave indeed to the natures which he created, that from them these too could be made (for he would not from them make what he himself had foreordained could not be made from them, since he is not more powerful than himself); yet he gave it in another mode, so that they did not have these things in natural motion, but in that by which they were so created that their nature would be more subject to a more powerful will. God the cause of things transcending them.

**18.** 33. God has, then, hidden in himself the causes of certain deeds, which he did not insert into created things; and he fulfills them not by that work of providence by which he establishes natures that they may be, but by that by which he administers them as he wills, which as he willed he established. There too is grace, by which sinners are saved. For as concerns nature depraved by its own iniquitous will, it does not have recourse through itself, but through the grace of God, by which it is helped and restored. For men are not to be despaired of in that statement in which it is written: *all who walk in her will not return*[^34]. For it was said according to the weight of their iniquity, so that whoever returns may attribute what returns not to himself but to the grace of God, not from works, lest perhaps he be lifted up[^35].

## The mystery by which the woman was made.

**18.** 34. Therefore the Apostle said that the mystery of this grace was hidden, not in the world, in which the causal reasons of all things that would arise naturally are hidden—as Levi was hidden in the loins of Abraham, when he too was tithed[^36]—but in God, who created all things.

Therefore all the things too which, to signify this grace, were done marvelously, not by the natural motion of things, had their hidden causes also in God: of which, if one was that the woman was made thus from the side of the man, and that of one sleeping—she who through him was made firm, as it were strengthened by his bone, while he, for her sake, was made weak, because in the place of the rib not a rib but flesh was supplied—the first establishment of things did not have this, when on the sixth day it was said: *male and female he made them*[^37], that the woman should be made altogether so; but it had only this, that she could be made so too, lest he do anything against the causes which he established by will, by a mutable will. But what would be done, so that something else altogether would not come to be, was hidden in God, who created all things.

## The ministry of angels up to the coming of Christ.

**18.** 35. But since he said it was hidden in such a way that it might become known to the principalities and powers in the heavenly places, through the Church, the manifold wisdom of God[^38], it is probably believed that, just as that seed to which the promise was made was ordained through angels in the hand of a mediator[^39], so all the things which, for foretelling or announcing the coming of that seed, were marvelously done in the nature of things beyond the customary course of nature, were done with angels ministering: yet so that everywhere the creator or restorer of creatures is none but God, who alone, whatever planter and waterer there be, gives the increase[^40].

## On Adam's ecstasy.

**19.** 36. And by this, that ecstasy too which God sent upon Adam, so that, made drowsy, he fell asleep, is rightly understood to have been sent for this purpose: that his mind too, through the ecstasy, might become a partaker, as it were, of the angelic court, and, entering into the sanctuary of God, might understand the last things[^41]. Finally, waking as though full of prophecy, when he saw his wife brought to him, he at once burst forth with what the Apostle commends as a great sacrament: *this now is bone of my bones, and flesh of my flesh. She shall be called woman, because she was taken from her husband; and for this reason a man will leave his father and mother, and will cleave to his wife; and the two will be in one flesh*[^42]*.*

Although Scripture itself testifies that these words were the first man's, the Lord nevertheless declared in the Gospel that God said them. For he says: *have you not read that he who made them from the beginning made them male and female? And he said: For this reason a man will leave father and mother, and will cleave to his wife; and the two will be in one flesh*[^43]—so that from this we might understand that, through the ecstasy which had preceded in Adam, he could divinely say this, as it were, as a prophet. But now this terminus of this book pleases me, so that the things that follow may, from another beginning, renew the attention of readers.
