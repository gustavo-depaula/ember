# Book I

## Sacred Scripture is to be received both literally and figuratively.

**1. 1.** All Divine Scripture is twofold, according to what the Lord signifies when he says that *a scribe instructed in the kingdom of God is like a householder who brings out of his treasure things new and old*[^1], which two are also called Testaments. In all the Holy Books one must look for what eternal things are intimated there, what deeds are narrated, what future things are foretold, what things to be done are enjoined or counseled. In the narration of things done, then, the question is whether all of it is to be received only according to a figurative understanding, or is also to be asserted and defended according to the truth of the events as they happened. For that it is not to be received figuratively, no Christian will dare to say, mindful of the Apostle who says, *now all these things happened to them in figure*[^2], and of what is written in Genesis, *and the two shall be in one flesh*[^3], which commends a great mystery in Christ and in the Church[^4].

## What is meant by "beginning" and "heaven and earth."

**1. 2.** If, then, that Scripture is to be examined in both ways, in what sense was it said, apart from any allegorical signification, *in the beginning God made heaven and earth*[^5]: whether in the beginning of time, or because it was first of all, or in the beginning that is the Word of God, the only-begotten Son? And how can it be shown that God, without any change in himself, made changeable and temporal things? And what is signified by the name *heaven and earth*: whether both spiritual and corporeal creation received the name of heaven and earth, or only the corporeal, so that in this book he is understood to have said nothing of the spiritual, and to have said *heaven and earth* in such a way as to mean the whole corporeal creation, higher and lower?

Or is the unformed matter of both called heaven and earth: spiritual life, that is, such as it can be in itself, not yet turned toward the Creator, for by such a turning it is formed and perfected, but if it is not turned it is unformed; and corporeal matter, if it can be understood through the privation of every corporeal quality that appears in formed matter, when there are now forms of bodies perceptible whether by sight or by any other bodily sense?

## What is meant by "heaven and earth."

**1. 3.** Or is *heaven* to be understood as the spiritual creation, perfect and ever blessed from the beginning in which it was made, but *earth* as corporeal matter still imperfect? For *the earth,* it says, *was invisible and unformed, and darkness was over the abyss*[^6], by which words it seems to signify the formlessness of corporeal substance. Or is the formlessness of both also signified by these later words: the corporeal by what was said, *the earth was invisible and unformed,* and the spiritual by what was said, *darkness was over the abyss*; so that by a transferred sense we understand the dark abyss to be the unformed nature of life, unless it be turned toward the Creator, by which alone it can be formed so as not to be an abyss, and illumined so as not to be dark? And in what sense was it said, *darkness was over the abyss*? Was it because there was no light? For if there were light, it would surely be above and as it were poured over it, which happens in the spiritual creation when it is turned toward the unchangeable and incorporeal light that is God.

## The creation of light.

**2. 4.** And in what sense did God say, *let there be light*[^7]? Was it in time, or in the eternity of the Word? And if in time, then surely changeably; how then can it be understood that God says this, except through a creature, since he himself is unchangeable? And if it was through a creature that God said, *let there be light,* how is light the first creature, if there was already a creature through which God might say, *let there be light*?

Or is light not the first creature, because it had already been said, *in the beginning God made heaven and earth*[^8], and a voice could have come to be in time and changeably through the heavenly creation, by which it might be said, *let there be light*?

And if this is so, the light that was made is this corporeal light which we discern with our bodily eyes, God saying, through the spiritual creation that God had already made when in the beginning God made heaven and earth, *let there be light*; in the manner in which, through the inner and hidden motion of such a creature, it could be said by God, *let there be light.*

## The voice of God creating light.

**2. 5.** Or did the voice of God saying *let there be light* also sound corporeally, just as the voice of God saying *you are my beloved Son*[^9] sounded corporeally, and this through the corporeal creation that God had made when in the beginning he made heaven and earth, before the light was made which came to be in this sounding voice? And if this is so, in what tongue did this voice sound when God said, *let there be light*, since there was not yet that diversity of tongues which was afterward made at the building of the tower after the flood[^10]? What was the one and only tongue in which God spoke, *let there be light*? And who was it that had to hear and understand, to whom a voice of this kind was directed? Or is this an absurd and carnal thought and suspicion?

## The voice of God creating light, and the Word.

**2. 6.** What then shall we say? Is it that what is understood in the sound of the voice when it is said *let there be light,* and not the corporeal sound itself, is rightly understood to be the voice of God? And does this very thing belong to the nature of his Word, of which it is said, *in the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God*[^11]? For when it is said of him, *all things were made through him*[^12], it is sufficiently shown that the light too was made through him, when God said, *let there be light.*

And if this is so, what God said, *let there be light,* is eternal, because the Word of God, God with God, the only Son of God, is coeternal with the Father, although by God speaking this in the eternal Word a temporal creature was made. For while the words are words of time, when we say "when" and "at some time," yet it is eternal in the Word of God when anything is to come to be; and it then comes to be when its having-to-come-to-be is in that Word in which there is no "when" and "at some time," since that whole Word is eternal.

## What is the light itself that was made by God?

**3. 7.** And what is the light itself that was made? Is it something spiritual or something corporeal? For if spiritual, it can itself be the first creature, perfected by this very utterance, which was first called heaven when it was said, *in the beginning God made heaven and earth*[^13]: so that the words *God said, 'let there be light'; and light was made*[^14]*,* may be understood as the Creator calling it back to himself, its conversion thus accomplished and illumined.

## Whether God created by "speaking" in his Word.

**3. 8.** And why was it said thus, *in the beginning God made heaven and earth,* and not said, "in the beginning God said, 'let there be heaven and earth'; and heaven and earth were made," as is narrated of the light, *God said, 'let there be light'; and light was made*? Was it that what God made had first to be comprehended and commended universally under the name of *heaven and earth,* and then to be set out part by part, how he made it, when it is said of each thing, *God said*; that is, because he made through his Word whatever he made?

## How unformed matter is formed.

**4. 9.** Or, when the formlessness of matter, whether spiritual or corporeal, was first coming to be, was it not to be said, *God said, 'let it be'*, because the form of the Word, ever cleaving to the Father, by which God eternally utters all things—not by the sound of a voice nor by a thought unrolling the times of sounds, but by the light, coeternal with himself, of the Wisdom begotten of himself—is not imitated by imperfection, when, unlike that which supremely and primally is, it tends by a certain formlessness toward nothing?

But it then imitates the form of the Word, ever and unchangeably cleaving to the Father, when it too, by a turning proper to its own kind toward that which truly and always is—that is, toward the Creator of its substance—takes on form and becomes a perfected creature: so that in what Scripture narrates, *God said, 'let it be,'* we may understand the incorporeal utterance of God, in the nature of his coeternal Word, calling back to himself the imperfection of the creature, so that it may not be formless but may be formed according to each thing that he sets out in order.

In which turning and formation, because in its own way it imitates God the Word, that is, the Son of God ever cleaving to the Father in full likeness and equal essence, by which he and the Father are one[^15]—but does not imitate this form of the Word if, turned away from the Creator, it remains formless and imperfect—therefore the Son is not commemorated as Word but only as beginning, when it is said, *in the beginning God made heaven and earth*[^16]; for the origin of the creature is here suggested while it is still in the formlessness of imperfection; but commemoration is made of the Son as also Word, in that it is written, *God said, 'let it be.'*

So that by what is the beginning he may suggest the origin of the creature existing from him while still imperfect; and by what is the Word he may suggest the perfection of the creature called back to him, so that it might be formed by cleaving to the Creator, and by imitating after its kind the form that everlastingly and unchangeably cleaves to the Father, from whom it at once becomes what that form is.

## The unformed creature is formed by turning itself toward the light of wisdom.

**5. 10.** For the Word, the Son, does not have a formless life, for whom not only is to be the same as to live, but to live is also the same as to live wisely and blessedly. But a creature, although spiritual and intellectual or rational, which seems to be nearer to that Word, can have a formless life; because, just as for it to be is not the same as to live, so to live is not the same as to live wisely and blessedly. For turned away from the unchangeable Wisdom, it lives foolishly and wretchedly, and this is its formlessness. But it is formed when turned toward the unchangeable light of Wisdom, the Word of God. For from him by whom it came to exist so as to be and to live in any way at all, it is turned toward him so as to live wisely and blessedly.

For the beginning of the intellectual creature is the eternal Wisdom; which beginning, remaining unchangeably in itself, would in no way cease, by the hidden inspiration of its call, to speak to that creature to which it is the beginning, that it might be turned toward that from which it is, since it cannot otherwise be formed and perfected. And so, when he was asked who he was, he answered, *the beginning, who also speak to you*[^17].

## The Trinity intimated both in the beginning and in the perfecting of the creature.

**5. 11.** But when the Son speaks, the Father speaks, because by the Father speaking the Word is uttered, which is the Son, after an eternal manner—if "manner" is the word to use—God uttering the coeternal Word. For there is in God the highest goodness, holy and just; and indeed a love coming into his works not from need but from beneficence. Therefore, before it was written, *God said, 'let there be light'*[^18], Scripture went before, saying, *and the Spirit of God was borne over the water*[^19]*.*

For whether by the name *water* he meant to call the whole corporeal matter, so as in that way to suggest whence were made and formed all the things that we can already distinguish in their kinds—calling it water because we see that all things are formed and grow together on the earth out of a moist nature through various forms—or whether he meant a certain spiritual life, as it were fluid before the form of conversion: in any case the Spirit of God was borne over it, because whatever that was which he had begun to form and perfect lay subject to the good will of the Creator.

So that, God saying in his Word *let there be light*, what was made might remain in his good will, that is, in his good pleasure, according to the measure of its own kind; and therefore it is right that it pleased God, as Scripture says, *and light was made; and God saw the light, that it was good*[^20]*.*

## How the Trinity is intimated in the very beginning of the creature.

**6. 12.** So that, just as in the very beginning of the inchoate creature—which was commemorated under the name of *heaven and earth* on account of what was to be perfected in it—the Trinity of the Creator is intimated (for when Scripture says, *in the beginning God made heaven and earth*[^21], we understand the Father in the name "God," and the Son in the name "beginning," who is the beginning not to the Father but, through himself, primarily and chiefly to the spiritual creature created first, and consequently to the whole creation as well; and when Scripture says, *and the Spirit of God was borne over the water*[^22]*,* we recognize the complete commemoration of the Trinity): so too, in the conversion and perfecting of the creature, that its forms may be set in order, the same Trinity is intimated: namely the Word of God, and the begetter of the Word, when it is said, *God said*; and the holy goodness in which whatever is perfect according to the measure of its own nature pleases God, when it is said, *God saw that it was good*[^23]*.*

## What is meant by "the Spirit of God was borne over the water."

**7. 13.** But why, when the creature, though imperfect, was commemorated first, is the Spirit of God commemorated afterward, Scripture first saying, *but the earth was invisible and unformed, and darkness was over the abyss*[^24], and then adding, *and the Spirit of God was borne over the water*[^25]? Is it because needy and indigent love loves in such a way that it makes itself subject to the things it loves? And therefore, when the Spirit of God was commemorated, in whom his holy goodwill and love is understood, it was said to be borne over, lest God be thought to love the works he was to make through the necessity of need rather than through the abundance of beneficence. Mindful of this, the Apostle, about to speak of love, says he will show a still more excellent way[^26]; and in another place he says, *the love of Christ, which surpasses knowledge*[^27]. Since, then, the Spirit of God had thus to be intimated as borne over, it was more fitting that something inchoate should first be intimated, over which it might be said to be borne; not over it in place, but in a power that surpasses and excels all things.

## God loves his creature so that it may be and remain.

**8. 14.** So too, when things were perfected and formed from that inchoate state, *God saw that it was good*[^28]: for what was made pleased him, in that same goodness by which it pleased him that it should come to be. For there are two things on account of which God loves his creature: that it may be, and that it may remain.

So that there might be something that would remain, *the Spirit of God was borne over the water*[^29]; and that it might remain, *God saw that it was good.* And what was said of the light is true of all things. For some things remain, surpassing all temporal mutability, in the fullest holiness under God; but others remain according to the measures of their own time, while the beauty of the ages is woven together through the passing and succession of things.

## When God said: Let there be light.

**9. 15.** What God said, then—*let there be light; and light was made*[^30]*—*did he say it on some day, or before any day? For if he said it in the Word coeternal with himself, he certainly said it without time; but if he said it in time, he said it not in the Word coeternal with himself but through some temporal creature; and on this account light will not be the first creature, since there already was a creature through which it might be said in time, *let there be light.*

And that is understood to have been made before any day which is meant by the words, *in the beginning God made heaven and earth*[^31]; so that by the name *heaven* we may understand the spiritual creation already made and formed, as it were the heaven of this heaven which is the highest among bodies. For on the second day the firmament was made, which he again called *heaven.* But by the name of the earth, invisible and unformed, and of the dark abyss, was signified the imperfection of corporeal substance, out of which those temporal things would come to be, the first of which would be light.

## Of what kind was the "voice" of God when he made the light.

**9. 16.** But how, through a creature that he made before times, it could be said in time, *let there be light*[^32]*,* is difficult to discover. For we do not understand it to have been said by the sound of a voice, since whatever is of that kind is corporeal. Or did he make, out of that imperfection of corporeal substance, some corporeal voice through which there might sound, *let there be light*? In that case some vocal body was created and formed before the light. But if this is so, there was already time through which the voice ran and the spaces of the successive sounds passed away.

But if there was already time before the light was made, in which time the voice might come to be that would sound, *let there be light*, to which day did that time belong? For one day, and that the first, begins to be counted from the time the light was made. Or does the whole span of time belong to that very day—both that in which the vocal body was made through which there might sound, *let there be light,* and that in which the light itself was made? But every such voice is uttered by the speaker for the bodily sense of the hearer; for it was so made that, the air being struck, the hearer perceives it. Did then that whatever-it-was, invisible and unformed, have such a hearing, that God should so resound to it and say, *let there be light*? Let this absurdity, then, depart from the mind of one who thinks.

## Whether the Word of God was uttered in time or outside time.

**9. 17.** Was it, then, a spiritual but nonetheless temporal motion by which we understand it to have been said, *let there be light,* expressed by the eternal God through the coeternal Word in the spiritual creature that he had already made, when it was said, *in the beginning God made heaven and earth*[^33]*,* that is, in that heaven of heaven? Or is even this utterance understood to have been—not only without any sound, but also without any temporal motion of the spiritual creature—in a way fixed in its mind and reason by the Word coeternal with the Father, and in a way impressed upon it, according to which that lower, dark imperfection of corporeal nature might be moved and turned toward form, and become light?

But it is a great and most difficult thing to grasp how, with God not commanding in time, nor the creature hearing it in time—the creature which by the contemplation of Truth exceeds all times, but which transmits to the things below it, as intelligible utterances, the principles impressed intellectually upon itself by the unchangeable Wisdom of God—temporal motions come to be in temporal things, whether to be formed or to be administered.

But if the light which was first bidden to be, and was made, is also to be understood as holding the primacy of creation, it is the intellectual life itself; which, unless it were turned to be illumined toward the Creator, would float formlessly. But when it is turned and illumined, there comes to be what was said in the Word of God, *let there be light.*

## From Genesis 1:5 the light seems to have been made over the span of a day.

**10. 18.** Nevertheless, just as it was said without time, because time does not fall upon the Word coeternal with the Father, someone may perhaps ask whether it was also made without time. But how can this be understood, when, the light being made and divided from the darkness, and the names "day" and "night" given, Scripture says, *and there was evening, and there was morning, one day*[^34]? From which it seems that that work of God was done over the span of a day, at the end of which evening was reached, which is the beginning of night. And likewise, the nocturnal span being completed, the whole day was finished, so that morning came on into another day, on which day God did something else in turn.

## Were not the light and its separation from the darkness made at once?

**10. 19.** Indeed, this very thing is most astonishing: since God said by the eternal reason of his Word, with no span of syllables, *let there be light*[^35], why the light was made with so great a delay, until the span of a day passed and evening came. Or was the light perhaps made quickly, but the delay of the daytime could be consumed in this, that it was being separated from the darkness, and each, once separated, marked with its own names? It would be strange if even this could be done by God with as great a delay as is taken in our saying it. For the separation of light and darkness certainly followed in the very work, when the light came to be; for light could not be unless it were separated from darkness.

## God called the light day, and so on, in the eternal reasons of his Wisdom.

**10. 20.** But that *God called the light day, and the darkness night*[^36]*,* with how great a delay could this have been done, even if he had done it syllable by syllable through the sound of a voice, except as much as it is said by us: "let light be called day, and let darkness be called night"? unless perhaps someone is so foolish that, because God is great above all things, he thinks that even very few syllables uttered by the mouth of God could have been stretched out through a whole span of a day. Add to this that it was by the Word coeternal with himself—that is, by the inner and eternal reasons of the unchangeable Wisdom, not by a corporeal sound of voice—that *God called the light day, and the darkness night.* For again it is asked, if he called by the words we use, in what tongue he called; and what need there was of passing sounds, where there was no bodily hearing of anyone: and no answer is found.

## How the sun makes day in one place and night in another.

**10. 21.** Or must it be said that, since this work of God was quickly accomplished, the light so stood, with no night succeeding, until the daytime span was completed; and the night so remained, succeeding the light, until the span of nocturnal time passed and the morning of the following day came, the one first day being now over? But if I say this, I fear I shall be laughed at both by those who have learned with the greatest certainty, and by those who can most easily observe, that at the time when it is night with us the presence of light illumines those parts of the world through which the sun returns from setting to rising; and that on this account, in all twenty-four hours, throughout the circuit of the whole sphere, there is never lacking day in one place, night in another.

Are we then going to place God in some region where evening would come to him, when the light withdrew from that region into another? For in the book called *Ecclesiastes* it is written thus: *and the sun rises, and the sun sets, and leads back to its own place*[^37]; that is, to that place from which it rose. For it follows and says, *rising, it goes there toward the south, and circles to the north*[^38]. When, therefore, the southern region has the sun, it is day for us; but when, circling, it is carried over to the northern region, it is night for us; yet it is not for that reason not day in the other region, where the presence of the sun is—unless perhaps the heart is to be inclined to poetic fictions, so that we believe the sun plunges itself into the sea, and thence, washed, rises in the morning from another quarter. Although, if this were so, the abyss itself would be illumined by the presence of the sun, and there it would be day. For the sun could also illumine the waters, since it could not be quenched by them. But it is monstrous to suspect this. And what of the fact that the sun did not yet exist?

## Was the first light spiritual or corporeal?

**10. 22.** Therefore, if a spiritual light was made on the first day, did it ever set, so that night might succeed it? But if a corporeal light, what is that light which after the setting of the sun we cannot see, since neither the moon was yet there, nor any stars? Or if it is always in that part of the heaven in which the sun is, so that it is not the light of the sun but as it were its companion, so joined to it that it cannot be discerned and distinguished, we return to the same difficulty in solving this question: because that light too, in the same way as the sun, as its companion, returns by circling from setting to rising, and is in another part of the world at the time when this part in which we are grows dark into night.

From which it forces us—God forbid—to believe that God was in one region, which that light would desert so that evening might come to him. Or had he perhaps made the light in that region in which he was to make man; and therefore, when the light had withdrawn from that region, evening is said to have been made, even though that light which had withdrawn from there was in another region, about to rise as morning when its circuit was completed?

## The first light made, and the alternation of days.

**11. 23.** Why, then, was the sun made *for the dominion of the day*[^39], *to give light upon the earth,* if that light, which was also called *day,* had sufficed for making the day? Or did that earlier light illumine the upper regions far from the earth, so that it could not be perceived on earth, and thus the sun had to be made, through which day might appear to the lower parts of the world? This too can be said: that the brightness was increased by the addition of the sun, so that the day is believed to have been less bright by that earlier light than it now is.

I know that this too has been said by someone: that the nature of light was first introduced into the work of the Creator, when it was said, *let there be light, and light was made*[^40]; but afterward, when the luminaries are spoken of, what was made out of that light is recalled, in the order of days in which it seemed good to the Creator that all things should be made. But where that nature of light went when evening came, so that night might in turn be passed through, he did not say, nor do I think it can easily be found. For we must not believe that it was extinguished, so that nocturnal darkness might succeed, and again kindled, so that morning might come, before this was carried out by the office of the sun; which, that same Scripture testifies, began to be done from the fourth day.

## Light and shadow: day and night before the creation of the sun.

**12. 24.** Before the sun was made, by what circuit three days and nights could succeed one another, the nature of the light first made remaining, if a corporeal light is then to be understood to have been made, is difficult both to discover and to explain. Unless perhaps someone should say that God called darkness the earthen and watery mass, before each was separated from the other—which is written to have been done on the third day—on account of the grosser corpulence which light could not penetrate, or on account of the deepest shadow of so great a mass, which a body must have on one side if on some side there be light. For at any place to which the mass of any body whatsoever does not allow light to reach, in that place there is shadow; since a place lacking that light by which it would be illumined, were not the opposing body to hinder, is the whole of what is called *shadow.* And if, in proportion to the mass of the body, this be so great as to occupy a space of the earth as great as the day occupies on the other side, it is called night. For not all darkness is night. For in vast caves too, into whose recesses the light is not allowed to break through the opposing mass, there is certainly darkness, because light is not there, and that whole space is a place lacking light; yet such darkness has not received the name of night, but only that which succeeds into the part of the earth from which day is removed. Just as not all light is called day; for there is also the light of the moon, and of the stars, and of lamps, and of lightning flashes, and of whatever things shine in this way: but that light is called day to which night succeeds, the preceding day withdrawing.

## By what light did days and nights succeed one another?

**12. 25.** But if that primary light, poured around on every side, enveloped the mass of the earth, whether it stood still or circled around, there was no side on which it could admit night to succeed it, because nowhere did it itself withdraw so as to make room for it. Or had it been made on one side, so that, itself circling around, it might consequently allow night also to circle around on the other side? For since water still covered the whole earth, nothing hindered that the watery and globe-shaped mass should make on one side day by the presence of light, on the other night by the absence of light, which would succeed into that part from the evening time, from which the light declined into the other.

## How the gathering of the waters was made.

**12. 26.** To where, then, were the waters gathered, if they had first occupied the whole earth? Those, that is, which were drawn off so that the earth might be laid bare—into what part were they gathered?

For if there was any part of the earth bare, into which they might be gathered, the dry land already appeared, and the abyss did not occupy the whole. But if they covered the whole, what place was there into which they might be collected, so that the dryness of the earth might appear?

Were they perhaps gathered up on high, as happens when threshed grain is heaped up for winnowing on the threshing floor, and, gathered into a mound, lays bare the place which, spread out, it had covered?

Who would say this, when he has seen the plains of the sea spread out evenly everywhere, which even when certain mountains, as it were, of the surging water are raised up, are leveled again when the storms have subsided? And if some shores are laid bare more widely, it cannot be said that there are no other spaces of land to which that which recedes from elsewhere may go, so that from there it may again come back to the place from which it had withdrawn. But when the whole earth altogether was covered by the watery nature, where could it give way so as to lay bare some parts?

Or did the water, perhaps rarer like a mist, cover the lands, which by gathering was thickened, so that out of many parts, in which the dry land could appear, it might lay them bare? Although the earth too, settling far and wide, could offer other hollow parts in which the converging and rushing waters might be received, and the dry land appear out of those parts from which the moisture withdrew.

**12. 27.** But matter is not utterly formless where even a misty appearance has appeared.

## When the waters and the earth were made.

**13. 27.** And therefore it can still be asked when God created these visible appearances and qualities of the waters and the lands; for it is found in none of the six days. And so, if he made this before any day, as it was written, before the mention of these first days, *in the beginning God made heaven and earth*[^41]—so that by the name of *earth* we understand the earthen appearance already formed, with the waters poured over it, these things being now declared in the visible appearance of their own kind—so that in what Scripture follows with, saying, *but the earth was invisible and unformed, and darkness was over the abyss, and the Spirit of God was borne over the waters*[^42]*,* we may suppose no formlessness of matter, but earth and water, without the light that was not yet made, founded already with their best-known qualities: so that the earth may be understood to be called invisible because, covered by waters, it could not be seen, even if there were someone who could see; and unformed because it was not yet distinguished from the sea, and girt with shores, and adorned with its own offspring and animals.

If, then, this is so, why were these appearances, which are beyond doubt corporeal, made before any day? Why was it not written, "God said, 'let there be earth,' and earth was made"; likewise, "God said, 'let there be water'; and water was made"; or both together, if they are contained as it were by one law of the lowest place: "God said, 'let there be earth and water,' and so it was made"?

## That the world was created out of unformed matter made by God.

**14. 27.** Why was it not said, when this had been made, "God saw that it was good"?

**14. 28.** For this consideration has urged it (since it is manifest that everything changeable is formed out of some formlessness; and since at once both the catholic faith prescribes and most certain reason teaches that there could be no matter of any natures except from God, the originator and Creator of all things not only formed but also formable—of which a certain Scripture also says to him, *you who made the world out of unformed matter*[^43]): namely, that this matter was commemorated in those words by which, by a spiritual prudence, it might be made suitable even for slower readers or hearers, in which, before the enumeration of the days, it was said, *in the beginning God made heaven and earth,* and so on, until it was said, *and God said,* so that thereafter the order of formed things might follow.

## The matter and the form of things were created together.

**15. 29.** Not that unformed matter is prior in time to formed things, since both are created together—both that from which a thing is made, and that which is made. For just as the voice is the matter of words, and words indicate a formed voice—yet one who speaks does not first emit an unformed voice which he can afterward collect and form into words—so God the Creator did not make unformed matter at a prior time, and afterward form it, as by a second consideration, through the order of the several natures; rather, he created matter already formed. But because that from which something is made is prior, if not in time, yet by a certain origin, to that which is made from it, Scripture could divide by times of speaking what God did not divide by times of making. For if it be asked whether we make voice out of words or words out of voice, no one is easily found of so slow a wit as not to answer rather that words are made out of voice: so, although one who speaks makes both at once, what he makes out of what is sufficiently clear to natural attention.

For which reason, since God made both at once—both the matter that he formed and the things into which he formed it—and since both had to be spoken of by Scripture, and both could not be spoken of at once, who would doubt that that from which something was made had to be spoken of before that which was made from it? For even when we say "matter" and "form," we understand both to exist together, and yet we cannot utter both at once.

But just as in the brevity of time it happens, when we utter these two words, that we utter one before the other, so in the length of a narration one had to be narrated before the other, although, as has been said, God made both at once; so that what is prior in making by origin alone is also prior in narrating by time: because two things, of which the one is in no way prior, cannot be named at once; how much less narrated at once? Therefore it is not to be doubted that this unformed matter, next to nothing, is in such a way that it was not made except by God, and was created together with the things that were formed out of it.

## How Scripture suggests the formlessness of matter.

**15. 30.** But if it is credibly said that it is signified by those words, *but the earth was invisible and unformed, and darkness was over the abyss, and the Spirit of God was borne over the water*[^44]—so that, apart from what is there said of the Spirit of God, we understand the rest to be names of visible things indeed, but said in order to suggest that formlessness, as far as could be done for slower minds—because these two elements, namely earth and water, are more easily handled than the rest by the hands of those working to make something, and therefore that formlessness was more fittingly suggested by these names.

## Another account of how day and night were made: perhaps by the emission and contraction of light?

**16. 30.** If, then, this is said with probability, there was no formed mass which light, illumining it on one side, would make dark on the other, so that night could succeed when day withdrew.

**16. 31.** But if we were to understand the emission and contraction of that light as day and night, we do not even see a reason why it should be so. For there were not yet animals to which this alternation might be healthfully presented, and to which, once they arose afterward, we see it presented through the circuit of the sun. Nor does any example occur by which we might prove this emission and contraction of light, such that the alternations of day and night came to be.

For the casting of rays from our eyes is indeed a casting of a certain light; and it can be contracted, when we gaze at the air nearest our eyes, and emitted, when along the same straight line we attend to things placed far off; nor indeed, when it is contracted, does it cease altogether to discern things that are far away, but certainly more dimly than when the gaze is sent out upon them.

But nevertheless that light which is in the sense of the one seeing is shown to be so slight that, unless it is aided by outside light, we can see nothing; and because it cannot be distinguished from it, by what example the emission of light into day and its contraction into night could be demonstrated is, as I said, difficult to find.

## Spiritual light, uncreated light, which illumines spiritual and rational creatures.

**17. 32.** But if a spiritual light was made when God said, *let there be light*[^45], it is not that true light, coeternal with the Father, through which all things were made, and which illumines every man, that is to be understood; but that of which it could be said, *wisdom was created before all things*[^46].

For when that eternal and unchangeable Wisdom, which was not made but begotten, transfers itself into spiritual and rational creatures, as into holy souls[^47], so that, illumined, they may be able to shine, there comes to be in them a certain affection of luminous reason, which can be taken as the light made when God said, *let there be light*: if there already was a spiritual creature, which was signified by the name *heaven*, in what was written, *in the beginning God made heaven and earth*[^48].

Not a corporeal heaven, but the incorporeal heaven of the corporeal heaven—that is, above every body, set over it not by degrees of place but by the sublimity of its nature. But in what way both that which was illumined and the illumination itself could come to be at once, and yet had to be narrated at different times, we said a little before, when we were treating of matter.

## The division of light from darkness explained allegorically.

**17. 33.** But how are we to understand night succeeding this light, so that evening came? And from what kind of darkness could such a light be divided, Scripture saying, *and God divided between the light and the darkness*[^49]?

Were there now sinners and fools falling away from the light of truth, between whom and those remaining in that same light God might divide, as between light and darkness; and calling the light day and the darkness night, might show himself not the worker of sins but the orderer, by the distribution of merits?

Or is *day* here the name of all time, and does it include under this word all the volumes of the ages, and therefore it was not called the first day but one day? *For there was evening,* it says, *and there was morning, one day*[^50]: so that by the evening that was made the sin of the rational creature, and by the morning that was made its renewal, may seem to be signified.

## What the division of light from darkness may suggest.

**17. 34.** But this is a discussion of prophetic allegory, which we have not undertaken in this discourse. For we have set out now to speak of the Scriptures according to the proper character of the events that happened, not according to the riddles of things to come. To the account, then, of the natures made and founded: how do we find evening and morning in the spiritual light?

Or is the division of light from darkness the distinction of the already formed thing from the unformed; while the naming of day and night is a suggestion of the distribution by which it is signified that God leaves nothing without order, and that the very formlessness through which things are changed by passing, as it were, from form to form is not disordered; nor that the failings and advances of the creature, by which temporal things succeed one another, are without a supplement to the beauty of the universe? For night is ordered darkness.

## The difficulty concerning spiritual light: how there is evening and morning in it.

**17. 35.** But it was for this reason that, when the light had been made, it was said, *God saw the light, that it was good*[^51]; though he could have said this after all the works of the same day, that is, when he had set forth, *God said, 'let there be light'; and light was made; and God divided between the light and the darkness; and God called the light day, and the darkness he called night*[^52]; then he would say, *and God saw that it was good*[^53]; and would thereupon add, *and there was evening, and there was morning*[^54]; as he does in the other works to which he gives names.

Here, then, he did not do so, for this reason: that from the formed thing that formlessness was so distinguished that its end was not in it, but it still remained to be formed through the other, now corporeal, creatures.

And so, if it were said, after they had been distinguished by that division and by the names, *God saw that it was good*[^55], we would take these things to be signified to which now, in their own kind, nothing was to be added. But because he had so perfected the light alone, *God saw,* it says, *the light, that it was good,* and by the division and by the names he distinguished it from the darkness.

Nor did he then say, *God saw that it was good*; for that formlessness had been distinguished to this end, that still other things might be formed from it. For this night which is most well known to us (for the circuit of the sun over the lands makes it), when it is divided from day through the distribution of the luminaries, after that very division of day and night it is said, *God saw that it was good.*

For this night was not some formless substance from which still other things might be formed, but a space of place full of air, lacking the daytime light; to which night, certainly, nothing was now to be added in its own kind, by which it might be more beautiful or more distinct. But the evening in that whole three-day period, before the luminaries were made, is perhaps not absurdly understood as the term of the completed work; and the morning as a signification of the work to come.

## That God works by the eternal reasons of his Word and by the love of his Spirit.

**18. 36.** But before all things let us remember—of which we have already said much—that God works not by temporal motions, as it were of his mind or of a body, as a man or an angel works, but by the eternal and unchangeable and stable reasons of his Word coeternal with himself, and by a certain, so to speak, fostering of his Holy Spirit, likewise coeternal. For that too which was said, through the Greek and Latin tongue, of the Spirit of God, that it *was borne over the waters,* is reported, according to the understanding of the Syriac tongue, which is close to the Hebrew (for this is said to have been expounded by a certain learned Christian Syrian), to be understood not *was borne over,* but rather *fostered.* Not as swellings or wounds in the body are fostered with waters, whether cold or tempered to a fitting warmth, but as eggs are fostered by birds, where that warmth of the mother's body in a way also assists in forming the chicks, through a certain affection of love proper to its kind.

Let us not, therefore, through the several days of these divine works, think carnally of God's voices as though they were temporal. For it was not for this that the very Wisdom of God, taken up in our weakness, came to gather the children of Jerusalem under her wings, as a hen gathers her chicks[^56], that we should always be little ones; but that, infants in malice, we should cease to be children in mind[^57].

## Prudence must be used in interpreting the Sacred Scriptures.

**18. 37.** And in matters obscure and most remote from our eyes, if we read even divine writings about them which can, with the faith in which we are instructed kept safe, bring forth one and another opinion, let us not throw ourselves headlong into any one of them by a precipitate affirmation, in such a way that, if perhaps the truth, more diligently examined, rightly overthrows it, we fall with it: fighting thus not for the opinion of the Divine Scriptures but for our own, in such a way that we want that which is ours to be the Scriptures', when rather we ought to want that which is the Scriptures' to be ours.

## In obscure passages of Scripture nothing is to be rashly asserted.

**19. 38.** For let us suppose that, in what was written, *God said, 'let there be light'; and light was made*[^58], one understood a corporeal light to have been made, and another a spiritual.

That there is a spiritual light in the spiritual creature, our faith does not doubt: but that there is a corporeal light, heavenly, or even above the heaven, or before the heaven, which night could succeed, is not against the faith so long as it is not refuted by most certain truth. And if this should happen, the Divine Scripture did not hold this, but human ignorance had thought it.

But if certain reason should demonstrate this to be true, it will still be uncertain whether the writer of those words of the Holy Books meant this to be understood, or something else no less true. And if the rest of the context of the discourse should prove that he did not mean this, the other thing which he himself meant to be understood will not therefore be false, but both true and more useful to know. But if the context of Scripture does not oppose his having meant this to be understood, it will still remain to ask whether he could not also have meant the other. And if we should find that he could also have meant the other, it will be uncertain which of them he meant; and it is not unfittingly believed that he meant both to be understood, if a certain context supports each opinion.

**19. 39.** For it often happens that even a non-Christian knows something about the earth, the heavens, the other elements of this world, about the motion and revolution or even the magnitude and intervals of the stars, about certain eclipses of the sun and moon, about the cycles of years and seasons, about the natures of animals, shrubs, stones, and other such things, and holds it by most certain reason or experience.

Now it is too disgraceful and ruinous, and greatly to be guarded against, that he should hear a Christian, speaking of these things as though according to the Christian writings, raving so far amiss that, seeing him, as the saying goes, err by a whole heaven, he can scarcely hold back his laughter.

And it is not so much that the erring man is laughed at, but that our authors are believed by those outside to have held such things, and, to the great ruin of those for whose salvation we are striving, are blamed and rejected as unlearned.

For when they have caught anyone of the number of Christians erring in a matter which they themselves know best, and asserting his empty opinion from our Books, by what means are they going to believe those Books concerning the resurrection of the dead, and the hope of eternal life, and the kingdom of heaven, when they suppose them to have been written falsely about these things which they have been able already to test, or to grasp by indubitable reckoning?

For how much trouble and sorrow rash presumers bring upon prudent brethren cannot be said sufficiently, when, if ever they begin to be reproved and convicted of their depraved and false opinion by those who are not held by the authority of our Books, then, to defend what they have said with the lightest rashness and the most open falsity, they try to bring forward those same Holy Books as proof of it, or even, from memory, recite many words out of them which they judge to avail as testimony, understanding neither *what they say nor the things about which they make assertions*[^59].

## In interpreting Genesis Augustine brings forward various opinions, confidently affirming none.

**20. 40.** For to consider and observe this, I have unfolded the book of *Genesis* in manifold ways, as far as I could, and have brought forward opinions about words obscurely set down for our exercise; not rashly affirming any one thing, with prejudice to another exposition perhaps better, so that each may choose, according to his measure, what he can grasp; but where he cannot understand, let him give honor to the Scripture of God, and fear to himself.

But since the words of Scripture which we have treated are expounded with so many outcomes, let those at last restrain themselves who, puffed up with secular letters, harry as something unskilled and unpolished these things set down in such a way as to nourish all pious hearts; creeping on the earth without wings, and mocking the nests of the birds by the flight of frogs.

But certain weak brethren err more dangerously, who, when they hear these impious men discoursing subtly and copiously about "the numbers of the heavenly bodies," or about whatever questions of the elements of this world,

lose heart; and, preferring these men to themselves with a sigh, and thinking them great, they return with disgust to the Books of most healthful piety, and what they ought to drink in sweetly they scarcely touch with patience; shrinking from the roughness of the cornfield, and gaping after the flowers of thorns.

For they have no leisure *to see how sweet the Lord is*[^60], nor do they hunger on the Sabbath; and therefore they are slothful, though the power has been received from the Lord of the Sabbath to pluck the ears of grain, and to busy themselves so long with their hands, rubbing and cleaning them, until they come to food[^61].

## What understanding is to be chosen, in plain or in obscure opinions.

**21. 41.** Someone will say: "With such great threshing of this disquisition, how many grains have you stripped out? What have you winnowed? Why does almost everything still lie hidden in questions? Affirm some one of the things which you have argued can be understood in many ways."

To whom I answer that I have come pleasantly to that very food by which I have learned that a man need not be at a loss in answering, according to the faith, what is to be answered to men who are bent on calumniating the Books of our salvation; so that whatever they have been able to demonstrate, by truthful documents, about the nature of things, we may show is not contrary to our Writings. But whatever they have brought forward, from any of their own volumes, contrary to these our Writings, that is, to the catholic faith, let us either show, by some ability, or believe, with no doubt, to be most false; and so let us hold our Mediator, *in whom are hidden all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge*[^62], that we may neither be seduced by the loquacity of false philosophy, nor terrified by the superstition of false religion.

And when we read the Divine Books, amid so great a multitude of true understandings which are drawn out of a few words and are fortified by the soundness of the catholic faith, let us choose above all that which it shall appear certain the one we read meant; but if this lies hidden, then certainly that which the context of Scripture does not hinder, and which accords with sound faith; but if even the context of Scripture cannot be thoroughly examined and discussed, then at least that alone which sound faith prescribes. For it is one thing not to discern what the writer above all meant, and another to err from the rule of piety. If both are avoided, the fruit of the reader is perfectly secured; but if both cannot be avoided, then, even if the will of the writer is uncertain, it is not useless to have drawn out an opinion in keeping with sound faith.
