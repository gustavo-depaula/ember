# Book VII

## The treatment of the soul is undertaken.

**1. 1.** *And God formed man, dust from the earth, and blew into his face the breath of life, and man was made into a living soul*[^1]. These words of Scripture we had proposed for consideration at the beginning of the earlier book too, and we have discoursed about the man made, and especially about his body, as far as we judged sufficient, on what seemed good according to the Scriptures. But because the question about the human soul is no small one, we thought it should be deferred to this book, not knowing how much the Lord would help us who desire to speak rightly; yet knowing this, that we would not speak rightly except as far as he himself helped. But to speak rightly is to speak truthfully and fittingly, refuting nothing boldly, affirming nothing rashly while it is still doubtful whether it is true or false, whether of faith or of Christian knowledge; but asserting without hesitation what can be taught either by the most evident reason of things or by the most certain authority of the Scriptures.

## What is meant by Genesis 2:7: God blew, or breathed, etc.

**1. 2.** And first let us consider what is written, *he blew,* or, *he breathed into his face the breath of life*[^2]. For some manuscripts have, *he breathed,* or, *he inbreathed into his face.* But since the Greek has enephysesen, there is no doubt that *he blew* or *he breathed* must be said. But we were inquiring in the earlier discourse about the hands of God, when man was thought of as formed from clay: what, then, is now to be said in the matter of what is written, *God breathed,* except that, just as he did not form with the hands of a body, so neither did he breathe with throat or lips?

**1. 3.** Nevertheless, by this word Scripture, in a most difficult question, helps us very greatly, as I think.

## That the soul is not of the same nature as God.

**2. 3.** For since some, from this word, have believed that the soul is something of the very substance of God—that is, of the same nature as he is—supposing this for the reason that, when a man blows, he casts out something of himself in the breath, we are rather to be admonished from this to reject this view, hostile to the Catholic faith. For we believe that the nature and substance of God, which in the Trinity is believed by many, understood by few, is wholly unchangeable. But who doubts that the nature of the soul can be changed, whether for the worse or for the better? And thus it is a sacrilegious opinion to believe that it and God are of one substance. For what else, in this way, than that he too is believed changeable? It is therefore to be believed, and understood, and in no way to be doubted, what the right faith holds: that the soul is from God as a thing he made, not as of the nature which he himself is, whether he begot it or in any way brought it forth.

## That the soul is neither a part of God's substance nor our breath.

**3. 4.** And how, they say, is it written, *he breathed into his face, and man was made into a living soul*; if the soul is not a part of God, or God's substance altogether?

Indeed rather, from this word it appears sufficiently that it is not so. For when a man breathes, the soul itself certainly moves the nature of the body lying beneath it, and makes a breath from that, not from itself. Unless perhaps these people are so dull as not to know that by this reciprocal breathing, which we draw in and give back from this air poured around us, a breath also comes to be, when we breathe by our will. But if, even not from this air which lies outside, received and given back, but from the nature of our own body of which it consists, we cast out something by breathing, the nature of the body and of the soul is not the same; which these people too certainly admit. For this reason, even so, the substance of the soul, which rules and moves the body, is one thing; the breath, which by ruling or moving it makes from the body subject to it, not from itself to which the body is subject, is another.

Since, then, although in a way far incomparable, yet the soul rules the subject body, and God the subject creation, why is it not rather understood that God made the soul from the creation subject to him in the fact that he is said to have breathed; since the soul itself, although it does not so rule its body as God rules the universe he founded, yet by its movement makes a breath, not from its own substance?

## On what condition the soul can be called the breath of God.

**3. 5.** We could indeed say that the soul of man is not even the breath of God itself, but that God by breathing made the soul in man: but lest the things he made by his word be thought better than what he made by his breath—because in us too the word is better than the breath—there is meanwhile, according to the reason stated above, nothing to make us doubt to call the soul itself the breath of God, provided it be understood not to be the nature and substance of God, but that this very thing is to breathe, which is to make a breath; and to make a breath is to make a soul.

With which view agrees what God says through Isaiah, *for the spirit will go forth from me, and I have made every breath*[^3]; for that he does not mean any corporeal breath whatever, the following words teach. For when he had said, *I have made every breath; and on account of sin,* he says, *I saddened him a little, and struck him*[^4]. What, then, does he call breath, but the soul, which on account of sin was struck and saddened? What, therefore, is, *I have made every breath,* but, "I have made every soul"?

## That the soul is neither from the substance of God nor from the body of the world.

**4. 6.** If, then, we were to say that God is as it were the soul of this corporeal world, to which the world itself were as it were the body of one living being, we would rightly say that he made the soul of man by breathing only a corporeal one, from this air lying beneath him, from his own body: yet we ought not to suppose that he gave from himself what he gave by breathing, but so from the air subject to him, of his own body, as the soul similarly makes a breath from a thing of this kind lying near—that is, from the body, not from itself. But now, because we say that not only the body of the world is subject to God, but that he is above every creature, whether corporeal or spiritual, he must be believed to have made the soul by breathing neither from himself nor from the corporeal elements.

## It is asked whether the soul is from nothing or from some thing.

**5. 7.** But whether from that which was not at all—that is, from nothing—or from some thing which had already been made by him spiritually, but was not yet soul, can rightly be asked. For if we do not believe that God still creates anything from nothing, after he created all things at once, and on this account believe that he rested from all the completed works which he had begun to make, so that whatever he afterward made, he might make from these—I do not see how we are to understand that he still makes souls from nothing. Or must it be said that in those works of the first six days he made that hidden day, or—if this is rather to be believed—the spiritual and intellectual nature, namely of the angelic unity, and the world, that is, heaven and earth; and that in those natures already existing he created the reasons of other natures to come, not the natures themselves? Otherwise, if they had already been created there as they were to be, would they still be to come? But if this is so, there was not yet in the founded things any nature of the human soul, and it then began to be, when God made it by breathing, and put it into man.

## It is asked whether the breath of God, made from nothing, is the soul of man.

**5. 8.** But the question is not thereby removed, by which it is still asked whether he created from nothing that nature which is called soul, and which did not exist before—as though the breath itself were not made from some underlying substance, as we were saying of the breath which the soul makes from its body; but the breath was then made wholly from nothing, when God willed to breathe, and the same was made the soul of man: or whether there was already something spiritual, although this, whatever it was, was not yet the nature of soul, and from this the breath of God was made which would be the nature of the soul; just as the nature of the human body was not yet, before God had formed it from the clay or dust of the earth. For the human flesh was not dust or clay; but yet there was something from which it might be made, which was not yet.

## It is asked whether, as for the body, so for the soul too, a certain matter preceded.

**6. 9.** Is it, then, credible that in those first works of the six days God founded not only the causal reason of the future human body, but also the matter from which it would be made—that is, the earth, from whose clay or dust it would be formed; but of the soul founded there only the reason according to which it would be made, not also a certain matter, after its kind, from which it would be made? For if the soul were something unchangeable, we ought in no way to seek its matter, as it were: but now its changeableness sufficiently indicates that it is meanwhile rendered deformed by faults and falsehoods, but is formed by virtues and the teaching of truth, yet in its own nature by which it is soul; just as flesh too, in its own nature by which it is now flesh, is both adorned by health and disfigured by diseases and wounds.

But just as flesh—apart from the fact that it is already flesh, in which nature it either advances so as to be beautiful, or fails so as to be deformed—had also matter, that is, earth, from which it would be made, so as to be flesh at all: so perhaps the soul too, before it became that very nature which is called soul, whose beauty is virtue and whose deformity is vice, could have some matter after its kind, spiritual, which was not yet soul; just as the earth from which flesh was made was already something, although it was not flesh.

**6. 10.** But the earth filled the lowest part of the world, before the body of man was made from it, conferring on the universe the whole of itself, so that even if no flesh of any living thing were made from it, yet by its own form it filled out the fabric and mass of the world, according to which the world is called heaven and earth.

## That it cannot be said what that matter would have been.

**7. 10.** But that spiritual matter, if there was any from which the soul would be made, or if there is any from which souls are made—what is it itself? What name, what form, what use does it hold among the founded things? Does it live, or not? If it lives, what does it do? What does it confer on the effects of the universe? Does it lead a blessed life, or a wretched one, or neither? Does it give life to anything? Or is it free even from this work, and does it rest idle in some secret of the universe, without waking sense and vital movement?

For if there was as yet no life at all, how would there be a certain incorporeal and non-living matter of future life? Either it is false, or it lies too deeply hidden. But if it already lived neither blessedly nor wretchedly, how was it rational? But if it was then made rational, when out of that matter the nature of the human soul was made, then was an irrational life the matter of the rational—that is, the human—soul? What, then, distinguished it from that of a beast? Or was it rational already in possibility, not yet in faculty? For if we see that the soul of an infant—already certainly the soul of a man—has not yet begun to use reason, and yet we already call it rational; why should it not be believed that, in that matter from which it was made, the movement even of sensing was at rest, just as in this infant soul, which certainly is already the soul of a man, the movement of reasoning is as yet at rest?

## That that matter could not have been a blessed life.

**8. 11.** For if the life from which the soul of man was made was already blessed, then it was made worse: and therefore that is now not the matter of this, but this is a flowing-down of that. For when any matter is formed, especially by God, it is without doubt formed for the better. But even if the human soul could be understood as the flowing-down of some life made blessed by God, not even so was it to be believed to have begun to be in some act of its own merits, except from the time it began to lead its own life, when, being made a soul, it gave life to flesh, and, using its senses as messengers, and feeling itself to live within itself by its own will, understanding, and memory. For if there is something from which God would breathe this flowing-down into the formed flesh, as it were making a soul by breathing, and the same is blessed; it is in no way moved, or changed, or loses anything, when this flows down from it, from which the soul is made.

## That that matter cannot be a certain irrational soul.

**9. 11.** For it is not a body, that it should be diminished as it were by exhaling.

**9. 12.** But if an irrational soul is in a manner the matter from which the rational—that is, the human—soul is made, then again it is asked whence the irrational one itself is made; because this too is made by none but the creator of all natures. Is it from corporeal matter? Why not then this one too? Unless perhaps someone will deny that God can do by a shortcut what is granted to be done as it were gradually. Accordingly, whatever intermediate step be applied, if a body is the matter of the irrational soul, and the irrational soul is the matter of the rational soul, then without doubt a body is the matter of the rational soul. Which I know no one ever dared to hold, except one who puts the soul itself in the class of some body.

## The transference of souls is exploded.

**9. 13.** Then care must be taken lest it be believed that a certain transference of the soul can happen from a beast into a man (which is wholly contrary to truth and the Catholic faith), if we should grant that an irrational soul lies beneath as matter, as it were, from which the rational soul is made. For thus it would come about that, if this is changed for the better, it will be a man's; that one too, changed for the worse, would be a beast's. At which mockery of certain philosophers even their successors blushed, and said that they did not mean this, but were not rightly understood.

And I believe it is so—as if someone should hold this even from our Scriptures, where it is said, *man, placed in honor, did not understand; he was compared to the senseless beasts, and was made like them*[^5]: or where it is likewise read, *do not deliver to the beasts the soul that confesses to you*[^6]. For not all heretics fail to read the catholic Scriptures; nor are they heretics for any other reason than that, not rightly understanding them, they obstinately assert their own false opinions against the truth of those Scriptures. But however the philosophers' opinion about the revolutions of souls may or may not stand, it does not befit the catholic faith to believe that the souls of beasts pass over into men, or those of men into beasts.

## That from a likeness of conduct it does not follow that a man's soul passes into a beast.

**10. 14.** That men do indeed become like beasts in their manner of life, both human affairs themselves cry out, and Scripture attests. Whence is that which I recorded, *man, placed in honor, did not understand; he was compared to the senseless beasts of burden, and was made like them*[^7]: but in this life, of course, not after death. Accordingly, it was to such beasts that he was unwilling to have his soul given into the power, who said, *do not deliver to the beasts the soul that confesses to you*[^8]; such as the Lord signifies are to be guarded against, where he says they are clothed in the clothing of sheep, but within are ravening wolves: or to the devil himself and his angels; for he too was called a lion and a dragon[^9].

## The philosophers' reasoning for the transference of souls is exploded.

**10. 15.** For what argument do the philosophers bring, who suppose that the souls of men can be transferred into beasts, or those of beasts into men, after death? This, certainly: that a likeness of conduct draws to it—as the greedy into ants, the rapacious into kites, the savage and proud into lions, the followers of unclean pleasure into pigs, and any such things. For they assert these things, and do not attend that, by this reasoning, it can in no way come about that a beast's soul be transferred after death into a man. For in no way will a pig be more like a man than a pig; and when lions grow tame, they become more like dogs or even sheep than men. Since, then, beasts do not depart from the conduct of beasts, and those that become somewhat unlike the rest are nonetheless more like their own kind than the human, and differ far more from men than from beasts: these will never be the souls of men, if the things that are more alike draw them.

But if this argument is false, how will that opinion be true; since they bring forward nothing else by which, even if not true, it may at least be held verisimilar? Whence I too would more readily believe that even their successors and followers wished those men who first put these things in their books to be understood rather in this life: that by a certain perversity and baseness of conduct men become like beasts, and so in a manner are changed into beasts, so that, by casting this disgrace in their teeth, they might recall them from the depravity of their desires.

## Fictitious transmigrations of certain souls.

**11. 16.** For those things that are reported to have happened—that some as it were remembered in the bodies of which animals they had been—are either narrated falsely, or this was done in their minds by the deceptions of demons. For if it happens in dreams that, by a deceptive memory, a man as it were remembers that he was what he was not, or did what he did not do; what wonder if, by a certain just and hidden judgment of God, demons are permitted to be able to do something of the sort in the hearts even of the waking?

## The Manicheans' opinion worse than the philosophers'.

**11. 17.** But the Manicheans, who either think themselves Christians or wish to be thought so, in this opinion of the transference or revolution of souls are, than those philosophers of the nations—or if any other vain men think this—more wicked and more detestable, because those distinguish the nature of the soul from the nature of God, while these, since they say the soul is nothing else than the very substance of God, and altogether that which God is, do not tremble to call it so basely changeable that there is no kind of herb or little worm where they do not, in marvelous madness, suppose it to be mingled, or into which it cannot be revolved.

Yet if they, removing from their mind the questions of the most obscure matters—which, turning over with a carnal heart, they must needs slip and roll forward into false, harmful, and monstrous opinions—would most firmly hold that one thing which is naturally and truly implanted in every rational soul without the windings of any disputation, that God is wholly unchangeable and incorruptible; their whole thousand-formed fable would suddenly collapse, which in their vain and sacrilegious minds they have fashioned out of nothing but the most base changeableness of God.

## That the soul is not from a corporeal element.

**11. 18.** The matter of the human soul, therefore, is not an irrational soul.

**12. 18.** What is it, then, from which the soul was made by the breath of God? Was it some body, earthly indeed and moist? In no way: for from this rather the flesh was made. For what else is clay than moist earth? Nor is the soul to be believed made from moisture alone, as though flesh were from earth and soul from water. For it is too absurd to suppose the soul of man made from that from which the flesh of fish and bird was made.

## That the soul is not from air either.

**12. 19.** From air, then, perhaps? For to this element breath too belongs; but ours, not God's. Whence we said above that this could fittingly be supposed, if we believed God to be the soul of the world as of one greatest living being, so that he breathed it from the air of his body, as ours breathes from the air of its own. But since it is established that God is above every body of the world, and above every spirit he has created, by a distance altogether incomparable; how can this rightly be said? Or perhaps, the more God is present to his whole creation by a singular omnipotence, the more he could make from air a breath that would be the soul of man?

But since the soul is not corporeal, while whatever is made from the corporeal elements of the world must necessarily be corporeal, and this air too is numbered among the elements of the world; not even if the soul were said to be made from the element of that pure and heavenly fire ought it to be believed. For there have not been lacking those who assert that every body can be changed into every body. But that some body, whether earthly or heavenly, is turned into soul, and becomes an incorporeal nature, I know neither that anyone has held, nor does faith hold. Certain opinions of physicians about the body of man.

**13. 20.** Then, if it is not to be despised what physicians not only say but even affirm that they can prove—that although all flesh bears earthly solidity ready at hand, it nonetheless has in it something of air too, which is both contained in the lungs, and spread from the heart through the veins they call arteries; and of fire not only the hot quality, whose seat is in the liver, but also the luminous, which they show is as it were distilled and flies up to the high place of the brain, as into the heaven of our body; whence too the rays of the eyes flash out, and from whose middle, as from a certain center, thin channels are led not only to the eyes but also to the other senses—namely to the ears, to the nostrils, to the palate, for hearing, smelling, and tasting; and that the very sense of touch, which is throughout the whole body, is directed from the same brain through the marrow of the neck, and that which is contained in the bones by which the spine of the back is joined together, so that thence certain most thin little streams, which make the sense of touch, spread through all the members. That the soul is by no means from the corporeal elements.

**14. 20.** Since, then, by these as it were messengers the soul receives whatever of corporeal things is not hidden from it, while it itself is to such a degree something else, that when it wishes to understand either divine things, or God, or even itself altogether, and to consider its own powers, so as to comprehend something true and certain, it turns itself away from this very light of the eyes themselves, and, feeling it to be for this business not only no help but even some hindrance, lifts itself into the gaze of the mind; how is it anything of that kind, when the highest of that kind is nothing but the light which flashes from the eyes, by which it is helped only to sense corporeal forms and colors; while it itself has innumerable things far unlike the whole class of bodies, which it beholds only by understanding and reason, to which no sense of the flesh aspires.

## The soul is by no means a body.

**15.** 21. The nature of the human soul, therefore, is in no way derived from earth, or from water, or from air, or from any fire; and yet it governs the matter of its grosser body—that is, a certain moist earth that has been turned into the quality of flesh—by means of the more subtle nature of the body, namely light and air. For without these two there is in the body neither sensation nor any voluntary motion of the body produced by the soul. And just as knowing must come before doing, so sensing comes before moving. Since, then, the soul is an incorporeal thing, it acts first upon the body that is nearest to the incorporeal—that is, fire, or rather light and air—and through these upon the rest of the body, the grosser parts such as moisture and earth, out of which the solidity of the flesh is built up, which are more subject to being acted upon than endowed with the power of acting.

## Why it is said: Man became a living soul.

**16.** 22. It does not seem to me, then, that it was said *man became a living soul*[^10], except because he began to have sensation in the body, which is the most certain sign of animate and living flesh. For shrubs too are moved, not only by a force impelling them from without, as when they are stirred by the winds, but also by that motion whereby there is stirred within whatever pertains to the growth and form of the tree, the motion by which sap is drawn into the root and turned into the elements that make up the nature of grass or wood—for none of these things happens without an interior motion. But that motion is not voluntary, like the one that is joined to sensation for the governing of the body, as in every kind of animal, which Scripture calls a *living soul*[^11]. For unless that motion too were present in us, our bodies would neither grow nor produce nails and hair. But if this alone were in us, without sensation and that voluntary motion, man would not be said to have been *made a living soul.*

## Why God is said to have breathed into man's face.

**17.** 23. Accordingly, since the front part of the brain, from which all the senses are distributed, is placed toward the forehead, and since the organs of sensation, as it were, are themselves in the face—except for the sense of touch, which is diffused throughout the whole body, though even this is shown to have its path from the same front part of the brain, a path that is led back over the crown and the neck to the spinal marrow of which we were speaking a little earlier, whence the face too has its sense of touch, just as the whole body does, apart from the senses of seeing, hearing, smelling, and tasting, which are situated in the face alone—for this reason I think it was written that *God breathed into man's face the breath of life,* when *he became a living soul*[^12]. For the front part is rightly set before the back part, because the one leads and the other follows, and from the one comes sensation, from the other motion, just as deliberation precedes action.

## On the three ventricles of the brain.

**18.** 24. And since there is no bodily motion following upon sensation without intervals of time, and since we cannot accomplish intervals of time by voluntary motion except with the help of memory, three ventricles, as it were, of the brain are pointed out: one in front, toward the face, from which comes all sensation; another behind, toward the neck, from which comes all motion; and a third between the two, in which they show that memory is active, lest, when motion follows upon sensation, a man fail to connect what is to be done, if he should have forgotten what he has done.

These things they say have been proved to them by sure indications, since, when these very parts were affected by some disease or defect and the functions of sensing, or of moving the limbs, or of recalling bodily motion had failed, it was made sufficiently clear what each was capable of, and, once treatment had been applied, it was ascertained for the recovery of what function it had availed. But the soul acts in these parts as though in instruments; it is none of them itself, but it gives life to and governs them all, and through them it provides for the body and for this life in which man was made a living soul.

## The excellence of the soul above all corporeal things.

**19.** 25. When, therefore, the question is asked whence the soul itself comes—that is, from what matter, as it were, God made this breath that is called the soul—nothing corporeal ought to come to mind. For just as God surpasses every creature, so the soul surpasses every corporeal creation in dignity of nature. Yet through light and air, which even in the world itself are the more excellent corporeal things and have more of the power of acting than the bulk for being acted upon that moisture and earth have, it governs the body by means of those things that are more like the spirit. For corporeal light announces something; but the one to whom it announces is not the same as that light; and it is the soul to which it announces, not the thing that announces.

And when the soul feels the afflictions of the body with distress, it finds that its own activity, by which it is present to the body to govern it, is impeded by the disturbance of the body's balance, and this hindrance is called pain. And the air that is infused into the nerves obeys the will so as to move the limbs, yet it is not itself the will. And that middle part announces the motion of the limbs so that it may be held in memory, yet it is not itself the memory.

Finally, when these services of the soul, as it were, fail altogether through any defect or disturbance, then, the messengers of sensation and the agents of motion having ceased, the soul withdraws, as having no reason to be present. But if they do not fail in the way that usually happens at death, its attention is disturbed, like one trying to restore what is slipping away and not having the power to do so. And in the things by which it is disturbed, it is thereby recognized which part of the services is the cause, so that, if it can, medicine may bring relief.

## That the soul is one thing and the organs of the body another.

**20.** 26. For that the soul itself is one thing and these its corporeal services, or vessels, or organs—or whatever they may more fitly be called—are another, becomes clearly evident from this: that very often, by an intense effort of thought, it turns itself away from everything, so that it fails to notice many things set before eyes that are open and seeing rightly; and if the effort is greater, while it was walking it suddenly halts, turning away the command to act, as it were, from the service of the motion by which the feet were being moved; but if the effort of thought is not so great as to fix the walker in place, yet great enough that it has no leisure to attend to that middle part of the brain which announces the body's motion, then sometimes it forgets both where it is coming from and where it is going, and unawares it passes by the farmstead to which it was heading—the nature of its own body sound, but its own attention called away to something else.

It is therefore not a question pertinent to our purpose whether God mingled or joined to the body of the living being those corporeal particles of the corporeal heaven—that is, of light and air—which first receive the impulses of the life-giving soul, in that they are nearer to incorporeal nature than moisture and earth are, so that the whole mass may be governed through their proximate service, drawing them from this heaven that surrounds and overspreads us; or whether he made even these too out of the mire, as he did the flesh. For it is credible that any body can be changed into any body; but to believe that any body can be changed into a soul is absurd.

## That we must not suppose there is a fifth element of the world out of which the soul is made.

**21.** 27. For this reason neither must we give ear to what certain men have supposed: that there is a kind of fifth body from which souls come, which is neither earth, nor water, nor air, nor fire—whether the more turbulent and earthly fire or that pure and shining heavenly fire—but I know not what else that lacks the usual name, yet is nonetheless a body[^13]. For if those who hold this opinion mean by it a body such as we too mean—that is, any nature that occupies a place by length, breadth, and depth—then the soul is neither this nor to be believed made from it. For whatever is of such a kind can, in any part of itself, be divided or circumscribed by lines, to say no more; and if the soul were subject to this, it could in no way know such lines as cannot be cut lengthwise, lines such as it nonetheless knows cannot be found in a body.

## The whole soul knows the whole of itself.

**21.** 28. Nor does anything of that sort present itself to the soul about itself, since it cannot fail to know itself, even when it inquires into itself in order to know itself. For when it seeks itself, it knows that it is seeking itself; and this it could not know if it did not know itself. For it seeks itself from nowhere else than from itself. When, therefore, it knows itself as seeking, it certainly knows itself; and everything it knows, the whole of it knows. Since, then, it knows itself as seeking, the whole of it knows itself, and therefore it knows the whole of itself; for it knows the whole not of anything else, but of itself. Why, then, does it still seek itself, if it knows itself as seeking? For if it did not know itself, it could not know that it is seeking itself.

But this is in the present; what it seeks about itself is what it was before, or what it will be. Let it then cease for now, in the meantime, to suspect that it is a body; for if it were anything of that kind, it would know itself to be such, seeing that it knows itself better than it knows heaven and earth, which it knows through the eyes of its body.

## On the power of the soul by which it retains in memory the likenesses of bodies.

**21.** 29. I forbear to say that that power of it which even beasts are understood to possess, or the birds of the air when they make their way back to their own dwellings or nests, the power by which images of all corporeal things are taken in, is in no way like any body; and surely it ought rather to be that part that resembles a body, the part in which the likenesses of corporeal things are contained. But if this is not a body, since it is certain that the likenesses of bodies are there not only retained in memory but even shaped in countless ways at will, how much less can the soul be like a body by any other power of its own?

## Why the soul is called "the spirit of life."

**21.** 30. But if, by some other notion, they call a body everything that exists—that is, every nature and substance—this way of speaking is indeed not to be admitted, lest we find no way, in speaking, of distinguishing the things that are not bodies from bodies; yet we need not labor too much over the word. For we too say that whatever the soul is, it is not one of these four most familiar elements, which are manifestly bodies; but neither is it that which God is. What it is, however, is not better stated than as soul or spirit of life. For "of life" is added because this air also is often called spirit. And yet they have called this same air "soul" as well, so that no name can now be found by which to distinguish properly this nature which is neither a body, nor God, nor a life without sensation, such as can be believed to be in trees, nor a life without a rational mind, such as is in beasts—but a life now lesser than that of the angels, and destined to be what the angels' is, if it lives here according to the command of its creator.

## Conclusion concerning the nature of the soul.

**21.** 31. But whence it comes—that is, from what matter, as it were, it was made, or from what perfect and blessed nature it flowed down, or whether it was made altogether out of nothing—even if this is doubted and inquired into, this at least must by no means be doubted: that, if anything existed before, what existed was made by God, and that it has now been made by God to be a living soul; for either it was nothing, or what it is, it was not. But that part in which we were inquiring, as it were, into the matter from which it was made, we have now treated sufficiently.

## It is asked whether the causal reason of the soul was established in the days of Genesis.

**22.** 32. Now, if it did not exist at all before, we must ask how it can be understood that its causal reason was said to have existed in the first works of God's six days, when God made man to his own image, which is not rightly understood except with respect to the soul. But we must beware lest, when we say that God then created, not the natures and substances themselves that were to be, while he was creating all things at once, but certain causal reasons of those future things, we be thought to be saying something empty. For what are these causal reasons, according to which God could already be said to have made man to his own image, whose body he had not yet formed from the mire, and for whom he had not yet made a soul by breathing? And of the human body indeed, even if there was some hidden reason by which it was to come about that it would be formed, there was also a matter from which it would be formed, namely earth, in which that reason can be seen to have lain hidden, as it were, in a seed; but for the making of the soul—that is, for the making of the breath that was to be man's soul—what causal reason was first established there, when God said, *let us make man to our image and likeness*[^14] (which cannot rightly be understood except with respect to the soul), if there was no nature in which it might be established?

## Whether the causal reason of the soul is in a spiritual nature.

**22.** 33. For if this reason was in God, not in the creation, then it had not yet been established; how, then, was it said: *God made man to the image of God*[^15]? But if it was already in the creation—that is, in those things that God had created all at once—in what creation was it? In a spiritual one, or in a corporeal one? If in a spiritual one, was it doing anything in the bodies of the world, whether heavenly or earthly? Or was this reason idle in it before man was established in his own proper nature, just as in man himself, who already leads his own proper life, there is latently and idly present the reason of generating, which does not operate except through intercourse and conception? Or did even that spiritual creation, in which this reason was latently present, do nothing of its own work? And to what end had it been created? Was it in order to contain the reason of the future human soul, or of future souls, as though they could not exist in themselves, but only in some creation already living its own life—just as the reason of generating cannot exist except in certain already existing and complete natures?

Some spiritual creation, then, was established as the parent of the soul, in which there would be the reason of the future soul, which does not come into existence from it except when God makes it to be breathed into man. For neither does anyone but God create and form, from man, the offspring of either the seed or the offspring itself already produced, through that Wisdom *that reaches everywhere by reason of her purity, so that nothing defiled can enter into her*[^16], while *she reaches mightily from one end to the other and orders all things sweetly*[^17].

But I do not know how it can be understood that there was created, for this purpose only, some spiritual creation or other, which was not mentioned among the things God established during those six days, since God is said to have made man on the sixth day—man whom he had not yet made in his own proper nature, but who was still present in causal reason in that creation which is not mentioned. For it ought rather to have been mentioned, since it had been so completed that it was not yet to be made according to a reason preceding it.

## That the causal reason of the soul was by no means implanted in the angelic nature.

**23.** 34. Or did God perhaps implant this causal reason for making the soul in the nature of that day which he first established—if that day is rightly understood as the intellectual spirit—when on the sixth day he made man to his own image, fixing beforehand, that is, the cause and reason according to which he would make him after those seven days; so that the causal reason of his body might be believed to have been created in the nature of earth, but that of his soul in the nature of that day? But what else is said, when this is said, than that the angelic spirit is, as it were, the parent of the human soul, if the preestablished reason for creating the human soul is present in it just as the reason of his future offspring is present in man—so that the parents of human bodies are men, but of souls the angels, while the creator of both souls and bodies is God, yet of bodies from men, of souls from angels; or of the first body from earth, and of the first soul from the angelic nature, where he had fixed beforehand their causal reasons, when he first made man among the things he created all at once; but thereafter men from men, body from body, soul from soul?

It is hard to call the soul the daughter of an angel or of angels; but far harder to call it the daughter of the corporeal heaven; how much more, then, of the sea and the earth?

Much less, therefore, was the causal reason of the soul preestablished in any corporeal creation, when God made man to his own image, before he animated him with the breath, formed in his own time from the mire—if it is absurd to believe that he established the soul causally in the angelic nature.

## First conjecture: the soul exists before it comes into the body.

**24.** 35. Let us see, then, whether perhaps this may be true—which certainly seems to me more tolerable to human opinion: that God, in those first works in which he created all things at once, also created the human soul, which in its own time he would breathe into the limbs of a body formed from the mire; and that the reason of this body he created causally among those things established all at once, according to which the human body would be made when it was to be made. For we rightly understand neither what was said, *to his own image,* except with respect to the soul, nor what was said, *male and female,* except with respect to the body.

Let it be believed, then, if no authority of the Scriptures or reason of truth contradicts it, that man was so made on the sixth day that the causal reason of the human body was in the elements of the world, but the soul itself was already created, just as the day was first established, and, once created, lay hidden in the works of God until in its own time he should insert it, by breathing—that is, by inspiring—into the body formed from the mire.

## If the soul existed outside the body, did it come into the body by its own will?

**25.** 36. But here again a question arises that is not to be despised. For if the soul had already been made and lay hidden, where could it be better off than there? What reason was there, then, that a soul living innocently should be inserted into the life of this flesh, in which, by sinning, it would offend the very one who created it, so that there would deservedly follow upon it the hardship of toil and the torment of damnation? Or must it be said that by its own will it was inclined to govern the body, so that in this life of the body—since one can live both justly and unjustly—it would have, according to what it chose, either a reward for justice or a punishment for injustice, so that this is not contrary to that apostolic statement in which he says that those not yet born had done nothing good or evil[^18]? For that inclination of the will toward the body is not yet an act either of justice or of injustice, for which an account is to be rendered at the judgment of God, when each one will receive according to the things he has done through the body, whether good or evil[^19].

Why, then, may not this too be believed: that it came to the body at God's bidding, where, if it should be willing to act according to his command, it would receive the reward of eternal life and the fellowship of the angels, but if it should despise him, it would pay the most just penalties, whether of long toil or of eternal fire? Or is it that this very thing—to have obeyed God's will—is already a good act, and so it will be contrary to say that those not yet born had done nothing either good or evil?

## If the soul was inserted into the body by its own will, it had no foreknowledge of the future.

**26.** 37. If these things are so, we shall acknowledge that the soul was not first created in such a class of things that it would have foreknowledge of its own future deed, whether just or unjust. For it is exceedingly incredible that it could have been inclined by its own will to the life of the body, if it had foreknown that it would so sin in certain respects as to be justly punished with perpetual torment. The Creator is rightly praised in all things, he who made all things very good. For he is not to be praised only on account of those to whom he gave foreknowledge, since he is rightly praised even because he created the beasts, than which human nature is more excellent even in those human beings who sin. For the nature of man is from God, not the iniquity in which he involves himself by misusing free choice—which, however, if he did not have, he would be less excellent in the nature of things. For a man living justly must be conceived of even as not foreknowing the future, and there it must be seen how little the excellence of a good will is impeded, with respect to living rightly and pleasing God, by the fact that he lives by faith, ignorant of the future. Whoever, then, would not wish such a creature to exist among things contradicts the goodness of God. But whoever would not wish it to pay penalties for its sins is an enemy of equity.

## That the soul is borne into the body by a natural appetite.

**27.** 38. But if the soul is made for this purpose, that it be sent into the body, it can be asked whether, if it should be unwilling, it is compelled. But it is better to believe that it wills this naturally—that is, that it is created in such a nature that it wills it—just as it is natural for us to will to live; but to live badly is no longer of nature, but of a perverse will, which punishment justly follows.

## It is more fittingly believed that the soul was made from spiritual matter.

**27.** 39. In vain, then, is it now asked from what matter, as it were, the soul was made, if it can rightly be understood to have been made among those first works, when the day was made. For just as those things that did not exist were made, so this too was made among them. But if there was also some formable matter, both corporeal and spiritual, yet it too was not established except by God, from whom are all things—matter that would precede its own formation not in time but in origin, as the voice precedes the song—what should we more fittingly believe than that the soul was made from spiritual matter?

## Second conjecture: that the soul was created together with the body.

**28.** 40. But if anyone is unwilling to think that the soul was made except when it was breathed into a body already formed, let him see what he answers when it is asked whence it was made. For either he will say that God made or makes something out of nothing after that completion of his works—and he must consider how he is to explain that man was made to the image of God on the sixth day (which cannot rightly be understood except with respect to the soul), that is, in what nature the causal reason was made of that thing which did not yet exist; or he will say that the soul was made not from nothing but from something already existing, and he will labor in inquiring what that nature is, whether corporeal or spiritual, according to the questions we have turned over above—that other difficulty also remaining, namely that it must still be asked in what substance of the creatures first established in the six days he made that causal reason of the soul which he had not yet made either from nothing or from something.

## Difficulties from Scripture concerning the origin and creation of the soul.

**28.** 41. And if he should wish to avoid this in such a way as to say that on the sixth day man too was made from the mire, but that this was mentioned later by way of recapitulation, let him see what he says about the woman, since he said, *male and female he made them, and he blessed them*[^20]. And if he should answer that she too was made on that day from the man's bone, let him attend to how he is to maintain that the birds were made on the sixth day, which were brought to Adam, when Scripture intimates that every kind of bird was created from the waters on the fifth day; likewise that on the sixth day were made even the trees that were planted in paradise, when the same Scripture has assigned this kind of creature to be spoken of on the third day.

Let him also consider the very words, what it means: *he brought forth besides from the earth every tree beautiful to behold and good for food*[^21]—as though those trees the earth had brought forth on the third day were not beautiful to behold and good for food, when they were among those works that God made all very good; and also what it means: *God further formed from the earth all the beasts of the field and all the birds of the heaven*[^22]—as though those that had first been produced were not all of them, or rather as though none had been produced before. For it was not said: *and God further formed from the earth* the remaining beasts of the field and the remaining birds of the heaven, as though the earth had produced fewer on the sixth day or the water fewer on the fifth; but, *all the beasts,* he says, *and all the birds.*

Let him also reflect on this: how God made all things in six days—first, the day itself; second, the firmament; third, the form of sea and land, and from the earth grass and trees; fourth, the lights and stars; fifth, the animals of the waters; sixth, those of the earth—and that afterward it is said: *when the day was made, God made heaven and earth, and every green thing of the field*[^23]; whereas when the day was made, he made nothing but the day itself. And how he made every green thing of the field before it was upon the earth, and all the hay before it sprang up[^24]. For who would say that it was made when it sprang up, and not before it sprang up, unless the words of Scripture called us back? Let him also remember that it is written: *he who lives forever created all things together*[^25]; and let him see how those things can be said to be created together whose creation is separated by intervals of time, not only of hours but even of days.

Let him also take care to show how both can be true, though they may seem contrary—both that God rested on the seventh day from all his works, as the book of Genesis says, and that he works even until now, as the Lord says. Let him also consider how the things said to be completed are likewise said to be begun.

## How God made things present and future.

**28.** 42. For by all these testimonies of divine Scripture, which no one doubts to be truthful except an unbeliever or an impious man, we have been led to that opinion, so that we said that God from the beginning of the world first created all things together, some with their natures already established, others with their causes preestablished; just as the Almighty made not only things present but also things future, and rested from them once made, so that thereafter, by their administration and governance, he might create also the orders of times and of temporal things—because he had both completed them, for the determination of all kinds, and begun them, for the propagation of the ages, so that on account of the things completed he might rest, and on account of the things begun he might work even until now. But if these things can be better understood, not only do I not resist, but I even favor it.

## Conclusion: what is to be firmly held concerning the soul, and what, being matters of opinion, is better further investigated.

**28.** 43. For the present, however, concerning the soul, which God breathed into man by breathing into his face, I affirm nothing except that it is from God in such a way that it is not the substance of God; and that it is incorporeal—that is, that it is not a body, but a spirit; not begotten of the substance of God, nor proceeding from the substance of God, but made by God; and not made in such a way that any nature of body or of irrational soul was turned into its nature; and therefore made from nothing; and that it is immortal according to a certain mode of life which it can in no way lose; but that according to a certain mutability, by which it can become either worse or better, it can not undeservedly be understood as even mortal—since he alone has true immortality of whom it was properly said: *who alone has immortality*[^26].

May the other things I have said in the course of debating in this book avail the reader to this end: that he may either learn how things that Scripture does not state openly are to be inquired into without rashness of affirming; or, if this manner of inquiring does not please him, may let me know how he himself has inquired, so that, if he can teach me, he may not refuse, but if he cannot, may seek along with me from him by whom we both may learn.
