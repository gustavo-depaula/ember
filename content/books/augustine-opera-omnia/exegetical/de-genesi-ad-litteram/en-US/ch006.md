# Book V

## That the days of Genesis could have been numbered by the repetition of one day.

**1. 1.** *This is the book of the creation of heaven and earth, when the day was made, God made heaven and earth, and every green thing of the field before it was upon the earth, and all the grass of the field before it sprang up. For God had not rained upon the earth, and there was no man to till the earth. But a spring rose up from the earth and watered the whole face of the earth*[^1]*.*

Now certainly that view becomes firmer by which it is understood that God made one day, from which now those six or seven days could be numbered by the repetition of this one; since Holy Scripture now says more openly, concluding in a manner all the things it had said from the beginning up to this place, and adds, *this is the book of the creation* or making *of heaven and earth, when the day was made.* For no one will say that heaven and earth are recorded here as they had been spoken of before the day was intimated to be founded, *in the beginning God made heaven and earth*[^2]. For if that is understood in such a way that God made something without a day, before he made the day, in what sense this can be taken I have said in its own place what I thought ought to be said, shutting off from no one the liberty of understanding it better.

But now, *this is,* he says, *the book of the creation of heaven and earth, when the day was made*: showing sufficiently, as I suppose, that he has not here recorded heaven and earth as in the beginning before the day was made, when there was darkness over the deep; but as heaven and earth were made when the day was made—that is, with the parts and kinds of things now formed and distinguished, by which the whole of creation, arranged and composed, presents this form which is called the world.

## Heaven and earth "before" and "after" the day was created.

**1. 2.** Here, then, that heaven is recorded which God, when he had created it, called the firmament, with all the things that are in it; and that earth, which together with the deep holds the lowest place, with all the things that are in it. For it follows and adds, *God made heaven and earth*; so that by the name of heaven and earth, both set down before he recorded the making of the day, and repeated once he had recorded it, he does not allow it to be suspected that he has now named heaven and earth in the way he did in the beginning, before the day was created.

For he so wove the words together, *this is the book of the creation of heaven and earth, when the day was made, God made heaven and earth*[^3]: that if anyone should wish to understand what was set down above, *the book of the creation of heaven and earth,* in the way it was said, *in the beginning God made heaven and earth,* before he founded the day—because here too heaven and earth are recorded first, and afterward the day was made—he is corrected by the words that follow: because even after the recording of the day being made, the name of heaven and earth is again added.

## The wording of Genesis 2:4 is further explained.

**1. 3.** Although even the fact that *when* is set down, and *the day was made* is so attached, would wrest from any contentious person the admission that there can be no other understanding. For if it were so interposed that it said, "this is the book of the creation of heaven and earth. The day was made, God made heaven and earth," someone might perhaps suppose that "the book of the creation of heaven and earth" was so called in the way it was called, *in the beginning God made heaven and earth,* before the day was founded; and then there was subjoined, *the day was made,* as there it was afterward narrated that God made the day: thence immediately again it was said, *God made heaven and earth,* as though now in the way these things were made after the day was founded.

But because it was so interposed that it said, *when the day was made*—whether you connect this with the preceding words, so that it is one sentence, *this is the book of the creation of heaven and earth when the day was made*; or with the following, so that likewise in this way it is a full sentence, *when the day was made, God made heaven and earth*—it compels us beyond doubt to understand that he recorded heaven and earth in the way they were made, when the day was made. Then, when it had been said, *God made heaven and earth*, there was added, *and every green thing of the field*[^4]: which it is certainly evident were made on the third day. From which it appears more clearly that it is that same one day which God made, by whose repetition the second too was made, and the third, and the rest, up to the seventh day.

## Why "the green thing of the field" is added.

**2. 4.** But since by the name of *heaven and earth,* by the usual practice of the Scriptures, he wished the whole of creation now to be understood, it can be asked why he added, *and every green thing of the field*; which it seems to me he set down for this reason, that he might more significantly intimate which day he commended, when he said, *when the day was made.*

For one might quickly suppose that the day commended here was that of corporeal light, by whose going round the alternation of daytime and nighttime is exhibited to us. But when we recall the order of the things founded, we also find that every green thing of the field was created on the third day, before the sun was made, which was made on the fourth day, by whose presence this daily and usual day is carried out.

When we hear, *when the day was made, God made heaven and earth, and every green thing of the field*, we are admonished to think of that day which we should try to track down by the understanding, whether as corporeal by some light unknown to us, or as spiritual in the society of angelic unity—yet not such as we know here.

## From the order of the narrative it is understood that all things were created at once.

**3. 5.** It will also be not beside the point to observe this: that, although he could have said, *this is the book of the creation of heaven and earth, when God made heaven and earth,* so that in *heaven and earth* we might understand whatever is in them, as Divine Scripture is accustomed to speak.

So that by the name of *heaven and earth* it very often—sometimes with *and the sea* added—suggests the whole of creation, occasionally adding and saying, *and the things that are in them*[^5], so that, whichever of these he said, we should understand there the day too, whether the one he first founded, or this one which the presence of the sun makes; he did not speak thus, but interposed the day, saying, *when the day was made.*

Nor did he speak so as to say, "this is the book of the creation of the day and heaven and earth," as though in the order in which the things made are narrated; nor thus, "this is the book of the creation of heaven and earth, when the day was made, and heaven and earth, when God made heaven and earth and every green thing of the field"; nor thus, "this is the book of the creation of heaven and earth, when God made the day and heaven and earth and every green thing of the field"; nor thus, "this is the book of the creation of heaven and earth. God made the day and heaven and earth and every green thing of the field."

For the custom of speaking rather called for these manners of expression; but he said, *this is the book of the creation of heaven and earth, when the day was made, God made heaven and earth and every green thing of the field,* as though suggesting this: that when the day was made, then God made heaven and earth and every green thing of the field.

## From the creation of the shrubs the sevenfold repetition of one day is proved.

**3. 6.** Moreover, the earlier narrative indicates the day made first, and reckons it *one day*; after which it counts the second, on which the firmament was made; and the third, on which the forms of land and sea were arranged, and the earth brought forth tree and herb. Or is this perhaps that which we labored to show in the earlier book—that God made all things at once—since that connection of the narrative, when it had recorded all things created and completed in the order of six days, now reduces all things to one day under the name of heaven and earth, with the kind of shrubs also added? No doubt for the reason I said above: so that if perhaps the day were understood from this custom of ours, the reader might be corrected, when he recalled that God said the green thing of the field was to be brought forth by the earth before that solar day. Thus now the testimony that God created all things at once is not brought forward from another book of Holy Scripture[^6]; but the nearby attestation of the following page admonishes us from this very matter, saying, *when the day was made, God made heaven and earth, and every green thing of the field.*

So that you may understand this day to have been repeated seven times, that there might be seven days; and when you hear that all things were then made, when the day was made, you may grasp, if you can, that sixfold or sevenfold repetition as made without intervals of delays or temporal spans.

If you cannot yet, leave these things to be beheld by those who are able; but you, advance with the Scripture that does not desert your weakness, and with motherly step walks more slowly along with you.

Which so speaks that by its height it mocks the proud, by its depth terrifies the attentive, by its truth feeds the great, by its kindliness nourishes the little ones.

## Why the green grass is said to have been made before it sprang up.

**4. 7.** What, then, does even what follows mean? For the discourse is woven thus: *when the day was made, God made heaven and earth, and every green thing of the field before it was upon the earth, and all the grass of the field before it sprang up*[^7]*?* What is this? Is it not to be asked where he made these things before they were upon the earth, and before they sprang up?

For who would not more readily believe that God made these things then, when they sprang up, not before they sprang up, unless he were admonished by the divine word that God made them before they sprang up—so that, even if he cannot find where they were made, anyone who piously believes this Scripture may nonetheless believe them made before they sprang up; for it is impious not to believe it.

## The things that are in the Word before all creation were by no means created.

**4. 8.** What, then, shall we say? Is it what some have supposed, that all things were made in the Word of God itself, before they sprang up on the earth? But if they were made in this way, they were made not when the day was made, but before the day was made; yet Scripture says openly, *when the day was made, God made heaven and earth, and every green thing of the field before it was upon the earth, and all the grass before it sprang up.*

If, then, it was when the day was made, then certainly not before the day was made; and thus not in the Word, which is coeternal with the Father before the day, before anything at all was made, but when the day was made. For those things that are in the Word of God before all creation were certainly not made; but these were made, when the day was made, as the words of Scripture declare—yet before they were upon the earth and before they sprang up; which was said of the green things and the grass of the field.

## The green things were created, as in their seeds, in their causal reasons.

**4. 9.** Where, then? Was it in the earth itself, causally and according to reason—just as in seeds all things already are, before they unfold in a manner, and unfold their growths and forms through the numbers of times? But those seeds that we see are already upon the earth, already sprung up: or were they not upon the earth, but beneath the earth; and therefore, before they sprang up, they were made, because they then sprang up when the seeds germinated, and by the addition of growths broke out into the open air—which we now see done through the delays of times, distributed each to its own kind?

Were the seeds, then, made "when the day was made," and was in them every green thing of the field, and all the grass—not yet in that form in which they are upon the earth, already sprung up, but in that power by which they are in the reasons of the seeds? Did the earth, then, first bring forth the seeds?

But Scripture did not speak thus, when it said, *and the earth brought forth the herb of pasture,* or the herb of grass, *sowing seed according to its kind and according to its likeness; and the fruitful tree making fruit, whose seed is in itself according to its kind upon the earth*[^8]*.* For by these words it rather appears that the seeds arose from the herbs and trees, and not those from seeds, but from the earth—especially because the very words of God are thus. For he does not say: let the seeds in the earth bring forth the herb of grass and the fruitful tree; but he says, *let the earth bring forth the herb of grass sowing seed*; so as to suggest the seed from the herb, not the herb from the seed. *And so it was made, and the earth brought forth*[^9]; that is, first it was so made in the knowledge of that day, and the earth then brought forth, so that this might also come to be in the creature itself, which was founded.

## How creatures are known by the angel and by man.

**4. 10.** How, then, were they before they were upon the earth, and before they sprang up—as though it was one thing for them to be made with heaven and earth, when that day was made, unusual and unknown to us, which God first made; and another to spring up now upon the earth, which happens only through these days that the going round of the sun makes through the delays of times, adapted each to its own kind?

And if this is so, and that day is the society and unity of the angels and virtues of the heights of heaven, then without doubt God's creation is known far otherwise to the angels than to us: apart from the fact that they have known it in the Word of God, *through whom all things were made,* I say that even in itself it is known to them far otherwise than to us.

For to them it is known primordially, so to speak, or originally, as God first founded it, and after founding it rested from his works by founding nothing further; but to us according to the administration of things founded before, now through the orders of times, according to which God, now that those things have been completed in the perfection of six, works up to now[^10].

## How creation causally differs from creation visibly.

**4. 11.** Causally, then, was it said that the earth brought forth herb and tree—that is, that it received the power of bringing them forth. For in it, as though in roots, so to speak, of times, had been made the things that were to be through times. For surely afterward *God planted a Paradise toward the east, and brought forth there from the earth every tree beautiful to the sight and good for food*[^11].

Yet it must not be said that he then added anything to creation which he had not made before, which should as it were be added afterward to that perfection by which he completed all things very good on the sixth day; but because all the natures of shrubs and trees had already been made in the first founding, from which founding God rested—then moving and administering, through their temporal courses, those very things that he had founded, and from which, once founded, he rested—he not only then planted Paradise, but even now makes all the things that are born.

For who else even now creates these things, but he who *is working still*? But he creates them now from those that already exist; while then they were created by him when they did not exist at all, when that day was made, which itself also did not exist at all—the spiritual and intellectual creature, namely.

## That time began from the creature.

**5. 12.** And so, once creatures were made, times began to run with their movements; whence times are sought in vain before the creature, as though times could be found before times. For if there were no movement, whether of the spiritual or of the corporeal creature, by which the future would succeed the past through the present, there would be no time at all. But the creature certainly could not be moved if it did not exist. Time, therefore, rather began from the creature than the creature from time; but both are from God. For *from him, and through him, and in him are all things*[^12]. Nor is what was said—time began from the creature—to be taken as though time were not a creature; since it is the movement of the creature from one thing to another, as things follow according to the ordering of God, who administers all that he created.

For this reason, when we think of the first founding of the creatures from which works God rested on the seventh day, we ought not to think of those days as like these solar days, nor of that working as in the way God now works something in time; but as he worked so that times might begin, as he worked all things at once, granting them also an order, not by intervals of times, but by the connection of causes, so that the things made at once might also be perfected when the number six of that day was made present.

## That the temporal order is other than the causal order.

**5. 13.** Not, then, in a temporal but in a causal order was the formless and formable matter first made, both spiritual and corporeal, from which would come what was to be made, since it too, before it was established, had not existed; nor was it established except by that supreme and true God, from whom all things are. Whether this was signified by the name of heaven and earth, which God made in the beginning before that one day which he founded—therefore already so called, because from it heaven and earth were made—or by the name of the invisible and unformed earth and the dark deep, has already been treated in the first book.

## In what order and by what plan all things were founded.

**5. 14.** But among the things that are now formed out of formlessness, and are more evidently called created, or made, or founded, the day was made first. For it was fitting that the primacy of creation be held by that nature which could recognize the creature through the Creator, not the Creator through the creature.

Second, the firmament, from which the corporeal world begins. Third, the forms of sea and land, and in the land, potentially, so to speak, the nature of herbs and trees. For thus the earth brought them forth at God's word before they sprang up, receiving all their numbers, which it would put forth through times according to its kind.

Then, after this as it were dwelling of things was founded, on the fourth day the lights and stars were created, so that the upper part of the world might first be adorned with the visible things that move within the world.

Fifth, the nature of the waters, because it is joined to heaven and air, brought forth at God's word its own native creatures—that is, all swimming and flying things; and these potentially in their numbers, which would be put forth through the fitting movements of times.

Sixth, the land animals likewise, as the last from the last element of the world; nonetheless potentially, whose numbers time would afterward unfold visibly.

## On the first day and the other seven days.

**5. 15.** All this order of ordered creation that day knew; and through this knowledge, made present as it were six times, it exhibited as it were six days, although it is one day—recognizing the things that were made first in the Creator, and consequently in them themselves, not remaining in them, but referring even their later knowledge to the love of God, it afforded evening and morning and noon in all things; not through the delays of times, but on account of the order of the things founded. Finally, representing the knowledge of the rest of its Creator—because he rests in himself from all his works—in which it has no evening, it merited for this reason to be blessed and sanctified. Whence Scripture commends the number seven itself as in a manner dedicated to the Holy Spirit[^13], and the Church knows it.

## Conclusion of the foregoing interpretations.

**5. 16.** This, then, is the book of the creation of heaven and earth, because *in the beginning God made heaven and earth*[^14], according to a certain formability of matter, so to speak, which was consequently to be formed by his word, preceding its own formation, not in time, but in origin. For surely *when it was being formed, the day was made first; when the day was made, God made heaven and earth, and every green thing of the field before it was upon the earth, and all the grass of the field before it sprang up*, as we have treated; or if anything more clear and more fitting could be or could have been seen and said.

## Why the grass of the field was created before it rained.

**6. 17.** But what follows, *for God had not rained upon the earth, and there was no man to till the earth*[^15], where it belongs and what it suggests to us, is difficult to track down. As though God made the grass of the field before it sprang up for the reason that he had not yet rained upon the earth: for if he had made the grass after the rain, it would seem to have sprung up by the rain rather than to have been made by him. For what then? What springs up after the rain—is it made by any other than the same God?

But why was there no man to till the earth? Had he not already made man on the sixth day, and rested on the seventh from all his works? Or does he record this by way of recapitulation, since when God made every green thing of the field, and all the grass, it had not yet rained upon the earth, and there was not yet a man? For on the third day he made these things, but man on the sixth. But when God made every green thing and all the grass of the field before it sprang up upon the earth, not only was there no man to till the earth, but neither was the grass itself upon the earth, which he certainly says was made before it sprang up. Or did God make these things on the third day for the reason that there was not yet a man who, by tilling the earth, would make them? As if so many trees and so many kinds of herbs were not born from the earth without any labor of men.

## Rain and the labor of man as regards the things the earth brings forth.

**6. 18.** Or were both set down for this reason—both that it had not yet rained upon the earth, and that there was not yet a man to till the earth? For where human labor is absent, these things are born by means of rain. But there are some that are not born even by means of rain unless human labor is added. Therefore now both aids are necessary, that all things may be born; but then either of them was lacking; and so God made these things by the power of his Word, without rain, without human labor.

For even now he himself makes them, but now by means of rain and the hands of men; *although neither he who plants is anything, nor he who waters, but God who gives the increase*[^16].

## The spring that watered the earth, rain, and the creation of green things.

**6. 19.** What, then, is it that he adds, *but a spring rose up from the earth and watered the whole face of the earth*[^17]? For that spring, flowing with such abundance, like the Nile for Egypt, could have served the whole earth in place of rain. Why, then, was it commended as something great that God made those growing things before it rained, when rain could have helped as much as a spring watering the earth? Yet even if somewhat less, those things would perhaps have been born smaller, but not none at all.

Or does Scripture here too, after its manner, speak weakly as to the weak, and yet hint at something that one who is able may understand? For surely, just as by that day recorded a little above it signified that one day was made by God, and that God then made heaven and earth, when the day was made—so that we might think, as best we could, that God made all things at once, although the earlier enumeration of six days seemed to have shown intervals of times, as it were.

So, when he had said that, with heaven and earth, God made every green thing of the field before it was upon the earth, and all the grass of the field before it sprang up, he added, *for God had not yet rained upon the earth, nor was there a man to till the earth*[^18]; as though he said, "God did not make these things in the way he now makes such things, when it rains and when men labor." For these things now happen through the delays of times, which were not then, when he made all things at once, from which times too might begin.

## That all the first beginnings of seeds are moist and grow together from moisture.

**7. 20.** But what follows, *but a spring rose up from the earth and watered the whole face of the earth*[^19]—from here, as I judge, it is now suggested what things come to be according to the intervals of times out of that first founding of creatures, where all things were made at once. And rightly did he begin from that element out of which all kinds are born, whether of animals, or of herbs and trees, so that they may carry out their temporal numbers distributed to their proper natures. For all the first beginnings of seeds, whether of all flesh or whence all shrubbery is brought forth, are moist, and grow together from moisture. But in them are the most efficacious numbers, drawing along with them their consequent powers from those perfected works of God from which he rested on the seventh day.

## What spring was that spring of Genesis 2:6?

**7. 21.** Nonetheless, what this spring was, capable of watering the whole face of the earth, is rightly asked. For if it existed, and was blocked up or dried, the cause is to be sought. For now we see that there is no spring by which the whole face of the earth is watered. Perhaps, then, the sin of men merited this penalty too, that the abundance of that spring, being repressed, took away from the lands their easy fertility, so that the labor of the inhabitants might be increased.

Human conjecture could affirm this, although no Scripture has narrated it, were it not that this occurs: that the sin of men, on which the penalty of labor was imposed, arose after the delights of Paradise; but Paradise had its own very great spring, of which we must speak afterward more carefully in its own place; from whose single source four great rivers, known to the nations, are said to flow. Where, then, was this spring, or these rivers, when that one greatest spring rose up from the earth and watered the whole face of the earth? For certainly then the Gihon, which is called the Nile, one of those four, was not watering Egypt, when the spring rose up from the earth and satisfied not Egypt only, but the whole face of the earth.

## Another conjecture about that one spring.

**7. 22.** Or is it to be believed that God first willed to water the whole earth with one greatest spring, so that the things he had principally founded in it, having received the help of moisture, might now be born through temporal spans as well, with even different numbers of days according to the diversity of their kinds; and that afterward, when Paradise was planted, he repressed that spring, and filled the earth with many springs, as we now see; and from the one spring of Paradise divided four huge rivers—so that the rest of the earth too, full of the kinds of its creatures carrying out the fitting numbers of their times, might also have its own springs and rivers, while Paradise, planted in a more choice place, might send forth those four rivers from the source of its spring?

Or that, from that one spring of Paradise overflowing much more abundantly, he first watered the whole earth, and made it fruitful for bringing forth, through the numbers of times, the kinds that he had created in it without intervals of times; and afterward repressed in that place the vast eruption of waters, so that they now flowed from the various sources of rivers and springs throughout the whole earth; and then, in the region of that spring—no longer watering the whole earth, but sending forth those four memorable rivers alone—he planted Paradise, where he might place the man he had made?

## To what extent it helps to bring forward by conjecture what Holy Scripture passes over in silence.

**8. 23.** For because not all things have been written—how, after that first founding of things, the times ran on, and there followed one another the administrations of creatures that were first made and completed on that sixth day, but only as much as the Spirit, who was in the one writing these things, judged sufficient, things that would avail not only for the knowledge of the things made, but also for the prefiguring of things to come—we, not knowing, conjecture what could have happened, which he, not unknowing, passed over.

Attempting only this, according to our measure, as far as we are helped: that no absurdity or contradiction be thought to be in the Holy Scriptures, which might offend the reader's opinion, so that, while he supposes that the things Scripture records as having been done could not have been done, he might either recoil from the faith or not approach the faith.

## The difficulty about the spring watering the earth.

**9. 24.** Accordingly, when we ask about this spring how what was said, *it rose up from the earth and watered the whole face of the earth*[^20]*,* may not seem impossible—if the things we have said seem impossible to anyone, let him seek something else by which this Scripture may nonetheless be shown true, which without doubt is true, even if it is not shown to be so.

For if he wishes to argue so as to convict it of being false, either he himself will say nothing true about the founding and administration of creatures; or, if he says what is true, he will, by not understanding it, suppose this Scripture false.

As if he should contend that the whole face of the earth could not have been watered by one spring, however great, for this reason: that if it did not water the mountains, it did not water the whole face of the earth; but if it watered the mountains too, then it was no longer a distribution of nourishment, but the inundation of a flood; and if the earth was then like that, it was all sea, and the dry land was not yet distinguished.

## How that spring is to be understood.

**10. 25.** To him it is answered that this could happen by alternations of times, just as at a certain time the Nile overflows across the plains of Egypt, and at another time returns to its banks; or if it is believed that this spring, I know not of what unknown and distant part of the world, gathers its yearly increase from waters and winter frosts—what can be said of the alternating tides of the Ocean, what of certain shores that are widely laid bare by the waves and in turn covered? to say nothing of what is reported about the marvelous alternation of certain springs, that at a fixed interval of years they so flood as to water that whole region, to which at another time they furnish water sufficient for drinking from deep wells. Why, then, should it be incredible if, from one source of the deep flowing and ebbing in alternating inundation, the whole earth was then watered?

But if, by the "magnitude of the deep" itself—excepting that part which is called sea, and surrounds the lands with evident breadth and bitter waves—in that part alone which the earth holds in hidden recesses, whence all the springs and rivers distribute themselves in various tracts and veins, and each bursts forth in its own places, Scripture wished to call it a spring, not springs, on account of the unity of its nature; and to describe it as rising up from the earth through countless ways of caverns and crevices, and everywhere, with its tresses as it were dispersed, watering the whole face of the earth, not in a continuous form like a sea or pool, but as we see the waters go through the channels of rivers and the windings of streams, and by their overflow drench what lies near—

who would not accept it, except one who toils with a contentious spirit?

For the whole face of the earth being watered can also be understood as said in the way "the whole face of a garment is colored" is said, even if it happens not continuously but in patches: especially because then, in the newness of the lands, even if not all, yet it is credible that more were level, where the bursting streams could be more widely dispersed and spread out.

## Conclusion of the conjectures for understanding that spring.

**10. 26.** For this reason, concerning the magnitude or the multitude of this spring—which either had one eruption from somewhere, or, on account of some unity in the hidden recesses of the earth, whence all the waters of all springs great and small well up over the earth, was called one spring, rising up from the earth through all its distributions and watering the whole face of the earth.

Or even—what is more credible—because he did not say, "one spring rose up from the earth," but said, *but a spring rose up from the earth*[^21]*,* he set the singular for the plural number; so that we should thus understand many springs watering throughout the whole earth their own places or regions.

Just as "soldier" is said, and many are understood; as the "locust and frog" are spoken of "in the plagues[^22]" by which the Egyptians were struck, when there was an innumerable number of locusts and frogs—let us now labor no further.

## That the first creation of things was made without delay of time, but their administration not so.

**11. 27.** But let us consider again and again whether the view by which we were saying can hold for us throughout: that God worked otherwise on all creatures in the first founding, from which works he rested on the seventh day; and otherwise on the administration of them, by which *he is working still*—that is, then all things at once without any intervals of temporal delays; but now through the delays of times, by which we see the stars move from rising to setting, the heaven change from summer to winter, the seeds sprout at fixed moments of days, grow large, grow green, grow dry.

And the animals too, by the appointed bounds and courses of times, are both conceived and brought to term and born, and run on through the ages until old age and death, and the rest of temporal things of this sort. For who works these things but God, even without any such movement of his own? For time does not befall him either.

Between those works of God, then, from which he rested on the seventh day, and these that he is working still, Scripture, interposing a certain joint of its narrative, indicated that it had unfolded the former, and began now to weave the latter. The commending of those unfolded works was made thus: *this is the book of the creation of heaven and earth, when the day was made, God made heaven and earth, and every green thing of the field before it was upon the earth, and all the grass of the field before it sprang up. For God had not rained upon the earth, nor was there a man to till the earth*[^23]*.* But the weaving of the latter began thus: *but a spring rose up from the earth and watered the whole face of the earth*[^24]*.* From this recording of this spring and thereafter, the things that are narrated were made through the delays of times, not "all at once."

## The works of God under a threefold consideration.

**12. 28.** Since, then, the unchangeable reasons of all creatures stand one way in the Word of God, those works of his from which he rested on the seventh day another way, and these which out of them he is working still another way—of these three, the one I have put last is in some manner known to us through the senses of the body and the custom of this life. But those two, removed from our senses and from the use of human thinking, are first to be believed on divine authority; then, through the things that are known, to be in some manner known, the more or less as each is able according to the measure of his capacity, with divine help that he may be able.

## a) in the Wisdom of God.

**13. 29.** As for those first divine, unchangeable, and eternal reasons, then, since the very Wisdom of God, through whom all things were made, knew them before they came to be, as Scripture attests: *in the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God; the same was in the beginning with God. All things were made through him, and without him was made nothing*[^25]. Who, then, would be so mad as to say that God did not make the things he knew?

Moreover, if he knew them, where but with himself, with whom was the Word, through whom all things were made? For if he knew them outside himself, who had taught him? *For who has known the mind of the Lord? Or who has been his counselor? Or who has first given to him, that he should be repaid? For from him, and through him, and in him are all things*[^26].

## That all things were created through the Word, the light of souls.

**13. 30.** Yet the things that follow in the Gospel sufficiently assert this view: for the Evangelist adds and says, *what was made, in him is life, and the life was the light of men*[^27]. Because, namely, the rational minds—in which kind man was made to the image of God—do not have their true light except the very Word of God, through whom all things were made, of which they will be able to be partakers, cleansed from all iniquity and error.

## How John 1:4 is to be punctuated.

**14. 31.** It ought not, then, to be pronounced thus, *what was made in him, is life,* so that we should make a subdivision, *what was made in him,* and then add, *is life.* For what was not made in him, when, after many earthly creatures too have been recorded, it is said in the Psalm, *you made all things in Wisdom*[^28]; and the Apostle too says, *for in him all things were founded, in heaven and on earth, visible and invisible*[^29]? It will follow, then, if we punctuate it so, that the earth itself, and whatever is in it, are life. And since it is absurd to say that all these things live, how much more absurd that they are even life?—especially because he distinguishes of what sort of life he is speaking, when he adds, *and the life was the light of men.*

Thus, then, it is to be punctuated, so that when we have said, *what was made,* we then add, *in him is life*: not in itself, that is, in its own nature, by which it was made so as to be a thing founded and a creature, but in him it is life, because all the things that were made through him he knew before they came to be; and thus in him they were, not as the creature that he made, but as the life and light of men, which is Wisdom itself, and the Word itself, the only-begotten Son of God. In that way, then, what was made is life in him, in the way it was said, *as the Father has life in himself, so he has given to the Son to have life in himself*[^30].

## That the life of rational souls is the light of the Word of God.

**14. 32.** Nor must it be passed over that the more correct manuscripts have, *what was made, in him was life,* so that *was life* is understood in the way *in the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God*[^31]. What, then, was made, already *was life in him,* and life not of any sort whatever; for beasts too are said to live, which cannot enjoy a sharing in wisdom; but *the life was the light of men.* For rational minds, purified by his grace, can arrive at that kind of vision, than which there is nothing higher, nothing more blessed.

## How all things were in the nature of their Maker before they came to be.

**15. 33.** But even if we read and understand it thus, *what was made, in him is life*, this view stands: that what was made through him is understood to be life in him, in which life he saw all things when he made them; and "as he saw, so he made": not seeing outside himself, but in himself thus "enumerating" all the things he made. Nor is his vision other than the Father's, but one, just as their substance is one.

For in the book of *Job* too the very Wisdom, through whom all things were made, is thus proclaimed: *but wisdom, from where has it been found? Or what is the place of knowledge? Mortal man does not know its way, nor will it be found among men*[^32].

And a little after he says, *we have heard its glory; the Lord has commended its way, and he knows its place. For he beholds everything that is under heaven, and knows the things that are on the earth, all that he made: the balancings of the winds, the measures of the water—when he made them, as he saw, he enumerated them*[^33].

By these and similar testimonies it is proved that all these things, before they came to be, were in the knowledge of their maker. And there indeed they are better, where they are truer, where they are eternal and unchangeable. Although it ought to be enough for anyone to know, or unshakably believe, that God made all these things, I do not think him so witless as to suppose that God made what he did not know. Moreover, if he knew them before he made them, then assuredly, before they came to be, they were "with him," known in the way they live everlastingly and unchangeably, and are life; but they were made in the way each creature exists in its own kind.

## We perceive God by the mind more easily than the creatures.

**16. 34.** Although, then, that eternal and unchangeable nature which God is, having in himself that he is, as was said to Moses, *I am who am*[^34], is far otherwise than these things that have been made: since that truly and primarily is, which is always in the same way, and not only is not changed, but cannot at all be changed; being none of the things he made, and yet having all things primarily, as he himself is.

For he would not make them unless he knew them before he made them; nor would he know them unless he saw them; nor would he see them unless he had them; nor would he have the things that were not yet made, except in the way that he himself is not made.

Although, I say, that substance is ineffable, and cannot be uttered to man by man in any way, except by borrowing certain words of places and times, since he is before all times and before all places; nevertheless, he who made us is nearer to us than the many things that have been made. *For in him we live and move and have our being*[^35]: but most of these things are removed from our mind on account of the unlikeness of their kind, since they are corporeal; nor is our mind itself capable of seeing them, in the very reasons by which they were made, with God, so that through this we might know how many and how great and of what sort they are, even if we do not see them through the senses of the body. For they are removed from the senses of our body, since they are far off, or are separated from our gaze and reach by other things set between or opposite.

From which it comes about that there is greater labor in finding them than in finding him by whom they were made, since it is, with incomparable felicity, more excellent to perceive him, even from however small a particle, with a devout mind, than to comprehend the whole of them. Whence in the book of *Wisdom* the searchers of this age are rightly blamed: *for if they were able to avail so much that they could estimate the world,* it says, *how did they not more easily find its Lord*[^36]? For the foundations of the earth are unknown to our eyes, and he who founded the earth is near to our minds.

## Another consideration of the whole of creation.

**17. 35.** Let us now consider the things that God made "all at once," from which, completed on the sixth day, he rested on the seventh, and afterward consider his works in which he is working still. For he himself is before the ages; but we say things are "from the age," from the time when the age began, like the world itself; and "in the age," like the things that are born "in the world."

When, then, Scripture had said, *all things were made through him, and without him was made nothing*; a little after it says, *he was in this world, and the world was made through him*[^37]. Of this work of God it is written in another place, *who made the world from formless matter*[^38].

This world is for the most part called by the name of *heaven and earth,* as we have already recorded, which Scripture says God made, *when the day was made*: about which words we have already disputed, as far as seemed good, how it fits the founding of this world, both that it was completed in six days with all that is in it, and that it was then made, when the day was made, so that that too may fit, that *he created all things at once*[^39].

## b) In the creation by which all things were made at once.

**18. 36.** Of this whole creation of God we do not know many things, whether those that are in the heavens too high for our sense to reach them, or those in regions of the lands perhaps uninhabitable, or those that lie hidden below, either in the depth of the deep, or in the hidden recesses of the earth. These things, then, before they came to be, certainly did not exist.

How, then, were the things that did not exist known to God? And again, how would he make the things that were not known to him? For he made nothing in ignorance. He made them, then, known; he did not come to know them once made. Accordingly, before they came to be, they both were and were not: they were in God's knowledge, they were not in their own nature.

And thus that day was made, to which they would become known in both ways, both in God and in themselves: to God by a morning or daytime knowledge, as it were, to themselves by an evening one, as it were. But of God himself I do not dare to say that they became known in another way, once he had made them, than in the way he knew them so as to make them, *with whom there is no change, nor shadow of a moment*[^40].

## The twofold knowledge of the angels.

**19. 37.** He certainly does not need messengers for the knowledge of lower things, as though through them he became more knowing; but in that simple and marvelous way he knows all things stably and unchangeably. Yet he has messengers for our sake and for their own; because in that way to obey and attend upon God—so as to consult him about lower things, and to comply with his supernal precepts and commands—is good for them in the order of their proper nature and substance. Messengers, moreover, are in Greek called angeloi; by which general name that whole supernal city is named, which we suppose to be the first day founded.

## That the angels of God are messengers, ministers of his precepts.

**19. 38.** For neither was that mystery of the kingdom of heaven hidden from them, which was revealed at the opportune time for our salvation, by which, freed from this pilgrimage, we may be joined to their company. For they would not be ignorant of this; since the very seed, which came at the opportune time, was ordained through them in the hand of the Mediator[^41]—that is, in the power of him who is their Lord, both in the form of God and in the form of a servant. The Apostle likewise says, *to me, the least of all the saints, was this grace given, to announce among the nations the unsearchable riches of Christ, and to enlighten what is the dispensation of the mystery which was hidden from the ages in God, who created all things; that the manifold Wisdom of God might become known, through the Church, to the principalities and powers in the heavenly places, according to the purpose of the ages, which he made in Christ Jesus our Lord*[^42].

So, then, this was hidden from the ages in God, in such a way that nonetheless the manifold Wisdom of God might become known, through the Church, to the principalities and powers in the heavenly places; because there is, first, that Church into which this Church too is to be gathered after the resurrection, so that we may be equal to the angels of God[^43]. To them, then, it became known from the ages; because every creature is not before the ages, but from the ages.

For from creation itself the ages arose, and creation is from the ages; since its beginning is the beginning of the ages: but the *only-begotten* is *before the ages, through whom the ages were made*[^44]. And therefore, in the person of Wisdom, it says, *before the ages he founded me*[^45]; so that in her he might make all things, she to whom it was said, *you made all things in Wisdom*[^46].

## That the mystery of the kingdom of heaven became known to the angels from the ages.

**19. 39.** But that what is hidden becomes known to the angels not only in God, but also appears to them here, when it is brought about and made public, the same Apostle is thus a witness: *and without doubt,* he says, *great is the mystery of godliness, which was manifested in the flesh, justified in the spirit, appeared to angels, preached among the nations, believed in the world, taken up in glory*[^47]. And, unless I am mistaken, it is to be wondered at if all the things that God is said to know as though for the present time are not said so for the reason that he makes them to be known, whether by angels or by men. For this manner of speaking, when by the agent that which is effected is signified, is frequent in the Holy Scriptures; especially when something is said of God which Truth herself, presiding over our minds, cries out does not befit him in the propriety of speech.

## c) In the things that God is still working.

**20. 40.** Now, then, let us distinguish the works of God which he is working still from those works from which he rested on the seventh day. For there are some who suppose that only the world itself was made by God, and that the rest are now made by the world itself, as he ordained and commanded; but that God himself works nothing.

Against them is brought forward that saying of the Lord, *My Father is working still.* And lest anyone should suppose that he works something within himself, not in this world, he says, *the Father, abiding in me, does his works; and as the Father raises the dead and gives them life, so the Son too gives life to whom he wills*[^48].

Then, because he works not only the great and chief things, but even these earthly and lowest things, the Apostle says thus: *foolish man, what you sow is not given life unless it dies; and what you sow, you do not sow the body that is to be, but a bare grain, perhaps of wheat or of some of the rest; but God gives it a body as he has willed, and to each of the seeds its own body*[^49]. So, then, let us believe, or, if we can, even understand, that God is working still, in such a way that if his working were withdrawn from the things founded by him, they would perish.

## That it is by no means to be believed that new kinds of things are established by God.

**20. 41.** But plainly, if we should suppose that he now establishes some creature in such a way that he did not insert its kind into that first founding of his, we openly contradict Scripture, which says that he completed all his works on the sixth day[^50]. For according to those kinds of things which he first founded, it is evident that he makes many new things, which he did not then make. But that he establishes a new kind cannot rightly be believed, since he then completed all things.

And so he moves his whole creation by a hidden power, and by that movement, as it is turned about—while the angels carry out his commands, while the stars go round, while the winds alternate, while the deep is stirred by the fallings of its waters and by various massings even through the air, while the green things sprout and unfold their seeds, while the animals are born and lead their proper lives with their varied appetites, while the wicked are permitted to exercise the just—he unfolds the ages, which he had implanted in creation, as it were folded up, when it was first founded.

Which, however, would not be unfolded into their courses, if he who founded them ceased to administer them by his provident movement.

## That all things are governed by the providence of God.

**21. 42.** But we ought to be admonished, by the things that are formed and born in time, how we ought to consider these matters. For it is not in vain written of Wisdom that to her lovers *she shows herself joyfully in the ways, and in all providence meets them*[^51]. Nor are those at all to be heeded who have supposed that the lofty parts of the world—that is, from the boundary of this denser air and above—are governed by divine providence, but that this lowest, earthly, and moist part, and of this nearer air which grows moist from the exhalations of the lands and waters, in which winds and clouds rise up, is rather driven by chances and fortuitous movements.

For against these speaks the *Psalm,* which, when it had unfolded the praise of heavenly things, also turned to these lower things, saying, *praise the Lord from the earth, you dragons and all deeps; fire, hail, snow, ice, the storm-wind, which do his word*[^52].

For nothing seems so rolled about by chances as all those stormy and turbulent qualities, by which the face of this lower heaven—which is not undeservedly reckoned also by the name of "earth"—is varied and turned. But when he added, *which do his word,* he sufficiently showed that the order of these things too is subject to the divine command, and that it is rather hidden from us than lacking to the nature of the universe.

But what of this? When the Savior with his own mouth says that one sparrow does not fall to the earth without the will of God[^53], and that he himself clothes the grass of the field that is to be cast a little later into the oven[^54]—does he not confirm that not only this whole part of the world assigned to mortal and corruptible things, but even its meanest and most abject particles, are ruled by divine providence?

## Arguments for proving the providence of God.

**22. 43.** And certainly those who deny this, and do not acquiesce in the holy words of such great authority, in this part of the world which they think is disturbed by fortuitous movements rather than ruled by the Wisdom of the divine Highness—and, to as it were "prove" this, abuse a twofold argument: either what I recorded above concerning the inconstancy of storms, or concerning the good and bad fortunes of men, which do not happen according to the merits of their lives.

If only they would see how great an order appears in the members of the flesh of any living thing—I do not say to physicians, who, for the necessity of their art, have examined these things diligently laid open and numbered, but to any man of even moderate heart and consideration—would they not cry out that not even for a moment of time does God, from whom is every measure of measures, every equality of numbers, every order of weights, cease from his governance?

What, then, more absurd, what more insipid, can be thought, than that the whole of that should be empty of the nod and rule of providence, whose lowest and tiniest things you may see formed with such arrangement that, when thought of somewhat more attentively, they strike one with an ineffable shudder of admiration?

And since the nature of the soul surpasses the nature of the body, what is more demented than to suppose that there is no judgment of divine providence concerning the conduct of men, when in their flesh such great signs of his skill shine forth and are demonstrated? But because these least things are at hand to our senses, and we easily investigate them, the order of things shines out in them: yet those things whose order we cannot see, those who think nothing exists except what they can see judge to be without order; or, if they think it exists, they think something of the sort they have been accustomed to see.

## How God created all things at once even though he is working still.

**23. 44.** But we, whose steps the same divine providence rules through Holy Scripture, lest we fall into that perversity, let us try, from the very works of God, with the same one helping, to track down where he created these things at once, when he rested from his completed works, whose forms he is working still through the order of times.

Let us consider, then, the beauty of any tree in its trunk, branches, foliage, fruits: this form certainly did not arise all at once so great and such, but in an order that we too know. For it rose from a root, which the first germination fixed in the earth; and thence all those things grew, formed and distinct. Moreover, that germination was from a seed: in the seed, then, all those things were first, not in the mass of corporeal magnitude, but in causal force and power. For that magnitude was heaped up from the abundance of earth and moisture.

But in the tiny grain there is a more marvelous and more excellent power, by which the adjacent moisture mixed with the earth, as matter, was able to be turned into the quality of that wood, into the spreading of branches, into the greenness and shape of leaves, into the forms and richness of fruits, and into the most ordered distinction of all. For what rises from or hangs on that tree which was not drawn out and brought forth from a certain hidden treasury of that seed?

But that seed is from a tree, though not that one but another, and that one again from another seed. Sometimes, however, a tree comes from a tree, when a shoot is taken and planted. Therefore both seed from tree, and tree from seed, and tree from tree. But seed from seed in no way, unless a tree intervenes first. A tree, however, comes from a tree, even if a seed does not intervene. By alternating successions, then, one comes from the other, but both from the earth, and not the earth from them: the earlier parent of them, therefore, is the earth. So too with animals, it can be uncertain whether the seeds are from them, or they from the seeds: yet whichever of these is earlier, it is most certain that it is from the earth.

## What things heaven and earth brought forth potentially and causally.

**23. 45.** But just as in the grain itself there were invisibly all at once all the things that through times would rise into a tree, so the world itself is to be thought of as having had, when God created all things at once, all at once the things that were made in it and with it, *when the day was made.*

Not only heaven with sun and moon and stars, whose form abides in a wheeling motion, and earth and the deeps, which suffer as it were inconstant movements, and, joined below, confer the other part on the world; but also those things that water and earth brought forth potentially and causally, before they sprang up through the delays of times in the way they are now known to us in those works which God *is working still*.

## Conclusion.

**23. 46.** Since this is so: *this is the book of the creation of heaven and earth, when the day was made, God made heaven and earth, and every green thing of the field before it was upon the earth, and all the grass of the field before it sprang up*[^55]: not in the way he does by the work by which he is working still, through rain and the farming of men (for to this is added, *for God had not rained upon the earth, nor was there a man to till the earth*[^56]); but in that way by which he created all things at once, and completed them in the number six of days, when he made present the day that he made to the things he made six times, not by an alternating span temporally, but by ordered knowledge causally. From which works he rested on the seventh day, deigning to afford his rest too to the knowledge and joy of that same day: and therefore he blessed and sanctified not it in any work of his, but in his rest.

Whence, establishing no further creature, but governing and ruling by an administrative act the things that he made all at once, he works without ceasing, both resting and working at once, as these things have already been treated. Of these works of his that he is working still, through the volumes of times to be unfolded, Scripture, taking as it were a beginning of narration, says, *but a spring rose up from the earth and watered the whole face of the earth*[^57]. Since about this spring we have said what we thought ought to be said, let us consider the things that follow from another beginning.
