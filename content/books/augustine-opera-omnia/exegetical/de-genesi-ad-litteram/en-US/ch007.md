# Book VI

## Whether Genesis 2:7 is to be understood of the first forming of man or of another made through time.

**1. 1.** *And God formed man, dust from the earth, and breathed into his face the breath of life; and man was made into a living soul*[^1]. Here first it must be seen whether this is a recapitulation, so that it is now told how the man was made whom we read was made on that sixth day; or whether indeed, when he made all things at once, in these he also made man hiddenly, like the grass of the earth before it had sprung up.

So that in that way he too, when he already existed in a certain secret of nature, made otherwise—like the things he created at once *when the day was made*—might, with the advance of time, also be made in this way, in which he leads his life in this visible form, whether ill or well; just as the grass that was made before it sprang up over the earth, with time now advancing and with the watering of that spring, sprang up so as to be upon the earth.

## First conjecture: man was made on the sixth day just as the day itself was first made.

**1. 2.** Let us, then, first try to take it according to recapitulation. For perhaps man was so made on the sixth day as the day itself was first made, as the firmament, as the earth and sea.

For these things must not be said to have lain hidden, already made in certain first beginnings, and then to have shone forth, as it were sprung up, with the advance of time, into this form in which the world was built up; but that from the beginning of the age, *when the day was made,* the world was founded, in whose elements were founded at once the things that would afterward, with the advance of time, spring up—whether shrubs, or any animals according to their kind.

For not even the stars themselves are to be believed to have been first made and stored away in the elements of the world, and afterward, with the advance of time, to have come forth and shone out into these forms that gleam from heaven; but all things were created at once by that number six of perfection, *when the day was made.*

Was man, then, so made—already in that visible form in which he lives and acts in his own nature, whether good or evil? Or was he too in hiding, like the grass of the field before it sprang up, so that this would be for him afterward, with the advance of time, to spring up—the fact that he was made from dust?

## The matter is explored from the context of Scripture.

**2. 3.** Let us, then, take him as made on that sixth day in this visible form from clay, but at that time not recorded what is now suggested by recapitulation, and let us see whether Scripture itself agrees with us. For it is certainly written thus, when the works of the sixth day were still being narrated: *and God said, 'Let us make man to our image and likeness; and let him have power over the fish of the sea and the birds of the air, and over all cattle, and all the earth, and all the creeping things that creep upon the earth.' And God made man; to the image of God he made him; male and female he made them, and God blessed them, saying, 'Increase and multiply, and fill the earth, and have dominion over it; and have power over the fish of the sea, and the birds of the air, and all cattle, and all the earth, and all the reptiles creeping upon the earth*[^2].' Already, then, he had been formed from clay, and to him, now put into a deep sleep, the woman had been made from his side, but this had not been recorded which is now recorded by recapitulation.

For the male was not made on the sixth day, and the female afterward made with the advance of time; but *he made him,* it says; *male and female he made them, and he blessed them.* How, then, when the man was already established in paradise, was the woman made for him? Did Scripture recall this too, which had been passed over? For on that sixth day paradise too was planted, and there the man was placed and put into a deep sleep so that Eve might be formed, and she, formed, woke him, and he gave her a name. But these things could not happen except through temporal delays. They were not, then, made in the way all things were created at once.

## The same question is discussed from other passages of Scripture.

**3. 4.** For however great a facility man may think of, by which God made these things too at once with the rest, we certainly know that the words of man cannot be uttered by the voice except through little temporal delays. When, then, we hear the words of the man, either when he gave a name to the living creatures, or to the woman, or when, following, he also said, *for this reason a man will leave his father and mother, and will be joined to his wife; and the two will be in one flesh*[^3]; in whatever syllables these things sounded, not even any two syllables in these words could sound at once: how much less could all these things be done at once with the things that were all created at once?

And thus, either those "all things" too were made not at once from the very first beginning of the ages, but through delays and intervals of times, and that day, founded first not in a spiritual but in a corporeal substance, made morning and evening either by some going round of light, I know not how, or by emission and contraction.

Or, if—all the things treated in the foregoing discourses being considered—a probable reasoning persuaded that that day was a certain wise light, sublimely and first founded, called day, by whose presence, through ordered knowledge, the founding of things in the number six was afforded; and that to this view the words of Scripture agree, which says afterward, *when the day was made, God made heaven and earth, and every green thing of the field before it was upon the earth, and all the grass of the field before it sprang up*[^4]; and that what is written elsewhere also attests it, *he who lives forever created all things at once*[^5].

There is no doubt that this fact—that man was formed from the dust of the earth, and a wife formed for him from his side—now belongs not to the founding by which all things were made at once, on whose completion God rested, but to that working which now happens through the volumes of the ages, by which *he is working still*.

## God created all things at once but is working still.

**3. 5.** To this is added that the very words by which it is narrated how God planted paradise, and placed in it the man he had made, and led to him the animals for him to give them names—among which, when a help like himself had not been found, he then formed for him the woman, with a rib drawn out of him—sufficiently admonish us that these things belong not to that working of God from which he rested on the seventh day, but rather to this by which, through the courses of times, he is working still. For when paradise was planted, it narrates thus: *and God planted paradise in Eden toward the east, and put there the man he had formed. And God further brought forth from the earth every tree beautiful to the sight and good for food*[^6].

**4. 5.** When he says, then, *he further brought forth from the earth every tree beautiful to the sight*[^7], he certainly makes plain that he now brought forth a tree from the earth in one way, and then in another, when on the third day the earth brought forth the herb of pasture, sowing seed according to its kind, and the fruitful tree according to its kind. For this is what *he further brought forth* means: namely, in addition to what he had already brought forth—then, of course, potentially and causally, in the work pertaining to creating all things at once, from which, completed, he rested on the seventh day; but now visibly, in the work pertaining to the course of times, as he is working still.

## The same is weighed with respect to Genesis 2:8. The question of the trees of paradise.

**4. 6.** Unless perhaps someone should say that not every kind of tree was created on the third day, but that something was deferred to be created on the sixth, when man was made and established in paradise. But what was created on the sixth day Scripture declares most openly: namely, "the living soul" according to each kind, of quadrupeds and creeping things and beasts, and man himself, to the image of God, male and female.

Accordingly, it could pass over how man was made, whom it nonetheless narrated to have been made on that very day, so as to suggest afterward, by recapitulation, in what way he had also been made—that is, from the dust of the earth, and the woman from his side; yet it could not pass over any kind of creature, whether in what God said, *let it be made,* or, *let us make,* or in what is said, *so it was made,* or, *God made.* Otherwise it was in vain that all things were so diligently distinguished through the individual days, if any suspicion of the mingling of days can remain—so that, since the herb and tree were attributed to the third day, we should believe that some trees were created on the sixth day too, which Scripture passed over in silence on that very sixth day.

## Among all the things that God made potentially or causally, man too was made in a general way.

**5. 7.** Finally, what shall we answer about the beasts of the field and the birds of the air, which God led to Adam, that he might see what he would call them? Which is written thus: *and the Lord God said, 'It is not good for man to be alone; let us make for him a help like himself.' And God further formed from the earth all the beasts of the field, and all the birds of the air, and led them to Adam, that he might see what he would call them: and whatever Adam called any living soul, that is its name. And Adam gave names to all the cattle, and all the birds of the air, and all the beasts of the field. But for Adam there was not found a help like himself. And God cast a deep sleep upon Adam, and he slept, and took one of his ribs, and filled up flesh in its place, and the Lord God built the rib, which he took from Adam, into a woman*[^8].

If, then, consequently, when among the cattle and beasts of the field and birds of the air no help like man had been found, God made for him a help like himself from the rib of his side; and this was done when he had further formed those same beasts of the field and birds of the air from the earth, and had led them to him: how can this be understood as done on the sixth day, since on that day the earth brought forth the living soul according to the word of God, while the birds the waters brought forth on the fifth day, likewise according to the word of God?

It would not, then, be said here, *and God further formed from the earth all the beasts of the field, and all the birds of the air,* except because the earth had already brought forth all the beasts of the field on the sixth day, and the water all the birds of the air on the fifth day. Then, therefore, in one way—that is, potentially and causally, as befitted that work by which he created all things at once, from which he rested on the seventh day; but now in another way, as we see the things he creates through temporal spans, as he is working still.

And thus it was now through these most familiar days of corporeal light, which are made by the going round of the sun, that Eve was made from the side of her husband. For then God further formed from the earth beasts and birds, among which, when a help like Adam himself had not been found, she was formed. In days of this sort, then, God also formed him himself from clay.

## Another conjecture: the twofold creation of man.

**5. 8.** For it must not be said that the male was made on the sixth day, but the female on later days; since on that very sixth day it was said most openly, *male and female he made them, and he blessed them*[^9], and the rest, which are said of both and to both.

In one way, then, both then, and now both in another way: then, namely, according to the power implanted in the world as it were seminally through the word of God, when he created all things at once, from which he rested on the seventh day, out of which all things would come to be, each in their own times, now through the order of the ages; but now according to the working to be afforded to the times, by which *he is working still,* and it was fitting that Adam now be made in his own time from the dust of the earth, and his woman from the side of the man.

## The first or causal creation rightly understood.

**6. 9.** In this distribution of the works of God—partly belonging to those invisible days on which he created all things at once, partly to these days, on which he daily works whatever is unfolded in time out of those as out of primordial wrappings—if we have followed the words of Scripture not inopportunely and absurdly, which led us to distinguish these things, care must be taken lest, on account of the somewhat difficult perception of the things themselves, which slower minds are not capable of attaining, we be thought to mean and say something which we know we neither mean nor say.

For although in the preceding discourses I have, as far as I could, prepared the reader beforehand, yet I think that very many are in a fog in these passages, and suppose that man was so present beforehand in that work of God by which all things were created at once, that he led some kind of life, that he discerned, believed, understood God's speech directed to him, when God said, *behold, I have given you every seed-bearing food*[^10]. Let him, then, who thinks this know that I have neither meant nor said it.

## That man was created causally in the first founding of things.

**6. 10.** But again, if I say that man was not present in that first founding of things, by which God created all things at once, in the way there is not only a man of complete age, but not even an infant, nor only an infant, but not even a birth in the mother's womb, nor only this, but not even the visible seed of man—he will suppose that he was not there at all. Let him then return to Scripture; he will find that on the sixth day man was made to the image of God, and that male and female were made[^11]. Likewise let him ask when the female was made; he will find it outside those six days: for she was made then, when God further formed from the earth the beasts of the field and the birds of the air; not when the waters brought forth the birds, and the earth brought forth the living soul, in which the beasts too are.

But then man was made, both male and female: therefore both then and afterward. For neither then and not afterward; nor, indeed, afterward and not then: nor were they others afterward, but the same ones, in one way then, in another way afterward. He will ask me "how." I will answer: "afterward visibly, as the form of human constitution is known to us; yet not by parents begetting, but he from clay, she from his rib." He will ask, "then how?" I will answer: "invisibly, potentially, causally, in the way things that are to be, not yet made, come to be."

## That those causes of man are prior to all visible seeds.

**6. 11.** Here perhaps he will not understand. For there are withdrawn from him all the things he knows, down to the very corporeality of seeds. For man was not yet even any such thing, when he had been made in that first founding of the six days. There is given, indeed, from seeds a certain likeness for this matter, on account of the things sown in them as future; nevertheless, prior to all visible seeds are those causes: but he does not understand.

What, then, shall I do, except, as far as I can, admonish him wholesomely to believe the Scripture of God—both that man was made then, when God, when the day was made, made heaven and earth, of which Scripture elsewhere says, *he who lives forever created all things at once*[^12]; and then, when, creating now not all at once but each in their own times, he formed him from the dust of the earth, and from his bone the woman? For Scripture allows us to understand neither that they were made on that sixth day in this latter way, nor yet that they were not made on that sixth day.

## It cannot be said that souls were created before bodies.

**7. 12.** Perhaps, then, their souls had been made on that sixth day, where the image of God too is rightly understood in the spirit of their mind, so that the bodies might afterward be formed? But neither does the same Scripture allow us to believe this.

First, on account of that completion of the works; which I do not see how it can be understood, if something was then lacking, not founded causally, which afterward would be founded visibly.

Then, because the very sex of male and female can only be in bodies.

And if anyone should suppose that, according to understanding and action, both sexes as it were are to be taken in one soul, what will he do about the things that God gave for food on that very day from the fruits of the trees, which is certainly suited only to a man having a body? For if anyone should wish to take this food too figuratively, he will depart from the propriety of the deeds done, which in narratives of this sort must first of all be established with every observance.

## A difficulty from God's speech to man on the sixth day.

**8. 13.** "How, then," he says, "did he speak to those who did not yet hear, nor understand, since there were not even any who would grasp the words?" I could answer that God addressed them in the way Christ addressed us, not yet born, even to be far in the future—and not only us, but also all those who will be after us. For he was speaking to all whom he saw would be his own: *behold, I am with you until the consummation of the age*[^13]: just as the prophet was known to God, to whom he said, *before I formed you in the womb, I knew you*[^14]; just as Levi was tithed while he was in the loins of Abraham[^15]. For why not so Abraham too in Adam, and Adam himself in the first works of the world, which God created all at once?

But the words of the Lord through the mouth of his flesh, and the words of God through the mouths of the prophets, are uttered by the temporal voice of the body, and take and consume temporal delays fitting to all their syllables; whereas when God said, *let us make man to our image and likeness, and let him have power over the fish of the sea and the birds of the air, and over all cattle, and all the earth, and all the reptiles that creep upon the earth;* and, *increase, and multiply, and fill the earth, and have dominion over it; and have power over the fish of the sea, and the birds of the air, and all cattle, and all the earth, and all the reptiles that creep upon the earth;* and, *behold, I have given you every seed-bearing food, sowing seed which is over all the earth, and every fruit-bearing tree, which has in itself the fruit of seed-bearing seed, which shall be for you for food*[^16]—his speech, before every sound of air, before every voice of flesh and cloud, in that supreme Wisdom of his, through which all things were made, was not as it were resounding in human ears, but was inserting into the things made the causes of the things to be made.

And by his almighty power he was making future things, and was founding man, to be formed in his own time, in the seed, as it were, or as it were the root, of times, when he was founding that from which the ages might begin, founded by him who is before the ages. For some creatures precede other creatures, some in time, some in their causes: but he precedes all the things he made, not only by the excellence by which he is also the effector of causes, but also by his eternity. But of this we must perhaps treat more fully afterward at more opportune places of the Scriptures.

## That creatures are known by God before they exist.

**9. 14.** Now let what was begun concerning man be brought to an end, with this moderation preserved: that in the deep sense of Scripture we should exhibit the diligence of inquiry rather than the rashness of affirming. For that God knew Jeremiah before he formed him in the womb, it is not right to doubt: for he says most openly, *before I formed you in the womb, I knew you*[^17]. But where he knew him before he had so formed him—even if for our weakness it is difficult or impossible to attain—whether in certain nearer causes, as Levi was tithed in the loins of Abraham; or in Adam himself, in whom the human race was established as it were at the root; and in that very one, whether already when he had been formed from clay, or made causally in those works by which he created all things at once; or rather before all creation, as he chose and predestined his saints *before the foundation of the world*[^18]; or rather in all the preceding causes, whether those I have recorded or those I have not recorded, before he was formed in the womb.

I do not think it ought to be inquired into more scrupulously, provided it is established that Jeremiah, from the time he was brought forth by his parents into this light, from that time led his own life, by which, growing with the advance of age, he could live either ill or well; but before, in no way—not only before he was formed in the womb, but not even already formed there, before he was born. For that Apostolic statement has no hesitation concerning the twins in Rebecca's womb who had not yet done anything good or evil[^19].

## That the not-yet-born have no merits.

**9. 15.** Yet it is not written in vain that not even an infant is *clean from sin, whose life is of one day upon the earth;* and that in the *Psalm*, *I was conceived in iniquities, and in sins did my mother nourish me in the womb*[^20]; and that in Adam all die, *in whom all have sinned*[^21].

But now let us hold clearly that—whatever merits of the parents are passed into the offspring, by whatever grace of God he may sanctify each before he is born—there is no iniquity with God, and no one does anything of good or evil pertaining to his own person before he is born; and that the view by which some suppose that souls sinned more or less elsewhere, and for the merits of their various sins were thrust down into various bodies, does not agree with the Apostolic statement; since it was said most openly that the not-yet-born did nothing either of good or of evil.

## A difficulty from the inheritance of sin.

**9. 16.** And thus there is a certain question to be taken up again in its own place: what the whole mass of the human race has contracted from the sin of the first parents, who were two alone; yet that man could have had no such merits before he had been formed from the dust of the earth, before he lived in his own time, is no question. For just as Esau and Jacob, whom the Apostle said had, not yet born, done nothing of good or evil[^22], we could not say drew any merit from their parents, if the parents themselves had not done anything of good or evil; nor that the human race sinned in Adam, if Adam himself had not sinned; but Adam would not have sinned unless he were already living in his own time, in which he could live either well or ill.

Thus it is in vain that his sin, or his right action, is sought, when, still founded causally among the things created at once, he neither yet lived his own life, nor was in parents living thus. For in that first founding of the world, when God *created all things at once,* the man who was to be was made—the reason for creating man, not the act of one created.

## Things existing in various ways.

**10. 17.** But these things are one way in the Word of God, where they are not made, but eternal; another way in the elements of the world, where all things made at once are future; another way in the things that, according to the causes created at once, are created not now at once but each in its own time, among which is Adam now formed from clay and "ensouled" by the breath of God, like the grass sprung up; another way in the seeds, in which again as it were primordial causes are repeated, drawn from the things that came to exist according to the causes which he first founded, as the herb from the earth, the seed from the herb.

In all of which those things, now made, have received the modes and acts of their own time, which from the hidden and invisible reasons that lie causally in creation have come forth into manifest forms and natures: as the herb sprung up over the earth, and man made into a living soul, and the rest of this kind, whether shrubs or animals, belonging to that working of God by which he is working still. But these too carry with them as it were themselves again invisibly, in a certain hidden power of generating, which they have drawn out of those first beginnings of their causes, in which they were inserted when the world was created *when the day was made,* before they sprang up into the manifest form of their kind.

## How the works of the sixth day are both already completed and still begun.

**11. 18.** For if those first works of God, when he created all things at once, were not perfect in their own mode, then without doubt there would be added afterward the things that had been lacking to their perfecting; so that a certain perfection of the universe would consist of the individual halves, as it were, of both, as though they were parts of some whole, by whose joining the whole itself, of which they were parts, would be completed. Again, if those things were so perfect as they are perfected when each is brought forth in its own times into manifest forms and acts, then assuredly either nothing would afterward be made from them through times, or that would be made which, from these things that now arise each in their own time, God does not cease to work.

But now, because the very things "that were to be unfolded in the following" times are both in a manner completed and in a manner begun, God first created all things at once, when he made the world: completed indeed, because those things have nothing in their proper natures, by which they carry out the courses of their own times, which was not made causally in these; but begun, since there were certain things as it were seeds of things to come, to be put forth through the span of the age from the hidden into the manifest in fitting places: the very words of Scripture too are vigorous enough to admonish us of this remarkably, if anyone keep awake in them.

For it says they were both completed and begun: for unless they had been completed, it would not be written, *and heaven and earth were completed, and all their array: and God completed on the sixth day his works which he made; and God blessed the seventh day, and sanctified it*[^23]; and again, unless they had been begun, it would not so follow that on that day *he rested from all his works which God began to make*[^24].

## Conclusion: that man was created in his own time, invisibly in soul, visibly in body.

**11. 19.** Here, then, if anyone should inquire how he completed and how he began—for he did not complete some things and begin others, but assuredly the very same things from which he rested on the seventh day—it is clear from the things we have said above. For we understand that God completed these things when he created all things at once so perfectly that nothing was yet to be created by him in the order of times which had not here already been created by him in the order of causes; but that he began them, so that what he had here prefixed in the causes he might afterward fulfill in their effects.

Accordingly, *God formed man, dust of the earth,* or clay of the earth—that is, from the dust or clay of the earth; *and he breathed* or blew *into his face the spirit of life, and man was made into a living soul*[^25]. Not then predestined; for this was before the age in the foreknowledge of the Creator: nor then causally either begun in a completed way or completed in a begun way; for this was from the age in the primordial reasons, when all things were created at once: but created in his own time, visibly in body, invisibly in soul, consisting of soul and body.

## Whether God formed man's body in a human manner.

**12. 20.** Now, then, let us see how God made him—first his body, from the earth; afterward we will also see about the soul, if we are able at all. For that God formed man from clay with corporeal hands is too childish a thought, so that, if Scripture had said this, we ought rather to believe that the one who wrote it used the word in a transferred sense, than that God was bounded by such outlines of members as we see in our bodies. For it is said, *your hand scattered the nations*[^26]; and, *you led out your people with a strong hand and a lofty arm*[^27]; but who is so foolish as not to understand that the name of this member is put for the power and might of God?

## How man is the chief work of God.

**12. 21.** Nor is that to be heeded which some suppose: that man is for this reason the chief work of God, because he spoke the rest, and they were made, but this one he himself made; but rather for this reason, because he made this one to his own image. For those things that *he spoke and they were made*—it is written so for the reason that they were made through his Word, just as through a man it was possible to say to men, in words that are thought temporally and uttered by the voice. But God does not so speak, except when he speaks through a corporeal creature, as to Abraham, as to Moses, as through the cloud about his Son. But before all creation, that creation might exist, it was said by that Word which in the beginning was *God with God*: and because *all things were made through him, and without him was made nothing*[^28], certainly man too was made through him.

For certainly he made heaven by his word, because *he spoke and it was made*: yet it is written, *and the heavens are the works of your hands*[^29]. And of this lowest as it were floor of the world it is written, *for the sea is his, and he made it, and his hands formed the dry land*[^30]. Let it not, then, be reckoned to the honor of man, as though *God spoke the rest, and they were made,* but this one he himself made; or made the rest by his word, but this one with his hands. But this is excellent in man, that God made man to his own image, for this reason: that he gave him an intellectual mind, by which he excels the beasts; whereof we have already discoursed in an earlier place.

Placed in which honor, if he does not understand, so as to act well, he will be likened to those same beasts over which he was set. For it is so written: *man, placed in honor, did not understand; he was compared to the senseless beasts, and was made like them*[^31]. For God made the beasts too, but not to his own image.

## That man was created by God through the Word, like the other animals.

**12. 22.** Nor must it be said, "man he himself made, but the beasts he commanded, and they were made": for he made both this one and those through his Word, through which all things were made. But because the same Word *is both his Wisdom and his Power,* it is also called his hand—not a visible member, but the power of effecting.

For this same Scripture, which says that God formed man from the clay of the earth, also says that he formed the beasts of the field from the earth, when he led them, with the birds of the air, to Adam, that he might see what he would call them. For it is so written: *and God further formed from the earth all the beasts*[^32]. If, then, he himself formed both man from the earth and the beasts from the earth, what has man more excellent in this matter, except that he himself was created to the image of God? Yet not according to the body, but according to the understanding of the mind, of which we will speak later.

Although even in his very body he has a certain property that indicates this: that he was made of upright stature, so that by this very thing he might be admonished not to pursue earthly things, like the beasts, whose every pleasure is from the earth, whence they are all bent down and prostrate toward the belly. His body too, then, agrees with the rational soul, not according to the outlines and shapes of its members, but rather according to the fact that it is raised toward heaven, to behold the things that are on high in the body of the world itself: just as the rational soul ought to be raised toward those things which most excel in nature among spiritual things, so as to *relish the things that are above, not the things that are upon the earth*[^33].

## At what age or stature Adam was founded.

**13. 23.** But how did God make him from the clay of the earth—suddenly in a perfect age, that is, manly and youthful, or as he now still forms in mothers' wombs? For no other does these things than he who said, *before I formed you in the womb, I knew you*[^34]: so that Adam had only this as his own, that he was not born from parents, but made from the earth; yet in such a way that, in the perfecting of him and the increasing through "ages," there were fulfilled those numbers of times which we see assigned to the natures of the human race. Or rather is this not to be inquired into? For whichever of the two he did, he did what it befitted God, both almighty and wise, to be able to do and to do.

For he so assigned fixed laws of times to the kinds and qualities of things to be brought from the hidden into the manifest, that his will is over all. For by his power he gave the numbers to creation; he did not bind that power itself by those same numbers. For his Spirit was borne over the world in making it in such a way that it is borne over it once made too, not by corporeal places, but by the excellence of power.

## That God does not need time to complete his works.

**13. 24.** For who does not know that water absorbed by the earth, when it comes to the roots of the vine, is drawn into the nourishment of that wood, and takes in it the quality by which it advances, little by little bursting forth, into a grape; and as that grows large, wine is made, and ripening grows sweet, which as yet ferments when pressed, and, strengthened by a certain age, comes to the use of drinking more usefully and pleasantly? Did the Lord, then, seek wood or earth, or these delays of times, when by a marvelous shortcut he turned water into wine, and such wine that even a drunken guest would praise it[^35]?

Did the founder of time need the help of time? Is it not by fixed numbers of days adapted each to its own kind that the whole nature of serpents grows together, is formed, is born, is strengthened? Were these days awaited, that the rod might be turned into a dragon from the hand of Moses and Aaron[^36]? Nor, when these things happen, do they happen against nature, except for us, to whom the course of nature has become known otherwise; but not for God, for whom this is nature, what he has done.

## Of what kind the causal reasons of the world were at first.

**14. 25.** But it can rightly be asked how those causal reasons, which he implanted in the world when he first created all things at once, were established: whether so that, as we see all things being born, whether of shrubs or of animals, in their conformings and increases, carry out their various spans of times according to the diversity of their kinds; or so that, as Adam is believed to have been made without any progress of increases at a manly age, they were formed all at once? But why do we not believe that they had both, so that there would be from them what had pleased their maker?

For if we say it in the former way, not only that water into wine, but also all the miracles that happen against the usual course of nature, will begin to seem done against these very reasons: but if in the latter way, it will be much more absurd that these very daily forms and appearances of nature carry out the spans of their own times against those primary causal reasons of all things being born.

It remains, then, that they were created apt for both modes: whether for this one by which temporal things run their course most usually, or for that one by which rare and marvelous things happen, as it pleased God to do what befits the time.

## That the first man was founded according to causal reasons.

**15. 26.** Nonetheless, man was made in the way those first causes had it that the first man should be made—who had to be not born from parents, since none had preceded him, but formed from clay, according to the causal reason in which he had first been made. For if he was made otherwise, God had not made him in the works of those six days: in which, when he is said to have been made, God had certainly made the very cause by which there was to be a man in his own time, and according to which he was to be made by him who had both completed the things begun, on account of the perfection of the causal reasons, and begun the things to be completed, on account of the order of times.

If, then, in those first causes of things, which the Creator first inserted into the world, he placed not only that he would form man from clay, but also in what manner he would form him—whether as in a mother's womb, or in a youthful form—then without doubt he so made him as he had prefixed there; for he would not act against his own arrangement.

But if he placed there only the force of possibility, that man should come to be in whatever way he came to be, so that he could be both this way and that—that is, that this too was there, because he could be both this way and that; but reserved the one mode by which he was going to make him in his own will, not weaving it into the founding of the world—it is manifest that even thus man was not made against what was in that first founding of causes; because there was there also the possibility of his being made thus, although there was not there the necessity of his being made thus: for this was not in the founding of creation, but in the good pleasure of the Creator, whose will is the necessity of things.

## That it is in the nature of things what each can be; that it will be, only in God's will.

**16. 27.** For we too, according to the grasp of human weakness, can already, in the very things that have arisen in time, know what is in the nature of each, which we have perceived by experience; but whether it also will be, we do not know. For it is in the nature of this youth, for example, that he should grow old; but whether this is also in God's will, we do not know. Yet it would not be in his nature, unless it had first been in the will of God, who founded all things.

And certainly the reason of old age is hidden in the youthful body, or of youth in the boyish body: for it is not discerned by the eyes, as boyhood itself in the boy, as youth in the youth; but by a certain other knowledge it is gathered that there is in the nature something latent, by which the hidden numbers are brought into the open—whether of youth from boyhood, or of old age from youth. Hidden, then, is that reason by which it comes about that this can be—but to the eyes; to the mind it is not hidden: but whether this is also necessary, we in no way know. And that reason by which it comes about that it can be, we know to be in the nature of the body itself: but that reason by which it comes about that it is necessary, it is manifest is not there.

## That the things God wills and foreknows will necessarily be.

**17. 28.** But perhaps it is in the world, that it is necessary that this man grow old. But if it is not even in the world, it is in God. For that will necessarily be which he wills, and those things are truly future which he has foreknown.

For many things are future according to lower causes; but if they are so also in the foreknowledge of God, they are truly future: but if they are there otherwise, then they are future rather as they are there, where he who foreknows cannot be deceived.

For old age is said to be future in the youth, but yet it is not future, if he is going to die first: but this will be as the other causes have it, whether woven into the world, or reserved in God's foreknowledge.

For according to certain causes of future things, Hezekiah was going to die, to whom God added fifteen years of life[^37]; certainly doing that which *before the foundation of the world* he foreknew he would do, and reserved in his own will. He did not, then, do what was not future: for this was rather future, which he foreknew he would do.

And yet those years would not rightly be said to be added, unless they were added to something that had stood otherwise in the other causes. According to some lower causes, then, he had already finished his life: but according to those that are in the will and foreknowledge of God, who from eternity knew what he would do at that time (and this was truly future), he was going to finish his life then, when he did finish his life.

Because even though it was granted to him as he prayed, that very thing—that he would pray in such a way that it would be fitting for such a prayer to be granted—he assuredly foreknew, whose foreknowledge could not be deceived: and therefore what he foreknew was necessarily future.

## That Adam was formed according to what had been established in the primordial causes.

**18. 29.** For this reason, if the causes of all future things are implanted in the world, when *that day was made,* when God created all things at once, then Adam was made no otherwise, when he was formed from clay—as is more credible, already of complete manhood—than he was in those causes, where God made man in the works of the six days. For there it was not only that he could be so made, but also that it was necessary that he be so made. For God did not act against a cause which he had without doubt willingly preestablished, any more than he acts against his own will.

But if he did not prefix all the causes in the creation first founded, but reserved some in his own will, then those that he reserved in his own will are indeed not dependent on the necessity of those that he created: yet the ones he reserved in his own will cannot be contrary to those he established by his will; because God's will cannot be contrary to itself. He founded the latter, then, in such a way that from them that whose causes they are can be, but need not be: but he hid the former in such a way that from them it is necessary that this be, which from the latter he made so that it could be.

## It is usually asked whether this animal body of ours was formed from clay, or a spiritual one.

**19. 30.** It is likewise usually asked whether the body first formed for man from clay was an animal one, such as we now have, or a spiritual one, such as we will have when we rise.

For although this will be changed into that—for *an animal body is sown, a spiritual body rises*—yet what was first made for man is disputed for this reason: that if an animal one was made, we will not receive what we lost in him, but something as much better as the spiritual is to be set before the animal, when we will be *equal to the angels of God*[^38].

But the angels can be set before others even in righteousness; can they also be set before the Lord? Yet of him it was said, *you made him a little lower than the angels*[^39]. For what reason, except on account of the weakness of the flesh which he took from the Virgin, receiving the form of a servant[^40], in which, dying, he might redeem us from slavery? But why should we discourse on this at greater length?

For the Apostle's view on this matter is not obscure; who, when he wished to bring forward a testimony to prove that there is an animal body, recalled and brought forward not so much from his own or any man's body, which was seen in the present, as from this very passage of this Scripture, saying, *if there is an animal body, there is also a spiritual one; for thus it is written, 'the first man Adam was made into a living soul; the last Adam into a life-giving spirit. But not first what is spiritual, but what is animal; afterward, the spiritual. The first man, of the earth, earthy; the second man, from heaven, heavenly. As is the earthy, such are the earthy also; and as is the heavenly, such are the heavenly also. And as we have borne the image of the earthy, let us bear also the image of him who is from heaven*[^41].' What can be said to this?

The image of the heavenly man, then, we now bear by faith, going to have in the resurrection what we believe: but the image of the earthly man we put on from the very beginning of human generation.

## A difficulty: how we are renewed in the body.

**20. 31.** Here another question occurs: how are we renewed, if we are not recalled through Christ to what we were before in Adam? For although many things are renewed not into their former state but into something better, yet they are renewed from a lower state than the one they were in before. Whence, then, was that son dead, and lived again; had perished, and was found[^42]: whence is the first robe brought out to him, if he does not receive the immortality that Adam lost? But how did Adam lose immortality, if he had an animal body? For it will not be an animal body, but a spiritual one, when *this corruptible has put on incorruption, and this mortal has put on immortality*[^43].

Some, hemmed in by these straits—so that both that view may stand by which the example of an animal body was given from here, so that it was said, *the first man Adam was made into a living soul*[^44]; and that this renewal and reception of immortality may not absurdly be said to be future into the former state, namely into that which Adam lost—have supposed that man was indeed at first of an animal body; but that, while he was established in paradise, he was changed, just as we too will be changed in the resurrection. The book of Genesis does not record this; but, so that both testimonies of the Scriptures may be able to agree with one another—both that which was said of the animal body, and those very many things that are found in the Holy Writings about our renewal—they have believed that this follows as it were necessarily.

## The foregoing difficulty is resolved.

**21. 32.** But if this is so, it is in vain that we try to take paradise and those trees and their fruits, apart from a figurative meaning, first according to the propriety of the deeds done. For who would believe that foods of that kind, from the fruits of the trees, could have been necessary to immortal and spiritual bodies? Nevertheless, if nothing else can be found, we choose rather to understand paradise spiritually than either to suppose that man is not renewed, although Scripture so often records this, or to think that he receives what he is not shown to have lost. To this is added that the very death of man—which many divine testimonies declare he merited by sin—indicates that he would have been without death, unless he had sinned. How, then, mortal without death? Or how not mortal, if an animal body?

## That Adam merited death by sin.

**22. 33.** Hence some suppose that he merited by sin not the death of the body, but the death of the soul, which iniquity wrought. For they believe that, on account of his animal body, he would have gone out of this body—namely to the rest which the saints who have already fallen asleep now have—and at the end of the age would receive the same members immortally; so that, evidently, the death of the body might seem to have happened not from sin, but naturally, as for the other animals.

But the Apostle again meets these and says, *the body indeed is dead on account of sin, but the spirit is life on account of righteousness. But if the Spirit of him who raised Christ from the dead dwells in you; he who raised Christ from the dead will give life also to your mortal bodies through his Spirit dwelling in you*[^45]. And thus the death even of the body is from sin. If, then, Adam had not sinned, he would not die even in body; and therefore he would have an immortal body too. How, then, immortal, if animal?

## Adam's body at once animal and conditionally immortal.

**23. 34.** But again, those who suppose that his body was changed in paradise, so that from animal it became spiritual, do not see that there is nothing to prevent—if he had not sinned—that after the life of paradise, which he would have lived justly and obediently, he should receive that same change of body in eternal life, where he would no longer need corporeal foods.

Why, then, is it necessary on this account to be forced now to understand paradise figuratively, not properly, because the body could not die except by sin? It is indeed true that he would not die even in body, unless he had sinned; for the Apostle says openly, *the body dead on account of sin*[^46]: yet it could be animal before sin, and after the life of righteousness, when God willed, become spiritual.

## How we will be renewed in this life and in the resurrection of bodies.

**24. 35.** "How, then," they say, "are we said to be renewed, if we do not receive what the first man lost, *in whom all die*?" This we plainly receive in a certain way, and do not receive in a certain way. We do not, then, receive the immortality of the spiritual body, which man did not yet have; but we receive the righteousness from which man fell by sin. We will be renewed, then, from the oldness of sin, not into the former animal body, in which Adam was, but into something better—that is, into a spiritual body, when we will be made *equal to the angels of God*[^47], fit for the heavenly dwelling, where we will not need the food that perishes. We are renewed, then, *in the spirit of our mind*[^48], *according to the image of him who created us,* which Adam lost by sinning. But we will be renewed also in the flesh, *when this corruptible puts on incorruption*[^49], so that there is a spiritual body; into which Adam was not yet changed, but was to be changed, unless by sinning he had merited the death even of the animal body.

**24. 36.** Finally, the Apostle does not say, "the body indeed mortal on account of sin"; but, *the body dead on account of sin*[^50].

## That Adam was mortal by the condition of the animal body, but immortal by the benefit of the Founder.

**25. 36.** For before sin he could be called both mortal according to one cause, and immortal according to another: that is, mortal, because he could die; immortal, because he could not die. For it is one thing not to be able to die, as God created certain immortal natures; but another thing to be able not to die, according to which mode the first man was created immortal—which was afforded him from the tree of life, not from the constitution of his nature: from which tree he was separated when he had sinned, so that he could die, who, unless he had sinned, could not die.

He was, then, mortal by the condition of the animal body, but immortal by the benefit of the Founder. For if the body was animal, it was certainly mortal, because it could even die; though immortal also, for the reason that it could even not die. For that which can in no way die will not be immortal except as spiritual, which is promised to be ours in the resurrection. And thus that animal—and on this account mortal—body, which on account of righteousness would become spiritual and on this account in every way immortal, was made, on account of sin, not mortal, which it was before too, but dead—which could have not come to be, if man had not sinned.

## Adam's body other than ours.

**26. 37.** How, then, does the Apostle call our body dead, when he was still speaking of the living, except because the very condition of dying, from the sin of the parents, has clung to the offspring? For this body too is animal, as the first man's was; but this is now, in the very kind of animal body, much worse: for it has the necessity of dying, which that one did not have. For although it still remained that it be changed, and, made spiritual, receive full immortality, where it would not need corruptible food; yet if man lived justly, and his body were changed into a spiritual condition, it would not go into death.

But in us, even living justly, the body is going to die; and on account of this necessity, coming from the sin of that first man, the Apostle called our body not mortal but dead, because we all die in Adam[^51]. Likewise he says, *as the truth is in Jesus, that you put off, according to your former way of life, the old man; him who is corrupted according to the desires of deception*[^52]; that is, Adam made so by sin. See, then, what follows: *but be renewed in the spirit of your mind, and put on the new man; him who was created according to God, in righteousness and the holiness of truth*[^53]: behold what Adam lost by sin.

## How we are renewed in mind and body to that which Adam lost.

**27. 37.** In this, then, we are renewed, according to what Adam lost—that is, according to the spirit of our mind: but according to the body, which is sown animal and will rise spiritual, we will be renewed into something better, which Adam was not yet.

**27. 38.** The Apostle likewise says, *stripping off the old man with his acts, put on the new, who is renewed unto the knowledge of God, according to the image of him who created him*[^54]. This image, impressed on the spirit of the mind, Adam lost by sin; which we receive through the grace of righteousness—not the spiritual and immortal body, in which he was not yet, and in which all the saints rising from the dead will be: for this is the reward of that merit which he lost. Accordingly, that *first robe*[^55] is either the very righteousness from which he fell; or, if it signifies the garment of corporeal immortality, even this he so lost, since on account of sin he could not arrive at it. For a man is said to have lost both a wife and an honor, who did not receive what he hoped for, having offended the one from whom he hoped for it.

## That Adam, spiritual in mind, was animal in body even in paradise.

**28. 39.** According to this view, then, Adam had an animal body, not only before paradise, but also when established in paradise: although in the inner man he was spiritual, according to the image of him who created him; which he lost by sinning, and merited also the death of the body, who by not sinning would have merited even the change into a spiritual body. For if he lived animally even within, we cannot be said to be renewed to that very state of his. For those to whom it is said, *be renewed in the spirit of your mind*[^56], it is said to them that they may become spiritual: but if he was not spiritual even in his very mind, how are we renewed to that which man never was?

But the apostles and all the just still certainly had an animal body, yet they nonetheless lived spiritually within; renewed, namely, "unto the knowledge of God, according to the image of him who created them": yet not for that reason were they now unable to sin, if they consented to iniquity. For the Apostle shows that even the spiritual can fall in the temptation of sin, where he says, *brothers, if a man be overtaken in any fault, you who are spiritual, instruct such a one in the spirit of gentleness, looking to yourself, lest you too be tempted*[^57]. I have said this, lest it seem to anyone impossible that Adam sinned, if he was spiritual in mind, although he was animal in body.

Since this is so, we nonetheless affirm nothing as yet too hastily, but rather wait to see whether the rest of Scripture too does not hinder this understanding.

## The question of the soul to be treated in the following book.

**29. 40.** For there follows a very difficult question about the soul, on which many have labored, and have left us where we may labor. For whether because I have not been able to read all the writings of all who, on this matter according to the truth of our Scriptures, have been able to arrive at something clear and least doubtful; or because the question is so great that even those who solve it truly are not easily understood by such as I am myself: I confess that no one has yet persuaded me to hold such a view about the soul that I should think nothing further to be inquired. But whether I shall now find and define anything certain, I do not know. Yet what I can, if the Lord helps my effort, I will take care to unfold in the following volume.
