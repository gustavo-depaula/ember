# Book XII

## The plan of this book: the paradise of which Paul speaks.

**1. 1.** From the beginning of Holy Scripture, which is entitled *Genesis*, until the first human being was sent out of paradise, we have, in eleven books, discussed and written down—whether by asserting and defending what is certain to us, or by inquiring, judging, and remaining in doubt about uncertain things—what we could, and as we could; not so much prescribing to each person what he should think about obscure matters, as showing that we ourselves needed to be taught in those things about which we were in doubt, and removing from the reader the rashness of affirming, where we have not been able to provide the firmness of knowledge.

But this twelfth book, now freed from that care by which the text of the *Sacred Letters* to be treated occupied us, will deal more freely and at greater length with the question of paradise; lest we be thought to have evaded what the Apostle seems to intimate—paradise in the third heaven—where he says: *I know a man in Christ fourteen years ago, whether in the body I know not, or out of the body I know not, God knows, that such a one was caught up even to the third heaven: and I know that such a man, whether in the body or out of the body I know not, God knows, was caught up into paradise, and heard ineffable words, which it is not lawful for a man to utter*[^1]. Is the third heaven the same as paradise?

**1. 2.** In these words it is usually asked, first, what he means by the third heaven; then, whether he meant paradise to be understood there, or whether, after he was caught up to the third heaven, he was caught up also into paradise, wherever paradise may be—so that to be caught up into the third heaven was not the same as into paradise, but first into the third heaven, and afterward from there into paradise. And this is so ambiguous that it does not seem to me capable of being resolved, unless someone find something—not from the present words of the Apostle, but perhaps from other places of the Scriptures, or by clear reasoning—by which he may teach either that paradise is in the third heaven, or that it is not; since even what the third heaven itself is does not clearly appear, whether it is to be understood among corporeal things, or perhaps among spiritual ones.

It might indeed be said that a man could be caught up with the body only into some corporeal place; but because he also stated this in such a way that he said he did not know whether he was caught up in the body or out of the body, who would dare to say that he knows what the Apostle said he did not know? Nevertheless, if neither can the spirit without the body be caught up to corporeal places, nor the body to spiritual ones, this very doubt of his, as it were, compels us to understand (since no one doubts that he wrote these things about himself) that what he was caught up to was of such a kind that whether it was corporeal or spiritual could not be discerned and distinguished.

## On things seen in dreams.

**2. 3.** For when, whether in dreams or in ecstasy, images of bodies are represented, they are not at all distinguished from bodies, except when a person, restored to the senses of the body, recognizes that he was among those images, which he was not drawing in through the senses of the body. For who, when he has awakened from sleep, does not at once perceive that what he was seeing was imaginary, although, while he saw them sleeping, he was unable to distinguish them from the corporeal sights of the waking?

Yet I know that it has happened to me, and on this account I do not doubt that it could or can also happen to others, that, seeing in dreams, I perceived in the dreams that I was seeing; and that those images, which are wont to make sport of our very assent, are not true bodies, but are presented in dreams—this I held and perceived most firmly, even while sleeping.

Yet in this I was sometimes deceived: that I tried to persuade of this very thing a friend of mine, whom I likewise saw in the dreams, that those were not bodies which we saw, but images of dreamers, when he too surely appeared to me among those things just as those things did; to whom I also said this, that what we were both speaking was not true either, but that he too was then, sleeping, seeing something else, and that I altogether did not know whether I was seeing these things; but when I labored to persuade that very man that he himself did not exist, I was led in part also to think that he too existed—to whom I surely would not be speaking, if I were in every way so affected as that he did not exist. Thus the soul of the sleeper, although wonderfully awake, could not but be led by the images of bodies, as though they were the bodies themselves.

## On visions in ecstasy.

**2. 4.** But as for ecstasy, I have been able to hear of one man, and he a rustic scarcely able to express what he perceived, who both knew himself to be awake and saw something not with the eyes of the body. For, to use his own words, as far as I can recall: "My soul," he said, "saw him, not my eyes. Yet I did not know whether it was a body or the image of a body." For he was not such a man as to distinguish these things, but so simply faithful that I heard him as if I myself had seen what he related himself to have seen.

## On the visions that Holy Scripture relates.

**2. 5.** And on this account, if Paul saw paradise in the way that the vessel let down from heaven appeared to Peter[^2], in the way that to John appeared whatever he wrote that he saw in the Apocalypse[^3], in the way that to Ezekiel appeared that field with the bones of the dead and their resurrection[^4], in the way that to Isaiah appeared God seated, and in his sight the Seraphim, and the altar from which the coal was taken that cleansed the prophet's lips[^5]: it is manifest that he could have been ignorant whether he saw these things in the body or out of the body.

## Of what kind the Apostle's vision was.

**3. 6.** But if these things were seen out of the body, and were not bodies, it can still be asked whether they were images of bodies, or that substance which bears no likeness of body, such as God, such as the mind of the human being itself, or the understanding, or reason, such as the virtues—prudence, justice, chastity, love, devotion, and whatever others there are, which by understanding and thinking we enumerate, distinguish, and define, surely not by gazing upon their outlines or colors, or how they sound, or what they smell, or what they taste in the mouth, or what they report to those who handle them about heat or cold, softness or hardness, smoothness or roughness; but by a certain other vision, another light, another evidence of things, and that far more excellent and more certain than the rest.

## Why Paul does not say how he could have seen what he saw.

**3. 7.** Let us therefore return again to those same words of the Apostle, and scrutinize them more carefully, this being first established without doubt, that the Apostle knew much more, and incomparably more fully, what we try in any way to know about incorporeal and corporeal nature. If, then, he knew that spiritual things can in no way be seen through the body, nor corporeal things out of the body, why did he not distinguish, by those very things which he saw, in what way too he could have seen them? For if he was certain that they were spiritual, why was he not consequently certain, no less, that he had seen them out of the body? But if he knew that they were corporeal, why did he not also know that they could be seen only through the body? Whence, then, does he doubt whether he saw them in the body or out of the body, unless perhaps he also doubts whether they were bodies or likenesses of bodies?

Let us therefore first see what there is in the framing of these words about which he does not doubt, and so, when there remains what he does doubt, perhaps from those things about which he does not doubt it will appear in what way he doubts even that.

## That it is in no way to be doubted that Paul was in fact caught up to the third heaven.

**3. 8.** *I know*, he says, *a man in Christ fourteen years ago, whether in the body I know not, or out of the body I know not, God knows, that such a one was caught up even to the third heaven*[^6]. He knows, then, that fourteen years ago a man in Christ was caught up to the third heaven; of this he does not doubt at all; nor therefore ought we to doubt: but whether in the body or out of the body, he doubts; and so, while he doubts, who of us would dare to be certain? Will it perhaps follow from this too that we should doubt that it was the third heaven to which he said the man was caught up? For if the thing itself was shown, the third heaven was shown: but if some image was made like to corporeal things, that was not the third heaven, but that showing was so ordered that he seemed to himself to ascend a first heaven, above which he saw a second, ascending to which again he saw yet another higher one, to which when he had come, he could say that he was caught up to the third heaven. But that it was the third heaven to which he was caught up, he neither doubted, nor did he wish us to doubt: for to this end he prefixed, *I know*, and began from there, so that what the Apostle says he knows, he alone would not believe to be true who does not believe the Apostle.

## That the third heaven is not some corporeal sign.

**4. 9.** He knows, then, that the man was caught up even to the third heaven: accordingly, that to which he was caught up is truly the third heaven; not some corporeal sign, which, when it was shown to Moses, he so perceived to be one thing—the very substance of God—and another—the visible creature in which God presented himself to human and corporeal senses—that he said, *show me yourself*[^7]; nor some image of a corporeal thing, which, when John saw it in spirit, he asked what it was, and it was answered to him, either, *it is a city*, or, *they are peoples*, or something else, when he saw a beast, or a woman, or waters, or something of that kind: but, *I know*, he says, *a man caught up even to the third heaven*[^8].

## That the third heaven is not a spiritual image.

**4. 10.** But if he had wished to call a spiritual image, like to a corporeal one, "heaven," then there was likewise an image of his body, in which he had ascended, caught up, to that place: thus, then, he would call his own body too—though an image of a body—just as that heaven, though an image of heaven; nor would he care to distinguish what he knew and what he did not know, that is, that he knew the man was caught up even to the third heaven, but did not know whether in the body or out of the body; but he would simply narrate the vision, calling the things he saw by the names of those things to which they were like. For we too, when we narrate our dreams or some revelation in them, say, "I saw a mountain, I saw a river, I saw three men," and so forth; attributing to those images the names which belong to the very things to which they were like. But the Apostle says, "This *I know*, that *I know not*."

## That neither the body nor the third heaven were seen by Paul as images.

**4. 11.** But if both appeared as images, both are equally known, or equally unknown: but if it was heaven properly, and therefore is known, how could the body of that man have appeared as an image?

## Of what kind was the heaven to which Paul was caught up?

**4. 12.** For if a corporeal heaven was seen, why was it hidden whether it was seen with corporeal eyes? But if it was uncertain whether it was seen with the eyes of the body or with the spirit (and therefore it was said, *whether in the body, or out of the body, I know not*[^9]), how was that not uncertain too, whether a corporeal heaven was truly seen, or was shown as an image?

And likewise, if an incorporeal substance was seen, not in some image of a body, but as justice, wisdom, and the like are seen, and this was heaven; this too is manifest, that something of that kind cannot be seen with the eyes of this body: and on this account, if he knew that he had seen something of that kind, he could not doubt that he had not seen it through the body. *I know*, he says, *a man in Christ fourteen years ago*: this *I know*, let no one who believes me doubt; but whether *in the body* or *out of the body, I know not, God knows*[^10].

## It is discussed whether that heaven was a body or a spirit.

**5. 13.** What, then, do you know, which you distinguish from what you do not know, lest believers be deceived? *The same man*, he says, *was caught up even to the third heaven*[^11]. But that heaven was either a body or a spirit: if it was a body, and was seen with corporeal eyes, why is it known to be that heaven, and not known to have been seen in the body? But if it was a spirit; either it presented the image of a body, and it is as uncertain whether it was a body as it is uncertain whether it was seen in the body; or it was seen as wisdom is seen by the mind, without any images of bodies, and it is no less certain that it could not be seen through the body: either, then, both are certain, or both are uncertain; or how is what was seen certain, but that through which it was seen uncertain?

For it is manifest that an incorporeal nature could not be seen by him through the body. But bodies, even if they can be seen without the body, are surely not seen in that way through the body, but that manner is far different, if there is any such; whence it would be a wonder if it could deceive the Apostle, as if most similar, or compel him to doubt, so that, if he saw a corporeal heaven with non-corporeal eyes, he would say it was uncertain to him whether he saw it in the body or out of the body.

## The manifold ways of estrangement from the body.

**5. 14.** It remains, therefore, perhaps, that—since the Apostle could not lie, who took such care to distinguish what he knew and what he did not know—we should understand that he was ignorant of this very thing: whether, when he was caught up to the third heaven, he was in the body, as the soul is in the body when the body is said to live, whether of one waking, or sleeping, or estranged in ecstasy from the senses of the body; or whether he had gone out of the body altogether, so that the body lay dead, until, that showing accomplished, the soul was restored to the dead members, and he did not awaken as one sleeping, or, estranged in ecstasy, return again to the senses, but, altogether dead, came back to life.

Accordingly, what he saw, caught up to the third heaven, which he also affirms that he knows, he saw properly, not as an image. But because this very estrangement from the body—whether it left the body altogether dead, or whether the soul was there according to a certain manner of a living body, but his mind was seized to see or hear the ineffable things of that vision—this was uncertain, therefore perhaps he said, *whether in the body or out of the body, I know not, God knows*[^12].

## On the three kinds of vision which are demonstrated in one commandment.

**6. 15.** But what is seen not as an image, but properly, and is not seen through the body, this is seen by that vision which surpasses all the rest. Their kinds and differences I will take care to set forth, as far as the Lord shall help me. Behold, in this one commandment, when we read, *you shall love your neighbor as yourself*[^13], three kinds of vision occur: one through the eyes, by which the letters themselves are seen; another through the spirit of the human being, by which the neighbor, even when absent, is thought of; the third through the gaze of the mind, by which love itself, once understood, is beheld. Of these three kinds, that first is manifest to all: for in it are seen heaven and earth, and all things in them that are visible to our eyes. Nor is it difficult to explain that second, by which absent corporeal things are thought of: for we think of heaven and earth themselves, and the things we can see in them, even when set in darkness; where, seeing nothing with the eyes of the body, we yet behold corporeal images in the mind, either true, as we have seen the bodies themselves, and retain them in memory; or fictitious, as thought has been able to form them. For we think of Carthage, which we know, in one way, and of Alexandria, which we do not know, in another.

But that third, by which love, once understood, is beheld, contains those things which have no images like themselves, which are not what they themselves are. For a human being or a tree or the sun, and whatever other bodies, whether celestial or terrestrial, are both seen present in their own forms, and thought of when absent in images impressed on the mind; and they make two kinds of things seen, one through the senses of the body, the other through the spirit, in which those images are contained. But is love seen in one way when present in the form in which it is, and in another way when absent in some image like itself? Surely not; but, as far as it can be discerned by the mind, it is itself discerned more by one, less by another: but if something of a corporeal image is thought of, it is not itself discerned.

## The names of the three visions: corporeal, spiritual, intellectual.

**7. 16.** These are the three kinds of vision, of which we said something also in the earlier books, as the matter seemed to require, yet without mentioning their number; and now, having briefly introduced them—since the question undertaken demands that we discuss these somewhat more fully—we ought to mark them with fixed and fitting names, lest by constantly using circumlocutions we cause delays. Let us therefore call the first corporeal, because it is perceived through the body and presented to the senses of the body. The second, spiritual; for whatever is not a body, and yet is something, is now rightly called spirit: and surely the image of an absent body is not a body, although it is like a body, nor is that very gaze by which it is discerned. The third, indeed, intellectual, from "intellect" (intellectus); because "mental," from "mind" (mens), is, by the very novelty of the word, too absurd for us to say.

## That something can be called "corporeal" properly or by transfer.

**7. 17.** If I were to render the rationale of these words more subtly, the discourse would be both longer and more involved, when this is demanded by no necessity, or at any rate by no such great necessity. It is enough, then, to know that something corporeal is called so either properly, when bodies are in question, or also by a transferred word, as it is said: *because in him dwells all the fullness of the Godhead bodily*[^14]. For the Godhead is not a body, but because he calls the sacraments of the Old Testament *shadows of what was to come*[^15], on account of the comparison with shadows he said that the fullness of the Godhead dwells bodily in Christ, in that there are fulfilled in him all the things that were figured by those shadows, and thus in a manner he is the body of those shadows, that is, he is the truth of those figures and significations. As, then, the figures themselves were called shadows significatively, surely by a transferred word, not properly; so too, when he says that the fullness of the Godhead dwells bodily, he used a transferred word.

## He shows that "spiritual" can be said in many ways.

**7. 18.** "Spiritual," however, is said in several ways. For the body too, which will be in the resurrection of the saints, the Apostle calls spiritual, where he says: *an animal body is sown, a spiritual body shall rise*[^16]; in that, in wonderful ways, it is subjected to the spirit for every ease and incorruption, and, without any need of corporeal nourishment, is given life by the spirit alone—not that it will have an incorporeal substance: for not even this body, such as we now have, has the substance of the soul, and is what the soul is, because it was called animal.

Likewise "spirit" is said of either this air, or its breath, that is, its motion, as it was said: *fire, hail, snow, ice, the spirit of the storm*[^17]. The soul too is called spirit, whether of a beast or of a human being, as it is written: *and who knows whether the spirit of the sons of man ascends upward, and whether the spirit of the beast descends downward into the earth?*[^18] The rational mind itself is also called spirit, where there is a certain thing, as it were, an eye of the soul, to which belong the image and the knowledge of God. Whence the Apostle says: *be renewed in the spirit of your mind, and put on the new man, who has been created according to God*[^19]; while he also says elsewhere of the inner man: *who is renewed in the knowledge of God, after the image of him who created him*[^20]. Likewise, when he had said, *therefore I myself with the mind serve the law of God, but with the flesh the law of sin*[^21], recalling the same statement in another place, he said: *the flesh*, he says, *lusts against the spirit, and the spirit against the flesh, so that you do not do the things you wish*[^22]—what he called mind, this he also called spirit. God too is called spirit, as the Lord says in the Gospel: *God is spirit, and those who worship him must worship in spirit and in truth*[^23].

## The kind of vision that is called spiritual.

**8. 19.** Out of all these ways which we have mentioned, by which "spirit" is named, we did not draw this word by which we called spiritual this kind of vision of which we are now treating; but from that one way which we find in the *Epistle to the Corinthians*, by which the spirit is distinguished from the mind by a most evident testimony. *For if I pray*, he says, *in a tongue, my spirit prays, but my mind is unfruitful*[^24]. Since, then, by "tongue" he is understood in this place to mean obscure and mystical significations, from which, if you remove the understanding of the mind, no one is edified by hearing what he does not understand; whence he also says: *for he who speaks in a tongue speaks not to men, but to God; for no one hears, but the spirit speaks mysteries*[^25]: he sufficiently indicates that he calls it "tongue" in this place, where there are significations like images and likenesses of things, which, in order to be understood, need the gaze of the mind. But when they are not understood, he says they are in the spirit, not in the mind: whence he says more openly: *if you bless with the spirit, how shall he who supplies the place of the unlearned say, amen, over your blessing, since he does not know what you say?*[^26]

Because, then, even by the tongue—that is, the member of the body which we move in the mouth when we speak—signs of things are surely given, not the things themselves brought forth; for this reason, by a transferred word, he called "tongue" any bringing forth of signs before they are understood: when understanding has been added to it, which is proper to the mind, there comes about revelation, or knowledge, or prophecy, or teaching. Accordingly he says: *if I come to you speaking in tongues, what shall I profit you, unless I speak to you in revelation, or in knowledge, or in prophecy, or in teaching?*[^27] that is, with signs; that is, when understanding has been added to the tongue, so that what is done is done not by the spirit only, but also by the mind.

## What the difference is between "spirit" and "mind."

**9. 20.** Accordingly, in those to whom signs were shown in the spirit through certain likenesses of corporeal things, unless the office of the mind had been added, so that they were also understood, there was as yet no prophecy; and he was more a prophet who interpreted what another had seen than the one who had seen it. Whence it appears that prophecy belongs more to the mind than to that spirit which, in a certain proper manner, is called spirit—a certain power of the soul lower than the mind, where the likenesses of corporeal things are represented. And so Joseph was more a prophet, who understood what the seven ears of grain and the seven oxen signified, than Pharaoh, who saw them in dreams[^28]. For the spirit of the one was informed, so that he saw; the mind of the other was illumined, so that he understood. And on this account in the one there was tongue, in the other prophecy; because in the one there was the imagining of things, in the other the interpretation of the imaginings.

Less a prophet, then, is he who sees in the spirit, through images of corporeal things, only the signs themselves of the things that are signified; and more a prophet is he who is endowed with the understanding of them alone: but most of all a prophet is he who excels in both, so that he both sees in the spirit the significative likenesses of corporeal things, and understands them by the liveliness of the mind, as the excellence of Daniel was tested and proved, who both told the king the dream he had seen, and disclosed what it signified[^29]. For both the corporeal images were represented in his spirit, and the understanding of them was revealed in his mind.

From this way, then, in which "spirit" is named in this distinction—according to which the Apostle said, *I will pray with the spirit, but I will pray also with the mind*[^30], so that both the signs of things might be formed in the spirit, and the understanding of them might shine in the mind—according to this distinction, I say, we have now called such a kind of things seen "spiritual," the kind by which we also think of the images of absent bodies.

## The intellectual kind of vision.

**10. 21.** But that intellectual vision is more excellent, which is proper to the mind. Nor does it occur to me at all that "intellect" can be said in as many ways as we have found "spirit" to be named. Whether, however, we say "intellectual" or "intelligible," we signify this same thing. Although some have wished there to be some difference, so that the intelligible is the thing itself, which can be perceived by the intellect alone; but the intellectual is the mind which understands: yet that there is some thing which can be discerned by the intellect alone, and does not also understand, is a great and difficult question.

But that there is a thing which perceives by intellect, and cannot also be perceived by intellect, I do not think anyone either thinks or says; for the mind is not seen except by the mind. Because, then, it can be seen, it is intelligible; because it can also see, it is intellectual, according to that distinction. Wherefore, setting aside that most difficult question, whether there is anything that is only understood and does not understand, we now call "intellectual" and "intelligible" under the same signification.

## The order and grades of the three kinds of things seen.

**11. 22.** These three kinds of vision, then—corporeal, spiritual, and intellectual—are to be considered one by one, so that reason may ascend from the lower to the higher. Already indeed above we set forth an example by which all three kinds may be seen in one statement. For when we read, *you shall love your neighbor as yourself*[^31], the letters are seen corporeally, the neighbor is thought of spiritually, love is beheld intellectually. But the letters too, when absent, can be thought of spiritually, and the neighbor, when present, can be seen corporeally; but love can neither be discerned through its own substance by the eyes of the body, nor thought of in the spirit through an image like to a body, but is known and perceived by the mind alone, that is, by the intellect.

Corporeal vision, indeed, presides over none of these kinds, but what is perceived through it is reported to that spiritual one as to one presiding. For when something is discerned by the eyes, at once an image of it is made in the spirit; but it is not recognized as made, except when, the eyes being turned away from what we were seeing through the eyes, we find its image in the mind. And if indeed the spirit is irrational, as that of a beast, the eyes report only this far. But if the soul is rational, it is reported also to the intellect, which also presides over the spirit, so that, if what the eyes have drawn in, and have reported to the spirit, that its image might be made there, is the sign of some thing, either it is at once understood what it signifies, or it is inquired into; since it can be neither understood nor inquired into except by the office of the mind.

## The vision of King Belshazzar.

**11. 23.** King Belshazzar saw the joints of a hand writing on the wall, and at once, through the sense of the body, the image of the thing corporeally done was impressed on his spirit, and, that very sight done and past, it remained in his thought: it was seen in the spirit, and not understood, nor was this sign then understood when it was being made corporeally and appeared to the corporeal eyes; yet it was already understood to be a sign, having this from the office of the mind. And because it was inquired what it signified, the very inquiry too the mind surely conducted. This not having been found out, Daniel approached, and, his mind illumined by a prophetic spirit, disclosed to the troubled king what was portended by that sign[^32]; he rather the prophet through this kind of vision which is proper to the mind, than the one who had both seen corporeally the sign corporeally made, and discerned the image of it, now past, by thinking in the spirit, and could do nothing by intellect except to know that it was a sign, and to inquire what it signified.

## The vision of Peter.

**11. 24.** Peter saw, in an estrangement of mind, a vessel bound by four lines let down from heaven, full of various animals, when he also heard a voice: *kill, and eat*[^33]. And when, restored to his senses, he was debating about the sight, behold, those whom Cornelius had sent—the spirit announced to him, saying: *behold, men are seeking you; but rise, go down, and go with them, for I have sent them*[^34]. And when he had come to Cornelius, what he had understood in that vision, where he had heard, *what God has cleansed, do not call common*[^35], he himself indicated, saying: *but God has shown me to call no man common or unclean*[^36].

When, therefore, he saw that vessel, estranged from the senses of the body, he was hearing those voices, *kill, and eat*, and, *what God has cleansed, do not call common*, in the spirit. But, restored to the senses of the body, he discerned that very thing which he had retained in memory as seen and heard, by thinking in the same spirit. All of which were not corporeal, but images of corporeal things, whether when they were first seen in that estrangement, or when afterward recalled and thought of. But when it was being debated, and it was inquired that those signs might be understood, it was the action of the mind striving, but the effect was lacking until those who came from Cornelius were announced; and when this corporeal vision too was added, since the Holy Spirit again said to him in the spirit, *go with them*, where he had both shown that sign and impressed the voices, the mind, divinely aided, understood what was being done by all those signs.

These and similar things being carefully considered, it appears sufficiently that corporeal vision is referred to spiritual, and that spiritual one referred to intellectual.

## Corporeal and spiritual vision.

**12. 25.** But when we are awake, the mind not estranged from the senses of the body, in corporeal vision, we distinguish from it the spiritual vision by which we think of absent bodies as images, whether recalling from memory things we know, or somehow forming in the very thought of the spirit things we do not know but which nevertheless exist, or feigning at will or by opinion things that exist nowhere at all. From all these we so distinguish those corporeal things which we see, and in whose presence the senses of our body are, that we do not doubt that these are bodies, but those are images of bodies.

But when, whether by an excessive intentness of thought, or by some force of disease, as is wont to happen to the delirious through fever, or by the admixture of some other spirit, whether evil or good, the images of corporeal things are so represented in the spirit as if bodies were presented to the very senses of the body—the intentness yet remaining also in the senses of the body—then the images of bodies which are made in the spirit are seen just as the bodies themselves are seen through the body; so that at the same time both some man present is discerned by the eyes, and another absent by the spirit as by the eyes. For we have known persons so affected, and speaking both with those who were truly present, and with others who were not present, as if they were present. And some, on recovering, report what they had seen; others cannot. For thus some forget their dreams, and some remember them.

But when the intentness of the mind is wholly turned away and snatched from the senses of the body, then it is usually called ecstasy. Then, whatever bodies are present, even with the eyes open, are not seen, nor are any voices at all heard: the whole gaze of the mind is either in the images of bodies, through spiritual vision, or in incorporeal things figured by no image of body, through intellectual vision.

**12. 26.** But when spiritual vision, the mind wholly estranged from the senses of the body, is held by the images of corporeal things, whether in dreams or in ecstasy, if what is seen signifies nothing, they are imaginings of the soul itself: just as even those who are awake and sound, and moved by no estrangement, turn over in thought the images of many bodies which are not present to the senses of the body. But this is the difference, that they distinguish them from present and true bodies by a steady disposition.

But if they signify something—whether they are presented to those sleeping, or to those waking, when they both see present bodies with the eyes and discern the images of absent ones with the spirit, as if they were at hand to the eyes, or in that which is called ecstasy, the mind wholly estranged from the senses of the body—it is a wonderful manner: but it can come about, by the admixture of another spirit, that what it itself knows it shows, through images of this kind, to him with whom it is mingled, whether to one understanding, or so that they may be disclosed when understood by another. For if these things are shown, and surely cannot be shown by a body, what remains but that they be shown by some spirit?

## It is refuted that there is in the soul a certain power of divination.

**13. 27.** Some indeed wish the human soul to have a certain power of divination in itself. But if it is so, why can it not always, when it always wishes? Is it because it is not always aided so that it can? When, then, it is aided, can it be aided for this by none, or by a body? Accordingly, it remains that it is aided by a spirit. Then, in what way is it aided? Is something done in the body, so that from there its intentness is, as it were, relaxed and flashes forth, by which it comes to that point where it sees in itself the significant likenesses which were already there, and were not seen—just as we have many things even in the memory which we do not always behold? Or are there made there things which were not before, or are they in some spirit, into which it, bursting and emerging, sees them there?

But if they were already in it as if properly, why does it not also consequently understand them? For sometimes, indeed for the most part, it does not understand. Or, just as its spirit was aided so that it saw them in itself, so too can the mind, unless it be aided, not understand the things the spirit has? Or perhaps the corporeal impediments are not removed, or as it were relaxed, so that the soul by its own impulse stretches out toward the things to be seen; but the soul itself is wholly taken up into these, whether to be discerned only spiritually, or to be known also intellectually? Or does it sometimes see these things in itself, sometimes through the admixture of another spirit? Whichever of these it is, it ought not to be rashly affirmed.

This, however, ought not to be doubted: that the corporeal images which are discerned by the spirit are not always signs of other things, whether in those waking, or sleeping, or sick: but it is a wonder if ecstasy can ever come about in which those likenesses of corporeal things signify nothing.

## What the power and action of good and evil spirits may be.

**13. 28.** It is indeed no wonder if those who have a demon sometimes say true things which are absent from the senses of those present; for this surely comes about by some hidden mingling of that same spirit, so that it is, as it were, one spirit of the sufferer and of the vexer. But when a good spirit takes up or seizes the human spirit into these things seen, it is in no way to be doubted that those images are signs of other things, and of those which it is useful to know: for it is the gift of God. The discernment, indeed, is most difficult, when a malignant spirit acts as it were more tranquilly, and, without any vexation of the body, the human spirit being taken up, says what it can; when it even says true things, and proclaims useful ones, transfiguring itself, as it is written, *as an angel of light*[^37], to this end, that when it has been believed in manifest goods, it may seduce to its own.

This one I do not think is discerned, except by that gift of which the Apostle speaks, when he was speaking of the various gifts of God: *to another the discerning of spirits*[^38].

**14. 28.** For it is no great thing to recognize him then, when he has arrived at, or led to, things that are against good morals or the rule of faith: for then he is discerned by many. But by that gift, in the very beginning, when he still appears good to many, it is at once judged whether he is evil.

## That intellectual vision is not deceived.

**14. 29.** Yet both through corporeal vision, and through the images of corporeal things which are shown in the spirit, both the good instruct and the evil deceive. But intellectual vision is not deceived. For either he does not understand who thinks otherwise than it is; or, if he understands, it is at once true. For what can the eyes do, when they have seen a similar body which they cannot distinguish from another: or what can the intentness of the mind do, when in the spirit a likeness of a body has been made which it cannot distinguish from a body? But the intellect is applied, inquiring what those things signify or what useful thing they teach; and either, finding it, it attains to its fruit, or, not finding it, it holds itself in debate, lest by some pernicious rashness it slip into deadly error.

## That error in spiritual things seen is not always harmful.

**14. 30.** But the sober intellect, divinely aided, judges what and how great are the things in which even to think otherwise than it is is not pernicious to the soul. For it is not to the peril of those thinking, but rather to his own destruction, that anyone is thought good by good people, even if he is secretly evil, provided there is no error in the things themselves, that is, in the very good by which anyone becomes good: nor does it harm all human beings at all that, when they sleep, they think those to be true bodies whose likenesses they see in dreams: nor did it harm Peter at all that, loosed from his chains, and with the angel leading him, it came about by a sudden miracle that he thought he was seeing a vision[^39]: or when in that ecstasy he answered, *by no means, Lord, for I have never eaten anything common and unclean*[^40], thinking those very things which were shown in the vessel to be true animals[^41].

When these things are found to be other than they were thought to be when they were seen, we do not repent that they so seemed to us, provided no hard unbelief, or vain or sacrilegious opinion, be charged. Wherefore, even when the devil deceives with corporeal things seen, it does no harm that the eyes are made sport of, provided there be no error in the truth of faith, and in the soundness of understanding, by which God teaches those subject to him. Or if he make sport of the soul by spiritual vision with images of bodies, so that it thinks that to be a body which is not, it does some harm to the soul only if it consents to the pernicious suggestion.

## How the consents of dreamers are to be judged.

**15. 31.** Hence sometimes a question arises about the consents of dreamers, when they even seem to themselves to be coupling, either against their own resolve, or even against lawful morals. This does not happen except when the things which we, even waking, think of—not with the approval of consent, but as we also speak of such things for some reason—are so suggested and represented in dreams, that the flesh is naturally moved by them, and emits through the genital passages what it naturally gathers; just as I surely could not even say this very thing, unless I also thought it. Now the images of corporeal things, which I necessarily thought of in order to say these things, if they were presented in dreams with as much representation as bodies are presented to those waking, that would come about which could not come about without sin in one waking.

For who, even when he speaks, and, the necessity of the discourse requiring it, says something about his own coupling, can avoid thinking what he says? Now that very phantasm, which is made in the thought of one speaking, when it has been so represented in the vision of one dreaming that between it and the true coupling of bodies there is no distinction, the flesh is at once moved, and there follows what is wont to follow that motion—this coming about as much without sin as it is said without sin by one waking, what was without doubt thought in order to be said.

Nevertheless, on account of the good disposition of the soul, which, cleansed by a better desire, slays many desires that do not belong to the natural motion of the flesh—which the chaste restrain and rein in when waking, but cannot when sleeping, because they do not have in their power what representation of a corporeal image, indistinguishable from a body, may be brought to bear: on account, then, of that good disposition of the soul, even in dreams certain of its merits shine forth. For Solomon too, even while sleeping, preferred wisdom to all things, and, neglecting the rest, prayed for it from the Lord: and, as Scripture testifies, this pleased the Lord, nor did he delay a good recompense for the good desire[^42].

## How the senses of the body are related to things seen.

**16. 32.** Since this is so, the sense of the body pertains to corporeal things seen, which is distributed through five, as it were, rivulets of differing power: when that which is most subtle in the body, and on this account nearer to the soul than the rest—that is, light—is first diffused alone through the eyes, and flashes forth in the rays of the eyes to behold visible things; then, by a certain mixture, first with pure air, second with murky and misty air, third with a more corpulent moisture, fourth with earthy thickness, it makes the five senses, together with that very sense of the eyes in which it alone excels: as I recall having discussed in the fourth book, and likewise in the seventh.

Now this heaven visible to the eyes, from which the luminaries and stars shine forth, is surely more excellent than all the corporeal elements, just as the sense of the eyes excels in the body. But because every spirit is, without doubt, more excellent than every body, it follows that, not by position of place, but by dignity of nature, the spiritual nature is more excellent than this corporeal heaven, even that nature in which the images of corporeal things are represented.

## How an image is made in the spirit.

**16. 33.** Here something wonderful arises: that, since the spirit is prior to the body, and the image of the body posterior to the body, nevertheless, because that which is posterior in time is made in that which is prior in nature, the image of the body in the spirit is more excellent than the body itself in its own substance. Nor indeed is it to be thought that the body makes anything in the spirit, as if the spirit were subjected, in place of matter, to a body making something. For in every way the one who makes is more excellent than the thing out of which he makes something; and in no way is the body more excellent than the spirit; rather, in a clear manner, the spirit is more excellent than the body.

Although, therefore, we first see some body which we had not seen before, and from there its image begins to be in our spirit, by which we may remember it when it is absent: yet that same image of it is made, not by the body in the spirit, but by the spirit itself in itself, with a wonderful swiftness, which is ineffably far from the slowness of the body; whose image, as soon as it is seen by the eyes, is formed in the spirit of the one seeing without the interposition of any point of time. Likewise in hearing, unless the spirit at once formed in itself the image of a voice perceived by the ears, and retained it in memory, it would be unknown whether the second syllable was second, since the first surely was no longer any, which, having struck the ear, had passed.

And thus every use of speech, every sweetness of singing, in short every corporeal motion in our acts, would, having slipped away, perish; nor would it gain any progress, if the spirit did not retain in memory the past motions of the body, by which it might connect the following ones in acting. These surely it does not retain, except as made by itself in itself as images. The images even of future motions anticipate the ends of our acts. For what do we do through the body which the spirit has not anticipated by thinking, and seen first in itself the likenesses of all visible works, and in a manner disposed them?

## How the spiritual likenesses become known even to demons.

**17. 34.** How these spiritual likenesses of corporeal things in our mind become known even to unclean spirits, or what obstacle our soul suffers from this earthly body, so that we cannot see them in one another's spirit, is difficult to find out and explain. Yet it has been established among us by most certain indications that the thoughts of human beings are announced by demons; who, however, if they could discern the inward form of the virtues in human beings, would not tempt: just as, without doubt, if the devil could discern that noble and wonderful patience in Job, he would surely not wish to be overcome by the one tempted.

As for the rest, that they announce things done somewhere far off, which after some days are confirmed to be true, is not to be wondered at. For they can accomplish this not only by a sharpness of discerning, even of corporeal things, incomparably more excellent than ours, but also by the wonderful swiftness of the bodies themselves, which are surely far more subtle.

## The visions and predictions of a certain possessed man, perhaps only delirious . . .

**17. 35.** We have also learned that a man set in a house, suffering an unclean spirit, was wont to say, when a presbyter had begun to come to him from twelve miles away, both through what places of the journey he was, and how near he was approaching, and when he was entering both the estate and the house and the bedroom, until he stood in his sight. All of which, even though that sufferer did not discern with the eyes, yet unless in some way he discerned it, he would not so truthfully announce it. But this man was feverish, and said these things as if in delirium. And perhaps in truth he was delirious, but on account of these things he was thought to suffer a demon.

He received no food of refreshment from his own people, but from that presbyter alone. He even resisted his own people violently, as much as he was able; when that presbyter alone came, he grew quiet, was subject to him alone, and answered submissively. Nor yet did that estrangement of mind or demon yield even to that same presbyter, except when he was sound from the fevers, as the delirious are wont to be made sound; nor afterward did he ever suffer anything of the kind.

## . . . and of a certain truly delirious man.

**17. 36.** We know also, without doubt, that a delirious man foretold the future death of a certain woman; not indeed in the manner of divining, but as if recalling something done and past. For when mention of her was made before him, he said, She is dead, I saw her carried out, they passed this way with her body; while she was living unharmed: but after a few days she suddenly died, and was carried out by that place by which he had foretold.

## The visions of a certain boy afflicted with a grave disease.

**17. 37.** There was likewise among us a boy who, at the onset of puberty, suffered a most acute pain in the genitals, the physicians being by no means able to recognize what it was, except that the nerve itself was withdrawn inward, so that, even with the foreskin cut—which hung down with immoderate length—it could not appear, but afterward was scarcely found. A viscous and sharp humor, oozing out, burned the testicles and the groin. But he suffered the acute pain not continuously, and when he suffered it, he wailed vehemently with a tossing of the limbs, his mind perfectly sound, as is wont to happen in the torments of corporeal pains.

Then, amid his cries, he was snatched away from all the senses, and lay with open eyes seeing none of those standing around, moving himself at no pinching. After a little while, as if awakening, and now no longer in pain, he indicated what he had seen. Then, after a few days had intervened, he suffered the same things. In all, indeed, or nearly all, his visions, he said that he saw two persons, one of more advanced age, the other a boy, by whom there were either spoken or shown to him the things which he related himself to have heard and seen.

## The visions of the blessed and the impious which this same boy had when deprived of his senses.

**17. 38.** One day he saw the gladness of the devout singing psalms in a wonderful light, and the diverse and most atrocious punishments of the impious in darkness; those two leading and showing him, and intimating the merit both of the happiness of some and of the unhappiness of others. But this he saw on the Lord's day of Easter, when through the whole of Lent he had felt no pain, he who scarcely before was spared at an interval of three days.

For at the very entrance of Lent he had seen those two promising him that for forty days he would feel no pain; afterward they themselves gave him, as it were, a medical counsel, that the length of his foreskin should be cut off, which being done, he was long without pain. But when again he was similarly in pain, and had begun to see similar things, he received from them again a counsel, that he should go into the sea up to the waist, and after some delay depart from there, they indeed promising that thenceforth he would not suffer that vehement pain, but only the trouble of that viscous humor: and so it followed; nor ever afterward did he suffer any such turning away of the mind from the senses, nor did he see anything such as before, when, in the midst of pains and dreadful cries, suddenly falling silent, he was snatched away. Afterward, however, with the physicians curing and healing the rest, he did not persevere in his resolve of holiness.

## Why and by what reasoning spiritual visions come about.

**18. 39.** If anyone can track down the causes and modes of these visions and divinations, and grasp them with certainty, I would rather hear him than that it be expected of me that I myself should discourse. Yet what I think, in such a way that neither the learned should deride me as one affirming, nor the unlearned receive me as one teaching, but both take me rather as one debating and inquiring than knowing, I will not conceal.

I compare all these things seen to the things seen by dreamers. For just as these are sometimes false, sometimes true, sometimes confused, sometimes tranquil; and the true ones themselves are sometimes altogether like the future, or openly spoken, sometimes foretold by obscure significations and, as it were, figured locutions: so too are all those. But human beings love to wonder at things they have not experienced, and to inquire into the causes of unusual things, while they do not care to know such everyday things, often even of a more hidden origin.

For just as in words—that is, in the signs we use in speaking—when an unusual word is heard, they first ask what it is, that is, what it signifies; and this known, they then ask whence it was so called, while they are without care ignorant of so many words they have in the use of speech, whence they were so called: so, when something unusual happens in things, whether corporeal or spiritual, they anxiously inquire into the causes and reason, and demand that these be rendered to them by teachers.

## Whatever the nature of visions is, let it suffice to know that it is not a body.

**18. 40.** Now I am wont, when someone asks me, for example, what "catus" means, and I answer, "prudent" or "sharp," and it does not suffice him, but he goes on to ask whence "catus" was so called, in turn to refer the matter back and ask whence "sharp" was so called—which he no less surely did not know, but because it was a familiar word, he patiently was ignorant of its origin: but what sounded new in his ears, he thinks he knows too little what it has the power to signify, unless he also inquires whence it is said.

Whoever, then, asks of me whence appear, in ecstasy, things seen like to corporeal things, which rarely happens to the soul, I in turn ask whence they appear to those sleeping, which the soul experiences daily, and no one either cares, or cares much, to inquire into this. As though the nature of such things seen were less wonderful because it is everyday; or less to be cared about because it belongs to all; or, if those do rightly who do not inquire into these things, they would do more rightly if they were not curious about those either.

But I much more admire, and am much more amazed at, with what swiftness and ease the soul fabricates in itself the images of bodies which it has seen through the eyes of the body, than at the visions of dreamers or even those in ecstasy. Yet whatever the nature of those things seen is, it is without doubt not a body. He for whom it does not suffice to know this, let him inquire of others whence they also exist; I confess that I do not know.

## Whence spiritual visions arise.

**19. 41.** This plainly is given to be gathered from the experiences of examples: that just as pallor, redness, trembling of bodies, or even disease, sometimes has its causes from the body, sometimes from the soul; from the body indeed, when either a humor is suffused, or food, or something else of the body is introduced from without; but from the soul, when it is either disturbed by fear, or confounded by shame, or grows angry, or loves, or anything of the kind; and not without reason, if that which animates and rules the body, even when it is more vehemently moved, more vehemently agitates it: so too the soul itself, that it may proceed into those things seen which are announced to it not through the senses of the body, but through an incorporeal substance, and so proceed that it does not distinguish whether they are bodies or likenesses of bodies.

Sometimes it happens from the body, sometimes from a spirit: and from the body indeed, whether by natural alternation, as are the things seen by dreamers—for to sleep belongs to a human being from the body—or by some ill health, the senses being disturbed, as when by the delirious both bodies are seen, and things seen like to bodies, as though these too were before the eyes: or, the senses being wholly shut off, as often those ill affected by some disease growing worse, the body present, but they long absent, and then restored to human company, have said that they saw many things.

But from a spirit, when, the body altogether sound and whole, they are snatched into estrangement, whether so that they both see bodies through the senses of the body, and certain like things in the spirit which they do not distinguish from bodies; or whether they are wholly turned away from the senses of the flesh, and, perceiving nothing at all through them, dwell, by that spiritual vision, in the likenesses of bodies. But when an evil spirit snatches into these things, it makes either demoniacs, or those carried away, or false prophets: but when a good spirit does, it makes the faithful speaking mysteries, or, understanding being added too, true prophets, or those seeing and narrating, for the time, what ought to be shown through them.

## How the body is related to spiritual things seen.

**20. 42.** But when the cause is from the body that such things are seen, the body does not present them; for it does not have the power to form anything spiritual: but, the path of intentness from the brain being lulled, or disturbed, or even shut off—the path by which the mode of perceiving is directed—the soul itself, which by its own motion cannot cease from this work, because through the body it is not allowed, or not fully allowed, either to perceive corporeal things, or to direct the force of its intentness to corporeal things, produces in the spirit the likenesses of corporeal things, or beholds them as set before it. And if indeed it produces them itself, they are only phantasms; but if it beholds them as set before it, they are showings.

Finally, when the eyes are in pain or are destroyed, because the cause is not in the seat of the brain, whence the intentness of perceiving is itself directed, visions of this kind do not come about, although there exists, for discerning corporeal things, an obstacle from the body. For the blind see something more when sleeping than when waking. For in those sleeping the way of perceiving in the brain, which leads the intentness to the eyes, is lulled to sleep, and therefore that intentness, turned to something else, discerns the things seen of dreams as though corporeal forms were present, so that the sleeper seems to himself to be awake, and thinks that he beholds, not things like to bodies, but the bodies themselves: but when the blind are awake, the intentness of discerning is led through those ways, which, when it has come to the place of the eyes, is not stretched forth outward, but remains there, so that they perceive themselves to be awake, and rather to be in darkness, awake even by day, than asleep, whether by day or by night.

For even those who are not blind, very many sleep with open eyes, seeing nothing through them, but not for that reason seeing nothing, since they discern with the spirit the things seen of dreams: but if they are awake with closed eyes, they are present neither to the visions of those sleeping nor to those of the waking. Yet so much avails the fact that the way of perceiving reaches from the brain to their eyes neither lulled, nor disturbed, nor shut off, and leads the intentness of the soul up to the doors of the body themselves, though closed, that images of bodies are indeed thought of, but in no way held to be those bodies which are perceived through the eyes.

## That the soul and the body cohere and are joined closely.

**20. 43.** So much does it matter where the impediment of perceiving corporeal things comes about, when it comes about in the body. For if it comes about only in the very approaches, and as it were the doors, of the senses—as in the eyes, in the ears, and the other senses of the body—only the perception of corporeal things is impeded; but the intentness of the soul is not so turned to something else that it holds the images of bodies for bodies.

But if the cause is within, in the brain, whence the ways are directed to perceiving the things that are without, the vessels of the intentness itself are lulled, or disturbed, or shut off, by which the soul strives to behold or perceive the things that are without. And because it does not lose this striving, it forms like things with such representation that, unable to distinguish the images of corporeal things from bodies, it does not know whether it is among those or among these; and when it knows, it knows in a far different way than when the likenesses of bodies are turned over in thinking or occur to it. Which way cannot in any case be grasped except by those who have experienced it. For from this it came that, sleeping, I knew myself to be seeing in dreams, and yet did not distinguish those likenesses of corporeal things which I saw from the corporeal things themselves in the way that we are wont to distinguish them when thinking of them, even with closed eyes or set in darkness.

So much does it avail whether the very intentness of the mind is led up to the senses, though closed, or whether, some cause existing in the brain itself, whence it strives toward these things, it is turned to something else; so that, although the soul sometimes knows itself to be discerning not bodies but likenesses of bodies, or, less instructed, even thinking these to be bodies, it perceives that it sees them not by the body but by the spirit, it is yet far from the disposition by which it is presented to its own body: whence even the blind know themselves to be awake, when by a sure notion they distinguish the thought-of likenesses of bodies from the bodies which they cannot see.

## Visions in which some alien power works.

**21. 44.** But when, the body sound and the senses not lulled by sleep, the soul is snatched, by some hidden spiritual work, into those things seen which are like to corporeal things, the nature of the things seen is not also different because the mode is different; since in those causes too which arise from the body there is surely a difference, and sometimes from the contrary. For the delirious have the ways of perceiving in the head disturbed by not sleeping rather, so that they see such things as those sleeping see, whose intentness, by sleeping, is turned away from the sense of waking, and converted to seeing those things. Since, then, the one comes about by not sleeping, but the other by sleeping, yet the things that are seen are not of another kind than from the nature of the spirit, from or in which the likenesses of bodies come about.

Thus, although the cause of the estranged intentness is different—when, the body sound, the soul of one waking is snatched by some hidden spiritual power, so that, in place of bodies, it sees in the spirit the represented likenesses of corporeal things—yet the nature of the things seen is the same. For it cannot be said that, when the cause is in the body, then the soul, without any presentiment of the future, turns over the images of bodies out of itself, as it is also wont to do in thinking; but when it is taken up by the spirit to see those things, these are shown divinely: since Scripture openly says, *I will pour out from my spirit upon all flesh, and the young shall see visions, and the old shall dream dreams*[^43], attributing both to a divine operation; and, *an angel of the Lord appeared to the man in dreams, saying, do not fear to take Mary your wife*[^44]; and again, *take the child, and go into Egypt*[^45].

## That the spirit of a human being is not taken up by a good spirit except to signify something.

**22. 45.** And so I do not think that the spirit of a human being is taken up by a good spirit to see these images, unless they signify something: but when the cause is in the body, that the human spirit may be directed to behold them more expressly, it is not always to be believed that they signify something; but they signify then, when they are inspired by a showing spirit, whether to one sleeping, or to one suffering something else from the body, so as to be estranged from the senses of the flesh.

We know also that to those waking, and afflicted by no disease nor agitated by frenzy, by some hidden prompting thoughts have been brought in which, in bringing them forth, they divined: not only those doing something else, as Caiaphas the high priest prophesied, when his intentness had no will of prophesying, but even those undertaking this, that in the manner of divining they should say something.

## That certain jesting young men were found to have divined.

**22. 46.** For certain young men, in jest, that they might deceive—where they were traveling abroad—pretended to be astrologers, being altogether ignorant whether the signs were called twelve. When they saw their host wondering at the things they said, and attesting that they were most true, they more boldly proceeded to more. But he, attesting, wondered at all. At last he asked them about the safety of his son, whom, long absent, he longed for, and, because he was unexpectedly delaying, he was anxious lest something had happened to him.

But they, not caring what truth would be known after their departure, provided that in the present they rendered the man glad, being soon about to set out, answered that he was safe and approaching, and that on that very day on which they were speaking these things he would come: for they did not fear that, when the whole day had passed, he would pursue them the next day to be refuted. Why say more? While they were now disposing to depart, behold, suddenly, while they were still set there, he came.

## That another young man, a player, unknowingly divined.

**22. 47.** Likewise another was dancing before a musician, where there were many idols, during a certain solemnity of the pagans, not seized by any spirit, but in a playful imitation rivaling those carried away, those standing around and watching knowing it. For it was the custom that, before the midday meal, the sacrifices accomplished and the frenzied ones agitated, if any youths wished after the meal to play in that manner, none was forbidden.

He, then, amid his dancing, silence having been made for him, jesting, and surrounded by a laughing crowd, foretold that on the night which was impending, in the wood which was nearby, a man would be killed by a lion, to whose corpse, to be looked at, a throng would flow together at daybreak, and would desert the place of that solemnity. And it came about; while it was sufficiently clear to all who were present, in all his movements, that he said this playing and jesting, his mind nowhere disturbed or estranged; he himself, too, all the more wondering at what happened, the more he knew with what mind and mouth he had brought it forth.

## That it is difficult to know how visions come into the spirit or are formed.

**22. 48.** In what way these things seen come into the spirit of a human being—whether they are first formed there, or, formed, are brought in, and are discerned by a certain conjunction, so that thus the angels show to human beings their thoughts, and the likenesses of corporeal things which they preform in their spirit by the knowledge of future things, just as they too see our thoughts, surely not with the eyes, because not with the body, but with the spirit; yet with this difference, that they know ours even if we are unwilling, but we cannot know theirs unless they are shown; because they have them, as I suppose, so in their power to conceal by spiritual modes, just as we, by certain interposed obstacles, hide our bodies lest they be seen by the eyes of others.

And what comes about in our spirit, so that sometimes only signifying images are discerned, and it is unknown whether they signify anything; but sometimes they are perceived to signify something, but it is not known what they signify; but sometimes, as if by a fuller showing, the human soul both sees the images themselves by the spirit and sees by the mind what they signify—both is most difficult to know, and, if we should now know it, it is most laborious to discuss and explain.

## Recapitulation: that there is in us a spiritual nature where the likenesses of corporeal things are formed.

**23. 49.** But what I now think it enough to introduce is certain: that there is in us a certain spiritual nature, where the likenesses of corporeal things are formed; whether when we touch some body by the sense of the body, and at once its likeness is formed in the spirit, and stored away in memory; or when we think of absent bodies already known, so that from them a certain spiritual sight is formed which was already in the spirit even before we thought of them; or when we behold the likenesses of those bodies which we do not know, but yet do not doubt to exist—not as they are, but as it occurs to us; or when we think of other things, which either do not exist, or are not known to exist, at our will or by opinion; or when, from wherever, neither doing it nor wishing it, various forms of corporeal likenesses are turned over in the mind.

Or when, about to do something corporeally, we dispose those very things which are to be in that action, and anticipate all by thought; or when, now in the very act, whether when we speak, or when we do, all corporeal motions, that they may be stretched forth, are anticipated by their likenesses within in the spirit; for no syllable, even the briefest, would have sounded in its order unless foreseen.

Or when dreams are seen by those sleeping, signifying either nothing or something; or when, by corporeal ill health, the ways of perceiving disturbed within, the spirit so mingles images of bodies with true bodies that they can scarcely, or cannot at all, be told apart; and they either signify something, or arise without any signification; or when, some disease or pain of the body growing wholly worse, and shutting off within the ways by which the intentness of the soul was stretched forth and strove to perceive through the flesh, the spirit being more deeply absent than in sleep, images of corporeal things arise or are shown, either signifying something or appearing without any signification.

Or when, no cause existing from the body, but some spirit taking up and snatching it, the soul is raised to see likenesses of bodies of this kind, mingling with them corporeal things seen, since it uses at the same time also the senses of the body; or when, a spirit so taking it up, it is estranged from all sense of the body, and turned away, so that it is held by likenesses of bodies alone, in spiritual vision, where I know not whether any things signifying nothing can be seen.

## What the difference is between spiritual and intellectual visions.

**24. 50.** This spiritual nature, then, in which not bodies but likenesses of bodies are represented, has visions of a lower kind than that light of mind and understanding by which both these lower things are judged, and those things are discerned which are neither bodies nor bear any forms like to bodies; such as the mind itself and every good disposition of the soul, to which its vices are contrary, which are rightly blamed and condemned in human beings. For in what other way is the intellect itself beheld except by understanding? *So too love, joy, peace, long-suffering, kindness, goodness, faith, gentleness, self-control*[^46], and the rest of this kind, by which one draws near to God: and God himself, from whom are all things, through whom are all things, in whom are all things[^47].

## On the order by which, of the three kinds of vision, one is more excellent than another.

**24. 51.** Although, therefore, the visions come about in the same soul—whether those that are perceived through the body, such as this corporeal heaven, the earth, and whatever can be known in them, in the way they can; or those that are seen by the spirit as like to bodies, about which we have already said much; or those that are understood by the mind, which are neither bodies nor likenesses of bodies—they surely have their own order, and one is more excellent than another. For spiritual vision is more excellent than corporeal, and again intellectual is more excellent than spiritual.

For corporeal vision cannot exist without spiritual; since at the same moment in which a body is touched by the sense of the body, there comes to be also in the mind something of the kind—not that this is that body, but that it is like it; which, if it did not come to be, neither would there be that sense by which the things that lie adjacent without are perceived. For the body does not perceive, but the soul through the body, which it uses as a messenger, as it were, for forming in itself what is announced from without. And so corporeal vision cannot come about unless spiritual also comes about at the same time: but it is not distinguished, except when the sense has been taken away from the body, so that what was seen through the body is found in the spirit. But spiritual vision can come about even without corporeal, when the likenesses of absent bodies appear in the spirit, and many are feigned at will, or shown apart from the will.

Likewise spiritual vision needs the intellectual in order to be judged, but the intellectual does not need that lower spiritual one; and on this account corporeal is subject to spiritual, but both to intellectual. When, then, we read, *the spiritual man judges all things, but he himself is judged by no one*[^48], we ought to take this not according to the spirit from which the mind is distinguished, as in that which was said, *I will pray with the spirit, I will pray also with the mind*[^49], but from that notion by which it was said, *but be renewed in the spirit of your mind*[^50]. For we have already taught above that in another way the mind itself too is called spirit, according to which the spiritual man judges all things. Wherefore I think it not absurd or unfitting that spiritual vision holds, as it were, a certain middle place between the intellectual and the corporeal. For I think it is not incongruously called a middle, that which is indeed not a body, but is like a body, between that which is truly a body, and that which is neither a body nor like a body.

## In what visions the soul can be enclosed.

**25. 52.** But the soul is deluded by the likenesses of things, not by their fault, but by its own opinion, when it approves the things that are like, in place of the things they are like, falling away from understanding. It is deceived, then, in corporeal vision, when it thinks that to come about in the bodies themselves which comes about in the senses of the body; just as to those sailing the things that stand still seem to move on the land, and to those gazing at the sky the stars that move seem to stand still, and, the rays of the eyes being spread apart, two forms of a lamp seem to appear, and in water the oar broken, and many things of the kind.

Or when it thinks that to be this thing which is similarly colored, or similarly sounds, or smells, or tastes, or is touched; for hence even some waxen medicament cooked in a pot is thought to be a vegetable, and the noise of a passing vehicle is thought to be from thunder, and, if it be explored by no other senses, but lie adjacent only to the smell, the citron is thought to be the herb called bee-balm, and food seasoned with some sweetish juice is thought to be flavored with honey; and an unknown ring, handled in the dark, is thought to be golden, when it is of bronze or silver.

Or when the soul, disturbed by sudden and unexpected corporeal things seen, either thinks itself to be seeing in dreams, or to be affected by some spiritual thing seen of this kind: whence, in all corporeal things seen, both the attestation of the other senses, and especially of the mind and reason itself, is applied, so that what is true in this kind of thing may be found, as far as it can be found.

But in spiritual vision, that is, in the likenesses of bodies which are seen by the spirit, the soul is deceived when it thinks those things which it so sees to be bodies themselves; or what it has feigned for itself by suspicion and false conjecture, this it thinks to be also in the bodies which, unseen, it conjectures. But in those intellectual things seen it is not deceived: for either it understands, and it is true; or, if it is not true, it does not understand: whence it is one thing to err in the things it sees, another to err because it does not see.

## That spiritual visions are brought about by God.

**26. 53.** Wherefore when the soul is snatched into those things seen which are discerned by the spirit as like to corporeal things, so that it is altogether turned away from the senses of the body more than it is wont in sleep, but less than in death; it is now a matter of divine admonition and aid that it should know itself to be discerning, not bodies, but things seen like to bodies, spiritually, just as those who, even before they wake, know themselves to be seeing in dreams. There, if even future things are seen, in such a way that the future things are wholly known, whose images are seen as present—whether by the mind of the human being itself divinely aided, or by someone, among the things seen themselves, expounding what they signify, as in the *Apocalypse* it was expounded to John[^51]—it is a great revelation: even if perhaps he to whom these things are shown is ignorant whether he has gone out of the body, or is still in the body, but, the spirit estranged from the senses of the body, sees these things; for one so snatched can be ignorant of this, if this too is not shown to him.

## How perfect and blessed intellectual vision is.

**26. 54.** But further, if, just as he is snatched from the senses of the body, so as to be among these likenesses of bodies which are seen by the spirit, so too he is snatched from these, so as to be carried up into that, as it were, region of intellectual or intelligible things, where, without any likeness of body, perspicuous truth is discerned; he is darkened by no clouds of false opinions: there the virtues of the soul are not toilsome and laborious. For neither is lust reined in by the work of temperance, nor are adversities endured by the work of fortitude, nor are iniquities punished by the work of justice, nor are evils avoided by the work of prudence.

There the one and whole virtue is to love what you see, and the highest happiness to have what you love. For there the blessed life is drunk at its source, whence something is sprinkled upon this human life, so that in the temptations of this age one may live temperately, bravely, justly, and prudently. For in order to attain that, where there will be untroubled rest and the ineffable vision of truth, labor is undertaken, both of restraining oneself from pleasure, and of bearing adversities, and of coming to the aid of the needy, and of resisting deceivers. There the brightness of the Lord is seen, not through a signifying vision, whether corporeal, as it was seen on Mount Sinai[^52], or spiritual, as Isaiah saw[^53], or John in the *Apocalypse*[^54]: but through its form, not through riddles, as far as the human mind can grasp it, according to the grace of God taking it up, so that he may speak mouth to mouth to him whom God has made worthy of such a colloquy; not the mouth of the body, but of the mind, as I think is to be understood what is written of Moses.

## By what kind of vision Moses saw God.

**27. 55.** For he had desired, as we read in *Exodus*, to see God; not indeed as he had seen him on the mountain, nor as he saw him in the tabernacle, but in that substance by which God is, no corporeal creature being assumed which might be presented to the senses of mortal flesh: nor in the spirit by figured likenesses of bodies; but through his own form, as far as the rational and intellectual creature can grasp it, called away from all sense of the body, and from every signifying riddle of the spirit.

For thus it is written: *if, then, I have found grace in your sight, show me yourself, that I may see you manifestly*[^55]; while a little earlier we read, *and the Lord spoke to Moses face to face, as a man speaks to his friend*[^56]. He perceived, then, what he saw, and desired what he did not see. For also a little after, when God had said to him, *for you have found grace in my sight, and I know you above all*, he answered him, *show me your brightness*[^57]. And then indeed he received from the Lord a figured answer, about which it is now long to discuss, when he said to him, *you cannot see my face, and live. For man shall not see my face, and live*[^58]. Then he added and said to him, *behold, a place beside me, and you shall stand upon the rock; as soon as my majesty passes, I will set you in a cleft of the rock, and I will cover you with my hand over you until I pass; and I will take away my hand, and then you shall see my back parts: for my face shall not appear to you*[^59].

Nor yet did the following Scripture narrate that this too was done corporeally, and by this it is sufficiently shown to have been said figuratively, in signification of the Church. For he himself is the place beside the Lord, because the Church is his temple, and it itself is built upon the rock, and the rest that is said there agrees with the same understanding. Yet, unless Moses had deserved to see the desired and longed-for brightness of God, God would not say to Aaron and Miriam, his brother and sister, in the book of *Numbers*: *hear my words: if there be a prophet of yours, in a vision I the Lord will be known to him, and in a dream I will speak to him; not so my servant Moses, who is faithful in all my house. Mouth to mouth I will speak to him, in his form, and not through riddles, and he has seen the brightness of the Lord*[^60].

For this is not to be understood according to the substance of the body, which is presented to the senses of the flesh; for surely he spoke thus to Moses *face to face*, in opposition over against, when nevertheless he said to him, *show me yourself*[^61]; and now too, to those very ones whom he was rebuking, and to whom he so preferred the merit of Moses, he spoke thus through a corporeal creature presented to the senses of the flesh.

In that manner, then, in that form by which God is, far more ineffably, more secretly and more presently, he speaks by an ineffable speech, where no one living sees him in this life by which we live mortally in these senses of the body: but unless one in some way die to this life, whether wholly going out of the body, or so turned away and estranged from the carnal senses that he rightly does not know, as the Apostle says, whether he is in the body or out of the body, when he is snatched and carried up into that vision.

## That the Apostle's vision was intellectual.

**28. 56.** Wherefore, if this third kind of vision, which is higher—not only than every corporeal one, by which through the senses of the body bodies are perceived, but also than all that spiritual one, by which the likenesses of bodies are discerned by the spirit and not by the mind—the Apostle called the third heaven; in this is seen the brightness of God, for whose seeing hearts are cleansed, whence it was said, *blessed are the clean of heart, for they shall see God*[^62]: not through some signification figured corporeally or spiritually, as through a mirror in a riddle, but face to face[^63], which of Moses was said, *mouth to mouth*; through the form, namely, by which God is whatever he is, however little of him the mind—which is not what he himself is, even when cleansed from every earthly stain, and estranged and snatched from every body and likeness of body—can grasp: from whom we are pilgrims, weighed down by a mortal and corruptible burden, as long as we walk by faith, not by sight[^64], and as long as we live justly here.

But why should we not believe that to so great an Apostle, the teacher of the gentiles, snatched up to that most excellent vision, God willed to show the life in which, after this life, one must live forever? And why should this not be called paradise, besides that in which Adam lived corporeally among the wooded and fruitful trees? Since the Church too, which gathers us into the bosom of love, is called *a paradise with the fruit of orchards*[^65]. But this was said figuratively, as though by that paradise where Adam properly was, the Church was signified through the figure of what was to come. Although to those considering more carefully it may perhaps occur that by that corporeal paradise, in which Adam was corporeally, there was signified both this life of the saints which is now led in the Church, and that which will be after this forever: just as Jerusalem, which is interpreted "Vision of peace," and yet shows forth a certain earthly city, signifies Jerusalem our eternal mother in the heavens, whether in those who have been saved by hope, and, hoping for what they do not see, await it through patience[^66], according to whom are the many children of the deserted one, rather than of her who has a husband; or in the holy angels themselves, through the Church, of the manifold wisdom of God[^67], with whom, after this pilgrimage, one must live without labor and without end.

## Augustine is unaware whether there are other heavens besides the third, and other kinds of vision.

**29. 57.** But if we so take the third heaven to which the Apostle was snatched that we believe there to be also a fourth, and several heavens higher beyond, below which is this third heaven—as some say there are seven, others eight, others nine or even ten, and affirm that in that one which is called the firmament there are many by degrees; and on this account either reason or hold by opinion that they are corporeal, about whose reasoning or opinion it is now long to discuss—it can also come about that someone contend that even among spiritual or intellectual things there are many degrees, or, if he can, show it, and that these are distinguished according to some advance in revelations more or less illustrious.

But however these things may stand, and be taken as one pleases, by one in this way, by another in that; I, of things seen or visions, besides these three kinds—either by body, or by spirit, or by mind—am thus far able neither to know nor to teach. But how many and how great are the differences of the several kinds, so that in each one is borne above another by degrees, I confess that I am ignorant.

## On the spiritual things seen in which the angels show images, and on other customary and human things seen.

**30. 58.** But just as in this corporeal light there is the heaven which we look up to above the earth, whence the luminaries and stars shine, bodies far better than terrestrial ones: so in that spiritual kind, in which the likenesses of bodies are seen by a certain incorporeal light of its own, there are certain excellent and deservedly divine things, which the angels show in wonderful ways; whether by a certain easy and most powerful joining or mingling making their own things seen to be ours too, or knowing in some way how to form our vision in our spirit, is a thing difficult to perceive, and more difficult to say.

But there are other things seen, customary and human, which either arise in manifold ways from our spirit itself, or are in some way suggested to the spirit from the body, according as we have been affected, whether in flesh or in mind. For not only do human beings awake turn over their cares by thinking in the likenesses of bodies, but even those sleeping often dream of what they lack: for they both conduct their business out of the desire of the mind, and eagerly press at feasts and cups, if perhaps they have slept hungry and thirsty. All which things I think are to be held, compared with those angelic showings, just as if in this nature of bodies earthly things were compared with heavenly ones.

## The various kinds of intellectual things seen.

**31. 59.** So too in that kind of intellectual things seen there are some which are seen in the soul itself, such as the virtues to which the vices are contrary; whether such as will abide, like devotion; or such as are useful to this life, and afterward will not be, like the faith by which we believe the things we do not yet see, and the hope by which we await future things with patience, and patience itself, by which we bear all adversities, until we come where we will. For these and such virtues, which are now very necessary for passing through this pilgrimage, will not be in that life for the attaining of which they are necessary: and yet these too are seen intellectually; for they are neither bodies, nor have forms like to bodies.

But another thing is that very light by which the soul is illumined, so that it may behold all things truly understood, whether in itself or in that light: for that is now God himself, but this is a creature, although rational and intellectual, made to his image, which, when it tries to behold that light, palpitates with weakness, and is less able. From there, nevertheless, is whatever it understands as it is able. When, therefore, it is snatched thither, and, withdrawn from the carnal senses, is presented more expressly to that vision—not by local spaces, but in a certain manner of its own—it sees even above itself that by which, aided, it sees whatever it also sees by understanding in itself.

## Whither the soul betakes itself when stripped of the body, and how it can rejoice or suffer.

**32. 60.** But if it is asked, when the soul has gone out of the body, whether it is borne to some corporeal places, or to incorporeal ones like to corporeal, or rather neither to these, but to that which is more excellent than both bodies and likenesses of bodies; I would quickly answer that it is either not borne to corporeal places except with some body, or not borne locally. Now whether it has some body when it has gone out of this body, let him show who can; but I do not think so.

But it is borne, according to merits, to spiritual things, or to penal places like to bodies: such as have often been shown to those who were snatched from the senses of the body, and lay like the dead, and saw the punishments of the underworld, since they too bore in themselves a certain likeness of their own body, through which they could be borne to those places, and experience such things by the likenesses of the senses. For I do not see why the soul should have a likeness of its own body, when, the body itself lying without sense, but not yet wholly dead, it sees such things as many, restored alive from that withdrawal, have narrated; and should not have it, when, by complete death, it has gone wholly out of the body. Either, then, it is borne to those penal things, or to those likewise like to corporeal things, yet not of punishments, but of rest and joys.

## That the joy and the distress which the soul, stripped of the body, suffers are true.

**32. 61.** For it cannot rightly be said either that those punishments are false, or that that rest and joy are false: for they are false then, when, through the error of opinion, some things are thought in place of others. For Peter, not only when he saw that vessel, and thought in it not likenesses of bodies but bodies[^68], was deceived in this; but also when at another time, loosed from his chains by the angel, he went walking in the body, and was presented with corporeal forms, and thought he was seeing a vision[^69], he was no less deceived. For both those in the vessel were spiritual forms like to corporeal ones; and that corporeal representation of one loosed from chains was, on account of the miracle, like to a spiritual one. But the soul was deceived in both only when it approved some things in place of others.

Although, therefore, the things by which souls stripped of bodies are affected, whether well or ill, are not corporeal, but like to corporeal—since they too appear to themselves like to their own bodies—yet there is both true joy and true distress, made of spiritual substance. For even in dreams it matters greatly whether we are among glad things or sad. Whence some, set among the things they had desired, grieved that they had awakened; and again, others, agitated and vexed by grave terrors and torments, when they had been awakened, feared to sleep, lest they be called back into the same evils. And surely it is not to be doubted that those things which are called of the underworld are more expressly represented, and on this account more vehemently felt. For those too who were withdrawn from the senses of the body—less indeed than if they died in every way, but yet more than if they slept—narrated that they had seen things more expressly than if they had narrated dreams. There is, then, indeed a substance of the underworld; but I judge it to be spiritual, not corporeal.

## That it is in no way to be doubted concerning the reality and truth of the underworld, which receives the souls of the dead.

**33. 62.** Nor are those to be heard who affirm that the underworld is unfolded in this life, and is not after death. Let them see how they interpret the fictions of the poets; we ought not to recede from the authority of the divine Scriptures, in which alone faith is to be had on this matter. Although we could show that even their wise men by no means doubted the substance of the underworld, which after this life receives the souls of the dead. But why the underworld is said to be beneath the earth, if there are no corporeal places, or why it is called "the underworld" (inferi), if it is not beneath the earth, is rightly asked.

But that the soul is not corporeal—not that I think so, but plainly that I know it—I dare to profess: yet whoever denies that it can have a likeness of a body and altogether of corporeal members, can deny that there is a soul, which in dreams sees itself either walking, or sitting, or borne here and there and back by walking or even flying, which does not come about without a certain likeness of body. Accordingly, if it bears this likeness even in the underworld—not corporeal, but like to a body—so too it is seen to be in places not corporeal, but like to corporeal ones, whether in rest or in pains.

## The seat of the just: the bosom of Abraham.

**33. 63.** Although I confess that I have not yet found "the underworld" called that where the souls of the just rest. And that the soul of Christ indeed came even to those places in which sinners are tormented, to loose from their torments those whom he judged, by his justice hidden from us, were to be loosed, is not without reason believed. For how otherwise is what was said to be taken—*whom God raised from the dead, the pains of the underworld being loosed, because he could not be held by them*[^70]—I do not see, except that we take it that he loosed the pains of certain ones in the underworld, by that power by which he is Lord, to whom every knee bows, of things in heaven, on earth, and under the earth[^71], by which power he too could not be held by those pains which he loosed.

For neither was Abraham, nor that poor man in his bosom—that is, in the secret place of his rest—in pains, between whose rest and those torments of the underworld we read that a great chasm was fixed: nor were they said to be in the underworld. *For it happened*, he says, *that the poor man died, and was carried by the angels into the bosom of Abraham: but the rich man also died, and was buried; and when he was in torments in the underworld*[^72], and the rest. We see, therefore, that mention of the underworld was not made in the rest of the poor man, but in the punishment of the rich.

## That sadness is no small evil of the soul.

**33. 64.** That also which Jacob says to his sons, *you will bring down my old age with sadness to the underworld*[^73], seems rather to have feared this, lest he be so disturbed by excessive sadness that he should go not to the rest of the blessed, but to the underworld of sinners. For sadness is no small evil of the soul, since the Apostle too feared so anxiously for a certain man, lest he be swallowed up by greater sadness[^74].

Accordingly, as I said, I have not yet found, and I still inquire, nor does it occur to me that Scripture—at least the canonical—has anywhere placed "the underworld" in a good sense: but that the bosom of Abraham, and that rest to which the devout poor man was carried by the angels, is to be taken in a good sense, I do not know whether anyone can hear otherwise; and therefore how we should believe it to be in the underworld, I do not see.

## It is asked where paradise is, and whether the bosom of Abraham can be it.

**34. 65.** But while we inquire into this, and either find it or do not find it, the length of this book urges us to conclude it at last. Wherefore, since we set up a discourse about paradise, on account of what the Apostle says—that he knows a man was caught up even to the third heaven, but does not know whether in the body or out of the body, and that he was caught up into paradise, and heard ineffable words which it is not lawful for a man to utter—we do not rashly affirm whether paradise is in the third heaven, or whether he was caught up both into the third heaven, and from there again into paradise. For if, properly indeed, paradise is a wooded place, but by a transferred word every spiritual region too, as it were, where it is well with the soul, can rightly be called paradise.

Not only is the third heaven—whatever it is, which is surely great and sublimely glorious—but also, in the human being himself, a certain gladness of a good conscience is paradise. Whence the Church too is rightly called a paradise for the saints living temperately, justly, and devoutly[^75], abounding in the affluence of graces and in chaste delights[^76]: since even in tribulations it glories, rejoicing greatly in patience itself, because according to the multitude of sorrows in the heart the consolations of God make glad its soul[^77]. How much more, then, after this life, can even that bosom of Abraham be called paradise, where there is now no temptation, where there is such great rest after all the sorrows of this life? For there too is not a certain light of its own and of its own kind, and surely a great one, which that rich man, from the torments and darkness of the underworld, surely from so far off, when there was a great chasm in the middle, yet so saw that he recognized there that poor man once despised.

## That the "place" of the underworld is spiritual.

**34. 66.** If these things are so, the underworld is said or believed to be beneath the earth for this reason: that it is fittingly shown in the spirit, through those likenesses of corporeal things, in such a way that, since the souls of the deceased worthy of the underworld sinned through love of the flesh, this is presented to them, through those likenesses of corporeal things, which is wont to befall the dead flesh itself, namely, that it is hidden away beneath the earth. Finally, the underworld is in Latin called "inferi" because it is below (infra): but just as, according to the body, if things keep the order of their own weight, all the heavier are lower; so, according to the spirit, all the sadder are lower: whence too, in the Greek language, the origin of the name by which the underworld is called is said to resound from this, that it has nothing sweet.

Yet our Savior, having died for us, did not disdain to visit even that part of things, that from there he might loose those whom, according to the divine and secret justice, he could not be ignorant were to be loosed. Wherefore to the soul of that thief to whom he said, *today you shall be with me in paradise*[^78], he surely did not grant the underworld, where the punishments of sinners are: but either that rest of the bosom of Abraham—for Christ is nowhere not present, since he himself is the Wisdom of God reaching everywhere on account of his purity[^79]—or that paradise, whether in the third heaven, or wherever else it is, to which the Apostle was caught up after the third heaven: if indeed it is not some one thing called by different names, where the souls of the blessed are.

## The three heavens are explained according to those three kinds of things seen.

**34. 67.** If, then, we rightly take the first heaven to be all this corporeal realm, by a general name, whatever is above the waters and the earth; the second, that which is discerned by the spirit in a corporeal likeness, such as that whence the vessel full of animals was let down to Peter in ecstasy[^80]; but the third, that which is beheld by the mind so secret and remote and altogether snatched away from the senses of the flesh and cleansed, that it is able ineffably to see and hear the things that are in that heaven, and the very substance of God, and the Word, God, through whom all things were made[^81], through the love of the Holy Spirit.

We judge it not incongruous both that the Apostle was caught up thither[^82], and that there perhaps is the paradise better than all, and, if it must be said, the paradise of paradises. For if the gladness of a good soul in good things is found in every creature, what is more excellent than that gladness which is in the Word of God, through whom all things were made?

## Why it is necessary for the spirits of the dead to receive their bodies in the resurrection.

**35. 68.** But if anyone is moved by the question what need there is for the spirits of the deceased to receive their bodies in the resurrection, if that highest blessedness can be granted them even without bodies; the question is indeed too difficult to be perfectly finished in this discourse: but yet it is in no way to be doubted that both the mind of a human being snatched from the senses of the flesh, and, after death, the flesh itself laid aside, the likenesses of corporeal things also transcended, cannot see the unchangeable substance as the holy angels see it; whether for some more hidden cause, or because there is in it a certain natural appetite for administering a body; by which appetite it is in some way retarded, lest with its whole intentness it proceed into that highest heaven, as long as there is no body present, by whose administration that appetite may come to rest.

But further, if the body be of such a kind that its administration is difficult and burdensome, like this flesh which is corrupted, and weighs down the soul[^83], arising from the propagation of the transgression, much more is the mind turned away from that vision of the highest heaven: whence it had necessarily to be snatched from the senses of that same flesh, that, in the way it could grasp it, that might be shown to it. Accordingly, when it has received this body—now not animal, but, through the future change, spiritual—made equal to the angels, it will have the perfect measure of its nature, obeying and commanding, given life and giving life, with such ineffable ease that it will be for glory what was a burden.

## How the three kinds of vision will be in the blessed.

**36. 69.** For surely these three kinds of vision will exist then too, but by no falsity will one thing be approved in place of another, neither in corporeal nor in spiritual things seen; much less in intellectual things, which, so presented and perspicuous, it will enjoy as those corporeal forms which we now touch by the sense of the flesh lie adjacent to us with far less evidence—forms to which many are so devoted that they think they alone exist, and that whatever is not such does not exist at all. But the wise are so disposed among these corporeal things seen that, although these seem more present, they are yet more certain about those things which, apart from the form of body and apart from the likeness of body, they somehow perceive by understanding; although they cannot so behold them by the mind as they gaze upon these by the sense of the body.

But the holy angels both preside over the judging and administering of these corporeal things, and are not inclined to them as to more present and familiar things; and they so discern their significative likenesses in the spirit, and in a manner handle them with such great power, that they can even, by revealing, mingle them with the spirits of human beings; and they so behold that unchangeable substance of the Creator that, by vision and love, they both prefer it to all things, and according to it judge of all things, and are directed into it so as to be moved, and out of it direct whatever they do.

Finally, although the Apostle was snatched from the senses of the flesh into the third heaven and paradise, this very thing certainly was lacking to the full and perfect knowledge of things which is in the angels: that he did not know whether he was in the body or out of the body. This surely will not be lacking, when, the bodies being received in the resurrection of the dead, this corruptible will put on incorruption, and this mortal will put on immortality[^84]. For all things will be evident, without any falsity, without any ignorance, distributed in their orders—both corporeal and spiritual and intellectual—in a nature made whole and a blessedness made perfect.

## That some have so expounded the third heaven that they wished the differences of the corporeal, the animal, and the spiritual man to be understood.

**37. 70.** I know indeed that some of those who are praised for having treated the holy Scriptures before us in the Catholic faith have also so expounded what the Apostle says, *the third heaven*, that they wished the differences of the corporeal, the animal, and the spiritual man to be understood here, and that the Apostle was caught up to contemplate, with excellent evidence, that kind of incorporeal things: which kind even in this life spiritual men love above other things, and desire to enjoy. But why I have preferred to say "spiritual" and "intellectual" what they perhaps said "animal" and "spiritual," so as to set down only other names for the same things, let it suffice that I have discussed in the first parts of this book. And if we have rightly debated these things according to our small measure, either the spiritual reader will approve them, or, that he may become spiritual, with the Holy Spirit aiding, he will profit something from this reading. But now this whole work, which is contained in twelve volumes, we at last conclude with this end.
