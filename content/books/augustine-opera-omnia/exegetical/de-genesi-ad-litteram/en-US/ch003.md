# Book II

## What is meant by the firmament in the midst of the waters.

**1. 1.** *And God said, 'let there be a firmament in the midst of the waters, and let it divide between water and water': and so it was made. And God made the firmament, and God divided between the water that was below the firmament and the water that was above the firmament. And God called the firmament heaven. And God saw that it was good. And there was evening, and there was morning, the second day*[^1]*.*

Concerning the word of God, in that God said, *let there be a firmament,* and so on, and concerning his good pleasure, by which he saw that it was good, and concerning the evening and the morning, there is no need here to discuss them again in the same way; and so thereafter, as often as these are repeated, we advise that they be considered for the present according to the earlier inquiry. But whether now that heaven comes to be which exceeds all the spaces of the air and its whole height, where also the luminaries and stars are established on the fourth day, or whether the air itself is called the firmament, can rightly be asked.

## Some deny that there are waters above the starry heaven.

**1. 2.** For many assert that the nature of these waters cannot be above the starry heaven, because they have their weight so ordered that they either float above the lands or are borne, as vapor, in the air nearest to the lands. Nor ought anyone to refute these men by saying that, according to the omnipotence of God, to whom all things are possible, we must believe that waters even as heavy as those we know and perceive are poured over the heavenly body in which the stars are. For now it is fitting that we inquire, according to his Scriptures, how God established the natures of things, not what he may wish to work in them or out of them as a miracle of his power.

For if God should at some time wish oil to remain under water, it would not fail to be done; yet the nature of oil would not on that account be unknown to us, namely that it is so made that, seeking its own place, even if it has been poured underneath, it breaks through the waters and sets itself placed above them. Now, therefore, we inquire whether the founder of things, who arranged all things *in measure and number and weight*[^2], assigned not one proper place to the weight of the waters around the earth, but also a place above the heaven, which beyond the limit of the air is poured around and made solid.

## The arguments brought forward by them.

**1. 3.** Those who deny that this is to be believed argue from the weights of the elements, denying that the heaven could in any way have been made solid above, as it were like a pavement, so that it could sustain the weights of waters; for such solidity could belong only to earth, and whatever is such is not heaven but earth. For the elements are distinguished not only by places but also by qualities, so that in keeping with their proper qualities they are also allotted their proper places: water, namely, above the earth, which, even if it stands or slides beneath the earth, as in hidden caves and caverns, is yet contained not by that part of the earth which it has above it, but by that which it has below it.

For if from the upper part any portion of earth should fall, it does not remain upon the water, but, the water being broken through, it sinks and proceeds to the earth; arriving there, it comes to rest as in its own place, so that the water is above and the earth below. From which it is recognized that even when it was above the waters it was not carried by the waters themselves, but was held by the framework of earth, as the vaults of caves hold themselves.

## Sacred Scripture and teaching about the nature of things.

**1. 4.** Here it occurs to warn that the error must be guarded against which I warned must be guarded against in the first book: lest, perhaps because it is written in the Psalms, *he founded the earth upon the water*[^3], someone of us think that, against those who discuss subtly about the weights of the elements, we must lean upon this testimony of the Scriptures: because they, not held by the authority of our Writings, and not knowing in what sense it was said, will more easily mock the Holy Books than reject that which they have either grasped by certain reasons or proved by the most manifest experiments.

For that passage in the Psalms can rightly be taken either as said figuratively: so that, since by the name of *heaven* and *earth* the spiritual and the carnal are often signified in the Church, he showed the heavens to belong to the serene understanding of truth, saying, *who made the heavens in understanding*[^4]; but the earth to the simple faith of little ones, not uncertain and deceptive with fabulous opinions, but most firm with prophetic and Gospel preaching, which is made solid through baptism, and so he added, saying, *he founded the earth upon the water.*

Or, if anyone insists on its being understood literally, not unfittingly are taken either the heights of the lands, whether of continents or of islands, which are above the waters; or the very coverings of caves, which over the waters are made firm by a hanging solidity. Wherefore not even literally can anyone so understand what was said, *he founded the earth upon the water,* as to think that the weight of the waters is, as it were, set under to support the earthen weight by a natural order.

## The air is higher than the earth.

**2. 5.** But that the air is higher than the waters, although, on account of its more ample spaces, it also covers the dry land, is understood from this: that no vessel pressed down by its mouth can be filled with waters; whence it sufficiently indicates that the nature of air seeks a higher place. For the vessel seems empty, but is proved full of air, when it is pressed down by its mouth into the water; because, since through the upper part it finds no place to dart out, nor is its nature allowed, downward, to go beneath the waters once they have broken in, by its own fullness it repels them and does not permit them to enter into the vessel.

But when the vessel is so placed that it does not have its mouth downward, but inclined to the side, the water enters below, the air going out above. And likewise, if the mouth of an upright vessel lies open to the sky, when you pour in water, the air escapes upward, from the other parts where you do not pour, and a place is made for the water to enter downward.

But if the vessel is pressed down by a greater force, so that the waters suddenly flow in either from the side or from above, and cover the mouth of the vessel on all sides, the air, striving upward, bursts through them, to make room for them at the bottom; and that very bursting is the gurgling of vessels, while it flees in parts, because the whole cannot escape so quickly, on account of the narrowness of that mouth.

Thus, if the air is forced to go above the waters, it scatters even those that converge, when, struck by its impulse, they leap forth and bubble up, and send it out with crackling bubbles as it hastens to its own place, and gives them an entry for falling to the bottom.

But if it is forced to go beneath the waters out of a vessel, so that, the air giving way, you wish to fill the vessel pressed from its mouth into the depths, it is more easily covered on every side by the waves than a tiny drop finds a place to enter through its mouth from the lower part.

## Fire is higher than the air.

**3. 6.** Now indeed, that fire, darting to the heights, wishes to transcend even the nature of the air itself, who would not perceive? Since, if anyone holds a burning torch with its head downward, nonetheless the tress of flame strives toward the upper regions. But because, by the prevailing density of the air poured around and over it, the fire is again and again extinguished, and, overcome by the abundance, is again and again changed and turned into its quality, it cannot endure so as to leap across the whole height of the air.

And so above the air the heaven is said to be pure fire, whence they also conjecture the stars and luminaries to have been made—the nature, namely, of that fiery light being gathered together and arranged into those forms which we discern in the heaven: and on this account, just as both air and water give way to the weights of the lands, so that they reach the earth, so to the weight of the waters the air itself also gives way, so that it reaches either the earth or the water.

From which they would have it understood that in this way it would necessarily follow that the air too, if anyone could release a particle of it in those lofty spaces of heaven, would fall by its own weight until it reached the airy spaces below. Wherefore they conclude that much less can there be any place for waters above that fiery heaven, since there the air, much lighter than waters, cannot remain.

## Why, according to a certain author, the air can in a way be called heaven and firmament.

**4. 7.** Yielding to such disputations of theirs, a certain author has commendably tried to demonstrate the waters above the heavens, so as to assert the faith of Scripture from the very visible and conspicuous natures. And first indeed, which was easiest, he showed that this air too is called heaven, not only in common speech, according to which we say a clear or a clouded sky, but also by the usage of our very own Scriptures, when the *birds of the heaven*[^5] are spoken of, since it is manifest that birds fly in this air: and when the Lord, speaking of the clouds, said, *you can read the face of the heaven*[^6]. But we often see clouds gathered even through the air nearest the lands, when they so settle along the slopes of the ridges that for the most part they are even overtopped by the peaks of the mountains.

When, therefore, he had proved that this air too is called heaven, he wished it to be thought called firmament for no other reason than that its expanse divides between certain vapors of waters and these waters which flow more corpulently on the lands. For clouds, as those have experienced who have walked among them on the mountains, render such an appearance by the gathering and conglobation of the tiniest drops; which, if they are thickened further, so that many least drops are joined into one large one, the air does not suffer to be held with it, but gives way at the bottom to their weight; and this is rain.

Therefore he wished to show that the heaven is between water and water, namely out of the air which is between the moist vapors—whence above the clouds are gathered—and the seas poured beneath. This diligence and consideration, then, I judge most worthy of praise. For what he said is neither against the faith, and can be believed on a document set ready at hand.

## The waters above the airy heaven.

**4. 8.** Although it may seem that the proper weights of the elements do not hinder how, even above that lofty heaven, there can be waters, through those minute particles through which they could be even above this space of air: which, although heavier and lower, lies beneath the highest heaven, is beyond doubt lighter than waters, and yet those vapors are prevented by no weight from being above it. So then, above that heaven too, a vapor of moisture, lighter through finer drops, can be extended, which is not forced to fall by weight.

For they, by a most subtle reasoning, persuade us that there is no corpuscle, however small, in which division comes to an end, but that all things are divided infinitely; because every part of a body is a body, and every body must of necessity have a half of its quantity. And on this account, if water can, as we see, reach to such minuteness of drops that it is borne as vapor above this air, which is by nature lighter than waters, why can it not also, with finer drops and lighter vapors, remain above that lighter heaven?

## The waters above the starry heaven.

**5. 9.** Certain of our own people too, against those who deny, on account of the weights of the elements, that there can be waters above the starry heaven, endeavor to convince them from the qualities and courses of the stars themselves. For these same men assert that the star which they call Saturn's is the coldest, and that it traverses the zodiacal circle over thirty years, because it moves in a higher and on that account more ample orbit. For the sun completes the same circle in a year, and the moon in a month; by so much briefer, as they say, as it is lower, so that the span of time may correspond to the span of place.

And so it is asked of them whence that star is cold which ought to be the hotter, the higher the heaven by which it is swept along. For beyond doubt, when a round mass is driven by a circular motion, its inner parts go more slowly, the outer more quickly, so that the greater spaces and the briefer alike arrive at the same revolutions together; and those which go more quickly are surely the hotter.

Accordingly, the aforesaid star ought to have been hot rather than cold; for although by its own motion, since the space is great, it traverses the whole orbit in thirty years, yet, rotated in the opposite direction more swiftly by the motion of the heaven—which it must necessarily undergo daily (for thus, as they say, the single revolutions of the heaven unfold single days)—it ought to have conceived a greater heat from the more rapidly stirred heaven.

No doubt, then, what makes it cold is that nearness of the waters set above the heaven, which those will not believe who discuss these things, which I have briefly stated, about the motion of the heaven and the stars. By these conjectures certain of our own people proceed against those who will not believe that there are waters above the heaven, and who hold that star to be cold which circles next to the highest heaven; so that from this they may be forced to admit that a nature of waters hangs there, no longer in vaporous thinness, but in glacial solidity.

But in whatever way and of whatever kind the waters there may be, let us by no means doubt that they are there: for greater is the authority of this Scripture than the whole capacity of human ingenuity.

## What is meant by the words: let there be, and so it was made, he made.

**6. 10.** But it has been observed by some—which I too do not think should be passed over—that not in vain, when God had said, *let there be a firmament in the midst of the waters, and let there be a division between water and water*[^7], it seemed too little to add, *and so it was made,* unless it were added, *and God made the firmament, and God divided between the water that was above the firmament and the water that was below the firmament*[^8]: which indeed they so understand that they say the person of the Father is declared in what is written, *and God said, 'let there be a firmament in the midst of the waters, and let there be a division between water and water': and so it was made.* And then, that the Son may be understood to have made what was said by the Father to come to be, they think it is subjoined, *and God made the firmament, and God divided,* and so on.

## By the words "let there be" and "so it was made," is the person of the Father alone, or also of the Son, intimated?

**6. 11.** But when we first read, *and so it was made,* by whom do we understand it to have been made? If by the Son, what need was there to say further, *and God made,* and what follows? But if we understand what is written, *and so it was made,* to have been made by the Father, then it is no longer the Father who speaks and the Son who makes; and the Father can make something without the Son, so that thereafter the Son makes not this, but something else in like manner; which is against the catholic faith.

But if that of which it is said, *and so it was made,* is this same thing that comes to be when it is likewise said, *and God made,* what prevents us from understanding the same one to make what he said, who said that it should come to be? Or do they wish—apart from what is written, *and so it was made*—only in these words, in which it is said, *and God said, 'let there be,'* and afterward it is said, *and God made,* the person of the Father and of the Son to be understood?

## Another conjecture: is creation the work of the whole Trinity?

**6. 12.** But it can still be asked whether we ought to take it that the Father, as it were, commanded the Son, in what is written, *and God said, 'let there be'?* But why did Scripture not take care to show also the person of the Holy Spirit? Or is the Trinity thus understood: *and God said, 'let there be'; and God made; and God saw that it was good*[^9]? But it does not befit the unity of the Trinity that the Son should be understood to have made as though commanded, while the Holy Spirit, with none commanding it, freely saw that what was made was good.

For with what words would the Father command the Son to make, since he himself is the principal Word of the Father, *through whom all things were made*[^10]? Or is it that, in the very fact that it is written, *let there be a firmament*[^11]*,* this very utterance is the Word of the Father, the only-begotten Son, in whom are all things that are created, even before they are created, and whatever is in him is life; because whatever was made through him, in him is life, and life indeed creating, but under him a creature?

In one way, then, the things made through him are in him, because he rules and contains them; but in another way the things that he himself is are in him. For he himself is life, which is in him in such a way that it is himself, since he himself, life, is *the light of men*[^12].

Because, therefore, nothing could be created—whether before times, which indeed is not coeternal with the Creator, or from the beginning of times, or at some time—the reason of whose creating, if "reason" is rightly said, did not live, as coeternal life, in the Word of God coeternal with the Father: therefore Scripture, before it introduces each creature, in the order in which it says it was founded, looks to the Word of God, first putting, *and God said, 'let that be.'* For it finds no cause for the thing to be created which it does not find ought to have been created in the Word of God.

## Every creature has its reason in the Word.

**6. 13.** Not, then, did God say "let this or that creature be" as many times as *and God said* is repeated in this book. For he begot one Word, in which he said all things, before they were made one by one; but the speech of Scripture, descending to the capacity of little ones, while it introduces the kinds of creatures one by one, through each looks to the eternal reason of each kind in the Word of God; and though that is not repeated, he nonetheless repeats, *and God said.*

For if he wished first to say, *the firmament was made in the midst of the waters, that there might be a division between water and water*[^13], if anyone should ask him how it was made, he would rightly answer, *God said, 'let there be'*: that is, in the eternal Word of God it was that it should come to be. From there, then, he begins to narrate each thing made, which even after the narration of the thing made he would have to answer, in giving the reason, to one asking how it was made.

## That the things created in the Word remain by the goodness of the Spirit of God.

**6. 14.** When, therefore, we hear, *and God said, 'let there be,'* we understand that it was in the Word of God that it should come to be. But when we hear, *and so it was made,* we understand that the creature made did not exceed the bounds of its kind prescribed in the Word of God. But when we hear, *and God saw that it was good,* we understand that in the goodness of his Spirit it pleased him not as something known after it was made, but rather that it pleased him, in that goodness, that what was made should remain, where it had pleased him that it should come to be.

## What is meant by: He made.

**7. 15.** And on this account there still remains cause to inquire why, after he said, *and so it was made,* where the completion of the work is already indicated, he added, *and God made*: since by the very fact that he says, *and God said, 'let that be'; and so it was made,* it is already understood that God said it in his Word, and that it was made through his Word; and there the person not only of the Father but also of the Son can already appear.

For if it is repeated, and it is said, *and God made,* in order to show the person of the Son, did he then not gather the water on the third day, through the Son, that the dry land might appear, because there it is not said, *and God made the water to be gathered,* or "God gathered the water"? And yet even there, after he said, *and so it was made,* then he repeated, saying, *and the water that is under the heaven was gathered together*[^14]*.*

Was the light, too, not made through the Son, where he in no way at all repeated? For he could have said there too: "and God said, 'let there be light,' and so it was made; and God made the light; and he saw that it was good": or at least, as in the gathering of the waters, so as not to say, *and God made,* but yet to repeat again: "and God said, 'let there be light,' and so it was made[^15]; and light was made; and God saw the light, that it was good." But in no way repeating, after he had set forth, *and God said, 'let there be light'*; he brought in nothing else but, *and light was made*; and thereupon, concerning the light that pleased him and was divided from the darkness, and each called by its own names, he narrated without any repetition.

## Concerning the light, why it was not added: and God made, as for the other creatures.

**8. 16.** What, then, does that repetition mean in the rest? Is it shown in this way that on the first day, on which the light was made, the founding of the spiritual and intellectual creature is intimated by the name of light; in which nature all the holy angels and powers are understood?

And therefore he did not repeat that it was made, after he said, *light was made*; because the rational creature did not first know its own conformation, and then was formed; but in its very conformation it knew, that is, by the illumination of the Truth, toward which, turned, it was formed: whereas the rest, which are below, are so created that they first come to be in the knowledge of the rational creature, and then in their own kind?

Wherefore the founding of light is first in the Word of God, according to the reason by which it was founded, that is, in the Wisdom coeternal with the Father; and then in the very founding of light, according to the nature that was founded: there not made but begotten; here indeed made, because formed out of formlessness: and therefore *God said, 'let there be light,' and light was made*[^16]; so that what was there in the Word might here be in the work.

But the founding of heaven was first in the Word of God according to the begotten Wisdom; then it was made in the spiritual creature, that is, in the knowledge of the angels according to the wisdom created in them: then heaven was made, so that there might now be the creature of heaven itself in its own kind.

So too the separation or appearance of the waters and the lands, so the natures of trees and herbs, so the luminaries of heaven, so the living things sprung from the waters and the earth.

## The angels know the reasons of things before the things themselves are founded.

**8. 17.** For the angels do not, like cattle, see these sensible things by the bodily sense alone; but if they use any such sense, they rather recognize the things which they have better known inwardly in the very Word of God, by whom they are illumined so that they may live wisely: since there is in them the light that was first made, if we understand a spiritual light to have been made on that day.

As, then, the reason by which a creature is founded is prior in the Word of God to the creature itself which is founded, so too the knowledge of that same reason first comes to be in the intellectual creature, which is not darkened by sin; and then the founding of the creature itself.

For the angels did not, like us, advance toward the perceiving of wisdom, so as to *behold the invisible things of God, understood through the things that are made*[^17]—they who, from the time they were created, enjoy the very eternity of the Word in holy and pious contemplation; and from there, looking upon these things, according to what they see within, either approve of things rightly done or disapprove of sins.

## That the creatures God willed to found were revealed by him to the angels.

**8. 18.** Nor is it strange that to his holy angels, formed in the first founding of the light, God first showed what he was thereafter going to create. For they would not know the mind of God except so far as he himself had shown it. *For who has known the mind of the Lord? Or who has been his counselor? Or who has first given to him, and it shall be repaid to him? For from him, and through him, and in him are all things*[^18]. From him, then, the angels learned, when there came to be in them the knowledge of the creature thereafter to be made, and then it came to be in its own kind.

## Conclusion about the formulas used in the narration of Genesis.

**8. 19.** Wherefore, the light now made—in which we understand the rational creature formed by the eternal light—when, in the other things to be created, we hear, *and God said, 'let there be,'* let us understand the intention of Scripture as recurring to the eternity of the Word of God.

But when we hear, *and so it was made,* let us understand that there came to be in the intellectual creature the knowledge of the reason, which is in the Word of God, of the creature to be founded; so that in that nature it was first made, in a way, which by a certain prior motion knew, in the very Word of God, that it was first to be made: so that finally, when we hear it repeated and said that *God made,* we may understand that the creature itself now comes to be in its own kind.

Further, when we hear, *and God saw that it was good,* let us understand that what was made pleased the goodness of God, so that, according to the measure of its kind, what it had pleased him should come to be might remain, when *the Spirit of God was borne over the water*[^19]*.*

## Sacred Scripture did not wish to teach men the shape of the heaven and other things of no profit to salvation.

**9. 20.** It is also commonly asked what form and shape of the heaven is to be believed according to our Scriptures. For many dispute much about those things which our authors, by a greater prudence, omitted, as not going to profit those who learn for the blessed life; and what is worse, taking up much precious time, which ought to be spent on healthful things. For what does it matter to me whether the heaven, like a sphere, encloses the earth on every side, poised in the midst of the mass of the world, or whether on one side it covers it from above like a disk?

But because it is a question of the faith of the Scriptures, for that reason which I have more than once mentioned, lest anyone, not understanding the divine words, when concerning these things he has either found something of the kind in our Books, or heard it from them, which seems to oppose the reasons grasped by himself, in no way believe them when they admonish, or narrate, or pronounce the other useful things.

It must be said briefly that, concerning the shape of the heaven, our authors knew what the truth holds; but that the Spirit of God, who was speaking through them, did not wish to teach men these things of no profit to salvation.

## Sacred Scripture cannot contradict itself, nor the disciplines that are engaged in the investigation of truth.

**9. 21.** But, someone says, how is it not contrary to those who attribute the shape of a sphere to the heaven, what is written in our Writings, *who stretches out the heaven like a skin*[^20]? Let it indeed be contrary, if what they say is false: for that is true which divine authority says, rather than that which human weakness conjectures.

But if perhaps they should be able to prove that by such documents that it ought not to be doubted, it must be demonstrated that this which was said among us about a skin is not contrary to those true reasons; otherwise it will be contrary even to our own Scriptures in another place, where the heaven is said to be suspended like a vault[^21].

For what is so diverse and opposed to itself as the flat stretching-out of a skin and the curved convexity of a vault? But if it is necessary, as it is necessary, so to understand these two that each is found to agree, and not to be in conflict with itself; so it is necessary also that neither of these be opposed to those disputations, if perhaps certain reason should declare them true, by which it is taught that the heaven is convex on every side in the shape of a sphere—if indeed it is proved.

## How the image of the vault and the skin, by which the heaven is suggested, can be understood.

**9. 22.** And indeed that likeness of a vault among us, even taken literally, does not hinder those who say it is a sphere. For it is rightly believed that Scripture wished to speak of the shape of the heaven according to that part which is above us. If, then, it is not a sphere, it is a vault on one side, on that side on which the heaven covers the earth; but if it is a sphere, it is a vault on every side. But that which was said about a skin presses harder, lest it be opposed not to the sphere, which is perhaps a human invention, but to our very vault.

But what I have thought of this allegorically, the thirteenth book of our *Confessions* contains[^22]. Whether, then, it is to be understood as I there set it down, or in some other way, that the heaven is stretched out like a skin—on account of those troublesome and excessive exactors of a literal exposition I say this, which, as I think, is plain to the senses of all: for perhaps each, that is, both "skin" and "vault," can be understood figuratively; but how each can be taken literally must be considered. For if a vault is rightly said to be not only curved but also flat, then surely a skin too is stretched out not only into a flat but also into a round fold. For a wineskin too, like a bladder, is a skin.

## The motion of the heaven and the name "firmament."

**10. 23.** Concerning the motion of the heaven too, some brethren raise a question, whether it stands still or is moved. For if it is moved, they say, how is it a firmament? But if it stands still, how do the stars, which are believed to be fixed in it, go around from east to west, the northern ones making shorter circles near the pole; so that the heaven, if there is another pole hidden from us at the other summit, seems to be rotated like a sphere, but if there is no other pole, like a disk? To them I answer that these things are inquired into by very subtle and laborious reasons, so that it may truly be grasped whether it is so or not so; but to enter upon and treat these I have now no time, nor ought those to have it whom we desire to be instructed for their own salvation and the necessary advantage of holy Church.

This, indeed, let them know: that neither does the name "firmament" compel us to think that the heaven stands still (for it is allowed to understand it called firmament not on account of standing still, but on account of firmness, or on account of the impassable boundary of the upper and lower waters); nor, if truth should persuade us that the heaven stands still, are we hindered from being able to understand the circuit of the stars.

For by those very men who have inquired into these things most curiously and most idly it has been found that, even with the heaven not moved, if the stars alone were moved, all the things could have come to be which have been observed and grasped in the very revolutions of the stars.

## On the separation of the waters from the earth, and the earth's formlessness.

**11. 24.** *And God said, 'let the water that is under the heaven be gathered into one gathering, and let the dry land appear.' And so it was made; and the water that is under the heaven was gathered into its gathering, and the dry land appeared. And God called the dry land earth, and the gathering of the water he called sea. And God saw that it was good*[^23]*.* Concerning this work of God we have already treated sufficiently, for the sake of inquiring into another matter, in our first volume[^24]. Here, then, we briefly advise that anyone whom it perhaps does not move to ask when the appearance of the waters and the lands was created, should take it that on this day nothing was done except that these two lower elements were separated.

But whoever is moved as to why on the days light and heaven were made, but outside the days, or before all the days, water and earth; and why those things were made at the word of God, God saying, *let there be,* while these are found indeed separated by God speaking, but not made by God speaking:

has something to understand with faith kept safe, namely that which was said before the enumeration of the days, *the earth was invisible and unformed*[^25]*,* when Scripture was commending what sort of earth God had made, because it had foretold, *in the beginning God made heaven and earth*[^26]: that it wished by these words to suggest nothing else than the formlessness of corporeal matter, choosing to call it in a more usual rather than a more obscure way. If only it does not creep upon a slow understanding to attempt, because Scripture separates matter and form in words, to separate these two also in time, as though matter were first, and to it, an interval of time being interposed, form were afterward added.

Whereas God created these things at once, and established matter formed, whose formlessness Scripture, as I said, foretold by the usual term either of earth or of water. For earth and water, even existing in their own qualities as that which we see, are nevertheless, on account of their easy corruption, nearer to that same formlessness than the heavenly bodies.

And since, through the enumeration of the days, the several things now formed out of the formless are counted, and he had already narrated heaven made out of that corporeal matter, whose appearance differs much from earthen things,

now what remained to be formed out of it in the lowest part of things, he did not wish to insert into the order of created things under these words, so that it should be said, *let there be*; this remaining formlessness not being about to receive such an appearance as the heaven had received, but one now lower and weaker and nearest to formlessness.

So that rather by these words, when it is said, *let the waters be gathered,* and, *let the dry land appear*[^27]*,* these two received their own appearances, most well known and manageable by us, the water mobile, the earth immobile: and therefore of the one it was said, *let it be gathered*; but of the other, *let it appear*: for water is slippingly fluid, earth stably fixed.

## The creation of living things.

**12. 25.** *And God said, 'let the earth bring forth the herb of fodder, bearing seed according to its kind and according to its likeness; and the fruit-bearing tree making fruit, whose seed is in it according to its likeness, upon the earth.' And so it was made. And the earth put forth the herb of fodder, having seed according to its kind and according to its likeness, and the fruit-bearing tree making fruit, whose seed is in it according to its kind, upon the earth. And God saw that it was good. And there was evening, and there was morning, the third day*[^28]*.* Here the governance of the orderer is to be noticed: that, since the creature of herbs and trees is something distinct from the appearance of the lands and the waters, so that they cannot be numbered among the elements, it was said of them separately that they should come forth from the earth; and separately the usual things were rendered to them, so that it was said, *and so it was made*; and then what was made was repeated: separately too it was indicated that God saw that it was good; yet because, fixed by their roots, they are continuous with the lands and connected to them, he willed these too to belong to the same day.

## Why the luminaries were founded on the fourth day.

**13. 26.** *And God said, 'let there be luminaries in the firmament of heaven, to give light upon the earth, for the beginning of day and night, and to divide between day and night; and let them be for signs and seasons, and for days, and for years; and let them be for brightness in the firmament of heaven, to give light upon the earth.' And so it was made. And God made two great luminaries: the greater luminary for the beginning of day, and the lesser luminary for the beginning of night; and the stars: and God set them in the firmament of heaven, to give light upon the earth, and to be for the beginning of day and night, and to divide between the light and the darkness. And God saw that it was good. And there was evening, and there was morning, the fourth day*[^29]*.*

On this fourth day it must be asked what this ordering means, that water and earth should first either be made, or separated, and the earth first put forth its growth, before the stars came to be in the heaven. For we cannot say that whatever was better was chosen, by which the series of days should be so distinguished that the end and the middle should stand out most adorned: for the middle of seven days is the fourth. For it occurs to one that on the seventh day no creature was made. Or does the light of the first day perhaps correspond rather to the rest of the seventh day, so that in this way, with the ends harmonizing, this order of things is woven, the lights of heaven standing out from the middle?

But if the first day harmonizes with the seventh, then the second too ought to harmonize with the sixth. But what has the firmament of heaven similar to man made in the image of God? Is it because the heaven occupies the whole upper part of the world, and to man the power of dominion over the whole lower part was granted? But what do we do about the cattle and the beasts, which on that same sixth day the earth produced in their kinds? What comparison can there be of these with the heaven?

## By what plan and order this world was created.

**13. 27.** Or rather, since by the name of light the formation of the spiritual creature is understood to have been made first, was it consequent that the corporeal creature too, that is, this visible world, should come to be; which was made in two days on account of its two greatest parts, of which the whole consists, namely heaven and earth—according to this comparison of reason by which the whole creation, both spiritual and corporeal, is often named heaven and earth: so that this globe too of the more turbulent air is reckoned to the earthen part (for by moist exhalations it "takes on body"); but whatever there is of tranquil air, where windy and stormy motions cannot exist, belongs to the heavenly part: after this whole of the corporeal mass was fashioned, which is wholly in one place, where the world is set, was it consequent that it should be filled with parts within the whole, which would be driven from place to place by fitting motions?

Of which kind herbs and trees are not. For these are fixed to the earth by their roots; and although they have the motions of their growth, yet they are not moved from their own places by their own efforts; but where they are fixed, there they are nourished and grow: therefore they belong rather to the earth than to the kinds of things that move in the waters and on the lands. Since, then, two days were assigned to establishing the visible world, that is, heaven and earth, it remains that the three remaining days be reckoned to these mobile and visible parts which are created within it.

And because, just as heaven was made first, so it is to be ordered first with parts of this kind; on the fourth day the stars are made, by whose shining over the earth the lower dwelling too may be illumined, lest its dwellers be led into a dark dwelling. And therefore, because the weak bodies of the lower dwellers are restored by rest succeeding their motions, it came to be that, with the sun going around, by the alternation of day and night, they might enjoy, on account of the alternation of sleeping and waking; but that night might not remain unbecoming, but, by the light of the moon and the stars, might both console the very men, who for the most part have a need to work even at night, and be tempered to sufficiency for certain animals which cannot bear the light of the sun.

## How the luminaries stand in relation to seasons, days, and nights.

**14. 28.** But that which was said, *and let them be for signs and seasons, and for days, and for years*[^30]*,* who does not see how obscurely it is set down, that times began on the fourth day, as though the earlier three days could pass without time? Who, then, penetrates in mind how those three days passed before times began, which are said to begin on the fourth day; or whether those days passed at all?

Or was day named with respect to the appearance of the thing made, and night with respect to its privation: so that matter not yet formed in appearance, out of which the rest were to be formed, was called night; just as, even in formed things, the formlessness of matter can nevertheless be understood from their very mutability—for it cannot be distinguished by spaces, as though more remote in place, or earlier in time?

Or rather was that same mutability in the thing itself, made and formed—that is, the possibility, so to speak, of failing—called night; because there is in things made, even if they are not changed, the ability to be changed?

But evening and morning are to be understood not as if through the passing-away and arrival of time, but through a certain term, by which is understood how far the measure of a thing's own nature extends, and whence consequently is the beginning of another nature: or is some other account of these words to be more diligently investigated?

## How it is to be understood that the stars are "signs."

**14. 29.** Who can easily break into so great a secret, and say what signs he means, when he says of the stars, *and let them be for signs*[^31]? For he does not mean those which it is a mark of vanity to observe; but certainly useful ones, necessary for the uses of this life, which either sailors observe in steering, or all men for foreseeing the qualities of the air through summer and winter, and the temperateness of autumn and spring. And no doubt he calls these *seasons*, which come to be through the stars—not spans of delay, but the alternations of the conditions of this sky.

For if any motion, whether corporeal or spiritual, preceded the founding of these luminaries, so as to carry something from future expectation through the present into the past, it could not be without time. And who would maintain that there was none except from the beginning of the founding of the stars? But the fixed hours, and days, and years, which we usually know, would not come to be except by the motions of the stars.

And so, if we understand "seasons, days, and years" in this way—as certain marks which we compute by clocks, or which are most well known in the heaven, when the sun rises from the east to its midday height, and thence again declines to the west; so that thereafter it can be noticed that either the moon or some star emerges from the east immediately after the setting of the sun; which likewise, when it has come to the midday height of the heaven, indicates the middle of the night, being then about to set when, with the sun returning, it becomes morning.

But whole days are the circuits of the sun from east to east: and years are either these usual windings of the sun, not when it returns to the east, which it does daily, but when it returns to the same places of the stars, which it does not do except when three hundred sixty-five days and six hours are completed, that is, a quarter of a whole day, which part, taken four times, compels one day to be interposed, which the Romans call the bissextile, so that it may return to the same circuit; or even greater and more hidden years: for when the spans of other stars are completed, greater years are said to come to be. If, then, we understand "seasons, days, and years" in this way, no one doubts that these come to be by the stars and luminaries.

For it is so set down that it is uncertain whether what was said pertains to all the stars, *let them be for signs and seasons, and for days, and for years*; or whether the signs and seasons pertain to the rest, but the days and years to the sun alone.

## Of what size the moon was made.

**15. 30.** Many also inquire most loquaciously of what kind the moon was made; and would that, while inquiring, they spoke, and not rather tried to teach! For they say that it was made full, because it was not fitting that God should make anything imperfect on that day, among the stars, on which it is written that the stars were made.

But those who resist say: "then it ought rather to have been called the first moon, not the fourteenth; for who begins to number in that way?" But I am in the middle between these, so as to assert neither; but I will plainly say that, whether God made the moon first or full, he made it perfect.

For God is the author and founder of the natures themselves. But every thing, whatever by natural progress through fitting times it in a way brings forth and unfolds, it contained beforehand, hidden, even if not in the appearance or bulk of a body, yet in the power and reason of nature. Unless perhaps a tree which through the winter is empty of fruits and stripped of leaves is then to be called imperfect; or indeed in its very beginnings, when it had as yet given no fruit, that nature was imperfect.

Which could be rightly said not only of the tree, but not even of its very seed, in which all the things that by the progress of time in a way come forth lie hidden in invisible modes. Although, if God were said to have made something imperfect, which he himself would afterward perfect, what reproach would that opinion carry? But it would rightly displease, if what had been begun by him were said to be perfected by another.

## The varieties of light in the moon are explained.

**15. 31.** Those, then, who do not complain about the earth, which God made when in the beginning he made heaven and earth, because it was invisible and unformed, and afterward on the third day is made visible and ordered—why do they make for themselves the darkness of questions about the moon? Or if they understand what was said of the earth to have been said not by an interval of time, since God created matter together with things, but by the distribution of the narration; why, in this matter which can even be seen with the eyes, do they not behold that the moon has a whole body, and is perfect in its whole roundness, even with a horned light, whether beginning to shine upon the lands, or ceasing to gleam?

If, then, the light in it grows, or is perfected, or is diminished, it is not the luminary itself, but that which is kindled, that is varied: but if it always shines from one part of its little sphere, but while it turns that part toward the earth, until it turns the whole, which happens from the first to the fourteenth, it seems to grow; it is always full, but does not always appear to the dwellers on earth. This same account holds, even if it is illumined by the rays of the sun. For not even thus can it, when it is nearest the sun, appear except with luminous horns; because the rest of the part, which is wholly illumined in its orb, is not toward the lands so that it can be seen, except when it is opposite the sun, so that the whole of what it illumines appears to the lands.

## Verses 8-9 of Psalm 136 are explained.

**15. 32.** Yet there are those who say that they think the moon was first made by God as the fourteenth, not because it is to be believed to have been made full, but because in the Scriptures of God the words have it thus, that *the moon was made for the beginning of night*[^32]; but it is seen at the beginning of night when it is full: at other times it begins to be seen even by day before its fullness, and in the progress of the night the more amply the more it is diminished.

But one who by "the beginning of night" understands nothing but the rule over it (for the Greek word too indicates this rather, when it was said ἀρχήν, and in the *Psalms* it is more openly written, *the sun for the dominion of the day, the moon and the stars for the dominion of the night*[^33]) is not compelled to number from the fourteenth, and to believe that the moon made first is not the first.

## Whether the brightness in the stars is equal.

**16. 33.** It is also commonly asked whether these conspicuous luminaries of heaven, that is, the sun and the moon and the stars, gleam equally, but, because they stand at different intervals from the earth, on that account appear to our eyes with different brightness, more and less. And of the moon indeed those who say these things do not doubt that it shines less than the sun, by which they also report it to be illumined.

But they dare to say that many stars are either equal to the sun, or even greater, but, being placed farther off, seem small. And to us perhaps it can suffice that, in whatever way this matter stands, the stars were founded by God the craftsman. Although let us hold what was said by apostolic authority: *there is one glory of the sun, another glory of the moon, and another glory of the stars; for star differs from star in glory*[^34].

But because they can still say, even if they do not resist the Apostle: "they differ indeed in glory, but to the eyes of earthly beings"; or, because the Apostle was saying this on account of the likeness of those who rise again, who certainly will not be otherwise to the eyes and otherwise in themselves, "the stars differ indeed even in themselves in glory, but yet some are even greater than the sun."

Let them see for themselves how they attribute so great a primacy to the sun that they say it both holds certain stars by its rays—and those indeed the principal ones, to which these men make more supplication than to the rest—and drives them back from their own course. For it is not made verisimilar that greater or even equal stars can be overcome by the violence of its rays.

Or if they assert that the higher stars of the signs, or the northern ones, are greater, which suffer nothing of the kind from the sun; why do they venerate more those that go around through the signs? Why do they report them to be the mistresses of the signs? For even if anyone should contend that those retrogressions of the stars, or perhaps their slownesses, do not come to be from the sun, but from other more hidden causes; yet that these men, in their ravings, by which, straying from the truth, they suspect a power of the fates, attribute a special power to the sun, is certainly manifest from their books.

## Star differs from star.

**16. 34.** But let them say what they will about the heaven, strangers to the Father who is in the heavens: but for us it is neither expedient nor fitting to inquire something more subtle about the intervals and magnitude of the stars, and to spend on such inquiry time necessary for graver and better things. And we better believe those to be the greater luminaries than the rest which holy Scripture so commends: *and God made two great luminaries*[^35]: which, however, are not equal. For consequently it says, when it has set them before the rest, that they differ among themselves. For it says, *the greater luminary for the beginning of day, and the lesser luminary for the beginning of night*[^36].

For certainly they will concede at least this to our eyes, that it is manifest that these shine over the earth more than the rest, and that the day is not bright except by the light of the sun, nor does the night, with so many stars appearing, shine, if the moon is absent, in the way that it is illumined by its presence.

## On the fates: why they lie by the markings of the stars.

**17. 35.** But as for the fates, whatever subtleties of theirs, and as it were the experiments of the documents of their reckoning, which they call *apotelesmata*, let us altogether reject them from the soundness of our faith: for by such disputations they try even to take from us the grounds of praying, and with impious perversity, in evil deeds which are most rightly reproved, they bring it about that God the author of the stars is to be accused rather than the man of the crimes. But that our souls are not by nature subject to bodies, not even to heavenly ones, let them hear even their own philosophers.

But that those higher bodies are not, with respect to the things which these men treat, more powerful than earthly bodies, let them sometime recognize even from this: that when many bodies of diverse kinds, whether of living things, or of herbs and shrubs, are sown at one and the same point of time, and at one point of time innumerable many are born, not only in diverse but even in the same places of the lands, there are such great varieties in their growths, in their actions and experiences, that truly these men, as the saying goes, lose the stars, if they consider these things.

## The astrologers are refuted by the case of twins, who, born under the same constellations, live differently.

**17. 36.** But what is more tasteless and dull than, when they are refuted by these matters, to say that the fatal account of the stars pertains only to men, to be made subject to them? In which, however, they themselves are refuted even by the case of twins, who, living differently, differently fortunate or unfortunate, and dying differently, for the most part receive the same constellations. For although there was some difference when they were poured from the womb, yet in some there is only as much difference as cannot be grasped by these men's computation.

The hand of Jacob, who followed, was found, as they were being born, holding the foot of his brother who preceded; so much were they born as though one infant, twice as long, seemed to be born[^37]. Of these, certainly, the constellations, as they call them, could in no way have been unlike. What, then, is more empty than that the astrologer, gazing at those constellations, with the same horoscope, the same moon, should say that one of them was loved by his mother, the other not loved?

For if he should say otherwise, he would surely say what is false; but if he should say this, he would say what is true, but not according to the inept little incantations of their books. But if they will not believe this history, because it is brought forward from our writings, can they then also abolish the nature of things? Since, then, they say that they are by no means deceived, if they have found the hour of conception, let them at least, like men, not disdain to consider the conception of twins.

## Why divination sometimes comes true.

**17. 37.** And therefore it must be confessed that, when true things are said by these men, they are said by a certain most hidden prompting, which human minds undergo without knowing it.

And when this happens for the deceiving of men, it is the working of seducing spirits, to whom it is permitted to know certain true things about temporal matters: partly by the keenness of a subtler sense, because they thrive in subtler bodies; partly by a craftier experience, on account of so great a length of life; partly by the holy angels revealing to them, even at his command, what they themselves learn from the omnipotent God, who distributes human merits with the sincerity of a most hidden justice.

But sometimes those same unspeakable spirits even foretell, as if by divining, the very things which they themselves are going to do. Wherefore, by a good Christian, whether astrologers or any of those who impiously divine, especially when they say true things, are to be guarded against, lest, the soul deceived by fellowship with demons, they ensnare it by a certain pact of partnership.

## It is commonly asked whether the stars are animate bodies.

**18. 38.** It is also commonly asked whether these conspicuous luminaries of the heaven are bodies alone, or whether they have certain spirits as their rulers; and if they have them, whether they are also vitally "inspired" by them, as flesh is "animated" by the souls of "animals," or only by their presence, without any commingling.

Although this cannot easily be grasped, yet I think that in the course of treating the Scriptures more opportune places can occur, where, concerning this matter, according to the rules of holy authority, even if we may not show anything certain, yet we may believe.

But now, always keeping the moderation of pious gravity, we ought to believe nothing rashly about an obscure matter; lest perhaps what truth shall afterward lay open—although it can in no way be opposed to the Holy Books, whether of the Old Testament or of the New—we should nevertheless hate, out of love of our own error. Now let us pass on to the third book of our work.
