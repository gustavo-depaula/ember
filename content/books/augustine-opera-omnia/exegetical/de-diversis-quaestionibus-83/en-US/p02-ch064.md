# On the Samaritan Woman

**64. 1.** The evangelical mysteries marked in the words and deeds of our Lord Jesus Christ do not lie open to all; and some, by interpreting them less carefully and less soberly, often bring forward destruction instead of salvation and error instead of knowledge of the truth.

Among these is that mystery which is written: that the Lord came at the sixth hour of the day to Jacob's well, and, weary from the journey, sat down, and asked a drink of the Samaritan woman, and the other things that are said for examination and discussion in the same passage of the Scriptures.

On this matter, this must first be held, which must be guarded with the greatest vigilance in all the Scriptures: that the exposition of a divine mystery be in accord with the faith.

**64. 2.** At the sixth hour of the day, then, our Lord came to the well. I see in the well a dark depth. I am therefore prompted to understand the lowest parts of this world, that is, the earthly parts, to which the Lord Jesus came at the sixth hour—that is, in the sixth age of the human race, as in the old age of the old man, which we are commanded to put off so that we may put on the new man who was created according to God[^1].

For the sixth age is old age, since the first is infancy; the second, childhood; the third, adolescence; the fourth, youth; the fifth, maturity. And so the life of the old man, which is lived out according to the flesh in the temporal condition, is brought to a close in the sixth age, as in old age.

In this old age, as I have said, of the human race, our Lord, our creator and restorer, came to us, so that, with the old man dying, he might establish a new man in himself, whom, stripped of the earthly stain, he might transfer into the heavenly kingdoms. Therefore the well, as has been said, signifies by its dark depth the earthly toil and error of this world.

And since the old man is outer and the new man inner—for it was said by the Apostle, *and though our outer man is being corrupted, yet the inner man is being renewed day by day*[^2]—most fittingly of all (since all visible things, which Christian discipline renounces, belong to the outer man) did the Lord come to the well at the sixth hour,

that is, at midday, when this visible sun now begins to decline toward its setting, since for us too, called by Christ, the delight in visible things diminishes, so that the inner man, renewed by the love of invisible things, may return to the inner light, which never sets—following the apostolic discipline of not seeking *the things that are seen, but the things that are not seen; for the things that are seen are temporal, but the things that are not seen are eternal*[^3].

**64. 3.** That he came weary to the well signifies the weakness of the flesh; that he sat down signifies humility, since he both took on the frailty of the flesh for us and deigned to appear humbly as a man to men.

Of this weakness of the flesh the prophet says, *a man set in affliction and knowing how to bear infirmity*[^4]*;* and of his humility the Apostle speaks, saying, *he humbled himself, becoming obedient unto death*[^5]*.* And yet his sitting down, since teachers are accustomed to sit, may by another interpretation show not the modesty of humility but the role of a master.

**64. 4.** But it may be asked why he asked to drink from the Samaritan woman, who had come for the sake of filling her water jar with water, when he himself afterward proclaimed that he could give an abundance of the spiritual fountain to those who asked him.

But of course the Lord thirsted for the faith of that woman, since she was a Samaritan, and Samaria is accustomed to bear the image of idolatry; for the Samaritans themselves, separated from the people of the Jews, had devoted the glory of their souls to the images of dumb animals, that is, to golden calves. But the Lord Jesus had come so that he might lead the multitude of the gentiles, who served images, to the stronghold of the Christian faith and of uncorrupted religion: for *it is not the healthy,* he says, *who need a physician, but the sick*[^6]*.* Therefore he thirsts for the faith of those for whom he shed his blood. *So Jesus said to her: 'Woman, give me to drink*[^7]*.'*

And that you may know what our Lord thirsted for, a little later his disciples come, who had gone into the city to buy food, and they say to him: *'Rabbi, eat.' But he said to them: 'I have food to eat that you do not know.' So the disciples said to one another: 'Has anyone brought him something to eat?' Jesus says to them: 'My food is to do the will of him who sent me, and to accomplish his work*[^8]*.'*

Is any other will of the Father, who sent him, here to be understood, and any other work of his that he answered he wished to accomplish, than that he might turn us from the destructive error of the world to faith in himself? Such, then, as his food is, such also is his drink.

For this reason he thirsted in that woman to do in her the will of the Father and to accomplish his work. But, understanding carnally, she answered: *since you are a Jew, how is it that you ask a drink of me, since I am a Samaritan woman? For Jews have no dealings with Samaritans.* To her our Lord said: *if you knew the gift of God, and who it is that says to you, 'give me to drink,' you would rather have asked of him, and he would have given you living water*[^9]*,* so that he might show her by this that he had not asked for such water as she had understood, but that, because he himself thirsted for her faith, he desired to give the Holy Spirit to her as she thirsted.

For we rightly understand this living water to be the gift of God, as he himself says, *if you knew the gift of God;* and as the same John the Evangelist testifies in another place, saying that *Jesus stood and cried out: 'If anyone thirsts, let him come and drink. He who believes in me, as Scripture says, out of his belly shall flow rivers of living water*[^10]*.'*

Quite consistently: *he who believes in me,* he says, *out of his belly shall flow rivers of living water,* because we first believe, that we may merit these gifts. These rivers of living water, then, which he wished to give to that woman, are the reward of the faith for which he first thirsted in her. And he subjoins the interpretation of this living water thus: *this he said,* he says, *about the Spirit whom those who were to believe in him were to receive. But the Spirit had not yet been given, because Jesus had not yet been glorified*[^11]*.* This, then, is the gift of the Holy Spirit, which after his glorification he gave to the Church, as another Scripture says: *ascending on high he led captivity captive; he gave gifts to men*[^12]*.*

**64. 5.** But that woman still understands carnally; for she answered thus: *Lord, you have nothing to draw with, and the well is deep; from where then do you have living water to give me? Are you greater than our father Jacob, who gave us this well, and drank from it himself, and his sons and his cattle?* Now indeed the Lord explains what he had said. *Everyone,* he says, *who drinks of this water will thirst again; but whoever drinks of the water that I shall give will never thirst forever; but that water which I shall give will become in him a fountain of water springing up into eternal life.* But the woman still clings to the prudence of the flesh.

For what did she answer? *Lord, give me this water, so that I may neither thirst nor come here to draw. Jesus says to her: 'Go, call your husband, and come here.'* Since he knew that she had no husband, it is asked why he said this. For when the woman had said, *I have no husband,* Jesus says to her: *you have rightly said that you have no husband; for you have had five husbands, and the one you now have is not your husband; this you have said truly*[^13]*.*

But these things are not to be taken carnally, lest we seem to be like this very Samaritan woman herself. Rather, if we have now tasted something of that gift of God, let us treat these matters spiritually.

**64. 6.** Some take the five husbands to be the five books that were administered through Moses. And what was said, *and the one you now have is not your husband*[^14]*,* they understand the Lord to have said of himself,

so that the sense is this: "first you served the five books of Moses as five husbands, but now the one you have, that is, the one you hear, who speaks with you, is not your husband, because you have not yet believed in him."

But since, not yet believing in Christ, she was still certainly held by the bond of those five husbands, that is, of the five books, it can give pause how it could be said, *you have had five husbands,* as though she no longer had them now, when she was still certainly living subject to them.

Then, since the five books of Moses proclaim nothing other than Christ, as he himself says, *if you believed Moses, you would perhaps believe me also; for he wrote of me*[^15]*,* how can it be understood that a person departs from those five books in order to pass over to Christ, when the one who believes in Christ embraces those five books all the more eagerly—not as to be abandoned, but as to be understood spiritually?

**64. 7.** There is therefore another interpretation, by which the five husbands are understood as the five senses of the body:

one which pertains to the eyes, by which we perceive this visible light and any colors and shapes of bodies; another, that of the ears, by which we sense the movements of voices and all sounds; the third, of the nostrils, by which we are delighted by the various sweetness of odors;

the fourth, taste in the mouth, which senses the sweet and the bitter and has the testing of all flavors; the fifth, by touching throughout the whole body, distinguishes hot and cold, soft and hard, smooth and rough, and whatever else there is that we sense by touching.

And so by these five carnal senses the first age of a person is steeped, by the necessity of mortal nature, in which we are so born after the sin of the first man that, the light of the mind not yet restored, we pass through a carnal life subject to the carnal senses, without any understanding of the truth.

Such must infants and little children be, who cannot yet receive reason. And because these senses are natural, which govern the first age and have been bestowed on us by God the maker, they are rightly called husbands, that is, lawful spouses, as it were, since it is not error by its own fault but the maker's craftsmanship of God that has bestowed them as nature.

But when anyone has come to that age in which he can already be capable of reason, if he is able straightway to grasp the truth, he will no longer use those senses as rulers, but will have as his husband the rational spirit, to which he reduces those senses into service, subjecting his body to servitude, when the soul is no longer subject to the five husbands, that is, the five senses of the body, but has the divine Word as its lawful husband—and, joined and clinging to it, when the spirit of the man too clings to Christ, since the head of the man is Christ[^16], it will enjoy eternal life in a spiritual embrace, with no fear of separation. For *who shall be able to separate us from the love of Christ?*[^17]

But since that woman was held by error—she who signified the multitude of the world subjugated to vain superstitions—after those times of the five carnal senses, by which the first age, as we have said, is governed, the word of God had not received her in marriage, but the devil possessed her in an adulterous embrace.

And so the Lord, seeing that she was carnal, that is, that she understood carnally, says to her: *go, call your husband, and come here,* that is, remove yourself from the carnal affection in which you are now placed, by which you cannot understand what I say; *and call your husband,* that is, be present in the spirit of understanding.

For the spirit of man is in a certain way like a husband to the soul, governing the animal affection as though a spouse—not that Holy Spirit who remains unchangeable with the Father and the Son and is given unchangeably to worthy souls, but the spirit of man, of whom the Apostle says, *no one knows what is in a man except the spirit of the man*[^18]*.*

For that Holy Spirit is the Spirit of God, of whom he again says, *so too no one knows the things of God except the Spirit of God.* This spirit of man, then, when it is present, that is, when it is within, and subjects itself in piety to God, the person understands what is said spiritually; but when the error of the devil rules in the soul, as though understanding were absent, it is an adulterer.

*Call,* then, he says, *your husband,* that is, the spirit that is in you, by which a person can understand spiritual things if the light of truth illumines it; let it be present when I speak to you, so that you may be able to receive the spiritual water.

And when she said, *I have no husband,* he says, *you have said well, for you have had five husbands,* that is, the five senses of the flesh governed you in your first age, *and the one you now have is not your husband*[^19]*,* because there is not in you the spirit that understands God, with whom you could have a lawful marriage, but rather the error of the devil holds sway, which corrupts you with an adulterous defilement.

**64. 8.** And perhaps, in order to indicate to those who understand that the five aforementioned senses of the body are signified by the name of five husbands, after five carnal responses this woman in her sixth response names Christ.

For her first response is: *since you are a Jew, how is it that you ask a drink of me?* The second: *Lord, you have nothing to draw with, and the well is deep.* The third: *Lord, give me this water, so that I may neither thirst nor come here to draw.* The fourth: *I have no husband.* The fifth: *I see that you are a prophet. Our fathers worshiped on this mountain.*

For this response too is carnal. For to carnal people an earthly place had been given where they might worship, but the Lord said that spiritual people would worship in spirit and in truth. And after he had spoken this, the woman in her sixth response confesses that Christ is the teacher of all these things; for she says: *I know that the Messiah is coming, who is called Christ. When he comes, he himself will announce all things to us*[^20]*.*

But still she errs, because she does not see that the one she hopes will come has already come. Yet now, by the mercy of the Lord, this error is cast out like an adulterer. For Jesus says to her: *I am he who speaks with you*. On hearing this, she did not respond, but at once, leaving her water jar behind, she went off into the city, hurrying not only to believe the gospel and the Lord's coming, but even to proclaim it.

Nor should it be passed over carelessly that she departed leaving her water jar behind. For the water jar perhaps signifies the love of this world, that is, the desire by which people draw for themselves, from the dark depth whose image the well bears—that is, from earthly conversation—the pleasure which, once received, they again burn to desire, just as *whoever drinks of that water,* he says, *will thirst again*[^21]*.* But it was fitting that, believing in Christ, she should renounce the world, and by leaving the water jar behind should show that she had left worldly desire behind, believing in her heart not only unto righteousness, but also about to confess and proclaim with her mouth what she believed, unto salvation[^22].
