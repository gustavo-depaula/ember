# The Opinion of Cicero, How the Virtues — of the Mind Were Divided and Defined by Him

**31. 1.** "Virtue is a habit of mind in accord with the measure of nature and with reason. Wherefore, once all its parts are known, the whole force of simple honorableness will have to be considered. It has, then, four parts: prudence, justice, fortitude, and temperance.

Prudence is the knowledge of things good, bad, and neither. Its parts are memory, understanding, and foresight. Memory is that by which the mind recalls the things that have been; understanding, that by which it perceives the things that are; foresight, that by which something future is seen before it has happened.

Justice is a habit of mind that, the common advantage being preserved, renders to each his own worth.

Its beginning proceeds from nature; then certain things came into custom by reason of advantage; afterward, things both proceeding from nature and approved by custom were sanctioned by the fear of laws and by religion.

The right that is of nature is that which opinion did not beget, but which a certain innate force implanted: such as religion, devotion, gratitude, retribution, deference, and truthfulness.

Religion is that which brings the care and ceremony of a certain higher nature, which they call divine; devotion is that by which loving service is rendered to those joined by blood and to the fatherland, and diligent honor is paid them;

gratitude is that in which is contained the memory of friendships and of the good offices of another, and the will to repay them;

retribution is that by which violence or injury, and in general everything that will be harmful, is driven off by defending or avenging;

deference is that by which men who excel in some dignity are thought worthy of a certain regard and honor; truthfulness is that by which the things that are, or were, or will be are stated unaltered.

The right that is of custom is that which either, drawn lightly from nature, usage has nourished and made greater—as religion, and any of those things we mentioned before which we see, having proceeded from nature, made greater on account of custom—or that which long age has brought into a usage by the approval of the populace.

Of this kind are agreement, equity, and adjudication.

Agreement is what is settled among certain parties; equity is what is fair toward all; adjudication is that about which something has already been established by the verdicts of someone or of several.

The right that is of law is what is contained in that writing which is set forth to the people for them to observe.

Fortitude is the considered undertaking of dangers and the endurance of toils. Its parts are magnificence, confidence, patience, and perseverance. Magnificence is the thinking out and carrying through of great and lofty matters with a certain ample and splendid purpose of mind.

Confidence is that by which, in great and honorable matters, the mind itself has placed much trust in itself with sure hope. Patience is the voluntary and lasting endurance of hard and difficult things for the sake of honorableness or advantage. Perseverance is a stable and perpetual remaining in a well-considered plan of reason.

Temperance is the firm and moderate mastery of reason over lust and other wrong impulses of the mind. Its parts are continence, clemency, and modesty. Continence is that by which desire is ruled by the governance of counsel; clemency is that by which minds rashly enticed and stirred up to hatred of someone are held back by kindliness; modesty is that by which honorable shame procures a precious and stable authority."

**31. 2.** "And all these things are to be sought for their own sake alone, so that no advantage be added. To show this neither pertains to our present design nor is consistent with the brevity of instruction.

But to be avoided for their own sake are not only those things which are contrary to these—as cowardice is to fortitude and injustice to justice—but also those which seem to be near and bordering on them, yet are very far off. Of this kind, distrust is contrary to confidence, and for that reason is a vice; boldness is not contrary, but adjacent and near to it, and is nevertheless a vice.

Thus for each virtue a bordering vice will be found, either called already by a fixed name—as boldness, which borders on confidence, obstinacy, which borders on perseverance, superstition, which is near to religion—or without any fixed name. And all these likewise, as things contrary to the good, will be placed among the things to be avoided. And concerning that kind of honorableness which is sought in every respect for its own sake, enough has been said. Now it seems we must speak of that in which advantage too is added, which we nevertheless call honorable."

**31. 3.** "There are, then, many things that draw us both by their worth and by their own fruit. In this kind are glory, dignity, eminence, and friendship.

Glory is frequent report about someone with praise; dignity is someone's honorable authority, worthy of regard and honor and respect.

Eminence is a great abundance of power or majesty or of certain resources; friendship is the will toward someone for the sake of good things, for the sake of that very one whom one loves, together with his equal will. Here, because we are speaking of civil matters, we add fruit to friendship, so that it may seem also to be sought for the sake of these, lest perhaps those who suppose we are speaking of friendship in general should find fault.

Although there are some who think friendship is to be sought only on account of advantage, some who think it is to be sought for its own sake alone, and some both for its own sake and on account of advantage. Which of these is to be settled most truly will be matter for another place to consider."
