# Whether Truth Can Be Perceived by the Senses of the Body

**9.** Everything that a bodily sense reaches, which is also called sensible, is changed without any interval of time; as when the hairs of our head grow, or the body inclines toward old age or blossoms into youth, this happens perpetually and does not at all cease happening. But what does not remain cannot be perceived; for that is perceived which is comprehended by knowledge, and what is changed without interval cannot be comprehended. The purity of truth is not, therefore, to be expected from the senses of the body.

But lest anyone say that there are some sensible things that remain always in the same way, and bring forward to us the question of the sun and the stars, in which the point cannot easily be carried, there is certainly no one who is not compelled to admit this: that there is nothing sensible which does not have something like the false, so that it cannot be told apart.

For, to pass over other things, all the things we sense through the body—even when they are not present to the senses—we nevertheless undergo images of them as though they were altogether present, whether in sleep or in madness; and when we undergo this, we are wholly unable to discern whether we sense those things by the senses themselves or whether they are images of sensible things.

If, therefore, there are false images of sensible things which cannot be told apart by the senses themselves, and nothing can be perceived except what is distinguished from the false, then the judgment of truth is not established in the senses.

Therefore we are most healthfully admonished to turn away from this world, which is assuredly corporeal and sensible, and to turn, with all eagerness, toward God—that is, toward the truth that is grasped by the intellect and the inner mind, which always remains and is of one same kind, which has no image of the false from which it cannot be distinguished.
