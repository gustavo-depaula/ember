# On what is written: — O man, who are you to answer back to God?

**68. 1.** Since the Apostle seems to have rebuked the inquisitive by saying, *O man, who are you to answer back to God?*[^1] they raise a question about this very thing, and they do not cease to be inquisitive about the very statement by which inquisitiveness itself was reproved—the impious indeed doing so with insult, so that they say the Apostle failed in resolving the question and reproved those asking because he could not expound what was asked.

But certain heretics, who deceive only when they promise a knowledge they do not display, and who, opposing the Law and the Prophets, charge that whatever the Apostle inserted into his discourse from them is false and inserted by corrupters, have preferred to number even this among the things they say were interpolated and to deny that Paul said, *O man, who are you to answer back to God?* since, if this is said to them as they slander in order to deceive people, they will doubtless fall silent, and will not dare to promise any knowledge concerning the will of almighty God to the unskilled whom they desire to deceive.

But some, reading the Scriptures with a good and pious mind, ask what can here be answered to either the slanderers or the calumniators. But let us, both wholesomely clinging to the apostolic authority and in no way supposing that the books which Catholic teaching guards have been falsified, hold what is true: that those for whom these things are closed are unworthy and too weak to understand the divine secrets,

and to those who murmur and are indignant that they do not learn the counsels of God, when they have begun to say, *therefore he has mercy on whom he wills, and whom he wills he hardens. Why does he still complain? For who resists his will?*[^2]: when, then, with these words they have begun either to slander the Scriptures or to seek a hiding place for their sins, so as to despise the precepts by which they are invited to a good life, let us answer most confidently: *O man, who are you to answer back to God*?,

and let us not, out of deference to them, give what is holy to dogs or cast our pearls before swine[^3]—if indeed we ourselves are not now dogs and swine; and concerning the merits of souls, with the Holy Spirit revealing it, let us conjecture something sublime and most remote from common surmise, even if only in part and in a riddle.

**68. 2.** For the Apostle in this passage did not forbid the saints from asking, but those who are not yet rooted and founded in love, so as to be able to comprehend with all the saints the breadth, length, height, and depth, and the other things he sets forth in that same passage[^4]. He did not, then, forbid from asking those of whom he says: *but the spiritual man judges all things, but he himself is judged by no one*[^5]*;* and especially that: *but we have not received the spirit of this world, but the Spirit who is from God, that we may know the things that have been given to us by God*[^6]*.*

Whom, then, did he forbid except the muddy and earthly, who, not yet inwardly regenerated and nourished, bear the image of that man who was first made of earth, earthly[^7], and who, because he was unwilling to obey him by whom he was made, fell into that from which he was made, and deserved after his sin to hear: *you are earth, and into the earth you shall go*[^8]?

To such people, therefore, the Apostle says: *O man, who are you to answer back to God? Does the thing formed say to him who formed it, 'why have you made me thus?'*[^9] As long, then, as you are a thing formed, not yet a perfect son—because you have not yet drawn in the fullest grace by which power was given to us to become sons of God[^10], by which you might be able to hear, *now I will no longer call you slaves but friends*[^11]*;*

*who are you to answer back to God*, and to wish to know the counsel of God, you who, if you had wished to know the counsel of a man your equal, would act imprudently unless you were first received into friendship? Therefore, just as we have borne the image of the earthly, let us bear also the image of the heavenly[^12], putting off the old man and putting on the new[^13], so that it may not be said to us, as to a muddy thing formed: *does the thing formed say to him who formed it, 'why have you made me thus?'*[^14]

**68. 3.** And so that it may be manifest that these things are said not to the sanctified spirit but to the carnal mud, see what follows: *or does the potter of the clay not have power to make from the same lump one vessel for honor, another for dishonor?*[^15] Ever since, then, our nature sinned in paradise, we are formed by that same divine providence, by mortal generation, not according to heaven but according to earth—that is, not according to the spirit but according to the flesh—and we have all been made one lump of mud, which is the lump of sin.

Since, then, we have lost merit by sinning, and, the mercy of God removed, nothing else is owed to sinners but eternal condemnation, what does a man of this lump mean by answering God and saying, *why have you made me thus?*

If you wish to know these things, do not be mud, but become a son of God through the mercy of him who gave power to become sons of God to those believing in his name—but not, as you desire, to those wishing to know divine things before they believe. For the reward of knowledge is rendered to merits; but merit is acquired by believing. And the grace itself, which was given through faith, was given with no merits of ours preceding. For what is the merit of a sinner and an impious man? But Christ died for the impious and for sinners[^16], so that we might be called to believe not by merit but by grace, and by believing might also lay up merit.

Sinners, therefore, are commanded to believe, so that by believing they may be purified from sins. For they do not know what they are to see by living rightly. For this reason, since they cannot see unless they live rightly, and cannot live rightly unless they believe, it is manifest that one must begin from faith, so that the precepts by which believers are turned away from this world may make the heart clean, where God can be seen. For *blessed are the clean of heart, for they shall see God*[^17]*;* and it is sung through the prophet: *in simplicity of heart seek him*[^18]*.*

For this reason it is rightly said to people remaining in the oldness of life and therefore bearing a darkened eye of the soul: *O man, who are you to answer back to God? Does the thing formed say to him who formed it, 'why have you made me thus?' Or does the potter of the clay not have power to make from the same lump one vessel for honor, another for dishonor?*

*Purge out the old leaven, that you may be a new lump*[^19], and in that very lump be not still a little one in Christ, to be given milk to drink[^20], but come to the perfect man, so that you may be among those of whom it is said: *we speak wisdom among the perfect*[^21]*.*

Then at last you will hear rightly, and not in the wrong order, whatever there is concerning the most hidden merits of souls and concerning the secret grace or justice of almighty God.

**68. 4.** For about Pharaoh the answer is easy: by his prior merits, by which he afflicted strangers in his kingdom, he was made worthy that his heart should be hardened, so that he would not believe even at the most manifest signs of the commanding God.

From the same lump, therefore—that is, of sinners—he brought forth both vessels of mercy, with which he might come to aid when the sons of Israel besought him, and vessels of wrath, by whose punishment he might instruct them, that is, Pharaoh and his people,

because, although both were sinners and therefore belonged to one lump, those who groaned to the one God, that he might come to their aid, were nevertheless to be treated differently from those under whose unjust burdens they had groaned.

*He endured,* therefore, *with much patience the vessels of wrath, which are fitted for destruction*[^22].

And by the very fact that he says *with much patience,* he sufficiently signified their prior sins, in which he endured them, so that he might take vengeance at the opportune moment, when by their vengeance aid was to be brought to those who were to be freed. *And that he might make known the riches of his glory upon the vessels of mercy, which he prepared for glory*[^23]*.*

Here, perhaps disturbed, you return to that question: *he has mercy on whom he wills, and whom he wills he hardens; why does he still complain? For who resists his will?*[^24] Indeed, *he has mercy on whom he wills and whom he wills he hardens*; but this will of God cannot be unjust.

For it comes from the most hidden merits, because even sinners themselves, though by the general sin they make one lump, nevertheless there is among them no lack of diversity.

Something therefore goes before in sinners by which, though they are not yet justified, they are made worthy of justification; and likewise something goes before in other sinners by which they are worthy of being dulled.

You have the same Apostle saying elsewhere: *since they did not see fit to have God in their knowledge, God gave them up to a reprobate mind*[^25]. That he gave them up to a reprobate mind is the same as that he hardened the heart of Pharaoh[^26]; but that they did not see fit to have God in their knowledge is the same as that they showed themselves worthy to be given up to a reprobate mind.

**68. 5.** Yet it is true that *it is not of him who wills, nor of him who runs, but of God who shows mercy*[^27]. For even if anyone, by lighter sins—or certainly even by graver and many sins, yet by great groaning and grief of repenting—becomes worthy of God's mercy, this is not of him who, if he were abandoned, would perish, but of the merciful God who comes to the aid of his prayers and grief.

For it is too little to will, unless God has mercy; but God, who calls to peace, does not have mercy unless the will has gone before, because *on earth peace to men of good will*[^28]. And since no one can even will unless he has been admonished and called—whether inwardly, where no man sees, or outwardly through a resounding word or some visible signs—it comes about that God works even the willing itself in us[^29].

For to that supper which the Lord says in the Gospel was prepared, not all who were called wished to come, nor could those who came have come unless they were called. And so neither should those who came attribute it to themselves, because they came when called; nor should those who refused to come attribute it to another, but only to themselves, since, that they might come, they had been called in free will[^30].

The calling, therefore, works the will before merit. For this reason, even if anyone attributes to himself that he came when called, he cannot attribute to himself that he was called.

But he who, called, does not come—just as he had no merit of reward in being called, so he begins to merit punishment when, called, he neglected to come. So there will be those two things: *mercy and judgment I will sing to you, Lord*[^31]*.*

To mercy belongs the calling; to judgment belong the blessedness of those who came when called and the punishment of those who refused to come. Was it then hidden from Pharaoh how much good those lands had obtained through the coming of Joseph[^32]?

The knowledge of that deed performed, therefore, was his calling, that, treating the people of Israel mercifully, he should not be ungrateful. But because he was unwilling to obey this calling, and exercised cruelty against those to whom kindness and mercy were owed, he deserved a penalty: that his heart should be hardened and that he should suffer so great a blindness of mind as not to believe at so many and so great and so manifest signs of God,

so that by his punishment—whether of hardening or of the final visible drowning—the people could be instructed, by whose affliction he himself acquired the merit both of the hidden dulling and of the manifest drowning[^33].

**68. 6.** Now this calling, which works—whether in individual people or in peoples and in the human race itself—through the opportunities of times, belongs to a high and deep ordering. To this pertains also that: *in the womb I sanctified you*[^34]; and: *when you were in the loins of your father, I saw you*[^35]; and: *Jacob I loved, but Esau I hated*[^36]: since it was said before they were born.

Nor can it be comprehended except perhaps by those who love the Lord their God with their whole heart and with their whole soul and with their whole mind, and love their neighbor as themselves[^37]. For, founded in so great a love, they can now perhaps comprehend with the saints the length, breadth, height, and depth[^38].

This, however, must be held by a most constant faith: that God does nothing unjustly, and that there is no nature which does not owe to God what it is—because to God is owed all comeliness and beauty and fittingness of parts, which, if you have followed it through thoroughly, and have removed even down to all the remnants from things, nothing remains.
