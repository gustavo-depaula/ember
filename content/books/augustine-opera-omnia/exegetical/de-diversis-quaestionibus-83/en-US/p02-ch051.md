# On Man Made to the Image and Likeness of God

**51. 1.** Since Divine Scripture mentions the outer and the inner man and distinguishes them so far that it was said by the Apostle, *and although our outer man is being corrupted, yet the inner one is being renewed day by day*[^1], it can be asked which of these was made to the image and likeness of God; for to ask this is foolish, if one was, which of them it was.

For who doubts that one should say it is rather he who is renewed than he who is corrupted? But whether both were is a great question. For if the outer man is Adam and the inner is Christ, both are well understood. But since Adam did not remain good, as he was made by God, and, by loving carnal things, was made carnal, it can seem not absurd that this very thing was for him to fall, namely to lose the image and likeness of God. And through this, he himself is renewed and he himself is also the inner man.

How, then, is he himself also the outer man? Is it according to the body, so that he is inner according to the soul, and there is a resurrection and renewal of the inner man, which now takes place according to the death of the former life, that is, of sin, and according to the regeneration of the new life, that is, of justice?

These same two men he likewise so names that he mentions one as old, whom we ought to put off, the other as new, and that one as to be put on[^2]. Of these, again, he calls the one *the image of the earthly man*, because it is conducted according to the sin of the first man, who is Adam, and the other *the image of the heavenly man*[^3], because it is conducted according to the justice of the second man, who is Jesus Christ. But the outer man, who is now being corrupted, will be renewed in the future resurrection, when he has paid off that death which he owes to nature by that law which was given in paradise through the precept.

**51. 2.** But how it is not incongruous that the body too is said to have been made to the likeness of God, one easily understands who diligently attends to what was said: *and God made all things very good*[^4]; for no one doubts that he himself is primordially good.

For in many ways things can be said to be like God: some made according to virtue and wisdom, because in him is virtue and wisdom not made; others insofar as they only live, because he supremely and primordially lives; others insofar as they are, because he supremely and primordially is.

And therefore those things which only are, and yet neither live nor are wise, are to his likeness not perfectly but slightly, because they too are good in their own order, since he is good above all things, from whom good things come.

But all things that live and are not wise participate the likeness a little more. For what lives also is, but not whatever is also lives. Now further, the things that are wise are so near to that likeness that among creatures nothing is nearer. For what participates wisdom both lives and is, but what lives must of necessity be, yet need not be wise. Wherefore, since man can be a partaker of wisdom according to the inner man, according to that inner man he is so to the image that he is formed with no nature interposed, and therefore nothing is more conjoined to God. For he is wise and lives and is, than which creature nothing is better.

**51. 3.** But if the outer man is taken to be that life by which we sense through the body with the five most familiar senses, which we have in common with the beasts—for it too can be corrupted by the sensible troubles which are brought on by persecutions—not undeservedly is this man too said to be a partaker of the likeness of God, not only because he lives, which appears even in beasts, but further because he turns toward the mind that rules him, which wisdom illumines, which cannot happen in the beasts, who lack reason.

The body of man too, because alone among the bodies of earthly animals it is not laid prostrate face-down upon the belly, since it is visible and erect for gazing at the heaven, which is the beginning of visible things—although it is known to live not by its own presence but by that of the soul—nevertheless, not only because it is, and insofar as it is, is surely good, but also because it is such that it is more fit for contemplating the heaven, it can rightly be seen to have been made more to the likeness of God than the other bodies of animals.

Yet because a human being is not rightly called such without life, the outer man is perhaps more rightly understood to be neither the body alone nor the life alone, which is in the sense of the body, but both together.

**51. 4.** Nor is it unskillfully distinguished that the image and likeness of God, who is also called the Son, is one thing, and that to the image and likeness of God, as we understand man to have been made, is another. There are also those who not without reason understand that two things were said, "to the image and likeness," when, they assert, if it were one thing, one name could have sufficed.

But they hold that the mind was made to the image, which, with no substance interposed, is formed by truth itself, and which is also called spirit—not that Holy Spirit who is of the same substance as the Father and the Son, but the spirit of man.

For the Apostle thus distinguishes these: *no one knows what takes place in a man except the spirit of the man, and no one knows what takes place in God except the Spirit of God*[^5]. Likewise, of the spirit of man he says: *may he keep your spirit and soul and body*[^6].

For this too was made by God, as also the rest of creation. For it is written in *Proverbs* in this way: *know that the Lord knows the hearts of all, and he who fashioned the spirit for all, he himself knows all things*[^7].

Therefore this spirit, with no one doubting, is taken to have been made to the image of God—the spirit in which is the understanding of truth; for it clings to truth with no creature interposed. The rest of man they hold to have been made to the likeness, because every image is indeed like, but not everything that is like is also properly an image, though perhaps it can be so called by a misuse of the word.

But in such matters one must take care that nothing be thought to need too much asserting—that point being, of course, healthfully kept, lest, because any body whatever is extended through local spaces, something of the kind be believed to be the substance of God. For a thing which is smaller in a part than in the whole does not befit the dignity of the soul, much less the majesty of God.
