# On the Gold and Silver That the Israelites Received from the Egyptians

**53. 1.** Whoever diligently beholds the dispensations of the two Testaments, distributed according to the fittingness of the times to the ages of the human race, understands well enough, as I think, what befits the earlier age of the human race and what the later.

For, divine providence beautifully ordering all things, the whole series of generations from Adam to the end of the world is so administered as that of a single man, prolonging his time from boyhood to old age and marking it off by the stages of age.

And therefore one who directs a devout mind to the divine readings ought to distinguish the stages of the virtues too in morals, until one comes to the highest and perfect virtue of man, lest perhaps, when he finds that at one time small things are commanded to the small, at another time greater things to the greater, reckoning that, in comparison with the greater, those which are lesser are sins, he should think it unbecoming that God should command such things to men.

But to discuss now the stages of the virtues would be too long; nevertheless, what is enough for examining the present question: as far as deceiving is concerned, the highest and perfect virtue is to deceive no one and to display what was said, *let it be on your lips: yes, yes; no, no*[^1].

But because this was commanded to those to whom the kingdom of heaven has already been promised—and it is a great virtue to fulfill these greater things, to whom this reward is owed, for *the kingdom of heaven suffers violence, and those who do violence seize it*[^2]—it must be asked by what stages one comes to that summit and perfection.

Among which stages, at any rate, are found those to whom as yet an earthly kingdom was being promised, by which promise, as little ones, they might play their first part, and, having for the time obtained from the one God, who is the Lord of all, the earthly joys for which they still gaped, advancing thence and growing in spirit, they might dare even to hope for heavenly things.

As, therefore, it is the highest and almost divine virtue to deceive no one, so it is the lowest vice to deceive anyone whatever. For those tending from this lowest vice toward that highest virtue, there is a stage: to deceive indeed no one, whether friend or stranger, but yet at some time an enemy.

Whence that saying, too, uttered by the poet, has by now nearly obtained the usage of a proverb: "Deceit or valor, who would ask in an enemy?"

But since the enemy himself can for the most part be deceived unjustly, as when some pact is made concerning a temporal peace, which they call a truce, and faith is not kept, and other such things, much more purified and nearer to that highest virtue is he who, although he may wish to deceive an enemy, nevertheless does not deceive him except by divine authority. For God knows, either alone or certainly far more excellently and purely than men, by what punishment or reward each one is worthy.

**53. 2.** Wherefore God indeed deceives no one through himself; for he is the Father of Truth and the Truth and the Spirit of Truth. Yet, distributing worthy things to the worthy—since this too pertains to justice and truth—he uses souls according to their merits and dignities, which are in their stages,

so that, if anyone is worthy to be deceived, he both does not deceive him through himself, nor through such a man as already fittingly loves and persists in keeping *let it be on your lips: yes, yes; no, no*[^3], nor through an angel to whom the role of deceit does not belong, but either through such a man as has not yet stripped himself of desires of this kind, or through such an angel as, by the perversity of his own will, has been ordered, for the punishment of sins or for the training and purification of those who are reborn according to God, among the lowest stages of nature.

For we read that a king was deceived by the false prophesying of false prophets, and we read it in such a way that we find it was not done without divine judgment, since he was worthy to be thus deceived, nor through that angel whom the office of deception would not befit, but through the angel of error, who of his own accord asked, with gladness, that such a part be laid upon him[^4].

For in certain places of the Scriptures something is set forth more openly, that the diligent and devout reader may understand it also in other places where it is less openly disclosed. For our God so regulated the divine books by the Holy Spirit, for the salvation of souls, that he willed not only to feed us with manifest things but also to train us with obscure ones.

From this ineffable and sublime administration of things, then, which takes place through divine providence, the natural law has been, as it were, transcribed into the rational soul, so that in the very conduct of this life and in earthly morals men preserve images of such distributions.

Hence it is that a judge thinks it unworthy of his person, and wicked, to strike a condemned man; yet at his command the executioner does this, who, according to his own desire, has been so ordered in his office that he strikes, by the regulation of the laws, a condemned man—he who could even strike an innocent one out of his own cruelty.

For the judge does this neither through himself nor through a magistrate or an advocate or anyone in his office on whom such a ministry is not fittingly laid. Hence it is also that we use irrational living beings for those things which it is wicked to have done through men.

For surely a thief is worthy to be torn by a bite; yet a man does this not through himself or through his son or a member of his household or even through his servant, but through a dog, which beast it befits to do such things according to the stages of nature.

Since, then, it befits certain ones to suffer something which it does not befit others to do, there are certain intermediate ministries to which worthy offices are enjoined, so that justice itself, using them, both commands each to suffer such things as it befits him to suffer, and also through those doing them whom it no less befits to do such things.

Wherefore, since both the Egyptians were worthy of deception, and the people of Israel, for that age of the human race, were as yet established in such a stage of morals that they would not unworthily deceive an enemy,

it came about that God commanded—or rather, in accordance with their desire, permitted—that the vessels of gold and silver, for which the seekers of an earthly kingdom still gaped, they should both ask of the Egyptians not meaning to return them and receive as though meaning to return them[^5].

And God willed this both to be a not unjust wage, according to the stage of such souls, for so long a labor and work, and a punishment of those whom he justly caused to lose what they ought to have returned.

God, therefore, is not a deceiver—which who would not understand to be wicked and impious to believe?—but a most just distributor of merits and persons, doing certain things through himself which are worthy of him alone and befit him alone, such as to illumine souls and, by offering himself to them for their full enjoyment, to render them wise and happy,

and other things through the creature serving him, ordered by most upright laws according to merits, commanding some of those things, permitting some, divine providence reaching out and coming even to the administration of sparrows, as the Lord says in the Gospel, and even to the comeliness of the grass, even to the number of our hairs[^6]. Of which it is also said: *it reaches mightily from end to end, and orders all things sweetly*[^7].

**53. 3.** But that God punishes through the ministries of souls serving his laws, and repays worthy punishments to the worthy, while he himself remains most tranquil, is written most openly thus:

*to condemn even him who ought not to be punished, you reckon foreign to your power. For your power is the beginning of justice; and for this, that you are the Lord of all, you make yourself spare all. For you show your power, you who are not believed to be consummate in power, and among those who know it you expose audacity. But you, Lord of powers, judge with tranquillity, and with great reverence order us*[^8].

**53. 4.** Likewise, that at first, in earthly matters, a stage is made toward heavenly justice, which is now commanded to the stronger, the Lord shows when he says: *if you have not been faithful in what is another's, who will give you what is your own?*[^9] And that souls are taught according to their stages, the Lord himself also demonstrates, saying: *I have many things to say to you, but now you cannot bear them*[^10];

and the Apostle when he says: *and I, brothers, could not speak to you as to spiritual men, but as to carnal. I gave you milk to drink, not food; for you were not able, and not even yet are you able, for you are still carnal*[^11].

What, therefore, was done in these according to their stages, this we recognize is done in the whole human race, so that some things were commanded to the carnal people, others to the spiritual, according to the fittingness of the times.

It is no wonder, then, if those who were as yet worthy to deceive an enemy were commanded to deceive an enemy worthy to be deceived. For they were not yet fit to be told, *love your enemies,* but were such that only this had to be said to them: *you shall love your neighbor and hate your enemy*[^12].

For as yet how broadly that *neighbor* was to be understood was not for that time to deliver. A certain beginning, then, was made under the guardian, so that perfection might be kept for the master, while nevertheless the same God both gave the guardian to the little ones—namely that Law through his servant—and the master to the more grown, that is, the Gospel through his Only One.
