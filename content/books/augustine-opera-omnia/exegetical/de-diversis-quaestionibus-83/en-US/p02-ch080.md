# Against the Apollinarists

**80. 1.** When certain heretics—who are said to be called Apollinarists from a certain Apollinaris, their authority—asserted that our Lord Jesus Christ, insofar as he deigned to become a man, did not have a human mind, some, clinging to them and eagerly listening to them, were indeed delighted by that perversity by which he diminished the man in God, saying that he did not have a mind, that is, a rational soul, by which a man differs from the beasts in respect of the mind.

But when they themselves considered within themselves that, if it is so, it must be confessed that the only-begotten Son of God, the Wisdom and Word of the Father, through whom all things were made, is believed to have taken on a certain beast with the figure of a human body, they were displeased with themselves,

not, however, unto correction, so that they would return to the way of truth and confess that the whole man was taken on by the Wisdom of God, with no diminution of nature,

but, using a greater audacity, alienating from him the soul itself and the whole vital part of the man, they said that he took on only human flesh, even adducing a testimony from the Gospel—rather, perverse by not understanding that statement, they dare to contend against the Catholic truth, saying it is written: *the Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us*[^1]*.* For under these words they wish the Word to be so joined and grown together with the flesh that there is there not only no mind, but not even a human soul.

**80. 2.** To whom it must first be answered that this was so placed in the Gospel because that assumption of human nature made by the Lord extended as far as the visible flesh, and in that whole unity of the taking on the Word is principal, while the flesh is the extreme and last.

And so the Evangelist, wishing to commend for us the abasement of the humility of God, who humbled himself, and expressing how far he humbled himself, named the Word and the flesh, passing over the nature of the soul, which is inferior to the Word, more excellent than the flesh. For it commends humility more that it was said, *the Word was made flesh,* than if it were said, "the Word was made man."

For if these words are regarded too closely, another, no less perverse, can so slander our faith from these words as to say that the Word itself was converted and changed into flesh and ceased to be the Word, because it is written: *the Word was made flesh,*

just as human flesh, when it becomes ash, is not flesh and ash, but ash from flesh, and, according to the more common usage of speaking, whatever comes to be that was not, ceases to be what it was. Yet we do not so understand these words. But they too understand with us in such a way that, the Word remaining what it is, it was said, *the Word was made flesh,* from the fact that it received the form of a servant, not from the fact that it was converted into that form by some change.

Then, if wherever flesh is named and the soul is passed over in silence, it must be so understood that the soul is believed not to be there, then those of whom it was said will not have a soul: *and all flesh will see the salvation of God*[^2]*;* and that in the *Psalm*: *hear my prayers; to you all flesh will come*[^3]*;* and that in the Gospel: *as you have given him power over all flesh, so that all that you have given him may not perish but have eternal life*[^4].

From which it is understood that men are accustomed to be signified by the naming of the flesh alone, so that according to this manner of speaking that too can be understood, so that what was said, *the Word was made flesh,* is nothing other than: "the Word was made man." For just as, by the whole from a part, a man is generally understood when the soul alone is named, as is that: *so many souls went down into Egypt*[^5]*,* so again, by the whole from a part, a man is understood even when the flesh alone is named, as are those passages that we have set down.

**80. 3.** Accordingly, just as we answer this objection of theirs, which they put forward from the Gospel, in such a way that no man is so foolish as to think that by these words we are compelled to believe and confess that the Mediator of God and men, the man Christ Jesus[^6], did not have a human soul, so I ask how they answer our objections, so manifest, by which we show that, in innumerable places of the gospel Scripture, it was narrated of him by the evangelists that he was in those affections which cannot exist without a soul.

For I do not bring these forth from myself, but the things which the Lord himself recalls so often: *my soul is sorrowful unto death*[^7]*;* and: *I have the power to lay down my soul and to take it up again*[^8]*;* and: *no one has greater love than to lay down his soul for his friends*[^9]*.*

These an obstinate contradictor can tell me were said figuratively by the Lord, just as it is manifest that he spoke many things in parables. For even if these are not so, there is nevertheless no need to act contentiously, where we have the narratives of the evangelists, through which we have come to know both that he was born of the Virgin Mary, and seized by the Jews, and scourged and crucified and killed, and buried in a tomb—all of which no one can understand to have been done without a body.

Nor would anyone, even the most demented, say that these are to be taken figuratively, since they were said by those who narrated the deeds done as they remembered them. Therefore, just as these things testify that he had a body, so those affections, which cannot exist except in a soul, indicate that he had a soul—affections which we nonetheless read of, the same evangelists narrating them.

And Jesus marveled[^10] and was angry[^11] and was saddened[^12] and was gladdened[^13] and innumerable other things, as also those which show duties joined together both of the soul and of the body: such as that he hungered[^14], that he slept[^15], that, wearied from the journey, he sat down[^16], and other things of this kind. For they cannot say that in the Old Books too the anger of God and joy and certain motions of this kind are spoken of, and yet that it does not follow that God must be believed to have had a soul.

For those things were said by prophetic imaginings, not by a narrative manifestation.

For the members of God too are spoken of, both hands and feet and eyes and face and the like; just as these do not indicate that he has a body, so neither do those indicate a soul. But just as something narrated, where the hands of Christ and his head and the rest are named, indicates his body, so too the things named, in the same tenor of narration, concerning the affections of the soul indicate his soul.

But it is foolish to believe the evangelist who narrates that he ate, and not to believe him that he hungered. For although it does not follow that everyone who eats hungers—for we read that an angel ate[^17], but we do not read that it hungered—nor that everyone who hungers eats, if he either restrains himself by some duty, or food and the means of eating are lacking to him, nevertheless, when the evangelist narrates both[^18], both must be believed; because he wrote both, as an indicator of deeds done, as having been done and performed. But just as, because he ate, it cannot be understood without a body, so, because he hungered, it could not have come about without a soul.

**80. 4.** Nor does that empty and inept slander frighten us, by which, resisting enviously, they say: therefore he was placed under necessity, if he had these affections of the soul truly. For we easily answer: therefore he was placed under necessity, because he was seized, scourged, crucified, and died,

so that at last, without obstinacy, if they are willing, they may understand that he took on the passions of the soul—that is, the affections—by the will of the dispensation, yet true ones, as it pleased him, in the same way that he took on the passions of the body by the same will of the dispensation, without any necessity.

Just as we do not die by will, so too we are not born by will; but he displayed both, as it was fitting, by will, and yet displayed them most truly.

Just as, then, by the name of necessity no one tears either us or them away from faith in the most true passion, through which his body is shown, so by the very name of necessity let no one frighten us away from faith in the most true affection, through which we recognize his soul—nor ought it to frighten them away from consenting to the Catholic faith, if the ruinous shame of changing an opinion, though false, yet long and rashly defended, does not frighten them away.
