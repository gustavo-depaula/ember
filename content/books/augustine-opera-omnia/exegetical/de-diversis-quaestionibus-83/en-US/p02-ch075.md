# On the Inheritance of God

**75. 1.** As the Apostle says to the Hebrews: *a testament is made firm by the death of the testator*[^1]; for this reason he asserts that, Christ having died for us, the New Testament was made firm, of which the Old Testament was a likeness, in which the death of the testator was prefigured by the victim. If, then, it is asked how we are, according to the words of the same Apostle, *coheirs of Christ and sons and heirs of God*[^2], when an inheritance too is held firm by the death of the one who departs, nor can an inheritance be understood in any other way: it is answered that we were indeed made heirs by his death, since we are also called his sons: *the sons of the bridegroom do not fast,* he says, *as long as the bridegroom is with them*[^3]*.*

We are therefore called his heirs, because he left us the possession of the Church's peace through faith in the temporal dispensation, which we possess in this life, which he attested when he said: *my peace I give to you, peace I leave to you*[^4]*.* But we shall be made his coheirs when, at the end of the age, *death will be swallowed up in victory*[^5]. For then *we shall be like him, because we shall see him as he is*[^6]*.* This inheritance we do not obtain by the death of that Father who cannot die, since indeed he himself becomes our inheritance, according to what is written: *the Lord is the portion of my inheritance*[^7],

but because, when we were called, while still little ones and less fit for contemplating spiritual things, the divine mercy extended itself even to our lowliest thoughts, so that, however we might strive to discern what we did not discern evidently and clearly, that very thing which we discerned in a riddle dies when we have begun to discern face to face. For what will be taken away is fittingly said to be about to die: *but when what is perfect comes, what is in part will be taken away*[^8]. So in a way the Father dies for us in the riddle, and the same Father becomes our inheritance, when he is seen face to face—not because he himself dies, but because our imperfect vision of him is destroyed by the perfect vision; and yet, unless that prior vision had nourished us, we would not be made fit for the other, fullest and most evident one.

**75. 2.** But if a pious understanding admits this even of the Lord Jesus Christ—not according to the Word who in the beginning was God with God[^9], but according to the boy who advanced in age and wisdom[^10], that proper taking on being preserved, which he does not have in common with other men—by whose death, as it were, he possesses an inheritance, it is manifest. For we cannot be his coheirs unless he himself is an heir. But if piety does not admit this—that the Lord's manhood should see first in part, then wholly, although he was said to advance in wisdom—let the heir be understood to be in his body, that is, in the Church, of which we are coheirs, just as we are called the sons of his mother, although it is composed of us.

**75. 3.** But again it can be asked by whose death we too were made the inheritance of God, according to that: *I will give you the nations as your inheritance*[^11], unless perhaps the death of this world, by which we were formerly held as by a master. But afterward, when we say, *the world is crucified to me, and I to the world*[^12], Christ possesses us, the one who possessed us having died; when we renounce it, we die to it and it to us.
