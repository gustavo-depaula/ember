# On What Is Written in the Gospel, — That the Lord Fed the Crowds on the Mountain with Five Loaves

**61. 1.** The five barley loaves with which the Lord fed the crowds on the mountain signify the old Law—whether because it was given not yet to spiritual but still to carnal men, that is, to those given over to the five senses of the body (for the crowds themselves were five thousand men[^1]), or because the Law itself was given through Moses; for Moses wrote five books.

And that the loaves were of barley signifies well either the Law itself, which was given in such a way that in it the vital nourishment of the soul was covered over by corporeal sacraments—for the pith of barley is covered by a most clinging husk—or the very people not yet stripped of carnal desire, which clung to their heart as a husk, that is, not yet circumcised in heart, so that not even by the threshing of tribulations, when they were led through the deserts for forty years, did they, the understanding being revealed, lay aside the carnal coverings, just as neither is barley stripped of that husky covering by the threshing of the threshing-floor. And so to that people a Law of such a kind was fittingly given.

**61. 2.** But the two fish, which gave a pleasant savor to the bread, seem to signify those two persons by whom that people was ruled, so that through them it might receive the regulation of its counsels, namely the royal and the priestly, to which that most sacred anointing also pertained.

Whose office it was never to be broken and corrupted by popular storms and waves, and often to break through the violent contradictions of the crowds as opposing waves, sometimes, their own integrity being kept, to yield to them—in short, after the manner of fish, to move about, as in a stormy sea, so in the turbulent administration of the people.

Yet these two persons prefigured our Lord; for he alone bore both, and fulfilled them, alone, not figuratively but properly. For Jesus Christ is also our king, who showed us an example of fighting and conquering, taking up our sins in mortal flesh, yielding to the enemy's temptations, neither the alluring nor the terrible ones, and at last putting off the flesh, despoiling the principalities and powers, confidently, and triumphing over them in himself[^2].

And so, with him as leader, we are freed from the burdens and labors of this our pilgrimage as from Egypt, and the sins pursuing us are overwhelmed as we escape by the sacrament of Baptism; and as long as we are in the hope of his promise which we do not yet see, we are led as through the deserts, the word of God consoling us in the Holy Scriptures as the manna from heaven did them.

With this same leader we presume that we can be brought into the heavenly Jerusalem as into the land of promise, and, with him reigning and guarding there forever, be preserved. Thus our Lord Jesus Christ is shown to be our king. He is also our priest forever according to the order of Melchizedek[^3], who offered himself as a holocaust for our sins, and commended the likeness of that sacrifice to be celebrated in memory of his passion, so that what Melchizedek offered to God[^4] we now see offered throughout the world in the Church of Christ.

Therefore, since our king took up our sins, that he might show us an example of fighting and conquering, the Evangelist Matthew, signifying the taking up of those same sins and the royal person, taking up his generation which is according to the flesh from Abraham, who is the father of the faithful people, and enumerating the succession of offspring downward, comes to David, in whom the establishment of the kingdom appears most manifest, and thence, through Solomon, born of her with whom his father had sinned, pursuing the royal stock, brings it down to the generation of the Lord[^5].

But Luke, the other Evangelist, since he too undertook to set out the generation of the Lord which is according to the flesh, but in the priestly person, to which person belongs the cleansing and abolition of sins, begins, not from the beginning of the book as Matthew does, but from that place where Jesus was baptized, where he prefigured the cleansing of our sins, to pursue step by step the origin of his parents—and not downward, like the one who showed him as descending to the taking up of sins, but upward, like one who presented him as ascending after the abolition of sins, nor naming the same parents as the other[^6].

For there was another, priestly origin, which had brought it about, through one of the sons of David obtaining, as is customary, a marriage from the priestly tribe, that Mary derived her kinship from both tribes, that is, from the royal and from the priestly. For when Joseph and Mary were enrolled, it is written that they were of the house, that is, of the lineage of David[^7]. And Elizabeth, who is none the less written as a kinswoman of Mary, was of the priestly tribe[^8]. But just as Matthew, who presents Christ the king as descending to take up our sins, descends from David through Solomon, because Solomon was born of her with whom David had sinned, so Luke, who presents Christ the priest as ascending after the abolition of sins, ascends through Nathan to David, because Nathan was the prophet who had been sent, by whose rebuke David, by doing penance, obtained the abolition of his own sin[^9].

And so, after Luke has passed the person of David, he is not at variance with Matthew in the names of the generations. For he names, ascending from David to Abraham, those whom the other names descending from Abraham to David.

For from David that origin was distributed into two families, the royal and the priestly; of which two families, as was said, Matthew, descending, followed the royal, and Luke, ascending, the priestly, so that our Lord Jesus Christ, our king and priest, both derived kinship from the priestly stock and yet was not of the priestly tribe, that is, of the tribe of Levi, but was of the tribe of Judah, that is, of the tribe of David, from which tribe no one attends at the altar. Therefore he is especially called the son of David according to the flesh, because both Luke ascending and Matthew descending met each other in David.

For it was fitting that he who was to empty out the sacrifices which were made in the Levitical priesthood according to the order of Aaron should not be of the tribe of Levi, lest the cleansing of sins should seem to pertain to that tribe and to that priesthood, which was temporally a shadow of what was to come—the cleansing which the Lord fulfilled by the offering of his holocaust, which was figured in the old priesthood.

And he gave the image of his holocaust to be celebrated in the Church in memory of his passion, that he might be a priest forever, not according to the order of Aaron, but according to the order of Melchizedek[^10]. The mystery of this matter can be considered still more diligently; but on account of the two fish, in which we said that the two persons, the royal and the priestly, were figured, let it suffice that it has been treated thus far on this point.

**61. 3.** But that the crowd reclined upon the grass signifies that those who had received the Old Testament, because a temporal kingdom and a temporal Jerusalem was promised them, had been placed in a carnal hope; for *all flesh is grass, and the glory of man as the flower of grass*[^11]. But that from the remnants of the fragments twelve baskets of morsels were filled signifies that, from the opening up and discussion of the Law itself, which the Jews had left and deserted, the disciples of the Lord were filled, among whom the number twelve holds the chief place. For there was not yet a writing of the New Testament when the Lord, as it were by breaking and opening what was hard and closed in the Law, filled the disciples, when after the resurrection he opened to them the Old Scriptures, beginning from Moses and all the prophets, interpreting to them in all the Scriptures the things that were about himself. For then too two of them recognized him in the breaking of the bread[^12].

**61. 4.** And therefore the second feeding of the people, which was done with seven loaves, is rightly understood to pertain to the preaching of the New Testament. For it was not said by any evangelist that these loaves were of barley, as John said of those five. This feeding, then, with seven loaves, pertains to the grace of the Church, which is recognized by that most familiar sevenfold working of the Holy Spirit. And therefore here it is not written that there were two fish, as in the old Law, where two alone were anointed, the king and the priest, but a few fish, that is, those who first believed in the Lord Jesus Christ, and were anointed in his name, and sent to the preaching of the Gospel, and to enduring the turbulent sea of this world, so that they might act as ambassadors on behalf of that great fish himself, that is, on behalf of Christ, as the apostle Paul says[^13].

Nor in this crowd were there five thousand men, as there, where those receiving the Law carnally, that is, those given over to the five senses of the flesh, are signified, but rather four thousand, by which number the spiritual are signified, on account of the four virtues of the mind by which one lives spiritually in this life: prudence, temperance, fortitude, justice.

Of which the first is the knowledge of things to be sought and to be fled; the second, the restraining of desire from those things which delight temporally; the third, firmness of mind against those things which are temporally troublesome; the fourth, which is diffused through all the rest, the love of God and neighbor.

**61. 5.** Indeed, both there five thousand and here four thousand, apart from women and children, are recorded to have been[^14], which seems to me to pertain to this: that we may understand that even in the people of the Old Testament there were some weak in fulfilling the justice which is according to the law, in which justice the Apostle Paul says he conducted himself without blame[^15], and that there were likewise others who were easily seduced into the worship of idols.

Which two kinds, that is, of weakness and of error, are figured by the names of women and children. For the sex of women is weak for action, and childhood is prone to play. And what is so like childish play as to worship idols, since the Apostle too referred this kind of superstition to this, when he said: *nor let us serve idols as some of them did, as it is written, the people sat down to eat and drink and rose up to play*[^16]? Like women, then, were those who, in the labors of waiting until they should arrive at the promises of God, not perseveringly and manfully, tempted God; and like children those who sat down *to eat and drink and rose up to play*.

But not only there, but also in the people of the New Testament, those who do not persevere in arriving at the perfect man[^17], whether by weakness of strength or by lightness of mind, are to be compared to women and children.

For to the former it is said: *if indeed we hold firm to the end the beginning of his substance*[^18]; and to the latter: *do not become children in your senses, but be infants in malice, that you may be perfect in your senses*[^19].

And therefore neither in the Old nor in the New Testament are such admitted to the number; but, whether there five thousand or here four thousand, they are said to have been, apart from women and children[^20].

**61. 6.** But although both there and here, on account of Christ himself, who is constantly called a mountain in the Scriptures, each people was fittingly fed on the mountain, here, nevertheless, they do not recline on grass but on the ground.

For there the loftiness of Christ is, on account of carnal men and the earthly Jerusalem, covered with carnal hope and desire; but here, all carnal desire being removed, the firmness of the abiding hope of the New Testament, as the solidity of the mountain itself, held the guests with no grass interposed.

**61. 7.** And since the Apostle most rightly says, *but before faith came, we were guarded under the law*[^21], the Lord too seems to signify this when he says, of those whom he was about to feed with five loaves: *they have no need to go; but you, give them to eat*[^22].

For under these words they are figuratively detained, as it were to be guarded, when the disciples had admonished him to send them away. But of this crowd, which pertains to the seven loaves, he says that he of his own accord has mercy, because it was now the third day since they had clung to him fasting.

For in the whole age of the human race there is a third period, in which the grace of the Christian faith has been given. The first is before the Law, the second under the Law, the third under grace; and since a fourth period still remains, in which we are to come to the fullest peace of the heavenly Jerusalem—the goal toward which everyone strives who rightly believes in Christ—therefore the Lord says that he refreshes that crowd, lest they faint along the way.

For this dispensation, by which the Lord deigned to appear to us in time and visibly in a man and gave us the Holy Spirit as a pledge—by whose sevenfold working we might be sustained, with the apostolic authority joined to it like the flavor of a few fish—what else does this dispensation accomplish but that we may be able to reach the prize of the heavenly calling without any failing of our strength? *For we walk by faith, not by sight*[^23]*.*

And the Apostle Paul himself says that he has not yet laid hold of the kingdom of God: *but forgetting what lies behind and straining forward to what lies ahead, I press on,* he says, *toward the prize of the heavenly calling. Nevertheless, let us hold true to what we have attained*[^24]*,* because, clinging to the Lord on the third day and fed by him, we will not faint along the way.

**61. 8.** Here too, of course, an end to the eating could not be reached, but food was left over. For it is not in vain that it was said of the future: *when the Son of man comes, will he find faith on earth?*[^25] And I believe it will be so on account of the women and children. Yet the remains of the fragments filled seven baskets, to which the sevenfold Church—which is also described in the *Apocalypse*[^26]—belongs, that is, everyone who will have persevered to the end.

For he who said, *when the Son of man comes, will he find faith on earth?,* indicated indeed that at the end of the banquet his food could be left and abandoned; but since he likewise said, *he who perseveres to the end will be saved*[^27]*,* he indicated that there will be no lack of a Church that may more abundantly receive those same seven loaves in the number seven, and that may contain, in the breadth of its heart, that very perseverance which the baskets seem to signify.
