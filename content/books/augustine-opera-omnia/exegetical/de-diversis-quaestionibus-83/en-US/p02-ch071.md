# On what is written: Bear one another's burdens, — and so you will fulfill the law of Christ

**71. 1.** Because the observance of the Old Testament had fear, it could not be more openly signified that the gift of the New Testament is love than in this place, where the Apostle says: *bear one another's burdens, and so you will fulfill the law of Christ*. For he is understood to call this the law of Christ by which the Lord himself commanded that we love one another, placing such weight of precept in that statement that he said: *by this it will be known that you are my disciples, if you love one another*[^1].

And the duty of this love is to bear one another's burdens. But this duty, which is not everlasting, will indeed lead to everlasting blessedness, in which there will be no burdens of ours that we are commanded to bear for one another. But now, while we are in this life, that is, on this road, let us bear one another's burdens, so that we may be able to come to that life which is free of every burden.

For just as some who are devoted to such observations have written about deer, *when they cross a strait to an island for the sake of pasture, they arrange themselves so that they carry the burdens of their heads, which they bear in their horns, upon one another, in such a way that the one behind lays its head upon the neck of the one in front.*

*And because there must be one which, going before the rest, does not have before itself one on whom to incline its head, they are said to do this by turns, so that the one who goes in front, wearied by the burden of its own head, returns behind them all, and there succeeds it the one whose head it was carrying while it itself went ahead. Thus, bearing one another's burdens, they cross the strait until they come to the firmness of land.* Perhaps Solomon had this nature of deer in mind when he said: *let the deer of friendship and the colt of your graces converse with you*[^2]. For nothing so proves a friend as the carrying of a friend's burden.

**71. 2.** And yet we would not bear one another's burdens if there were one and the same time of weakness for both who bear their burdens, or one and the same kind of weakness.

But different times and different kinds of weakness bring it about that we are able to bear one another's burdens. For example, you will bear a brother's anger at the time when you are not angry against him, so that in turn, at the time when anger has seized you beforehand, he may support you with gentleness and tranquility. This example pertains to the case where the times of those bearing their burdens are different, although the weakness itself is not different; for in both, anger is borne by the other.

But for a different kind of weakness another example must be seen: as if someone has conquered talkativeness in himself but has not yet conquered obstinacy, while another is still talkative but is now not obstinate; the former ought to bear the latter's talkativeness, and the latter the former's obstinacy, through love, until that fault is healed in the one and this in the other.

For if an equal weakness in two people occurs at one time, they are unable to bear each other, when it is directed against each other.

For against some third person, two who are angry agree with each other and bear with each other—although they are not to be said to bear with each other, but rather to console each other.

Just as those who are sad about one and the same thing bear with each other more, and, as it were, lean on each other, than if one were sad and the other rejoiced. But if they are sad against each other, they can by no means bear with each other.

And therefore, in affections of this kind, the very sickness from which you wish another to be freed through you must be taken up a little, and so taken up that it serves for help and not for an equality of misery,

just as one who stretches out a hand to someone lying down bends himself; for he does not throw himself down so that both may lie down, but only stoops so as to raise up the one lying down.

**71. 3.** Nor does anything make this dutiful labor of bearing the burdens of others be spent gladly, except when we consider how much the Lord endured for us.

For admonishing us about this, the Apostle says: *have this mind in you that was also in Christ Jesus, who, though he was in the form of God, did not consider it robbery to be equal to God, but emptied himself, taking the form of a servant, made in the likeness of men, and found in appearance as a man. He humbled himself, becoming obedient unto death, even death on a cross*[^3].

For above he had said: *each one looking not to his own things but to the things of others*[^4]. To this statement he joined what was said; for it continues thus: *have this mind in you that was also in Christ Jesus*, precisely to this end: that, just as he, in that *the Word was made flesh and dwelt among us*[^5], and, though he was without sin, took on our sins, looking not to his own things but to ours, so we too may gladly, in imitation of him, bear one another's burdens.

**71. 4.** To this consideration is added also that consideration: that he *took on* a man, but we *are* men and ought to consider that the sickness, whether of mind or of body, which we see in another man we too could have had or can have. Let us therefore show to him whose weakness we wish to bear what we would wish to be shown to us by him, if perhaps we were in it and he were not. To this pertains what the Apostle himself says: *I have become all things to all, that I might gain all*[^6], by considering, namely, that he too could have been in the fault from which he desired to free another. For he did this rather by compassion, not by lying, as some suppose—and those especially who, in defending their lies, which they cannot deny, seek the patronage of some great example.

**71. 5.** Then this too must be considered: that there is no man who cannot have some good that you do not yet have, even if it is hidden, in which without doubt he can be superior to you.

This consideration serves to crush and tame pride, lest you think that, because your own goods stand out and appear, another therefore has none that are hidden—and perhaps goods of greater weight, by which he surpasses you without your knowing.

For the Apostle does not command us to deceive ourselves or rather to use flattery when he says: *nothing through contention nor through vainglory, but in humility of mind esteeming one another superior to yourselves*[^7]. We ought not to esteem this in such a way that we pretend to esteem it, but let us truly esteem that there can be something hidden in another by which he is superior to us, even if our own good, by which we seem to be superior to him, is not hidden.

These considerations, pressing down pride and sharpening love, bring it about that a brother's burdens are borne for one another not only with an even mind, but even most gladly. But in no way is judgment to be passed on any unknown man; and no one is known except through friendship. And therefore we bear the evils of friends more firmly, because their goods delight and hold us.

**71. 6.** And so the friendship of no one who presents himself for the joining of friendship is to be rejected—not that he should be received at once, but that one to be received should be wished for and so treated that he can be received. For we can say that the one received into friendship is the one to whom we would dare to pour out all our counsels.

And if there is anyone who does not dare to present himself for making friendship, being held back by some temporal honor or dignity of ours, we must go down to him, and offer him, with a certain courtesy and submission of mind, what he himself does not dare to ask for.

Of course, though more rarely, it nevertheless sometimes happens that the evils of the one whom we wish to receive into friendship become known to us before his goods, and, offended and in a way driven back by them, we abandon him and do not come to the searching out of his goods, which perhaps are more hidden.

And so the Lord Jesus Christ, who wishes us to become his imitators, admonishes us to bear with his weak ones, so that, through the forbearance of love, we may be led to certain sound ones in whose delight we may rest. For he says: *the healthy do not need a physician, but the sick*[^8].

And therefore, if for the love of Christ we ought not to drive from our mind even one who is perhaps sick in every part, since he can be healed through the Word of God, how much less the one who can seem to us wholly sick for this reason: that we could not endure certain wounded parts of him at the first entrance into friendship—and, what is graver, that with offense of mind we dared to pass a rash sentence of prejudgment about the whole man, not fearing what was said: *do not judge, lest you be judged*; and: *with the measure you have measured, it will be measured back to you*[^9].

But often those things that are good appear first, in which too a rash judgment of benevolence must be guarded against, lest, when you have thought the whole to be good, the things that afterward appear bad find you secure and unprepared and offend you more gravely, so that you hate more bitterly the one you had loved rashly—which is wicked.

For even if none of his goods went before, and these evils that appeared afterward stood out first, they would nevertheless have to be borne, until you had carried out with him all the things by which such things are usually healed—how much more when those goods went before, which, as pledges, ought to bind us to bearing later things.

**71. 7.** This, then, is the law of Christ: that we bear one another's burdens. And by loving Christ we easily bear the weakness of another, even one whom we do not yet love on account of his own goods. For we consider that the Lord whom we love died for him. This love the apostle Paul instilled in us when he said: *and the weak one will perish in your knowledge, brother, the one for whom Christ died!*[^10] so that, if we love that weak one less on account of the fault by which he is weak, we may consider in him the one who died for him. But not to love Christ is not weakness but death. For this reason, with immense care and the mercy of God implored, we must take thought lest we neglect Christ on account of the weak one, when we ought to love the weak one on account of Christ.
