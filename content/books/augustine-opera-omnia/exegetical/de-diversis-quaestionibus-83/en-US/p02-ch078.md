# On the Beauty of Images

**78.** That highest art of almighty God, through which all things were made from nothing—which is also called his wisdom—itself works also through craftsmen, so that they make beautiful and fitting things, although they work not from nothing, but from some matter, such as wood or marble or ivory, or any other kind of matter subjected to the hands of the craftsman.

But these men cannot fashion anything from nothing for this reason: that they work through the body, while nevertheless those numbers and that fittingness of outlines, which they impress upon a body through the body, they receive in the mind from that highest wisdom, which impressed those very numbers and that very fittingness far more skillfully upon the whole body of the world, which it fashioned from nothing. In which there are also the bodies of animals, which are now fashioned from something—that is, from the elements of the world—but far more powerfully and more excellently than when human craftsmen imitate those same figures and forms of bodies in their works.

For not all the numerical proportion of the human body is found in a statue, but nevertheless whatever is found there is conveyed from that wisdom, through the mind of the craftsman, which fashions the human body itself by nature. Nor, however, are those who fashion or love such works to be held in high regard, because the soul, intent on lesser things, which it makes corporeally through the body, clings less to that highest wisdom from which it has these powers.

These powers it uses badly while it exercises them outwardly; for, loving those things in which it exercises them, it neglects their stable inner form, and is made emptier and weaker.

But how far those who even worship such works have strayed from the truth can be understood from this: that if they worshiped the very bodies of animals, which are fashioned far more excellently and of which those are imitations, what more wretched thing could we call them?
