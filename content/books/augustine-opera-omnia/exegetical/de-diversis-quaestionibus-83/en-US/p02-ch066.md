# On what is written: Or do you not know, brothers — (for I speak to those who know the law), that the law has dominion over a person — so long as he lives in time?, up to that place where — it is written: He will also give life to your mortal bodies — through his Spirit who dwells in you

**66. 1.** In this comparison in which the Apostle speaks of a man and a woman, since a woman is bound by the law of her husband, he commends three things for consideration: the woman, the husband, and the law—namely, the woman subjugated to the husband by the bond of the Law, by which bond she is freed at the death of the husband, so that she may marry whom she wishes.

For he says: *for a woman, while her husband lives, is bound by Law to her living husband; but if her husband dies, she is released from the law of the husband. Therefore, while the husband lives, she will be called an adulteress if she is with another man; but if her husband dies, she is freed from the Law, so that she is not an adulteress if she is with another man*[^1]*.* Up to this point it is a comparison. Then he begins to speak of the matter, for the explaining and proving of which he introduced the comparison.

In this matter too, three things must likewise be attended to: the person, sin, and the Law. For he says that a person is under the law so long as he lives in sin, just as a woman is under the law of the husband so long as the husband lives. And this sin must here be understood as the sin that came in through the Law.

He says that this sin is beyond measure, since, though it appears to be sin, it nevertheless becomes sin and is heaped up with the added transgression. *For where there is no law, there is no transgression*[^2]*.* And this is what he says: *that it might become beyond measure a sinner, or sin, through the commandment*[^3]*.*

For this reason he says that the Law, although it forbids sinning, was nevertheless not given so as to free from sin, but so as to make sin manifest—sin to which the soul, while it serves it, ought to turn itself to the grace of the liberator, so that it may be freed from sin. *For through the Law comes the knowledge of sin*[^4]*.*

And in another place he says: *but sin, that it might appear as sin, worked death in me through what is good*[^5]*.* Where, then, there is no grace of the liberator, the prohibition of sins increases the desire to sin. This is indeed useful for this purpose: that the soul may perceive that it is not sufficient of itself to draw itself out of the slavery of sin, and that in this way, with all pride deflated and extinguished, it may be made subject to its liberator, and that a person may sincerely say, *my soul has clung to you*[^6]*,* which is now to be not under the law of sin, but in the law of righteousness.

It is called the law of sin, not because the Law itself is sin, but because it is imposed on sinners; for this reason also the law of death, because *the wages of sin is death*[^7]; *the sting of death is sin, but the power of sin is the Law*[^8]. For by sinning we slip into death.

And we sin more vehemently when the Law forbids than if we were forbidden by no law. But when grace comes, that very thing which the law had burdensomely commanded we now fulfill without burden and most gladly.

The law of sin and death, therefore, that is, the law that was imposed on those who sin and die, only commands that we not covet, and yet we covet. But the law of the spirit of life, which pertains to grace and frees from the law of sin and death, brings it about that we do not covet and that we fulfill the commands of the Law—no longer slaves of the Law through fear, but friends through love, and servants of the righteousness from which that Law was promulgated.

Righteousness, however, is to be served not in a servile but in a free way, that is, by love rather than by fear. And therefore it was most truly said: *do we then nullify the Law through faith? By no means. Rather, we establish the Law*[^9]*.* For faith accomplishes what the Law commands. The Law, then, is established through faith. And if this faith is not present, the Law only commands, and it holds guilty those who do not fulfill the commands, so that, groaning and unable to fulfill what is commanded, it may at some time turn them to the grace of the liberator.

**66. 2.** Since, then, we see three things in that comparison—the woman, the husband, and the Law—and again three things in this matter for the sake of which the comparison was applied—the soul, sin, and the law of sin—this alone is different: that in that comparison the husband dies, so that the woman may marry whom she wishes and be released from the law of the husband, but here the soul itself dies to sin, so that it may marry Christ.

And when it dies to sin, it also dies to the law of sin. *And so,* he says, *my brothers, you too have died to the Law through the body of Christ, so that you may belong to another, who rose from the dead, that we might bear fruit to God. For when we were in the flesh,* he says—that is, when we were held bound by carnal desires—*the passions of sins, which are through the Law, were at work in our members, so as to bear fruit unto death*[^10]*.*

With desire increased—the desire that the Law forbids—where there was no faith, and with the charge of transgression added to the heap of sins, since *where there is no Law, there is no transgression*[^11], he calls these the passions *which are through the Law, at work in our members, so as to bear fruit unto death.*

Under these passions, as under a ruling husband, the soul acted before grace came through faith. To these passions, then, he dies who now serves the law of God with the mind, although the passions themselves are not yet dead, so long as with the flesh he serves the law of sin.

There still remains, therefore, something in the one who is under grace which does not conquer him or lead him captive, until everything that has been strengthened by bad habit is mortified; and on this account the body is said to be even now dead, so long as it does not perfectly serve the spirit. And it will come about that it serves perfectly when the mortal body itself has been given life.

**66. 3.** From this we grasp that there are four distinct conditions even in one person, which, passed through by degrees, lead to abiding in eternal life. For since it was fitting, and it was just, that after our nature sinned, having lost the spiritual blessedness signified by the name "paradise," we should be born animal and carnal, the first condition is before the Law, the second under the Law, the third under grace, the fourth in peace.

The condition before the Law is when we are ignorant of sin and follow carnal desires. The condition under the Law is when we are now forbidden from sin, and yet, overcome by the habit of it, we sin, since faith does not yet help us. The third condition is when we now most fully believe in our Liberator, and attribute nothing to our own merits but, by loving his mercy, are no longer conquered by the delight of bad habit when it strives to lead us to sin—though we still endure it as it solicits us, even while we are not handed over to it. The fourth condition is when there is absolutely nothing in a person that resists the spirit, but all things, harmoniously joined and connected together, guard some one thing in firm peace—which will come about when the mortal body has been given life, when this corruptible has put on incorruption and this mortal has put on immortality[^12].

**66. 4.** For demonstrating the first condition, these testimonies come to hand for now: *through one man sin entered into this world, and through sin death, and so it passed through to all men, in whom all sinned. For until the Law sin was in the world. But sin was not reckoned when there was no Law*[^13]*;* and again: *for without the Law sin is dead, but I once lived without the Law*[^14]*.* For what he here calls *dead* is what above he calls *not reckoned,* that is, it lay hidden. He makes this clear in what follows, where he says: *but sin, that it might appear as sin, worked death in me through what is good*[^15]*,* that is, through the Law, because the Law is good if one uses it lawfully[^16]. If, then, he here says *that sin might appear,* it is manifest that above he therefore called it *dead* and said it was *not reckoned* because it did not appear before it was shown by the prohibiting Law.

**66. 5.** For the second condition, these testimonies are fitting: *but the Law slipped in, so that the offense might abound*[^17]*,* for transgression too was added, which did not exist before; and that which has already been mentioned: *for when we were in the flesh, the passions of sins, which are through the Law, were at work in our members, so as to bear fruit unto death*[^18]*;*

and that: *what then shall we say? Is the Law sin? By no means. But I did not know sin except through the Law; for I would not have known desire unless the Law said, 'you shall not covet.' But sin, seizing the occasion, worked in me through the commandment every kind of desire*[^19]*;* and a little later: *when the commandment came,* he says, *sin revived. But I died, and the commandment, which was unto life, was found to be unto death for me. For sin, seizing the occasion, deceived me through the commandment, and through it killed me*[^20]*.*

So that he says *I died*, he wishes it to be understood: I knew that I was dead, because he who sees through the Law what he ought not to do, and yet does it, now sins even by transgression; and that he says, *sin deceived me, seizing the occasion through the commandment*[^21]*,* is either because the enticement of delight toward sinning is more vehement when the prohibition is present, or because, even if a person does something according to the commands of the Law, if there is not yet the faith that is in grace, he wishes to attribute this to himself and not to God, and by being proud sins all the more.

He therefore continues and says: *and so the Law indeed is holy, and the commandment holy and just and good. Did then what is good become death to me? By no means. But sin, that it might appear as sin, worked death in me through what is good, so that the sinner, or sin offending through the commandment, might become beyond measure. But we know that the Law is spiritual, but I am carnal*[^22]*,* that is, I consent to the flesh, not yet freed by spiritual grace, *sold under sin*[^23]*,* that is, sinning for the price of temporal pleasures. *For what I do, I do not know*[^24]*,* that is, I do not recognize it to be among the precepts of truth, where there is true knowledge.

According to this manner of speaking, the Lord says to sinners: *I never knew you.* For nothing is hidden from him; but because sins are not found in the rules of the precepts that Truth holds, therefore Truth itself says to sinners: *I never knew you.* For just as darkness is perceived by the eyes through not seeing, so sins are perceived by the mind through not knowing.

From this manner of speaking I judge it was said in the *Psalms*: *who understands his faults?*[^25] *For I do not do the good that I will, but the evil that I hate, that I do. But if I do what I do not will, I consent to the Law, that it is good. But now it is no longer I who work it, but the sin that dwells in me. For I know that good does not dwell in me, that is, in my flesh. For to will lies close at hand for me, but to accomplish the good I do not find. For I do not do the good that I will, but the evil that I do not will, that I do. But if I do what I do not will, it is no longer I who work it, but the sin that dwells in me. I find, then, a law for me when I wish to do good, since evil lies close at hand for me. For I delight in the law of God according to the inner man; but I see another law in my members, fighting against the law of my mind, and taking me captive in the law of sin, which is in my members*[^26]*.*

Up to this point are the words of a person established under the Law, not yet under grace, who, even though he does not wish to sin, is conquered by sin. For carnal habit has grown strong, and the natural bond of mortality by which we are propagated from Adam. Let him, then, who is so placed implore help, and let him know that what fell was his own, but that it is not his own that he rises. For now, freed and recognizing the grace of his liberator, he says: *wretched man that I am, who will free me from the body of this death? The grace of God through Jesus Christ our Lord*[^27]*.*

**66. 6.** And now there begin to be spoken the words of a person established under grace in the condition we have shown to be the third—the condition which indeed has the mortality of the flesh struggling against it, but not conquering and taking it captive into consent to sinning. For he says: *therefore I myself with the mind serve the law of God, but with the flesh the law of sin. There is therefore now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus. For the law of the spirit of life in Christ Jesus has freed me from the law of sin and death. For what was impossible for the Law, in that it was weakened through the flesh*[^28]*,* that is, through carnal desires—for the law was not fulfilled for this reason, that there was not yet the love of righteousness itself, which would hold the mind by an inner delight, lest it be drawn to sin by delight in temporal things.

Therefore the Law was weakened through the flesh, that is, it did not make righteous those given over to the flesh, but *God sent his Son in the likeness of sinful flesh*[^29]*.* For it was not sinful flesh, since it was not born of carnal delight, yet there was in it the likeness of sinful flesh, because it was mortal flesh. But Adam did not deserve death except by sin.

But what did the Lord do? *From sin he condemned sin in the flesh*[^30]*,* that is, by taking on the flesh of a sinful man, and by teaching how we should live, he condemned sin in that very flesh, so that the spirit, burning with the love of eternal things, might not be led captive into consent to lust. *That the righteousness,* he says, *of the Law might be fulfilled in us, who walk not according to the flesh, but according to the spirit*[^31]*.*

And so the precepts of the law were fulfilled through love, which could not be fulfilled through fear.

*For those who are according to the flesh set their minds on the things of the flesh,* that is, they desire carnal goods as the highest goods; *but those who are according to the spirit set their minds on the things of the spirit. For the prudence of the flesh is death, but the prudence of the spirit is life and peace, because the prudence of the flesh is hostile toward God*[^32]*.*

He himself shows what he meant by *hostile*, lest anyone suppose that some other first principle comes from the opposite side; for he adds and says: *for it is not subject to the law of God, nor indeed can it be*[^33]*.* Therefore to act against the Law is to be hostile toward God—not because anything can harm God, but because whoever resists the will of God harms himself; for this is to kick against the goad, as was said by God to the apostle Paul when he was still persecuting the Church[^34].

And it was said thus: *for it is not subject to the law of God; nor indeed can it be*[^35]*,* as though it were said: "snow does not warm; nor indeed can it. For as long as it is snow, it does not warm; but it can be melted and grow hot, so as to warm; but when it does this, it is now no longer snow."

So too it is called the prudence *of the flesh* when the soul desires temporal goods as great goods; for as long as such an appetite is in the soul, it cannot be subject to the law of God, that is, it cannot fulfill what the Law commands. But when it has begun to desire spiritual goods and to despise temporal ones, it will cease to be the prudence of the flesh, and will not resist the spirit.

For the same soul, when it desires lower things, is said to have the prudence of the flesh; when higher things, the prudence of the spirit—not because the prudence of the flesh is a substance with which the soul is clothed or unclothed, but it is an affection of the soul itself, which will cease to exist altogether when the soul has turned itself wholly to the things above.

*But those who are in the flesh,* he says, *cannot please God*[^36]*,* that is, those who acquiesce in the pleasures of the flesh. For lest anyone should think this was said of those who have not yet departed from this life, he most opportunely added: *but you are not in the flesh, but in the spirit*[^37]*.*

Certainly he is still speaking to those established in this life; for they were in the spirit, because in faith and hope and love they acquiesced in the desires of spiritual things. *If, however,* he says, *the Spirit of God dwells in you. But if anyone does not have the Spirit of Christ, he does not belong to him. But if Christ is in you, the body indeed is dead because of sin, but the spirit is life because of righteousness*[^38]*.*

He calls the body dead so long as it is such that it troubles the soul by the lack of bodily things, and, by certain motions arising from that very lack, solicits it to desire earthly things. Yet, although these exist, the mind does not consent to doing unlawful things—the mind that now serves the law of God and is established under grace.

For to this purpose serves what was said above: *with the mind I serve the law of God, but with the flesh the law of sin*[^39]*.* And that person is now described as being under grace who does not yet have the perfect peace that will come about by the resurrection and transformation of the body.

**66. 7.** It remains, then, that he speaks of the peace itself of the resurrection of the body, which is the fourth condition—if indeed that can be called a condition which is the highest rest. For he continues and says: *if then the Spirit of him who raised Jesus from the dead dwells in you, he who raised Jesus Christ from the dead will also give life to your mortal bodies through his Spirit who dwells in you*[^40]*.*

Here there is a most evident testimony concerning the resurrection of the body; and it is sufficiently apparent that, so long as we are in this life, there is no lack of troubles through the mortal flesh, nor of certain ticklings of carnal delights. For although the one established under grace, who serves the law of God with the mind, does not give way, he nevertheless with the flesh serves the law of sin.

When a person has been perfected by these degrees, no substance is found to be evil. Nor is the Law evil, which shows a person in what bonds of sins he lies, so that through faith, the help of the Liberator being implored, he may deserve to be both loosed and raised up and most firmly established. In the first condition, then, which is before the Law, there is no fight with the pleasures of this world; in the second, which is under the Law, we fight but are conquered; in the third we fight and conquer; in the fourth we do not fight, but rest in perfect and eternal peace. For what is lower in us is subjugated to us—which on this account was not subjugated, because we had deserted God, who is higher than us.
