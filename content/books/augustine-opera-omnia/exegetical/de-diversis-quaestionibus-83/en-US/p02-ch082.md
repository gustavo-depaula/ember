# On what is written: — For whom the Lord loves he rebukes, — and he scourges every son whom he receives 418

**82. 1.** Many, murmuring under the discipline of God, raise a question when they see the just often suffer grave troubles in respect of this life, as though it profits them nothing that they serve God, because they suffer either the common labors—indifferently, of bodies and of losses and insults and all the other things that mortals think evils—or even greater ones than the rest, on account of the word of God and righteousness, which, being burdensome to those who sin, stir up tumultuous seditions or plots or hatreds against its preachers.

To whom it is answered that, if this life alone belonged to men, it would seem not altogether absurd that the just life either profits nothing or even harms. Although there have not been lacking those who would so weigh the sweetness of righteousness and its inner joy against all the bodily labors and troubles which the human race suffers by the condition of its mortality—against all the things too which, on account of righteousness itself, are most injuriously stirred up against those who live justly—that, the hope of the future life set aside, they would be tortured more pleasantly and more gladly for the love of truth than the luxurious would feast for the desire of drunkenness.

**82. 2.** But nevertheless to those who think God unjust, when they see the just in pains and labors—or certainly, if they do not dare to call God unjust, who either suppose that he does not care for human affairs, or that he has once established the necessities of the fates, against which not even he himself does anything, lest he be believed to disturb by inconstancy the order of things arranged by him, or who hold some other opinion: that God, weak in some respect, cannot forbid these evils to the just—

it must be said that there would have been no righteousness in men if God did not care for human affairs, because all this righteousness of men—which the human mind can both hold by acting rightly and lose by sinning—would not be impressed on the soul unless there were some unchangeable righteousness, which would be found whole by the just when they turned to it, and would be left whole by sinners when they turned away from its light.

This unchangeable righteousness is certainly God's, nor would he extend it to illumine those who are turned to him, if he did not care for human affairs.

But if he allowed the just to suffer grave things for this reason: that he was unwilling to come against the order of things arranged by him, then he himself would not be just—not because he wishes to preserve his arrangement, but because he so arranged the very order of things that the just are afflicted with unmerited punishments.

But whoever holds the opinion that God is in some respect weak for repelling the evils that the just suffer is foolish for this reason: that he does not understand that, just as it is wicked to call God unjust, so it is wicked to deny him almighty.

These things being briefly established for the present occasion of the question taken up—since it is the most pernicious iniquity to doubt both that God exists and that he is just and almighty—no more probable cause occurs why just men generally labor in this life, except that this is to their advantage. For the righteousness of men which now is, for receiving everlasting salvation, is one thing; the righteousness which ought to have belonged to man, then established in paradise, for retaining and not losing that same everlasting salvation, was another.

For just as it is the righteousness of God to enjoin useful things, and to distribute punishments to the disobedient but rewards to the obedient, so it is the righteousness of man to obey the useful precepts.

But since blessedness is in the soul as health is in the body—just as in the body itself one medicine is prescribed lest good health be lost, but another so that, once lost, it may be recovered—so in the whole state of man certain precepts were then given lest he lose immortality, and others are now prescribed so that he may receive it.

And just as in the health of the body, if anyone, by not obeying the precepts of the physician by which that same good health is to be guarded, falls into some disease, he receives other precepts so that he can be healed—which often do not suffice, if the disease is such, unless certain remedies are applied by the physician, generally harsh and bringing pains, which nevertheless have force for recovering health, whence it comes about that a man, although now obeying the physician, nevertheless still suffers pains, not only from the disease itself not yet healed, but also from the remedy of the medicine;

so a man, fallen through sin into the diseased and calamitous mortality of this life—because he was unwilling to obey the first precept, by which he would have guarded and held everlasting salvation—received, sick, second precepts, obeying which he is now indeed said, not absurdly, to live justly, but nevertheless suffers troubles which he suffers either from the disease itself not yet healed or from the remedy of the medicine.

To which remedy is reckoned what is written: *for whom the Lord loves he rebukes, and he scourges every son whom he receives*[^1]. But those who, not obeying the most wholesome precepts, live wickedly increase their diseases more and more; and either from these they suffer innumerable labors and pains of miseries even in this life, or, with punishments also applied so that what is not sound may be touched and feel pain, they are mercifully admonished of the evil they are in, so that, turned to the medicine, they may be made sound through the grace of God.

And if they despise all these things—that is, the precepts of words and of pains—they will deserve, after this life, a just everlasting condemnation. And therefore he can say that these things happen unjustly who, thinking that the only life is the mortal one we now lead, does not believe the things divinely proclaimed as future, being about to pay the gravest punishments for the perseverance of his sins and his unbelief.

[^1]: Heb 12:6.
