# On the Ideas

**46. 1.** Plato is reported to have been the first to use the name "ideas." Yet if this name did not exist before he established it, it does not on that account follow either that the things themselves, which he called ideas, did not exist, or that they were understood by no one; but they were perhaps named by others, now by one name, now by another. For one is permitted to impose any name whatever on a thing one knows that has no usual name. For it is not likely either that there were no wise men before Plato, or that they did not understand those things which Plato, as has been said, calls ideas, whatever things they are, since so great a force is established in them that no one can be wise without understanding them.

It is credible, too, that beside Greece there were wise men also among other peoples, which Plato himself sufficiently attests, not only by traveling abroad for the sake of perfecting his wisdom, but also by recording it in his books. These men, then, if there were any, are not to be thought to have been ignorant of the ideas, although perhaps they called them by another name. But let what has been said about the name suffice. Let us look at the thing itself, which is especially to be considered and known, the words being within our power, so that everyone may call by what he will the thing he has come to know.

**46. 2.** Ideas, therefore, we can in Latin call either "forms" (formae) or "species," so as to seem to translate word for word. But if we call them "reasons" (rationes), we depart indeed from the propriety of interpretation; for reasons in Greek are called λόγοι, not ideas. Yet whoever wishes to use this word will not be out of keeping with the thing itself.

For the ideas are certain principal forms or stable and unchangeable reasons of things, which themselves have not been given form, and through this are eternal and ever holding themselves in the same way, which are contained in the divine understanding. And although they themselves neither come to be nor pass away, nevertheless everything that can come to be and pass away, and everything that comes to be and passes away, is said to be formed according to them.

But the soul, it is denied, can behold them unless it is rational, by that part of itself by which it is preeminent, that is, by the mind and reason itself, as by a certain face or by its own inner and intelligible eye. And even that rational soul itself, not every one and any whatever, but the one that has been holy and pure—this is asserted to be fit for that vision, that is, the one that has its very eye, by which these things are seen, sound and clear and serene and like the things which it intends to see.

But what religious person, imbued with true religion, although he cannot yet behold these things, would dare nevertheless to deny—indeed, would not also profess—that all things which are, that is, whatever are contained in their own kind by a certain proper nature so as to be, have been brought into being with God as their author, and that with him as author all things that live, live, and that the universal soundness of things, and the very order by which the things that are changed carry out their temporal courses with a sure regulation, are contained and governed by the laws of the most high God?

This being established and granted, who would dare to say that God founded all things irrationally?

But if this cannot rightly be said or believed, it remains that all things were founded by reason, and not man by the same reason as the horse; for it is absurd to think this.

Individual things, therefore, were created by their own proper reasons. But where is it to be thought that these reasons are, except in the very mind of the Creator?

For he was not beholding anything placed outside himself, so as to establish according to it what he was establishing; for to suppose this is sacrilegious. But if these reasons of all things to be created or created are contained in the divine mind, and nothing can be in the divine mind except what is eternal and unchangeable, and Plato calls these principal reasons of things ideas, then not only are there ideas, but they themselves are true, because they are eternal and remain of the same kind and unchangeable. By participation in them it comes about that whatever is, is, in whatever way it is.

But the rational soul, among the things that have been founded by God, surpasses all and is nearest to God when it is pure; and insofar as it has clung to him in love, so far, suffused and in a way illumined by him with that intelligible light, it discerns—not through the bodily eyes, but through its own principal part by which it is preeminent, that is, through its understanding—these reasons, by the vision of which it is made most happy.

These reasons, as has been said, may be called either ideas or forms or species or reasons, and many are permitted to call them what they please, but to very few is it given to see what is true.
