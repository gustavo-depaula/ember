# On Nourishing Love

**36. 1.** By "love" I mean that by which are loved the things which are not to be despised in comparison with the lover himself, that is, what is eternal and what can love the eternal itself. God and the mind, then, when they are loved, are properly called love—most purified and consummated, if nothing else is loved; and this may also be called affection. But when God is loved more than the mind, so that a man would rather be his than his own, then true and supreme provision is made for the mind, and consequently also for the body, while we take no care for it by some busy appetite, but take up only what is ready and offered.

But the poison of love is the hope of acquiring or retaining temporal things; its nourishment is the diminishing of cupidity; its perfection, no cupidity. The sign of its advance is the diminishing of fear; the sign of its perfection, no fear, since both *cupidity is the root of all evils*[^1] and *consummated affection casts out fear*[^2]. Whoever, therefore, wishes to nourish it, let him press on to diminish his desires. Now cupidity is the love of acquiring or obtaining temporal things. The beginning of diminishing it is to fear God, who alone cannot be feared without love. For thus one tends toward wisdom, and nothing is more true than what was said: *the beginning of wisdom is the fear of the Lord*[^3].

For there is no one who does not flee pain more than he seeks pleasure, since indeed we see that even the most savage beasts are deterred from the greatest pleasures by the fear of pains; and when this has turned into a habit in them, they are called tamed and gentle.

Wherefore, since there is in man reason, which, when it serves cupidity by a wretched perversion so that men may not be feared, suggests that what is committed can lie hidden, and contrives the most cunning deceits to cover up hidden sins,

it comes about that men whom the beauty of virtue does not yet delight, unless they are deterred from sinning by the punishments which are most truly proclaimed through holy and divine men, and unless they accept that what they conceal from men cannot be concealed from God, are tamed with more difficulty than wild beasts.

But that God may be feared, it must be persuaded that all things are ruled by divine providence—not so much by reasonings, for one who can enter upon these can already perceive also the beauty of virtue, as by examples, whether recent ones, if any occur, or from history, and especially those which, by the providing of divine providence itself, have received in the Old or in the New Testament the most excellent authority of religion. At the same time, one must treat both of the punishments of sins and of the rewards of right actions.

**36. 2.** But now, when some habit of not sinning has persuaded that what was thought burdensome is easy, let the sweetness of devotion begin to be tasted and the beauty of virtue to be commended, so that the liberty of love may stand out above the slavery of fear.

Then it must already be persuaded to the faithful—the sacraments of regeneration having gone before, which must of necessity move them greatly—what difference there is between the two men, the Old and the New, the outer and the inner, the earthly and the heavenly, that is, between him who pursues carnal and temporal goods and him who pursues spiritual and eternal ones,

and they must be warned not to expect from God perishable and passing benefits, in which even wicked men can abound, but firm and everlasting ones, for the receiving of which all the things that are thought in this world to be goods and evils are utterly to be despised.

Here that most excellent and unique example of the Lord's Man must be set forth, who, although by so many miracles he showed that he had such great power over things, both spurned those things which the unskilled think great goods and bore those which they think great evils. And lest anyone dare the less to take up these morals and this discipline the more he honors him, it must be shown, both from his promises and exhortations and from the multitude of those who imitate him—Apostles, martyrs, and innumerable saints—how those things are not to be despaired of.

**36. 3.** But when the enticements of carnal pleasures have been overcome, care must be taken lest the cupidity of pleasing men creep in and take their place, whether through certain wondrous deeds, or through difficult continence or patience, or through some bestowing of gifts, or under the name of knowledge or eloquence.

In that class is also the cupidity of honor. Against all these let there be brought forward the things that are written about the praise of love and the emptiness of boasting, and let it be taught how shameful it is to wish to please those whom you would not wish to imitate; for either they are not good, and it is nothing great to be praised by the wicked, or they are good, and one ought to imitate them.

But those who are good are good by virtue; and virtue does not seek what is in the power of other men. He, then, who imitates the good seeks the praise of no man, since one who imitates the wicked is not worthy of praise. But if you wish to please men in order that you may profit them toward loving God, then you no longer desire this but something else. And one who desires to please still has fear as a necessity: first, lest by sinning secretly he be counted by the Lord among the hypocrites; then, if he seeks to please by good deeds, lest, hunting for this reward, he lose what God is going to give.

**36. 4.** But when this cupidity has been overcome, pride must be guarded against. For it is difficult for one who no longer desires to please men, and thinks himself full of virtue, to deign to associate with men. And so fear is still necessary, lest even what he seems to have be taken away from him[^4], and, his hands and feet bound, he be cast into the outer darkness[^5]. Wherefore the fear of God not only begins but also perfects wisdom, that is, in him who supremely loves God and his neighbor as himself. But what dangers and difficulties on this road are to be feared, and what remedies one ought to use, is another question.

[^1]: 1 Tim 6:10.
[^2]: 1 John 4:18.
[^3]: Sir 1:16.
[^4]: See Matt 25:29.
[^5]: See Matt 22:13.
