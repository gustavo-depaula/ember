# On what the apostle James says: — But do you wish to know, O empty man, that — faith without works is idle?

**76. 1.** Since the apostle Paul, in preaching *that a man is justified by faith without works*, was not well understood by those who took what was said in such a way as to think that, once they had believed in Christ, even if they acted badly and lived in a criminal and disgraceful way, they could be saved by faith, this passage of this Letter expounds how the same meaning of the apostle Paul is to be understood[^1].

And therefore he rather uses the example of Abraham [to demonstrate] that faith is empty if it does not work well, since the apostle Paul too used the example of Abraham to prove that a man is justified by faith without the works of the Law[^2].

For when he recalls the good works of Abraham, which accompanied his faith, he sufficiently shows that the apostle Paul does not, through Abraham, teach that a man is justified by faith without works in such a way that, if anyone has believed, it does not pertain to him to work well, but rather to this end: that no one should think that he has come to the gift of justification, which is in faith, by the merits of prior good works.

For in this the Jews wished to prefer themselves to the gentiles believing in Christ: that they said they had come to the grace of the Gospel by the merits of the good works which are in the Law; and therefore many of those who had believed from among them were scandalized that the grace of Christ was being handed over to the uncircumcised gentiles. For which reason the apostle Paul says that a man can be justified by faith without preceding works.

For how can one justified by faith do anything thereafter except justly, although beforehand, having worked nothing justly, he came to the justification of faith not by the merit of good works but by the grace of God, which cannot be idle in him, since he now works well through love?

But if, when he has believed, he soon departs from this life, the justification of faith remains with him—neither with preceding good works, because he came to it not by merit but by grace, nor with subsequent ones, because he is not permitted to be in this life.

From which what the apostle Paul says is manifest: *for we judge that a man is justified by faith without works*[^3]. Yet it is not to be understood in such a way that, faith having been received, if he lives, we should call him just, even if he lives badly. Therefore the apostle Paul too uses the example of Abraham, because without the works of the Law, which he had not received, he was justified by faith; and James too, because he shows that good works followed the faith of Abraham himself, showing how what the apostle Paul preached is to be understood.

**76. 2.** For those who think that this statement of the apostle James is contrary to that statement of the apostle Paul can suppose that Paul himself too is contrary to himself, because he says in another place: *for the hearers of the Law are not just before God, but the doers of the Law will be justified*[^4]; and in another place: *but faith which works through love*[^5]. And again: *for if you live according to the flesh, you will die; but if by the spirit you put to death the deeds of the flesh, you will live*[^6].

But what the deeds of the flesh are, which must be put to death by spiritual works, he shows in another place, saying: *now the works of the flesh are manifest, which are fornications, impurities, the service of idols, sorceries, enmities, contentions, jealousies, animosities, dissensions, heresies, envies, drunkennesses, carousings, and things like these; which I foretell to you, as I have foretold, that those who do such things will not possess the kingdom of God*[^7]; and to the Corinthians: *do not err: neither fornicators, nor those who serve idols, nor adulterers, nor the effeminate, nor men who lie with men, nor thieves, nor the greedy, nor drunkards, nor revilers, nor the rapacious will possess the kingdom of God. And these things indeed you were; but you have been washed, but you have been sanctified, but you have been justified in the name of our Lord Jesus Christ and in the Spirit of our God*[^8]*.*

By which statements he teaches most manifestly that they did not come to the justification of faith by a past good work; nor was this grace given to their merits, when he says: *and these things indeed you were*. But when he says, *those who do such things will not possess the kingdom of God*, he sufficiently shows that, from the time they believed, they ought to work well.

This both James says, and in very many places the same apostle Paul sufficiently and openly proclaims: that all who have believed in Christ must live rightly, lest they come to punishments. This the Lord himself also recalls, saying: *not everyone who says to me, 'Lord, Lord,' will enter the kingdom of heaven; but he who does the will of my Father who is in heaven, he will enter the kingdom of heaven*[^9]; and elsewhere: *why do you call me, 'Lord, Lord,' and do not do what I say?*[^10]; and: *everyone who hears these words of mine and does them, I will liken to a prudent man, who built his house upon the rock*, and the rest. *And he who hears these words of mine and does not do them, I will liken to a foolish man, who built his house upon the sand*[^11] and the rest.

For this reason the statements of the two apostles Paul and James are not contrary to each other, when the one says *that a man is justified by faith without works*, and the other says that faith is empty without works, because the former speaks of the works that precede faith, the latter of those that follow faith, as Paul himself too shows in many places.
