# On what is written: And found in appearance as a man

**73. 1.** We speak of "habit" in many ways: either the habit of the mind, such as the grasp of any discipline strengthened and made firm by use; or the habit of the body, according to which we say that one person is more full-bodied and stronger than another, which is more properly called "constitution"; or the habit of those things that are fitted to our members from without, according to which we speak of one clothed, shod, armed, and anything of that kind.

In all these kinds (since indeed this word is derived from that verb which is "to have"), it is manifest that "habit" is spoken of in regard to that which befalls someone in such a way that he can also not have it. For both teaching befalls the mind, and fullness and strength the body, and there is no doubt that clothing and arms befall our members—in such a way that the mind can be unskilled, if teaching did not befall it, and the body thin and languid without the fullness of the inward parts and strength, and a person can be naked without clothing and unarmed without arms and barefoot without footwear. "Habit," then, is spoken of in regard to that which befalls us so as to be had.

Nevertheless there is this difference: that certain of the things which befall us so as to make a "habit" are not changed by us, but they themselves change us into themselves, remaining whole and unshaken—as wisdom, when it befalls a person, is not itself changed, but changes the person, whom it makes wise from foolish. But certain things befall us in such a way that they both change and are changed—as food itself, losing its own form, is turned into our body, and we, restored by the food, are changed from thinness and languor into strength and vigor.

There is a third kind, when the things that befall are themselves changed so as to make a "habit," and are in a way formed by those for which they make a "habit,"

as is clothing; for when it has been thrown off or put away, it does not have the form it takes when it is put on and drawn over the members. Therefore, put on, it receives a form that it did not have when taken off, while the members themselves, both when they are clothed and when they are unclothed, remain in their own state.

There can also be a fourth kind, when the things that befall so as to make a "habit" neither change those to which they befall nor are themselves changed by them, as a ring on a finger, if it is not considered too subtly. Nevertheless this kind is either nonexistent, if you examine it diligently, or altogether most rare.

**73. 2.** When, therefore, the Apostle was speaking of the only-begotten Son of God, as far as pertains to his divinity, according to that whereby he is most truly God, he said that he was *equal to the Father*, which was not for him as something *seized*—that is, as though to desire what belonged to another—if, always remaining in that equality, he were unwilling to be clothed with man and to appear to men as a man;

but *he emptied himself*, not changing his own form, but *taking the form of a servant*—neither converted and transmuted into a man, with his unchangeable stability lost, but, by taking on, as it were, a true man, himself the one who took on, *made in the likeness of men*, not for himself but for those to whom he appeared in man, *and was found in appearance as a man*[^1], that is, by having a man *he was found as a man*.

For he could not be found by those who had an unclean heart and could not see the Word with the Father, except by taking on this which they could see, and through which they might be led to that inner light.

But this "habit" is not from the first kind; for the nature of man, remaining in itself, did not change the nature of God; nor from the second, for man did not both change God and was changed by him; nor from the fourth, for man was not taken on in such a way that he neither changed God nor was changed by him; but rather from the third: for he was taken on in such a way that he was changed for the better, and was formed by him ineffably more excellently and more closely than clothing is by a man when it is put on.

By this word "*habit*," then, the Apostle sufficiently signified in what sense he said *made in the likeness of men*, because he was made not by a transfiguration into man, but by a habit, since he was clothed with a man whom, uniting and in a way conforming to himself, he would join to immortality and eternity.

But that habit which lies in the grasp of wisdom and discipline the Greeks call hexis, while this one, according to which we speak of one clothed or armed, they rather call schema. From which it is understood that the Apostle spoke of this kind of habit, since indeed in the Greek copies there is written what we in the Latin have as "habit."

By which word it must be understood that the Word was not changed by the taking on of man, just as the members are not changed when clothed with a garment, although that taking on ineffably joined the one taken on to the one taking on.

But, as far as human words can be fitted to ineffable realities, lest God, the one who took on human frailty, be understood to be changed, it was chosen that in Greek and in Latin that taking on should be called a habit.

[^1]: Phil 2:6-7.
