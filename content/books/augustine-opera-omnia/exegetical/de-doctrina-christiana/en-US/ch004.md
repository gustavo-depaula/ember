# Book II

## On things and signs.

**1.** 1. Since, when I was writing about "things," I first gave warning that no one should attend in them to anything but what they are, not also to whether they signify something else besides themselves; in turn, discussing signs, I say this: that no one should attend in them to what they are, but rather to the fact that they are signs—that is, to what they signify. For a "sign" is a thing which, besides the appearance it presents to the senses, causes something else of itself to come into thought; just as, when we see a footprint, we think that the animal whose footprint it is has passed by; and when we see smoke, we recognize that fire is beneath it; and when we hear the voice of a living being, we notice the disposition of its mind; and when the trumpet sounds, soldiers know that they ought either to advance or to retreat, and whatever else the battle demands.

## On natural signs.

**1.** 2. Of signs, then, some are natural, others given. Natural signs are those which, without any will or any desire of signifying, cause something else besides themselves to be known from themselves, such as smoke signifying fire. For it does not signify by willing to do so, but by the observation and noting of things experienced it is recognized that fire is beneath, even if smoke alone appears. But the footprint of a passing animal also belongs to this class; and the face of an angry or sad person signifies the disposition of the mind, even though there is no will on the part of the one who is angry or sad; or if any other motion of the mind is betrayed by the indicating face, even when we are not acting so that it be betrayed.

But it is not my purpose now to discuss this whole class. Since, however, it has fallen within our division, it could not be passed over altogether, and let it suffice to have noted it thus far.

## On what class of signs we here undertake to treat.

**2.** 3. Given signs, however, are those which living beings give one another to show, as far as they can, the motions of their minds, or anything they have sensed or understood. And we have no reason for signifying—that is, for giving a sign—except to bring forth and transfer into another’s mind what the one who gives the sign bears in his own mind. We have determined, then, to consider and treat this class of signs, insofar as it concerns human beings, since even the divinely given signs contained in the holy Scriptures have been indicated to us through the human beings who wrote them down.

Some beasts, too, have signs among themselves by which they disclose the appetite of their minds. For the cock, when it has found food, gives a sign by its voice for the hen to run up, and the male dove calls the female by cooing or is in turn called by her, and many things of this kind are commonly observed. Whether these, like the face or the cry of one in pain, follow the motion of the mind without any will to signify, or are truly given in order to signify, is another question, and does not pertain to the matter in hand. We remove this part from this work as not necessary.

## Among signs, words hold the chief place.

**3.** 4. Of the signs, then, by which human beings communicate their thoughts to one another, some pertain to the sense of the eyes, very many to that of the ears, and very few to the other senses. For when we nod, we give a sign only to the eyes of the one whom we wish to make, by this sign, a sharer in our will. And some signify very many things by the motion of the hands; and actors, by the motions of all their limbs, give certain signs to those in the know and converse, as it were, with their eyes; and military banners and standards convey through the eyes the will of the commanders. And all these are, as it were, certain visible words.

But those that pertain to the ears, as I said, are more numerous, especially in words. For the trumpet, the flute, and the lyre also for the most part give a sound that is not only pleasant but also significant. Yet all these signs, compared with words, are very few. For words have plainly obtained the chief place among human beings for signifying whatever is conceived in the mind, if anyone wishes to bring it forth.

For the Lord too gave a certain sign by the odor of the ointment with which his feet were anointed,[^1] and by the sacrament of his body and blood he signified through taste what he willed,[^2] and when the woman by touching the hem of his garment was made well, it signifies something.[^3] But the countless multitude of signs by which human beings bring forth their thoughts is established in words. For all those signs whose classes I have briefly touched on I could express in words, but words I could in no way express by those signs.

## Whence letters come.

**4.** 5. But because, when the air is struck, they at once pass away and do not last longer than they sound, signs of words were established through letters. Thus utterances are shown to the eyes, not through themselves, but through certain signs proper to them. These signs, then, could not be common to all nations, because of a certain sin of human discord, when each one seizes the chief place for himself. A sign of this pride is that tower raised up to heaven, where impious human beings deserved to have not only their minds but even their voices discordant.[^4]

## On the biblical languages.

**5.** 6. From this it came about that the divine Scripture too, by which such great diseases of human wills are relieved, having set out from one language by which it could opportunely be disseminated throughout the world, and spread far and wide through the various languages of translators, became known to the nations for their salvation. Those who read it seek nothing else than to find the thoughts and the will of those by whom it was written down, and through them the will of God, according to which we believe such men spoke.

## To what end the obscurity of Scripture in tropes and figures is useful.

**6.** 7. But by many and manifold obscurities and ambiguities those are deceived who read rashly, taking one thing for another; and in some places they do not even find what they might falsely suspect, so darkly are certain things said that they cast a most dense fog. I do not doubt that all this was provided by God in order to subdue pride by labor and to renew the understanding from disdain, since for the most part things easily searched out grow cheap to it.

For what is it, I ask, that if someone were to say that they are holy and perfect human beings, by whose life and morals the Church of Christ cuts off from any superstitions those who come to it, and by the imitation of the good incorporates them somehow into itself—those good, faithful, and true servants of God who, laying aside the burdens of the world,[^5] have come to the holy bath of Baptism, and rising from there, by the conception of the Holy Spirit bring forth the fruit of twofold love, that is, of God and of neighbor;

what is it, then, that if someone says these things, it delights the hearer less than if he expounds toward the same sense that place from the *Song of Songs* where it is said to the Church, when she was being praised as a beautiful woman: *your teeth are like a flock of shorn ewes coming up from the washing, which all bear twins, and there is none barren among them*? Does a person learn anything else than when he hears that in the plainest words without the help of this likeness? And yet somehow I gaze more sweetly upon the saints when I see them as the teeth of the Church, cutting human beings off from errors and, their hardness having been softened, transferring them as though bitten off and chewed into her body. I also recognize most delightfully the sheep, shorn, having laid aside their worldly burdens as fleeces, and coming up from the washing—that is, from Baptism—all bearing twins, that is, the two precepts of love, and I see that none is barren of this holy fruit.

## Things sought with difficulty are found more sweetly.

**6.** 8. But why I should see it more sweetly than if no such likeness were drawn from the divine books, when the matter and the knowledge are the same, is difficult to say and is another question. For now, however, no one doubts both that everything is learned more readily through likenesses, and that what is sought with some difficulty is found much more pleasingly. For those who do not find at all what they seek labor under hunger; but those who do not seek, because they have it at hand, often wither from disdain: in both cases languor is to be guarded against.

Magnificently and healthfully, then, the Holy Spirit so regulated the holy Scriptures that by the more open places he might meet hunger, while by the more obscure ones he might wipe away disdain. For almost nothing is dug out of those obscurities which is not found stated most plainly elsewhere.

## To those who have turned to God, Scripture will lie open.

**7.** 9. Before all things, then, it is necessary to be turned by the fear of God[^6] toward knowing his will, what he commands us to seek and what to avoid. And this fear must necessarily strike into us the thought of our mortality and of our future death, and, as though our flesh were pierced with nails, fasten all the motions of pride to the wood of the Cross. Then it is necessary to grow gentle through piety, and not to contradict the divine Scripture, whether understood, if it strikes at some vices of ours, or not understood, as though we could be wiser and command better; but rather to think and believe that what is written there is better and truer, even if it lies hidden, than what we can be wise about by ourselves.

## The gifts of the Holy Spirit are utterly necessary.

**7.** 10. After those two steps of fear and piety one comes to the third step, that of knowledge, of which I have now undertaken to treat. For in it every student of the divine Scriptures exercises himself, about to find in them nothing else than that God is to be loved for the sake of God, and the neighbor for the sake of God; and to love him indeed with the whole heart, the whole soul, the whole mind, but the neighbor as oneself,[^7] that is, so that the whole love of neighbor, like the love of ourselves, may be referred to God. We treated of these two precepts in the previous book when we were dealing with things.

It is necessary, then, that each one first find in the Scriptures that he, entangled in love of this world—that is, of temporal things—is far removed from such great love of God and such great love of neighbor as the Scripture prescribes. Then indeed that fear by which he thinks of the judgment of God, and that piety by which he cannot but believe and yield to the authority of the holy books, compel him to mourn for himself. For this knowledge makes the person of good hope not boast but lament; and by this disposition he obtains, by diligent prayers, the consolation of the divine help, lest he be broken by despair, and he begins to be on the fourth step, that of fortitude, on which one hungers and thirsts for righteousness.[^8] For by this disposition he extracts himself from every deadly delight in passing things, and turning away from there, he turns to the love of eternal things—namely, the unchangeable unity and the same Trinity.

## The wisdom of the heart is prepared for by the other gifts.

**7.** 11. When he has glimpsed this, as far as he can, radiating in the distance, and has felt that by the weakness of his sight he cannot bear that light, on the fifth step—that is, in the counsel of mercy[^9]—he purifies his soul, which is in a way tumultuous and clamoring against him because of the filth contracted from the appetite for lower things. Here he diligently exercises himself in the love of neighbor and is perfected in it. And now full of hope and sound in strength, when he has come even to the love of his enemy, he ascends to the sixth step,[^10] where now he purifies that very eye by which God can be seen, as far as he can be by those who die to this world as far as they can. For they see to the extent that they die to this world, but to the extent that they live here, they do not see.

And therefore, although the appearance of that light now begins to appear more certain and not only more bearable but even more pleasant, it is nevertheless still said to be seen *in an enigma and through a mirror*, because we walk by faith rather than by sight while we are on pilgrimage in this life, although our way of life is in heaven.[^11]

On this step, however, he so purifies the eye of the heart that he does not prefer or even compare his neighbor to the truth; therefore not himself either, since not even the one whom he loves as himself. This holy person, then, will be of such a simple and cleansed heart[^12] that he will neither be turned aside from the truth by zeal to please human beings, nor by regard for avoiding any of his own discomforts that are opposed to this life. Such a son ascends to wisdom, which is the last and seventh, which he enjoys fully, at peace and tranquil.[^13] For *the fear of the Lord is the beginning of wisdom*. For from that one stretches and comes, through these steps, to wisdom itself.

## On the canonical and apocryphal books.

**8.** 12. But let us refer our consideration back to that third step,[^14] concerning which I have undertaken to discuss and treat whatever the Lord may suggest. He, then, will be the most skillful investigator of the divine Scriptures who first has read them all and become acquainted with them, and, if not yet by understanding, at least by reading—that is, those which are called canonical.

For the rest[^15] he will read more safely, instructed in the faith of the truth,[^16] lest they preoccupy a weak mind and, deluding it with dangerous lies and phantasms, prejudge something against sound understanding.

But in the canonical Scriptures let him follow the authority of as many of the Catholic Churches as possible, among which let those certainly be that have deserved to have apostolic Sees and to receive epistles.

He will hold, therefore, this measure in the canonical Scriptures: that he prefer those which are received by all the Catholic Churches to those which some do not receive. But among those which are not received by all, let him prefer those which the more numerous and weightier Churches receive to those which fewer Churches and of lesser authority hold.

But if he finds some held by the more numerous, others by the weightier—although he cannot easily find this[^17]—I think they are nevertheless to be held of equal authority.

## The canon of the divine books is set forth.

**8.** 13. The whole canon of the Scriptures, in which we say this consideration is to be exercised,[^18] is contained in these books: the five of Moses, that is, *Genesis, Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers, Deuteronomy*, and one book of *Jesus Nave*,[^19] one of *Judges*, one little book called *Ruth*, which seems to pertain rather to the beginning of *Kingdoms*; then four of *Kingdoms*[^20] and two of *Chronicles*,[^21] not following in sequence but, as it were, joined on at the side and proceeding alongside. This is the history that contains times connected together and the order of events. There are others, as it were of a different order, which are connected neither to this order nor to one another, such as *Job* and *Tobit* and *Esther* and *Judith* and the two books of *Maccabees* and the two of *Esdras*,[^22] which seem rather to follow upon that ordered history brought to a close at *Kingdoms* or *Chronicles*.

Then the Prophets, among which there is one book of David, the *Psalms*, and three of Solomon: *Proverbs, the Song of Songs*, and *Ecclesiastes*. For those two books, one inscribed *Wisdom* and the other *Ecclesiasticus*, are said to be of Solomon by a certain likeness. For it is most consistently held that Jesus Sirach wrote them;[^23] yet, since they have deserved to be received into authority, they are to be numbered among the prophetic books. The remaining books are those of the ones properly called Prophets, the individual books of the twelve Prophets, which, being connected to one another, since they have never been separated, are reckoned as one; the names of which Prophets are these: *Hosea, Joel, Amos, Obadiah, Jonah, Micah, Nahum, Habakkuk, Zephaniah, Haggai, Zechariah, Malachi*. Then there are four Prophets of larger volumes: *Isaiah, Jeremiah, Daniel, Ezekiel*.[^24] With these forty-four books the authority of the Old Testament is brought to a close;

but that of the New is brought to a close with the four books of the Gospel: *according to Matthew, according to Mark, according to Luke, according to John*; the fourteen *Epistles* of the apostle Paul: *to the Romans, to the Corinthians* two, *to the Galatians, to the Ephesians, to the Philippians, to the Thessalonians* two, *to the Colossians, to Timothy* two, *to Titus, to Philemon, to the Hebrews*; two of Peter; three of John; one of Jude and one of James; the one book of the *Acts of the Apostles*[^25] and the one book of the *Apocalypse* of John.

## By what method one should devote oneself to the study of Scripture.

**9.** 14. In all these books those who fear God and are gentle through piety seek the will of God. The first observance of this work and labor is, as we have said, to know these books, and, even if not yet to the point of understanding, at least by reading either to commit them to memory or not to leave them altogether unknown. Then those things which are openly set down in them, whether precepts for living or rules for believing, are to be investigated more skillfully and diligently. And each one finds the more of these the more capacious his understanding is. For in those things which are set down openly in the Scriptures are found all those which contain faith and the morals of living—namely, hope and love, of which we treated in the previous book.

Then indeed, once a certain familiarity with the very language of the divine Scriptures has been gained, one must proceed to opening up and discussing the things that are obscure, so that examples may be taken from the more manifest passages for illustrating the more obscure expressions, and certain testimonies of established statements may remove doubt from uncertain ones. In this matter memory is of the greatest value; and if it is lacking, it cannot be supplied by these precepts.[^26]

## Scripture is often not understood because of signs.

**10.** 15. There are two causes why the things that are written are not understood: if they are concealed by either unknown or ambiguous signs. Now signs are either literal or figurative. They are called literal when they are employed to signify those things on account of which they were instituted, as when we say "ox" when we mean the animal that all who share the Latin language with us call by this name. They are figurative when the very things which we signify by literal words are taken to signify something else, as when we say "ox" and by these two syllables understand the animal that is usually called by this name, but again, through that animal, we understand the Evangelist,[^27] whom Scripture signified, as the Apostle interprets, saying: *you shall not muzzle the ox that treads out the grain*.

## To remove ignorance of signs, knowledge of the languages is necessary, and especially of Greek and Hebrew.

**11.** 16. Against unknown literal signs the great remedy is knowledge of the languages. And those who speak the Latin language, whom we have now undertaken to instruct, need two others for the knowledge of the divine Scriptures—namely, Hebrew and Greek—so that one may have recourse to the prior exemplars if the boundless variety of the Latin translators has brought any doubt. Although we also often find Hebrew words not translated in the books, such as *amen* and *alleluia* and *racha*[^28] and *hosanna* and any others there may be.

Of these, some, on account of a more sacred authority, have had their antiquity preserved, although they could have been translated, such as *amen* and *alleluia*;[^29] others, however, are said not to have been able to be carried over into another language, such as the other two we set down. For there are certain words of particular languages which cannot pass into the use of another language through translation. And this happens especially with interjections, which are words that signify a motion of the mind rather than any part of a connected thought.[^30] For these two also are reported to be such; for they say that *racha* is the voice of one indignant, *hosanna* of one rejoicing.

But not on account of these few things, which it is very easy to note and inquire about, but on account of the diversities, as has been said, of the translators of those languages, the knowledge is necessary. For those who translated the Scriptures from Hebrew into Greek[^31] can be counted, but the Latin translators in no way. For as in the first times of the faith a Greek manuscript came into anyone’s hands and he seemed to himself to have a little ability in both languages, he ventured to translate.

## The diversity of translations is useful. From the ambiguity of words the error of translators arises.

**12.** 17. This very thing has helped understanding more than it has hindered it, provided only that the readers are not negligent. For the inspection of several manuscripts has often clarified some of the more obscure statements, as in that saying of the prophet Isaiah, where one translator says: *and you shall not despise the members of your own seed*,[^32] but another says: *and you shall not despise your own flesh*; each bears witness to the other in turn. For the one is explained by the other, since "flesh" could be taken literally, so that each would think himself admonished not to despise his own body; and "the members of one’s own seed" could be understood figuratively as Christians, born spiritually with us from the same seed of the word.[^33]

But now, the sense of the translators having been compared, the more probable view is that the precept is literally about not despising blood relatives, since, when you refer "the members of one’s seed" to the flesh, blood relatives most readily come to mind. Hence I think comes that saying of the Apostle: *if by any means I may provoke to emulation my own flesh, that I may save some of them*; that is, that by provoking to emulation those who had believed, they too might believe. For he called the Jews his own flesh, on account of blood relationship.

Likewise that saying of the same Isaiah: *unless you believe, you will not understand*, another translated: *unless you believe, you will not abide*. Which of them followed the words is uncertain, unless the exemplars of the prior language are read. But nevertheless from both something great is conveyed to those who read knowingly. For it is difficult for translators to become so different from one another that they do not touch one another by some nearness.

Therefore, since understanding is in the eternal vision, but faith nourishes the little ones, as if with milk, in certain cradles of temporal things, and now *we walk by faith, not by sight*,[^34] and unless we walk by faith we cannot arrive at the vision which does not pass away but abides, when we cleave to the Truth through a purified understanding—for this reason the one said: *unless you believe, you will not abide*, and the other: *unless you believe, you will not understand*.

## Examples from which the necessity of learning the languages becomes clear.

**12.** 18. And from the ambiguity of the prior language a translator is often deceived who does not know the meaning well, and he carries over that signification which is utterly foreign to the sense of the writer. As certain manuscripts have: *their feet are sharp to shed blood*;[^35] for the Greek word means both "sharp" and "swift." He, then, saw the meaning who translated: *their feet are swift to shed blood*; but that other one, dragged off in the other direction by the double sign, went astray. And some things indeed are not obscure, but false. Their condition is different; for one must rather prescribe that such manuscripts are not to be understood but corrected. Hence too comes this: that, since the Greek word for "calf" is so spelled, some did not understand that the other Greek word means "plantings" and translated it *calf-shoots*.[^36] This error has preoccupied so many manuscripts that it is scarcely found written otherwise. And yet the meaning is most manifest, because it becomes clear from the following words; for *adulterous plantings will not put down deep roots* is more fittingly said than *calf-shoots*, which walk on the ground with their feet and do not cling by roots. The rest of the context in that place also preserves this rendering.

## In the use of the Latin language, if one grasps the matter, the words will follow.

**13.** 19. But since even what the meaning itself is, which several translators each try to express according to his own ability and judgment, does not appear unless it is examined in the language they are translating, and since the translator for the most part wanders astray from the sense of the author if he is not most learned;

either knowledge of those languages from which Scripture has come into Latin is to be sought, or the translations of those who have bound themselves too closely to the words are to be kept—not because they suffice, but so that from them the liberty or the error of others may be detected, those who preferred to follow in translating the meanings rather than the words.

For not only single words but also expressions are often carried over which cannot pass into the use of the Latin language at all, if one wishes to keep to the custom of the ancients who spoke Latin. These sometimes take nothing from the understanding, but nevertheless they offend those who take more delight in things, since even in the signs of things a certain integrity of their own is preserved.

For what is called a solecism is nothing else than when words are not fitted together by the law by which those fitted them together who spoke before us not without some authority. For whether one says *inter homines* or *inter hominibus*[^37] does not concern one who knows things.

Likewise, what else is a barbarism than a word uttered not with those letters or that sound with which it is usually uttered by those who spoke Latin before us? For whether one says *ignoscere* with the third syllable long or short does not much concern the one who asks God to forgive his sins, in whatever way that word may have been able to sound. What, then, is integrity of expression except the preservation of another’s custom, confirmed by the authority of the ancient speakers?

## One should not be enslaved to the language of profane authors.

**13.** 20. But nevertheless people are the more offended by such things the weaker they are, and they are the weaker the more they wish to seem learned—not in the knowledge of things, by which we are built up, but in that of signs, by which it is altogether difficult not to be puffed up,[^38] since even the knowledge of things itself often raises the neck, unless it is held down by the Lord’s yoke.[^39]

For what harm does it do to one who understands that it is written thus: *What is the land in which these dwell upon it, whether it is good or bad; and what are the cities in which they themselves inhabit in them*?[^40] I think this expression belongs rather to a foreign language than to any deeper sense.

That too, which we can no longer remove from the mouth of the singing peoples—*but upon him shall my sanctification flourish*—takes nothing at all from the meaning. The more skilled hearer, however, would prefer this to be corrected, so that it might be said not *floriet* but *florebit*; and nothing hinders the correction except the custom of the singers. These things, then, can easily even be disregarded, if one is unwilling to guard against things that take nothing from sound understanding.

But as for what the Apostle says: *what is foolish of God is wiser than men, and what is weak of God is stronger than men*,[^41] if anyone had wished to preserve the Greek expression in it, so as to say, "what is foolish of God is wiser of men; and what is weak of God is stronger of men,"[^42] the attention of a watchful reader would indeed reach the truth of the meaning, but nevertheless someone slower would either not understand or even understand perversely. For such an expression in the Latin language is not only faulty, but it also falls into ambiguity, so that "the foolish of men" or "the weak of men" might seem to be wiser or stronger than that of God. And yet even *wiser than men* is not free of ambiguity, even though it is free of solecism. For whether *hominibus* is said as the dative or the ablative does not appear except by the illumination of the meaning.[^43] It is therefore better said thus: "is wiser than men," and "is stronger than men."

## Whence knowledge of an unknown word and an unknown expression is to be dug out.

**14.** 21. About ambiguous signs we shall speak later; now we are dealing with unknown ones, of which there are two forms, as far as words are concerned. For either an unknown word makes the reader stick, or an unknown expression. If these come from foreign languages, either they must be sought from people who know those languages, or those very languages, if there is both leisure and ability, must be learned, or a comparison of several translators must be consulted. But if we are ignorant of some words and expressions of our own language, they become known by the habit of reading and hearing. Indeed, nothing is more to be committed to memory than those kinds of words and expressions that we do not know; so that when either someone more skilled occurs about whom we can inquire, or such a reading as may show, from what precedes or what follows or both, what force it has, or what that which we do not know signifies, we may, with memory helping, easily notice and learn.

And yet so great is the force of habit even for learning that those who have somehow been nourished and brought up in the holy Scriptures wonder more at other expressions and think them less Latin than those which they learned in the Scriptures and which are not found in the authors of the Latin language.

Here too the great number of translators, examined and discussed by comparing manuscripts, helps very much. Only let falsity be absent; for the skill of those who desire to know the divine Scriptures ought first of all to keep watch over correcting the manuscripts, so that the uncorrected may yield to the corrected, at least when they come from one and the same kind of translation.

## The Latin Itala version and the Greek of the Seventy translators are commended.

**15.** 22. But among the translations themselves, let the *Itala* be preferred to the rest; for it holds more closely to the words together with clarity of meaning. And for correcting any Latin versions, let the Greek ones be employed, among which, as far as the Old Testament is concerned, the authority of the *Seventy* translators excels.[^44] These, throughout all the more skilled Churches,[^45] are now said to have translated with such great presence of the Holy Spirit that there was one mouth of so many men.

And if, as is reported—and many not unworthy of belief proclaim it—when, separated each into his own cell, they had each translated, nothing was found in the manuscript of any one of them that was not found in the same words and in the same order of words in the rest, who would dare to compare anything with this authority, let alone prefer anything to it?

But if they conferred so that out of the common treatment and judgment of all there might be one voice, not even so does it befit or become any one man, of whatever skill, to aspire to correct the consensus of so many elders and learned men.

For this reason, even if something is found in the Hebrew exemplars otherwise than these set down, I think we must yield to the divine dispensation that was carried out through them: that the books which the Jewish nation was unwilling, whether out of religion or out of envy, to betray to the other peoples, were betrayed so long beforehand, by the power of King Ptolemy through his minister, to the nations who would believe through the Lord. And so it can be that they translated in such a way as the Holy Spirit, who was driving them and who had made one mouth for all, judged fitting for the nations. But nevertheless, as I said above, the comparison of these translators too, who clung more closely to the words, is not useless for often explaining the meaning.

The Latin manuscripts, then, of the Old Testament, as I had begun to say, are to be corrected, if necessary, by the authority of the Greek ones, and especially of those who, being the *Seventy*, are reported to have translated with one mouth. As for the books of the New Testament, if anything wavers among the Latin variants, there is no doubt that they ought to yield to the Greek, and especially to those found among the more learned and diligent Churches.

## For figurative signs to be understood, both knowledge of languages and of things helps.

**16.** 23. But in figurative signs, if perhaps any unknown ones force the reader to stick, they are to be investigated partly by knowledge of the languages, partly by knowledge of things. For the pool of Siloam has some value for likeness and without doubt conveys something secret—where the man whose eyes the Lord had anointed with mud made from spittle was ordered to wash his face. Yet, this being a name of an unknown language, unless the Evangelist had translated it, so great an understanding would lie hidden.[^46]

So too many Hebrew names which were not translated by the authors of those same books are, there is no doubt, of no small force and help for solving the enigmas of the Scriptures, if one could translate them. Certain men skilled in that language have conferred no small benefit on posterity by translating all those same words separately out of the Scriptures; and what Adam is, what Eve, what Abraham, what Moses; or even the names of places, what Jerusalem is, or Zion, or Jericho, or Sinai, or Lebanon, or Jordan, and whatever other names in that language are unknown to us. When these are opened up and translated, many figurative expressions in the Scriptures are made manifest.

## The use of figurative expressions is illustrated by examples.

**16.** 24. But ignorance of things makes figurative expressions obscure, when we are ignorant of the natures of animals, or stones, or herbs, or other things, which for the most part are set down in the Scriptures for the sake of some likeness. For as to what is known about the serpent—that it offers its whole body to those who strike it in place of its head—how greatly it illuminates that sense in which the Lord bids us be cunning as serpents,[^47] so that, namely, in place of our head, which is Christ,[^48] we may rather offer our body to persecutors, lest the Christian faith be, as it were, killed in us if, sparing our body, we deny God!

Or that other thing—that, squeezed through the narrows of a cave, it lays aside its old tunic and is said to receive new strength—how well it harmonizes with imitating that very cunning of the serpent and putting off the old man, as the Apostle says, that we may be clothed with the new;[^49] and putting it off through narrows, the Lord saying: *enter through the narrow gate*!

As, then, knowledge of the nature of the serpent illuminates the many likenesses that Scripture is accustomed to draw from this animal, so ignorance of certain animals, which it mentions no less by likenesses, hinders the interpreter very much. So too of stones, so of herbs, or whatever things are held by roots. For knowledge of the carbuncle, that it shines in the dark, illuminates many obscure things of the books too, wherever it is set down for the sake of likeness; and ignorance of the beryl or the diamond often shuts the doors of understanding. Nor for any other cause is it easy to understand that perpetual peace is signified by the little olive branch which the dove brought back when returning to the ark,[^50] except that we know both that the gentle touch of oil is not easily corrupted by another fluid, and that the tree itself is leafy perennially. But many, on account of ignorance of hyssop—while they do not know what force it has either for cleansing the lung or, as is said, for penetrating rocks with its roots, though it is a short and lowly herb—cannot at all find why it was said: *you shall sprinkle me with hyssop, and I shall be cleansed*.

## Access to many mysteries of the Scriptures is shut off when numbers are not known.

**16.** 25. Ignorance of numbers, too, makes many things set down figuratively and mystically in the Scriptures not understood.[^51] For a native wit, so to speak, cannot but be moved by what it means that Moses and Elijah and the Lord himself fasted for forty days.[^52] A certain figurative knot of this action is not loosed except by knowledge and consideration of this number. For it has ten taken four times, as though the knowledge of all things woven into times. For by the number four both daily and yearly courses are accomplished: the daily by the morning, midday, evening, and nocturnal spaces of the hours; the yearly by the spring, summer, autumn, and winter months. But while we live in times, we must abstain and fast from the delight in times, for the sake of the eternity in which we wish to live, although by the very courses of the times the very teaching of despising times and seeking eternal things is conveyed to us.

Furthermore, the number ten signifies the knowledge of the Creator and of the creature; for the Trinity is of the Creator, and the number seven indicates the creature, on account of life and body. For in that life there are three, whence also *God is to be loved with the whole heart, the whole soul, the whole mind*; but in the body the four elements of which it consists appear most manifestly.[^53]

In this ten, then, while it is conveyed to us temporally—that is, multiplied by four—we are admonished to live chastely and continently away from the delight in times; that is, to fast for forty days. This the Law, whose person is in Moses, this prophecy, whose person Elijah bears, this the Lord himself admonishes; who, having as it were testimony from the Law and the Prophets, shone forth in the midst between them on the mountain, with three disciples seeing and amazed.[^54]

Then it is asked thus, how fifty arises from the number forty—a number not slightly hallowed in our religion on account of Pentecost[^55]—and how, multiplied by three, on account of the three times, before the Law, under the Law, under grace, and on account of the name of the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit, with the Trinity itself more eminently added, it is referred to the mystery of the most purified Church and arrives at the one hundred and fifty-three fish which the nets caught when cast to the right side after the Resurrection of the Lord.[^56] Thus by many and various other forms of numbers certain secrets of likenesses are set down in the holy books, which are closed to readers on account of ignorance of numbers.[^57]

## Number and music are honorably set down in the Scriptures.

**16.** 26. Ignorance of certain musical matters also closes off and conceals not a few things. For someone has not inelegantly opened up some figures of things from the difference between the psaltery and the lyre. And it is asked, not without point, among the learned whether the *psaltery of ten strings* has some law of music that compels it to such a number of strings; or whether, if it does not, the very number is for that reason all the more to be received in a sacred sense, either on account of the Decalogue of the Law—about which number too, if it is asked, it is to be referred only to the Creator and the creature[^58]—or on account of the ten itself expounded above.

And that number of the building of the temple, which is mentioned in the Gospel—namely, *forty-six years*—sounds something musical[^59] and, referred to the structure of the Lord’s body, on account of which the mention of the temple was made, compels some heretics to confess that the Son of God was clothed not with a false but with a true and human body. For we find both number and music honorably set down in many places in the holy Scriptures.

## The origin of the fable of the nine Muses as told by Varro.

**17.** 27. For the errors of the gentile superstitions are not to be heeded, which have feigned that the nine Muses are the daughters of Jupiter and Memory.[^60] Varro refutes them, than whom I do not know whether anyone among them could be more learned or more curious about such matters. For he says that some city—I do not recall the name—commissioned from three craftsmen three statues each of the Muses, to place as a gift in the temple of Apollo, so that whichever of the craftsmen had fashioned the more beautiful ones, they would choose and buy from him especially. And so it happened that those craftsmen too produced their works equally beautiful, and all nine pleased the city and all were bought to be dedicated in the temple of Apollo. He says that the poet Hesiod afterward imposed names upon them. Therefore Jupiter did not beget the nine Muses, but three workmen created three each.

But that city had not commissioned three because it had seen them in dreams, or because they had shown themselves in that number to the eyes of any of those men, but because it was easy to observe that every sound, which is the material of songs, is by nature threefold. For it is either produced by the voice, as is that of those who sing with the throat without an instrument; or by breath, as of trumpets and flutes; or by striking, as in lyres and drums and any other things that are tuneful by being struck.[^61]

## If profane authors have said anything well, it is not to be spurned.

**18.** 28. But whether what Varro related is so or not, we nevertheless ought not, on account of the superstition of profane people, to flee music, if we can seize from it anything useful for understanding the holy Scriptures; nor to turn aside to their theatrical trifles, if we discuss something about lyres and instruments that has value for grasping spiritual things.

For neither ought we to have refused to learn letters because they say Mercury is their discoverer; nor, because they dedicated temples to Justice and Virtue and preferred to adore in stones what ought to be borne in the heart, must justice and virtue therefore be fled by us.

On the contrary, let whoever is a good and true Christian understand that truth, wherever he finds it, belongs to his Lord; and, conferring and acknowledging it even in the sacred writings, let him repudiate superstitious figments, and let him grieve over and beware of those *who, knowing God, did not glorify him as God or give thanks, but became vain in their thoughts, and their foolish heart was darkened; for, saying that they were wise, they became fools, and changed the glory of the incorruptible God into the likeness of an image of corruptible man and of birds and of four-footed beasts and of serpents*.

## Two kinds of teaching found among the heathen.

**19.** 29. But, to explain this whole topic more diligently, for it is especially necessary: there are two kinds of teaching that are practiced even in gentile customs. One is of those things which human beings have instituted, the other of those which they have observed as already done or as divinely instituted.[^62] That which is according to human institutions is partly superstitious, partly not.

## On the emptiness and malice of superstition.

**20.** 30. Superstitious is whatever has been instituted by human beings pertaining to the making and worshiping of idols, or to worshiping the creature or any part of the creature as God, or to consultations and certain pacts of significations agreed and confederated with demons—such as are the endeavors of the magical arts, which the poets are accustomed rather to mention than to teach.[^63] Of this kind, but as it were with a more licensed vanity, are the books of the soothsayers and augurs. To this kind belong also all amulets and remedies, which the discipline of physicians too condemns, whether in incantations or in certain marks which they call "characters," or in any objects to be hung up and bound on, or even, in a way, to be danced—not for the tempering of bodies, but for certain significations either hidden or even manifest,

which they call by the milder name "physical," so that they may seem to entangle one not in superstition, as it were, but to benefit one by nature—such as are earrings on the top of each ear, or little rings made from the bones of ostriches on the fingers, or when you are told, while hiccupping, to hold your left thumb with your right hand.

## The folly of superstition to be laughed at. A witty saying of Cato.

**20.** 31. To these are joined thousands of most empty observances: if some limb has twitched; if, when friends are walking arm in arm, a stone or a dog or a boy has come between them. And the fact that they tread on a stone, as though it were a sunderer of friendship, is less troublesome than when they strike an innocent boy with a fist, if he runs between them as they walk together. But it is amusing that the boys are sometimes avenged by the dogs. For some are often so superstitious that they dare to strike even a dog that has come between them—not with impunity; for sometimes the dog quickly sends his striker from a vain remedy to a true physician.

Hence too are these: to tread on the threshold when one passes in front of one’s house; to go back to bed if anyone sneezes while putting on his shoes; to go back home if, setting out, he has stumbled; when a garment is gnawed by mice, to tremble more at the suspicion of future evil than to grieve at the present loss. Hence that elegant saying of Cato, who, when he was consulted by a certain man who said that his boots had been gnawed by mice, replied that this was no portent, but that it would truly have had to be reckoned a portent if the boots had gnawed the mice.

## The superstition of the astrologers, a notable sign of madness.

**21.** 32. Nor are those to be separated from this kind of pernicious superstition who are called genethliacs on account of their consideration of birthdays, but now are commonly called mathematicians.[^64] For these, although they pursue the true position of the stars when anyone is born, and sometimes even investigate it beforehand,[^65] nevertheless, in that they try from this to predict either our actions or the outcomes of our actions, they err too greatly and sell to unskilled people a wretched slavery. For when any free man has gone in to a mathematician of this kind, he gives money so that he may come out a slave either of Mars or of Venus, or rather of all the heavenly bodies, on which those who first went astray and proffered their error to posterity imposed names, either of beasts on account of likeness, or of human beings for honoring those very human beings.[^66]

For it is not to be wondered at, since even in nearer and more recent times they tried to dedicate the star which we call Lucifer to the honor and name of the Roman Caesar. And perhaps it would have been done and would have passed into antiquity, had not his grandmother Venus preoccupied this estate of the name, and could by no right transmit to her heirs what she had never possessed while alive or sought to possess.[^67]

For where a place was empty and was not held by the honor of any of the earlier dead, what usually happens in such matters was done. For instead of the months Quintilis and Sextilis[^68] we call them July and August, named from the honors of the men Julius Caesar and Augustus Caesar, so that anyone who wishes may easily understand that those stars too formerly wandered the sky without these names; but when those men died, whose memory people were either compelled by royal power to honor or were pleased by human vanity to honor, by imposing their names on the stars they seemed to themselves to lift those very dead men into heaven.

But by whatever names they are called by human beings, they are nevertheless stars which God instituted and ordered as he willed, and there is a fixed motion of theirs, by which times are distinguished and varied. To note this motion, how it stands when anyone is born, is easy by means of the rules found and written down by those whom holy Scripture condemns, saying: *for if they were able to know so much that they could estimate the world, how did they not more easily find its Lord*?

## The observation of the stars in order to know the course of life is a great error.

**22.** 33. But to wish, from that noting, to predict the morals, acts, and outcomes of those being born is a great error and a great madness. And among those, indeed, who have unlearned such things to be learned, this superstition is refuted without any doubt. For what they call constellations is a noting of the stars,[^69] how they stood when the one was born about whom these wretched people are consulted by the more wretched. But it can happen that some twins are brought forth from the womb in such close succession that no interval of time between them can be apprehended and noted in the numbers of the constellations. Hence it is necessary that some twins have the same constellations, while they do not have equal outcomes of the things they either do or suffer, but for the most part so unequal that one lives most happily, the other most unhappily,

as we have received that Esau and Jacob were born twins, so that Jacob, who was being born later, *was found holding with his hand the heel of his preceding brother*. Certainly the day and hour of these men being born could not be noted otherwise than that the constellation of both would be one. But how great a difference there is between the morals, deeds, labors, and successes of both, Scripture is witness, now spread abroad through the mouth of all the nations.

## Vain signs too, introduced by human presumption.

**22.** 34. For it is not to the point that they say the very smallest and narrowest moment of time which marks off the birth of twins has great power in the nature of things and in the most rapid swiftness of the heavenly bodies. For even if I should grant that it has the greatest power, nevertheless it cannot be found by the mathematician in the constellations, by inspecting which he professes to tell the fates. What, then, he does not find in the constellations—which he must necessarily inspect as one and the same, whether he is consulted about Jacob or about his brother—what does it profit him if it differs in the sky, which he rashly and securely defames, and does not differ on the chart, which he vainly and anxiously gazes upon?

For this reason these opinions too, established by human presumption upon certain signs of things, are to be referred to those same things as though to certain pacts and agreements with demons.

## A demon often deceives a person through a person.

**23.** 35. For from this it comes about that, by a certain hidden divine judgment, people desirous of evil things are handed over to be mocked and deceived according to the merits of their wills, by the prevaricating angels who mock and deceive them, to whom this lowest part of the world, according to the most beautiful order of things, has been subjected by the law of divine providence.[^70] By these mockeries and deceptions it comes about that, by those superstitious and pernicious kinds of divinations, many past and future things are told, and happen no otherwise than they are told, and many things turn out for the observers according to their observations; and entangled in these, they become more curious and insert themselves more and more into the manifold snares of a most pernicious error.

This kind of fornication of the soul the divine Scripture has healthfully not passed over in silence, nor did it so deter the soul from it as to deny that such things are to be shunned merely because they are spoken falsely by their professors, but it says even: *if they tell you, and it should so happen, do not believe them*. For not because the image of the dead Samuel foretold true things to King Saul are such sacrileges, by which that image was presented, any the less to be execrated; nor, because in the *Acts of the Apostles* a ventriloquist woman bore true testimony to the apostles of the Lord, did the apostle Paul therefore spare that spirit, rather than cleanse the woman by the rebuke and expulsion of that demon.[^71]

## The pestilent fellowship of human beings and demons is tolerated by God.

**23.** 36. All such arts, then, of either trifling or harmful superstition, established from a certain pestilent fellowship of human beings and demons as though by pacts of a faithless and deceitful friendship, are utterly to be repudiated and fled by a Christian: *not that an idol is anything*, says the Apostle, *but that what they sacrifice, they sacrifice to demons and not to God; and I do not wish you to become partners of demons*. But what the Apostle said about idols and about the sacrifices that are offered for their honor must be thought about all imaginary signs which draw one either to the worship of idols, or to worshiping the creature and its parts as God, or which pertain to the care of remedies and other observances. These are not established divinely, as though publicly, for the love of God and neighbor, but through private appetites for temporal things they scatter the hearts of the wretched.

In all these teachings, then, the fellowship of demons is to be dreaded and avoided, who, together with their prince the devil, try nothing else than to close and bar our return.[^72] But just as, concerning the stars which God founded and ordered, human and deceptive conjectures have been established by human beings, so too, concerning whatever things come into being at birth, or exist in any way by the administration of divine providence, many people have committed to writing many things conjectured by human suspicions, as though by rule, in case they should happen unusually—as if a mule should give birth or something should be struck by lightning.

## Curiosity and anxiety are the roots of many superstitions.

**24.** 37. All these have as much power as they have been confederated with demons by the presumption of minds, as though by a certain common language. Yet all these are full of pestilent curiosity, tormenting anxiety, deadly slavery. For they were not observed because they had power, but by observing and marking them it came about that they had power. And therefore they turn out differently for different people according to their thoughts and presumptions. For those spirits who wish to deceive procure such things for each one as they see him entangled by, through his suspicions and consents.

For just as, for example, the single shape of the letter "X," which is marked crosswise,[^73] means one thing among the Greeks and another among the Latins, not by nature but by agreement and consent in signifying; and therefore one who knows both languages, if he wishes to signify something in writing to a Greek, does not put this letter in that signification in which he puts it when he writes to a Latin; and "beta" is, with one and the same sound, the name of a letter among the Greeks, of a vegetable among the Latins;[^74] and when I say "lege," in these two syllables a Greek understands one thing, a Latin another;[^75]

just as, then, all these significations move minds according to the consent of each one’s society, and because the consent is different, they move differently; nor did human beings consent to them because they already had power for signification, but they have power because human beings consented to them;

so too those signs by which the pernicious fellowship of demons is procured have power according to the observances of each one. This is shown most manifestly by the rite of the augurs, who both before they observe and after they have held the observed signs act so as not to see the flights or hear the voices of birds, because these are no signs at all unless the consent of the observer is added.[^76]

## Among human institutions that are not superstitious, some are superfluous . . .

**25.** 38. When these have been cut away and uprooted from the Christian mind, the institutions of human beings that are not superstitious are next to be considered—that is, those established not with demons but with human beings themselves. For all things that have power among human beings because it has pleased them that they should have power are institutions of human beings; of which some are superfluous and luxurious institutions, some convenient and necessary.

For if those signs which actors make by dancing had power by nature, not by the institution and consent of human beings, in former times a herald would not have announced beforehand to the people of Carthage, while the pantomime danced, what the dancer wished to be understood. Many old men still remember this, by whose account we are accustomed to hear these things. And this is to be believed, because even now, if anyone unskilled enters a theater of such trifles, unless it is told him by another what those motions signify, he is wholly intent in vain.

Yet all seek a certain likeness in signifying, so that the signs themselves may, as far as they can, be like the things that are signified. But because something can be like something else in many ways, such signs do not hold among human beings unless consent is added.

## . . . but some are useful, indeed necessary.

**25.** 39. But in pictures and statues and other such imitative works, especially those of skilled craftsmen, no one errs when he sees likenesses, so as to recognize what things they are like. And this whole class is to be numbered among the superfluous institutions of human beings, except when it matters which of them is made for what cause and where and when and by whose authority. Finally, thousands of feigned fables and falsehoods, by whose lies human beings are delighted, are human institutions. And nothing is to be reckoned more proper to human beings, as having it from themselves, than whatever is false and mendacious.

But convenient and necessary institutions of human beings with human beings are whatever difference in the dress and adornment of the body has been agreed upon for distinguishing the sexes or ranks, and the innumerable kinds of significations without which human society is either not at all or less conveniently carried on; and whatever in weights and measures, and in the stamping or valuing of coins, are proper to each city and people, and other things of this kind, which, unless they were human institutions, would not be various among the different peoples, nor would they be changed even among the individual peoples at the discretion of their rulers.

## The wise Christian is capable of distinguishing among human institutions.

**25.** 40. But this whole part of human institutions, which serves the necessary use of life, is by no means to be fled by a Christian; rather, it is even to be considered and retained in memory as far as is sufficient.

**26.** 40. For certain human institutions are shadowy and somehow like natural things. Of these, those which pertain to the fellowship of demons, as has been said, are utterly to be repudiated and detested; but those which human beings have with human beings are to be taken up, insofar as they are not luxurious and superfluous—and especially the shapes of letters without which we cannot read, and the variety of languages as far as is sufficient, about which we disputed above.[^77] Of that kind too are the marks which those who have learned them are now properly called notaries for knowing.[^78]

These are useful, and they are not learned unlawfully, nor do they entangle one in superstition, nor enervate one with luxury, provided only that they occupy one so far as not to be an impediment to the greater things which they ought to serve in being attained.[^79]

## The treatment of human institutions is continued.

**27.** 41. But now those things which human beings have brought forth not by instituting, but by investigating things either accomplished in past times or divinely instituted, wherever they are learned, are not to be reckoned human institutions. Of these, some pertain to the sense of the body, others to the reason of the mind. But those which are reached by the sense of the body, we either believe when narrated, or perceive when demonstrated, or conjecture when experienced.[^80]

## On the usefulness of history.

**28.** 42. Whatever, then, that which is called history indicates about the order of past times helps us very much toward understanding the holy books, even if it is learned outside the Church in childhood education. For we are often asked about many things by means of the Olympiads and by the names of the consuls.[^81] And ignorance of the consulship in which the Lord was born and in which he suffered compelled some to err, so that they thought the Lord suffered at the age of forty-six, because it was said by the Jews that the temple was built over so many years, which had the image of the Lord’s body.[^82]

And we hold, indeed, by the authority of the Gospel that he was baptized at about thirty years of age;[^83] but how many years he afterward spent in this life, although it can be noticed from the very text of his actions,[^84] nevertheless, lest a fog of doubt arise from elsewhere, it is gathered more clearly and certainly from the history of the nations compared with the Gospel. For then it will be seen not to have been said in vain that the temple was built in forty-six years, so that, since this number could not be referred to the Lord’s age, it may be referred to the more secret instruction of the human body, which the only Son of God, *through whom all things were made*, did not disdain to put on for our sake.

## History is a strong aid for solving difficulties.

**28.** 43. But as for the usefulness of history—to pass over the Greeks—how great a question our Ambrose resolved, against the slanderers who are readers and lovers of Plato, who dared to say that our Lord Jesus Christ learned from the books of Plato all those sayings of his which they are compelled to admire and proclaim, since it cannot be denied that Plato existed long before the human coming of the Lord! Did not the said bishop, having considered the history of the nations, when he had found that Plato had set out for Egypt in the times of Jeremiah, where that Prophet then was, show that it is more probable that Plato was rather instructed in our writings through Jeremiah, so that he could teach or write those things which are rightly praised?[^85]

For before the writings of the Hebrew nation, in which the worship of the one God shone forth, from which *according to the flesh* our Lord came, not even Pythagoras himself existed, from whose successors these people assert that Plato learned theology. Thus, with the times considered, it becomes much more credible that these people rather had from our writings whatever good and true things they said, than that the Lord Jesus Christ had them from Plato’s—which is most insane to believe.

## A great difference between history and the inventions of the pagans.

**28.** 44. But when, in a historical narration, the past institutions of human beings too are narrated, history itself is not to be numbered among human institutions, because things that have passed and cannot now be made undone are to be held in the order of times, of which God is the founder and administrator.

For it is one thing to narrate things done, another to teach things to be done. History narrates things done faithfully and usefully; but the books of the soothsayers and any similar writings aim to teach things to be done or observed, with the boldness of an adviser, not with the faithfulness of an informant.

## Knowledge of animals, herbs, and other things conduces to the understanding of the Scriptures.

**29.** 45. There is also a narration like demonstration, by which not past but present things are indicated to those who do not know them. In this class are whatever things are written about the situation of places and the natures of animals, trees, herbs, stones, or other bodies.

We dealt with this class above[^86] and taught that knowledge of it has value for solving the enigmas of the Scriptures, not so that it may be employed as certain signs, as though for the remedies or contrivances of some superstition; for we have already distinguished and separated that kind from this lawful and free one. For it is one thing to say, "if you drink this herb ground up, your stomach will not hurt," and another to say, "if you hang this herb on your neck, your stomach will not hurt." There the healthful tempering is approved; here the superstitious signification is condemned.

Although, where there are no incantations and invocations and "characters,"[^87] it is for the most part doubtful whether the thing which is bound on or in any way attached for healing the body has power by the force of nature, which is to be freely employed, or comes about by a certain significative obligation.[^88] This a Christian ought to beware of the more prudently the more efficaciously it will seem to benefit. But where it lies hidden by what cause something has power, it matters with what intention each one uses it—at least in healing or tempering bodies, whether in medicine or in agriculture.

## On observing the stars.

**29.** 46. But of the stars to be known there is not a narration but a demonstration, very few of which Scripture mentions. Now just as the course of the moon, which is also solemnly employed for celebrating the Lord’s Passion annually, is known to very many, so to very few are the rising or setting or any other moments of the other stars also most well known, without any error. This knowledge by itself, although it does not bind one in superstition, nevertheless helps the treatment of the divine Scriptures not much—indeed almost nothing—and by its fruitless intentness rather hinders; and because it is familiar to the most pernicious error of those who sing foolish fates, it is more conveniently and honorably disregarded. But besides the demonstration of present things, it also has something like the narration of past things, since from the present position and motion of the stars one may regularly run back to their past traces as well.

It also has regular conjectures of future things, not suspicious and ominous but settled and certain; not so that we may try to draw something from them onto our own fates[^89] and outcomes, such as are the deliriums of the genethliacs, but only insofar as it pertains to the stars themselves.

For just as one who reckons the moon, when he has observed today what its phase is, can say what phase it was however many years before and what phase it will be however many years after, so about each of the stars those who reckon them skillfully are accustomed to answer. About this whole knowledge, as far as concerns its use, I have disclosed what seems to me.

## What the mechanical arts contribute to the same end.

**30.** 47. Of the other arts too, by which something is fabricated—whether what remains after the operation of the craftsman as something made by him, such as a house and a bench and some vessel and other such things; or those which exhibit a certain ministry to God as he works, such as medicine and agriculture and navigation; or those whose whole effect is the action, such as those of dances and runnings and wrestlings: of all these arts, then, experiences of past things also make future things to be conjectured.

For no craftsman of these moves his limbs in working unless he weaves the memory of past things together with the expectation of future things. But the knowledge of these is to be employed slightly and in passing in this human life, not for working—unless perhaps some duty compels it, about which we are not now treating—but for judging, lest we be altogether ignorant of what Scripture means to convey when it inserts some figurative expressions about these arts.

## On the art of dialectic and its usefulness and dangers.

**31.** 48. There remain those things which pertain not to the senses of the body but to the reason of the mind, where the discipline of argument and of number reigns. But the discipline of argument is of the greatest value for penetrating and resolving all the kinds of questions that are in the holy writings. Only there is the lust for wrangling and a certain childish ostentation of deceiving an adversary to be guarded against.

For there are many things called sophisms, false conclusions of reasonings, and for the most part so imitating true ones that they deceive not only the slow but also the clever who are less diligently attentive. For someone proposed, saying to the one with whom he was speaking: "What I am, you are not." And the other agreed. For it was true in part, even by the very fact that the one was insidious, the other guileless. Then the first added: "But I am a human being." And when he had received this too from the other, he concluded, saying: "You, therefore, are not a human being."

This kind of captious conclusion Scripture, as I judge, detests in that place where it is said: *he who speaks sophistically is hateful*. Although even speech that is not captious, but nevertheless pursues ornaments of words more abundantly than gravity befits, is called sophistical.[^90]

## Prudence is necessary before a dialectician.

**31.** 49. There are also true connections of reasoning that have false statements, which follow upon the error of the one with whom the discussion is held. Yet these are brought in by a good and learned man for this purpose, that the one whose error they follow upon, blushing at them, may abandon that same error; for if he should wish to remain in it, he is necessarily compelled to hold also those things which he condemns. For the Apostle was not bringing in true things when he said: *neither has Christ risen*, and those other things: *our preaching is empty, and your faith is also empty*; and then other things which are altogether false, since both Christ has risen, and the preaching of those who announced this was not empty, nor the faith of those who had believed it. But these false things were most truly connected to that statement by which it was said that there is no resurrection of the dead. And when these false things have been rejected, since they were true if the dead do not rise, the consequence will be the resurrection of the dead.

Since, then, there are true connections not only of true but also of false statements, it is easy to learn the truth of connections even in those schools which are outside the Church. But the truth of statements is to be investigated in the holy ecclesiastical books.[^91]

## The truth of connections was not instituted by human beings but only observed.

**32.** 50. The very truth of connections, however, was not instituted but noticed and noted by human beings, so that they could either learn or teach it. For it is perpetual in the reason of things and divinely instituted. For just as one who narrates the order of times does not himself compose it, and one who shows the situations of places or the natures of animals or plants or stones does not show things instituted by human beings; and one who demonstrates the stars and their motions does not demonstrate a thing instituted by himself or by any human being: so too one who says, "When that which follows is false, it is necessary that that which precedes be false," says most truly, and does not himself make it to be so, but only demonstrates that it is so.

From this rule is that which we mentioned about the apostle Paul; for the premise is that there is no resurrection of the dead, which those said whose error the Apostle wished to destroy. Now upon that preceding statement, by which they said there is no resurrection of the dead, there necessarily follows: *neither has Christ risen*.[^92] But this which follows is false; for Christ has risen: therefore that which precedes is also false. But that which precedes is that there is no resurrection of the dead; therefore there is a resurrection of the dead. The whole of which is briefly said thus: if there is no resurrection of the dead, *neither has Christ risen*; but Christ has risen, therefore there is a resurrection of the dead.

This, then—that when the consequent is removed, that which precedes is also necessarily removed—human beings did not institute but showed. And this rule pertains to the truth of connections, not to the truth of statements.

## In false statements conclusions can be true, and in true ones false.

**33.** 51. But in this place, when the resurrection was being treated, both the rule of connection is true, and the statement itself in the conclusion. In false statements, however, the truth of connection is in this manner: let us suppose that someone has conceded, "If a snail is an animal, it has a voice." This being conceded, when it has been proved that the snail does not have a voice, since, the consequent being removed, that which precedes is removed, it is concluded that the snail is not an animal. This statement is false, but from the false thing conceded the connection of the conclusion is true.

And so the truth of a statement has value through itself, but the truth of a connection[^93] consists in the opinion or concession of the one with whom the discussion is held.

But, as we said above, what is false is inferred by a true connection so that the one whose error we wish to correct may repent of having held the premises, whose consequences he sees are to be rejected. From this it is now easy to understand that, just as in false statements there can be true conclusions, so in true statements there can be false ones.

For suppose that someone has proposed, "If that man is just, he is good," and it has been conceded; then has assumed, "But he is not just"; this likewise being conceded, has drawn the conclusion, "Therefore he is not good." And although all these may be true, nevertheless the rule of the conclusion is not true.

For just as, the consequent being removed, that which precedes is necessarily removed, it is not so that, the antecedent being removed, that which follows is necessarily removed. For it is true when we say, "If he is an orator, he is a human being"; from which proposition, if we assume, "But he is not an orator," it will not follow when you infer, "Therefore he is not a human being."

## It is one thing to know the laws of conclusions, another the truth of statements.

**34.** 52. For this reason it is one thing to know the rules of connections, another the truth of statements. In the former is learned what is "consequent," what "not consequent," what "incompatible." Consequent: "If he is an orator, he is a human being"; inconsequent: "If he is a human being, he is an orator"; incompatible: "If he is a human being, he is a quadruped." Here, then, judgment is made about the connection itself.

But in the truth of statements the statements themselves through themselves, not their connection, are to be considered. But when uncertain statements are joined to true and certain ones by a true connection, they too necessarily become certain.

But some boast of themselves, when they have learned the truth of connections, as though it were the truth of statements itself. And again, some, for the most part holding a true statement, wrongly despise themselves because they are ignorant of the laws of conclusion; whereas the one who knows that there is a resurrection of the dead is better than the one who knows that it is consequent that, if there is no resurrection of the dead, *neither has Christ risen*.

## The science of defining and dividing is not in itself false.

**35.** 53. Likewise the science of defining, dividing, and partitioning, although it is for the most part employed even on false things, is nevertheless not itself false, nor instituted by human beings, but discovered in the reason of things. For it is not, because the poets have been accustomed to employ it in their fables, and false philosophers, or even heretics—that is, false Christians—in the opinions of their error, therefore false that, neither in defining nor in dividing or partitioning, anything is to be embraced which does not pertain to the matter itself, or anything that pertains to be passed over. This is true even if the things that are defined or distributed are not true.

For the false itself too is defined, when we say that the false is the signification of a thing not standing as it is signified, or in some other way; which definition is true, although the false cannot be true. We can also divide, saying that there are two kinds of the false: one of those which can in no way be, the other of those which are not, although they could be. For one who says that seven and three are eleven says what can in no way be; but one who says, for example, that it rained on the Kalends of January, although it did not happen, nevertheless says what could happen. The definition, therefore, and division of false things can be most true, although the false things themselves are certainly not true.

## On argument of a higher grade, which is called eloquence.

**36.** 54. There are also certain precepts of richer argument which is now named eloquence, which are nonetheless true, although false things too can be persuaded by them;[^94] but because true things too can, the faculty itself is not blameworthy, but the perversity of those who use it badly.

For neither was this instituted by human beings—that the expression of love should win over the hearer, or that a brief and open narration should easily convey what it intends, and that its variety should hold the attentive without disdain, and the other such observances, which, whether in false or in true causes, are nevertheless true insofar as they make something to be either known or believed, or move minds to seeking or fleeing; and they were discovered rather because they are so, than instituted in order that they should be so.

## On the art of rhetoric and dialectic.

**37.** 55. But this part, when it is learned, is to be employed more for bringing forth the things that have been understood than for understanding. That other part, of conclusions and definitions and distributions, helps the interpreter very much: only let there be absent the error by which people seem to themselves to have learned the very truth of the happy life when they have learned these things. Although it for the most part happens that people more easily attain those things for the attaining of which these are learned, than the most knotty and thorny disciplines of such precepts.

As if someone wishing to give precepts of walking should warn that the hinder foot is not to be lifted unless you have set down the front one, and then should describe in detail how the hinges of the joints and knees ought to be moved. For he speaks the truth, nor can one walk otherwise; but people more easily walk by doing these things than they notice when they do them, or understand when they hear them. But those who cannot walk care much less for the things which they cannot attend to even by experience. Thus for the most part the clever person more quickly sees that a conclusion is not valid than he grasps its precepts, but the slow person does not see it, and much less what is prescribed about it.

And in all these things the very spectacles of truth often delight us more than we are helped by them in arguing or judging—except perhaps that they render minds more practiced, provided they do not also render them more malicious or more puffed up; that is, so that those who have learned these things either love to deceive by verisimilar speech and questions, or think they have attained something great by which they may set themselves before the good and innocent.

## On the science of numbers.

**38.** 56. But now it is clear to even the slowest person that the discipline of number was not instituted by human beings, but rather searched out and discovered. For not as Virgil willed the first syllable of "Italia," which the ancients pronounced short,[^95] and it was made long, can anyone bring it about, when he wills, that three times three either not be nine, or not be able to make a square figure, or not be triple in relation to the number three, sextuple in relation to six, and double in relation to none—since intelligible numbers do not have a half.[^96] Whether, then, numbers are considered in themselves, or applied to the laws of figures or of sounds or other motions,[^97] they have unchangeable rules, in no way instituted by human beings, but discovered by the sagacity of the clever.

## It is not given to all to transcend creatures.

**38.** 57. But whoever so loves all these things that he wishes to boast of himself among the unskilled, and does not rather seek whence are true the things which he has only felt to be true, and whence certain things are not only true but even unchangeable, which he has comprehended to be unchangeable, and thus, passing from the form of bodies up to the human mind, when he has found that this too is changeable, since it is now learned, now unlearned, yet set between the unchangeable truth above it and the changeable other things below it, so as to turn all to the praise and love of the one God, from whom he knows all things to be—he can seem learned, but to be wise in no way.

## Precepts of salvation are above all to be imparted to young people.

**39.** 58. For this reason it seems to me that it is healthfully prescribed to studious and clever young people who fear God and seek the happy life, that they not dare to follow securely any teachings which are practiced outside the Church of Christ, as though for laying hold of the happy life, but that they judge them soberly and diligently; and if they find any instituted by human beings, various on account of the diverse will of those instituting them, and unknown on account of the suspicions of those in error, especially if they also have a fellowship entered into with demons through certain significations as though by certain pacts and agreements, let them repudiate and detest them utterly; let them also estrange their zeal from the superfluous and luxurious institutions of human beings.

But those institutions of human beings which have value for the society of those living together, let them not neglect on account of the very necessity of this life. But in the other teachings which are found among the nations, besides the history of things either of past or present time pertaining to the senses of the body—among which the experiences and conjectures of the useful corporeal arts too are numbered—and besides the reason of argument and of number, I think there is nothing useful. In all of which this is to be held: *nothing too much!*[^98] and especially in those things which, pertaining to the senses of the body, are revolved in times and contained in places.

## On auxiliary aids for grasping Scripture.

**39.** 59. But just as certain people have made it possible for all the Hebrew and Syriac[^99] and Egyptian words and names, or any in another language that can be found in the holy Scriptures placed in them without translation, to be translated separately, and as Eusebius[^100] did concerning the history of times for the sake of the questions of the divine books which demand its use—what these, then, did about these matters, so that it should not be necessary for a Christian to labor in many things for the sake of a few;

so I see it can be done, if it should delight any of those who are able to expend kindly effort indeed on fraternal usefulness, that whatever places of the earth, or animals, or herbs and trees, or stones, or unknown metals, and whatever kinds of things Scripture mentions, he may, arranging them by class, commit to writing the exposition of them alone. It can also be done concerning numbers, that the explained reason only of those numbers which the divine Scripture mentions may be written down.

Some or all of which things have perhaps already been done, just as we have found many things which we did not think had been worked out and written down by good and learned Christians; but they lie hidden either on account of the throngs of the negligent or on account of the concealments of the envious.

Whether this can be done concerning the reason of argument I do not know. And it seems to me it cannot, because it is bound together throughout the whole text of the Scriptures in the manner of sinews; and therefore it helps readers more for resolving and explaining ambiguities, about which we shall speak later, than for knowing unknown signs, about which we are now treating.

## If the heathen have said anything rightly, it is to be turned to our use.

**40.** 60. But the philosophers, as they are called, if they have perhaps said anything true and accommodated to our faith—especially the Platonists—are not only not to be dreaded, but are even to be claimed from them, as from unjust possessors, for our use. For just as the Egyptians had not only idols and heavy burdens, which the people of Israel detested and fled, but also vessels and ornaments of gold and silver, and clothing, which that people, going out from Egypt, secretly claimed for itself as though for a better use—not by its own authority, but by the precept of God, the Egyptians themselves unknowingly lending the things which they were not using well;[^101]

so all the teachings of the gentiles have not only simulated and superstitious figments and heavy loads of superfluous labor, which each one of us, going out from the fellowship of the gentiles under Christ’s leadership, ought to abominate and avoid, but they also contain liberal disciplines more apt for the use of truth, and certain most useful precepts of morals; and some true things are found among them concerning the worship of the one God himself.

This gold and silver of theirs, as it were—which they did not institute themselves, but dug out, as from certain mines of divine providence, which is everywhere infused, and which they perversely and injuriously abuse for the service of demons—the Christian, when he separates himself in mind from their wretched fellowship, ought to take from them for the just use of preaching the Gospel. Their clothing too—that is, institutions indeed of human beings, but nevertheless accommodated to human society, which we cannot lack in this life—it is lawful to receive and to have, to be turned to Christian use.

## Christian teachers were skilled in the knowledge of the pagans.

**40.** 61. For what else have many good believers of ours done? Do we not see with how much gold and silver and clothing Cyprian, that most sweet teacher and most blessed martyr, came out laden from Egypt? With how much Lactantius? With how much Victorinus, Optatus, Hilary, to be silent about the living?[^102] With how much innumerable Greeks? This very thing Moses, that most faithful servant of God, had done before, of whom it is written that *he was instructed in all the wisdom of the Egyptians*.

To all of these men the superstitious custom of the nations—and especially in those times when, shaking off the yoke of Christ, it persecuted Christians—would never have lent the disciplines which it held useful, had it suspected that they would be turned to the use of worshiping the one God, by which the vain worship of idols would be cut down. But they gave their gold and silver and clothing to the people of God going out from Egypt, not knowing how the things they gave would be rendered *to the service of Christ*. For that which was done in *Exodus* was without doubt figurative, so as to prefigure this.[^103] Let me have said this without prejudice to another understanding either equal or better.

## What kind of soul the study of sacred Scripture requires. On the properties of hyssop.

**41.** 62. But the student of the divine Scriptures, instructed in this way, when he begins to approach them for searching them out, let him not cease to think of that apostolic saying: *knowledge puffs up, but love builds up*. For thus he perceives that, although he comes out rich from Egypt, nevertheless, unless he has kept the Passover, he cannot be saved. *But Christ our Passover has been sacrificed*, and nothing teaches us more by the sacrifice of Christ than what he himself cries out, as though to those whom he sees laboring in Egypt under Pharaoh: *Come to me, you who labor and are burdened, and I will refresh you. Take my yoke upon you and learn from me, for I am gentle and humble of heart; and you will find rest for your souls. For my yoke is easy and my burden is light*. To whom, except to the gentle and humble of heart, whom knowledge does not puff up, but love builds up?

Let them remember, then, that those who celebrated the Passover at that time through the imaginary forms of shadows,[^104] when they were ordered to mark the doorposts with the blood of the lamb, marked them with *hyssop*. This herb is humble and gentle, and nothing is stronger and more penetrating than its roots, so that, *rooted and grounded in love*, we may be able *to comprehend with all the saints what is the breadth and length and height and depth*—that is, the Cross of the Lord.

Of which the breadth is said to be in the transverse wood, on which the hands are stretched out; the length, from the ground up to that breadth, where the whole body from the hands downward is fixed; the height, from the breadth upward to the top, where the head adheres; but the depth is that which, fixed in the earth, is hidden.

By this sign of the cross every Christian action is described: to work well in Christ and to adhere to him perseveringly, to hope for heavenly things, not to profane the sacraments.[^105] Purified by this action, we shall be able *to know also the love of Christ which surpasses knowledge*, by which he is equal to the Father, through whom all things were made,[^106] so that we may be filled unto all the fullness of God.

There is also in hyssop a purgative power, lest, with knowledge puffing one up over the riches carried off from Egypt, the swollen lung breathe out something proudly: *you shall sprinkle me*, he says, *with hyssop, and I shall be cleansed; you shall wash me, and I shall be made whiter than snow. To my hearing you shall give exultation and gladness*. Then he consequently adds, to show that purification from pride is signified by hyssop: *the humbled bones shall exult*.

## A comparison of sacred Scripture with profane knowledge.

**42.** 63. But by however much smaller is the supply of gold, silver, and clothing which that people carried off with itself from Egypt, in comparison with the riches which they afterward obtained at Jerusalem, which are shown especially in King Solomon,[^107] so great becomes all the knowledge—useful knowledge, that is—gathered from the books of the gentiles, if it is compared with the knowledge of the divine Scriptures. For whatever a person has learned outside, if it is harmful, is condemned there; if it is useful, is found there. And when anyone has found there all the things he usefully learned elsewhere, he will find there much more abundantly the things which are learned nowhere else at all, but only in the wonderful sublimity and wonderful lowliness of those Scriptures.[^108]

Endowed, then, with this instruction, when unknown signs do not hinder the reader, gentle and humble of heart, gently subjected to Christ and burdened with a light load, grounded and rooted and built up in love which knowledge cannot puff up, let him approach the consideration and discussion of the ambiguous signs in the Scriptures, about which I shall undertake to speak in the third volume, as the Lord shall deign to grant.
