# Book III

## Introduction to this book.

**1.** 1. The person who fears God diligently inquires after his will in the holy Scriptures. And lest he love quarrels, he is made gentle by piety; he is also forearmed with knowledge of the languages, lest he stick at unknown words and expressions; forearmed too with knowledge of certain necessary things, lest he be ignorant of the force or nature of those which are employed for the sake of likeness;

aided too by the correctness of the manuscripts, which the skillful diligence of correction has procured; let him come thus instructed to discussing and resolving the ambiguities of the Scriptures.

And so that he may not be deceived by ambiguous signs, insofar as he can be instructed by us—but it can happen that he may laugh at these ways, which we wish to show, as childish, either by the greatness of his native ability or by the clarity of greater illumination; but nevertheless, as I had begun to say, insofar as he can be instructed by us who is in such a state of mind as to be able to be instructed by us—let him know that the ambiguity of Scripture is either in literal words or in figurative ones, which kinds we demonstrated in the second book.

## Ambiguity is to be removed by distinguishing the words.

**2.** 2. But when literal words make Scripture ambiguous, the first thing to be seen is that we have not divided or pronounced it badly. When, therefore, applied attention has perceived that it is uncertain how it is to be divided or how pronounced, let it consult the rule of faith, which it has received from the plainer places of the Scriptures and from the authority of the Church; about which we treated sufficiently when we spoke of *things* in the first book. But if both, or even all the parts of the ambiguity, should there be several, sound according to the faith, the text of the discourse itself, from the preceding and following parts which placed that ambiguity in the middle, remains to be consulted, so that we may see to which of the several meanings that present themselves it casts its vote and allows itself to be woven together.

## Examples illustrating the doctrine of dividing rightly.

**2.** 3. Now consider examples.[^1] That heretical division: *In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and God was*, so that the sense is different: *This Word was in the beginning with God*, refuses to confess that the Word is God. But this is to be refuted by the rule of faith, by which it is prescribed for us, concerning the equality of the Trinity, that we say: *and the Word was God*, then subjoin: *this was in the beginning with God*.[^2]

## Likewise other examples.

**2.** 4. But that ambiguity of division which resists the faith on neither side, and is therefore to be judged by the text of the discourse itself, is where the Apostle says: *and what I should choose I do not know. But I am pressed from the two: having a desire to be dissolved and to be with Christ; for that is by much the best: to remain in the flesh is necessary on your account*. For it is uncertain whether it should be *having a desire from the two*, or *I am pressed from the two*, so that the other is joined on, *having a desire to be dissolved and to be with Christ*. But since it follows thus: *for that is by much the best*, it appears that he says he has a desire for that best, so that, although he is pressed from the two, he nevertheless has a desire for the one, a necessity for the other—namely, a desire to be with Christ, a necessity to remain in the flesh.

This ambiguity is judged by one following word, which is placed there: *for*. Those translators who took away this particle were led rather by that meaning, so that he should seem not only to be pressed from the two, but also to have a desire for the two. So, then, it is to be divided thus: *and what I should choose I do not know. But I am pressed from the two*; which division is followed by: *having a desire to be dissolved and to be with Christ*. And as though it were asked why he has a desire for this rather: *for that is by much the best*, he says. Why, then, is he pressed from the two? Because there is a necessity of remaining, which he subjoined thus: *to remain in the flesh is necessary on your account*.

## The ambiguity of certain divisions is unavoidable.

**2.** 5. But where the ambiguity can be unfolded neither by the prescript of faith nor by the text of the discourse itself, nothing prevents dividing it according to whichever of the meanings that present themselves. As is that passage to the Corinthians: *Having therefore these promises, dearly beloved, let us cleanse ourselves from all defilement of flesh and spirit, perfecting sanctification in the fear of God. Receive us; we have wronged no one*.[^3] For it is doubtful whether it is *let us cleanse ourselves from all defilement of flesh and spirit*, according to that meaning: *that she may be holy both in body and in spirit*, or *let us cleanse ourselves from all defilement of flesh*, so that the sense is different: *and, perfecting the sanctification of the spirit in the fear of God, receive us*. Such ambiguities of division, then, are in the power of the reader.

## By what method ambiguity arising from pronunciation is settled.

**3.** 6. But whatever we have said about ambiguous divisions, the same things are to be observed also in ambiguous pronunciations. For these too, unless they are vitiated by the excessive carelessness of the reader, are corrected either by the rules of faith or by the connection of the preceding or following discourse. And if neither of these is employed for correction, they will nonetheless remain doubtful, so that in whatever way the reader pronounces, he is not at fault.

For unless faith calls one back—by which we believe that God will not accuse against his elect and that Christ will not condemn his elect—that passage can be pronounced thus: *Who shall accuse against the elect of God*? so that, as it were, this answer follows the question: *God who justifies*; and likewise it is asked: *Who is it that condemns*? and it is answered: *Christ Jesus who died*.

Since to believe this is most insane, it will be pronounced so that a question of inquiry precedes and a question expecting yes or no follows. Now between an inquiry and such a question the ancients said this difference exists: that to an inquiry many things can be answered, but to such a question either "no" or "yes."

It will be pronounced, then, so that, after the inquiry by which we say *Who shall accuse against the elect of God*? what follows is uttered in the tone of one questioning: *God who justifies*? so that the silent answer is "no"; and likewise we inquire: *Who is it that condemns*? and again we ask: *Christ Jesus who died, or rather who rose again, who is at the right hand of God, who also intercedes for us*? so that everywhere the answer is "no."

But in that place where he says: *What then shall we say? That the gentiles who did not pursue righteousness have laid hold of righteousness*, unless, after the inquiry by which it was said, *What then shall we say*? the answer is subjoined, *that the gentiles who did not pursue righteousness have laid hold of righteousness*, the following text will not cohere. But in whatever tone of voice that which Nathanael said is pronounced: *From Nazareth can anything good be*—whether of one affirming, so that only what he says, *From Nazareth*? pertains to the question;[^4] or the whole with the doubt of one questioning—I do not see how it is to be distinguished. But either sense does not impede the faith.

## The text itself resolves certain ambiguities.

**3.** 7. There is also ambiguity in the doubtful sound of syllables, and this certainly pertains to pronunciation. For what is written: *My bone is not hidden from you, which you have made in secret*,[^5] does not show clearly to the reader whether he should pronounce the word with the vowel short or long. For if he shortens it, it is understood from the word for "bones"; but if he lengthens it, from the word for "mouths," in the singular number. But such things are judged by inspection of the prior language. For in the Greek it is not the word for "mouth" but the word for "bone" that is set down.[^6] Hence for the most part the common custom of speaking is more useful for signifying things than literate correctness. For I should prefer that it be said, with a barbarism, "My ossum is not hidden from you," than that it should be less open because it is more Latin.

But sometimes the doubtful sound of a syllable is judged even by a neighboring word pertaining to the same statement, as is that passage of the Apostle: *which I foretell to you, as I foretold, that those who do such things will not possess the kingdom of God*.[^7] If he had only said *which I foretell to you* and had not subjoined *as I foretold*, one would have to have recourse only to the manuscript of the preceding language, in order to know whether, in what he said *praedico*, the middle syllable was to be lengthened or shortened. But now it is manifest that it is to be lengthened. For he does not say "as I preached," but *as I foretold*.

## By what method ambiguity of wording is settled.

**4.** 8. But not only these, but also those ambiguities which pertain neither to division nor to pronunciation, are to be considered similarly; such as that one to the Thessalonians: *therefore we have been consoled, brothers, in you*.[^8] For it is doubtful whether it is "O brothers" or "these brothers"; but neither of these is against the faith. But the Greek language does not have these cases alike, and so, when that is inspected, the vocative is reported—that is, "O brothers." But if the translator had wished to say "therefore we have had consolation, brothers, in you," the words would have been less served, but there would be less doubt about the meaning; or at least "our" would be added. For hardly anyone would doubt that it is the vocative case when he heard: *therefore we have been consoled, our brothers, in you*.[^9]

But now this is permitted rather riskily.[^10] So it was done in that passage to the Corinthians, when the Apostle says: *I die daily, by your glory, brothers, which I have in Christ Jesus*. For a certain translator says: *I die daily, by your glory I swear*, because in the Greek the voice of one swearing is manifest, without doubtful sound.[^11] Most rarely, therefore, and most with difficulty, can ambiguity be found in literal words, as far as the books of the divine Scriptures are concerned, which is not resolved either by the very circumstance of the discourse, by which the writer’s intention is known, or by the comparison of translators, or by inspection of the prior language.

## To take the figurative expressions of Scripture literally is a wretched slavery.

**5.** 9. But the ambiguities of figurative words, about which we must speak next, require no slight care and industry. For at the outset one must beware lest you take a figurative expression literally. And to this pertains what the Apostle says: *the letter kills, but the spirit gives life*. For when what is said figuratively is taken as though it were said literally, it is understood carnally. And no death of the soul is more fittingly called death than when that too which in it excels the beasts—that is, the understanding—is subjected to the flesh by following the letter.

For one who follows the letter holds figurative words as literal, and does not refer that which is signified by the literal word to another signification. But if he hears "Sabbath," for example, he understands nothing but one day of the seven which are repeated in a continuous cycle; and when he hears "sacrifice," he does not go beyond in thought that which is usually done with the victims of cattle and the fruits of the earth. This indeed is the wretched slavery of the soul: to take signs for things, and not to be able to lift the eye of the mind above the corporeal creation to drink in the eternal light.

## On the slavery of the Jews and the advancement of the apostolic Church.

**6.** 10. Yet this slavery in the Jewish people differed far from the death of the other nations,[^12] since they were so subjected to temporal things that in all of them the one God was commended to them. And although they observed the signs of spiritual things in place of the things themselves,[^13] not knowing to what they referred, they nevertheless had this implanted in them: that by such slavery they were pleasing the one God of all, whom they did not see. The Apostle writes that this watch-keeping was as though under the pedagogue of little ones.[^14]

And therefore those who clung pertinaciously to such signs could not bear the Lord who despised them, when the time of their revelation had now come;[^15] and hence their leaders contrived slanders, because he healed on the Sabbath;[^16] and the people, bound to those signs as though to things, did not believe that he was God, or had come from God, who was unwilling to attend to them as they were observed by the Jews.

But those who believed, from whom the first Church of Jerusalem was made, showed sufficiently how great a usefulness it had been to be kept in that way under a pedagogue, so that the signs which had been temporally imposed on those who served might bind the opinion of the observers to the worship of the one God, *who made heaven and earth*.

For those people, because they were close to spiritual things—for even in their temporal and carnal vows and signs, although they did not know how these were to be understood spiritually, they had nevertheless learned to venerate the one eternal God—became so capable of the Holy Spirit that they sold all their possessions and *laid the price of them before the feet of the Apostles to be distributed to the needy*, and dedicated themselves wholly to God as a new temple, of whose earthly image—that is, the old temple—they had been servants.

For it is not written that any of the churches of the gentiles did this, because those who had idols made by hand as gods had not been found so near.[^17]

## The slavery of the gentiles under useless signs.

**7.** 11. And if ever any of them tried to interpret those things as signs, they referred them to worshiping and venerating the creature. For what does it profit me that the statue, for example, of Neptune is not itself to be held a god, but by it is signified the whole sea, or even all the other waters that burst forth from springs? As it is described by one of their poets, if I recall rightly, saying thus:

> *You, father Neptune, whose hoary temples, girt with the crashing brine, resound; for whom mighty Ocean flows forth from his unceasing chin, and rivers wander through his hair*.

This husk rattles its sounding pebbles within a sweet shell; it is, however, the food not of human beings but of pigs. He who knows the Gospel knows what I mean.[^18]

What, then, does it profit me that the statue of Neptune is referred to that signification, except perhaps that I should worship neither? For to me any statue whatever, just as much as the whole sea, is not God. Yet I confess that those who think the works of human beings are gods are sunk deeper than those who think the works of God are. But we are commanded to love and worship the one God,[^19] who made all these things, whose images those people venerate, either as gods or as signs and images of gods. If, then, to follow a usefully instituted sign in place of the very thing for signifying which it was instituted is carnal slavery, how much more to take signs of useless things, instituted in place of things! And if you refer these to those very things which are signified by them, and bind your mind to worshiping them, you will nonetheless not lack a servile and carnal burden and veil.[^20]

## The Jews were freed from the slavery of signs in one way, the gentiles in another.

**8.** 12. For this reason Christian liberty freed those whom it found under useful signs, as having been found near,[^21] by interpreting the signs to which they were subject and raising them to the things of which those are signs. From these were made the Churches of the holy Israelites.

But those whom it found under useless signs it frustrated and removed not only the servile activity under such signs but also the signs themselves, all of them, so that the gentiles might be turned, from the corruption of a multitude of feigned gods—which Scripture often and properly calls fornication—to the worship of the one God; not now about to serve even under those useful signs, but rather to exercise the mind in their spiritual understanding.

## Who is oppressed by the slavery of signs, who is not. Baptism. Eucharist.

**9.** 13. For he serves under a sign who works at or venerates some signifying thing without knowing what it signifies. But he who either works at or venerates a useful sign divinely instituted, whose force and signification he understands, does not venerate that which is seen and passes away, but rather that to which all such things are to be referred.

Such a person is spiritual and free, even in the time of slavery, when it is not yet fitting that those signs, under whose yoke carnal minds are to be tamed, be revealed to them. But such spiritual people were the Patriarchs and the Prophets, and all in the people of Israel through whom the Holy Spirit ministered to us the very aids and consolations of the Scriptures.

But at this present time, after the most manifest indication of our liberty has shone forth in the resurrection of our Lord, we are not burdened even by the heavy performance of those signs which we now understand, but the Lord himself and the apostolic discipline handed down certain few in place of many, and these very easy to do, most august to understand, and most chaste to observe—such as is the sacrament of Baptism and the celebration of the body and blood of the Lord. When each one receives these, instructed to what they are referred, he recognizes them, so that he may venerate them not with carnal slavery but rather with spiritual liberty.

But just as to follow the letter and to take signs for the things which are signified by them is the mark of servile weakness, so to interpret signs uselessly is the mark of badly wandering error.[^22] But he who does not understand what a sign signifies, and yet understands that it is a sign, is not himself oppressed by slavery. It is better, however, to be oppressed by unknown but useful signs than, by interpreting them uselessly, to insert the neck, drawn out from the yoke of slavery, into the snares of error.

## How it may be discerned whether an expression is literal or figurative.

**10.** 14. But to this observance, by which we beware of following a figurative—that is, transferred—expression as though literal, this other too is to be joined: that we should not wish to take a literal one as though figurative. The method, therefore, of discovering whether an expression is literal or figurative is first to be demonstrated. And this method is altogether this: that whatever in the divine discourse can be referred literally neither to honesty of morals nor to the truth of faith, you should recognize to be figurative. Honesty of morals pertains to loving God and neighbor; the truth of faith to knowing God and neighbor. But each one’s hope is in his own conscience,[^23] according as he feels himself to be making progress toward the love of God and neighbor and the knowledge of them. Of all of which it was spoken in the first book.

## On human custom in judging human acts.

**10.** 15. But since the human race is prone to estimate sins not from the weight of the desire itself, but rather from that of its own custom, it for the most part comes about that each one of human beings thinks that only those things are to be blamed which the people of his own region and time have been accustomed to censure and condemn; and that only those things are to be approved and praised which the custom of those with whom he lives admits.

And so it happens that if Scripture either commands something that is repugnant to the custom of the hearers, or blames something that is not repugnant, then, if the authority of the word has already bound their minds, they think it a figurative expression.[^24] But Scripture commands nothing except charity, and blames nothing except cupidity, and in this way it forms the morals of human beings.

Likewise, if the opinion of some error has preoccupied the mind, whatever Scripture asserts otherwise, people think figurative. But it asserts nothing except the Catholic faith, concerning things past and future and present. Of past things there is narration, of future things foretelling, of present things demonstration; but all these have value for nourishing and corroborating that same charity, and for conquering and extinguishing cupidity.

## On the scope of charity and cupidity.

**10.** 16. I call charity the motion of the mind toward enjoying God for his own sake, and oneself and one’s neighbor for the sake of God; but cupidity the motion of the mind toward enjoying oneself and one’s neighbor and any body whatever not for the sake of God. Now what untamed cupidity does for corrupting one’s own mind and body is called debauchery; what it does so as to harm another is called a crime. And these are the two kinds of all sins; but debauchery comes first. When it has emptied the mind and led it to a kind of want, one leaps forth into crimes, by which the impediments to debauchery may be removed or aids sought.

Likewise, what charity does so as to profit itself is usefulness; but what it does so as to profit the neighbor is named beneficence. And here usefulness comes first, because no one can profit another from what he does not have. But by however much the reign of cupidity is destroyed, by so much is that of charity increased.

## A rule concerning things that smack of cruelty and are nonetheless referred to the person of God or of the saints.

**11.** 17. Whatever, then, is read in the holy Scriptures as harsh and, as it were, cruel to do or say, on the part of God or his saints, has value for destroying the reign of cupidity.

But if it sounds clearly, it is not to be referred to something else as though it were said figuratively. As is that saying of the Apostle: *you store up for yourself wrath on the day of wrath and of the revelation of the just judgment of God, who will render to each one according to his works: to those indeed who, by patience in good work, seek glory and honor and incorruption, eternal life; but to those who are of contention and distrust the truth, but believe iniquity, wrath and indignation. Tribulation and anguish upon every soul of the human being who works evil, of the Jew first and of the Greek*. But this is addressed to those in whom cupidity itself is being overthrown, who were unwilling to conquer it.[^25]

But when, in the human being over whom they held dominion, the reigns of cupidity are overturned, that is an open expression: *those who are of Jesus Christ have crucified their flesh with its passions and concupiscences*. Except that here too certain transferred words are dealt with, such as *the wrath of God*, and *have crucified*. But they are not so many, nor so placed, as to obscure the sense and make an allegory or enigma, which I properly call a figurative expression.

But what is said to Jeremiah: *behold, I have set you today over the nations and kingdoms, to pluck up and to destroy and to scatter and to ruin*, there is no doubt that the whole is a figurative expression, to be referred to that end which we have stated.

## A rule concerning sayings and deeds that seem debauched, which are attributed to God or to holy men.

**12.** 18. But those things which seem debauched to the unskilled, whether only said or even done, whether on the part of God or of human beings whose holiness is commended to us, are wholly figurative. Their secrets are to be unfolded for the nourishment of charity. But whoever uses passing things more restrictively than the morals of those with whom he lives are, is either temperate or superstitious. But whoever uses them so as to exceed the bounds of the custom of the good among whom he lives, either signifies something or is debauched. For in all such things it is not the use of things, but the desire of the one using, that is at fault.

And in no way would any sober person believe that the Lord’s feet were thus drenched with precious ointment by the woman[^26] in the manner of luxurious and worthless people, whose such banquets we detest. For a good odor is a good reputation, which whoever has by the works of a good life, while he follows in the footsteps of Christ, drenches his feet, as it were, with the most precious odor. Thus what in other persons is for the most part debauchery is, in a divine or prophetic person, the sign of some great thing. For the joining of a harlot is one thing in corrupt morals, another in the prophecy of the prophet Hosea.[^27] Nor, if bodies are stripped debauchedly in the banquets of the drunken and the lascivious, is it therefore debauchery to be naked in the baths.[^28]

## Attention must be paid to places, times, and persons.

**12.** 19. What, therefore, befits places and times and persons must be diligently attended to, lest we rashly reprehend debaucheries. For it can happen that a wise person, without any vice of daintiness or voracity, uses the most precious food; but a foolish one blazes with the most foul flame of gluttony toward the cheapest. And any healthy person would prefer to feed on fish, in the manner of the Lord, than on lentils, in the manner of Esau, the grandson of Abraham,[^29] or on barley, in the manner of beasts of burden.

For most beasts are not on that account more continent than we, because they are fed on cheaper foods. For in all things of this kind, what we do is to be approved or disapproved not from the nature of the things which we use, but from the cause of using and the manner of desiring.

## On the morals of the ancient Fathers.

**12.** 20. By an earthly kingdom the ancient just men imaged and foretold the heavenly kingdom.[^30] For the sake of supplying offspring, there was a blameless custom of one man having several wives at once.[^31] And therefore it was not honorable for one woman to have several husbands; for a woman is not thereby more fruitful, but it is rather a harlot’s baseness to seek either gain or children indiscriminately. In morals of this kind, whatever the holy people of those times did without lust—although they did things which at this time cannot be done except through lust—Scripture does not blame. And whatever such thing is narrated there is to be interpreted not only historically and literally, but also figuratively and prophetically, up to that end of charity, whether of God or of neighbor or of both.

For just as among the ancient Romans it was a disgrace to have tunics reaching to the ankles and with sleeves, but now it is a disgrace for those born in an honorable station, when they are in tunics, not to have these,[^32] so it must be noticed that in the rest of the use of things too lust ought to be absent—lust which not only basely abuses the very custom of those among whom it lives, but also, often going beyond its bounds, manifests its foulness, which lay hidden within the enclosures of customary morals, by a most debauched eruption.

## Continuation of the same argument.

**13.** 21. But whatever agrees with the custom of those with whom this life is to be passed, whether imposed by necessity or undertaken as a duty, is to be referred by good and great men to usefulness and beneficence,[^33] either literally, as we too ought, or even figuratively, as is permitted to the Prophets.

## The error of those who suppose that there is no justice in itself.

**14.** 22. When the unlearned of another custom run upon such deeds in reading, unless they are restrained by authority, they think them debaucheries; nor can they notice that their whole way of life, whether in marriages or in banquets or in clothing and the rest of human sustenance and adornment, seems debauched to other nations and other times. Moved by this variety of innumerable customs, certain people, drowsing, so to speak—who were neither lulled in the deep sleep of folly nor able to wake into the light of wisdom—supposed that there is no justice in itself, but that to each nation its own custom seems just; and since this is diverse among all nations, while justice ought to remain unchangeable, it becomes manifest that there is no justice anywhere.

They did not understand, not to mention many things: *what you do not want done to yourself, do not do to another*,[^34] which can in no way be varied by any gentile diversity of theirs. When this maxim is referred to the love of God, all debaucheries die; when to that of the neighbor, all crimes. For no one wants his own dwelling to be corrupted; he ought not, therefore, to corrupt the dwelling of God, namely himself. And no one wants to be harmed by anyone; let him not, therefore, harm anyone himself.

## A rule to be observed in figurative expressions is set forth.

**15.** 23. Thus, with the tyranny of cupidity overturned, charity reigns by the most just laws of the love of God for the sake of God, and of oneself and one’s neighbor for the sake of God. In figurative expressions, therefore, a rule of this kind is to be observed: that what is read be turned over by diligent consideration so long, until the interpretation is brought through to the reign of charity. But if this already sounds literally, let no figurative expression be supposed.

## The rule concerning preceptive expressions is illustrated by examples.

**16.** 24. If a preceptive expression forbids either debauchery or a crime, or commands either usefulness or beneficence, it is not figurative. But if it seems to command debauchery or a crime, or to forbid usefulness and beneficence, it is figurative. *Unless you eat*, he says, *the flesh of the Son of man and drink his blood, you will not have life in you*. He seems to command a crime or a debauchery. It is, therefore, a figure, prescribing that one is to share in the Lord’s Passion, and to store up sweetly and usefully in memory that his flesh was crucified and wounded for us.

Scripture says: *if your enemy is hungry, feed him; if he is thirsty, give him drink*. Here, with no one doubting, it prescribes beneficence. But what follows: *for in doing this you will heap coals of fire upon his head*, you might think a crime of ill will is commanded. Do not, then, doubt that it is said figuratively. And since it can be interpreted in two ways, one toward harming, the other toward bestowing, let charity rather recall you to beneficence, so that you understand the coals of fire to be the burning groans of repentance, by which the pride of one is healed who grieves that he had been the enemy of a man by whom his misery is relieved.

Likewise, when the Lord says: *he who loves his soul will lose it*,[^35] he is not to be thought to forbid the usefulness by which each one ought to preserve his own soul, but it is said figuratively, *let him lose his soul*—that is, let him destroy and let go the use of it which he now has, namely the perverse and preposterous one, by which it is inclined toward temporal things, so as not to seek eternal ones.

It is written: *give to the merciful, and do not receive the sinner*. The latter part of this maxim seems to forbid beneficence; for it says: *do not receive the sinner*. Understand, then, that *sinner* is set figuratively for sin, so that you do not receive his sin.

## Scripture commands some things in common to all, others to individuals separately.

**17.** 25. But it often happens that anyone who is, or thinks himself to be, at a higher level of the spiritual life supposes that the things commanded at the lower levels are said figuratively. For example, if someone has embraced the celibate life and *has made himself a eunuch for the sake of the kingdom of heaven*, he may contend that whatever the holy books command about loving and governing a wife[^36] must be taken not literally but figuratively; and if someone has resolved to keep his virgin unmarried,[^37] he may try to interpret as figurative the saying, *Give your daughter in marriage, and you will have done a great work*[^38].

This too, then, will be among the observations to be made in understanding the Scriptures: that we recognize that some things are commanded to all in common, others to each particular class of persons, so that the medicine may reach not only the universal state of health but also the proper weakness of each member. For what cannot be raised up to a better class must be cured within its own.

## Distinguish the times, and you will reconcile the laws of Scripture.

**18.** 26. Likewise, we must beware lest anyone suppose that what in the Old Scriptures, given the condition of those times, is no outrage or crime even when understood not figuratively but literally, can also be carried over into our own times for use in living. No one will do this unless cupidity is lording it over him and seeking the support of the very Scriptures by which it ought to be overthrown. And the wretched man fails to understand that these things are set down precisely for this benefit: that people of good hope may see, to their advantage, both that a custom they spurn can have a good use and that one they embrace can be damnable, if in the one case we attend to the love of those who use it and in the other to their cupidity.

## The use of several wives was tolerable among the Hebrews.

**18.** 27. For if one man, given his time, could chastely make use of many wives, another can use a single wife lustfully. For I have more regard for the man who makes use of the fruitfulness of many for the sake of something else than for the one who enjoys the flesh of one for its own sake. In the former case what is sought is an advantage suited to the opportunities of the times; in the latter a cupidity entangled in temporal pleasures is being sated.

And those to whom the Apostle, by way of concession, grants carnal relations with their individual spouses on account of their lack of self-control[^39] are at a lower level on the way to God than those men who, though each had several wives, looked in their intercourse to nothing but the procreation of children, just as the wise man looks in food and drink to nothing but the health of the body.

And so, if the Lord's coming had found them in this life, when it was now *a time not to scatter stones but to gather them*, they would at once have made themselves eunuchs for the sake of the kingdom of heaven.[^40] For there is no difficulty in doing without something unless there is cupidity in having it.

Those men indeed knew that even with one's own spouse there can be the wantonness of intemperate misuse. The prayer of Tobias bears witness to this, when he was joined to his wife. For he says: *Blessed are you, O Lord of our fathers, and blessed is your name for all the ages of ages. Let the heavens and all your creation bless you. You made Adam, and gave him Eve as a helper. And now, Lord, you know that I am not taking my sister for the sake of lust, but in truth itself, that you may have mercy on us, Lord*[^41].

## The wicked judge others by their own way of conducting themselves.

**19.** 28. But those who, dissolute through unbridled lust, roam about in many debaucheries, or who, even with one and the same wife, not only exceed the measure pertaining to the procreation of children but also, with an utterly shameless license of a kind of servile freedom, pile up the squalor of an all too inhuman intemperance—these do not believe it could have happened that the men of old made temperate use of many women, observing nothing in that use but the duty, suited to the time, of propagating offspring. And what they themselves, bound by the snares of lust, do not do even with one woman, they suppose can in no way be done with many.

## If the patriarchs of old are praised, there is no fraud in this.

**19.** 29. But these people could say that good and holy men ought not even to be honored and praised, since they themselves, when honored and praised, swell up with pride, all the more greedy for the emptiest glory the more often and widely a flattering tongue fans them. By it they become so frivolous that the breeze of rumor, whether reckoned favorable or adverse, sweeps them into whatever abysses of disgrace, or dashes them against the very rocks of crime. Let them see, then, how arduous and difficult it is for a person neither to be enticed by the bait of praise nor pierced by the stings of abuse—and let them not measure others by themselves.

## In whatever manner of living, good men know how to conduct themselves . . .

**20.** 29. Let them rather believe that our apostles were neither puffed up when they were looked up to by people, nor crushed when they were looked down upon. Neither temptation, in fact, was lacking to those men; for they were celebrated by the acclaim of believers and defamed by the curses of persecutors.

As these men, then, made use of all these things to suit their time and were not corrupted, so those men of old, relating their use of women to the suitability of their own time, did not suffer that domination of lust to which those who do not believe these things are enslaved.

## . . . which to the depraved seems absurd.

**20.** 30. And therefore these people would in no way restrain themselves from an implacable hatred of their sons, if they learned that their wives or concubines had been approached or fondled by them—should anything of the sort happen to befall them.

## King David lovingly wept for the dead Absalom.

**21.** 30. But King David, when he had suffered this from his impious and monstrous son, not only bore with him in his savagery but even mourned him when he was destroyed.[^42] For he was not ensnared and held by carnal jealousy; it was in no way his own injuries that moved him, but his son's sins.

For this reason, in fact, he had forbidden his son to be killed if he were defeated, so that, once subdued, a place for repentance might be preserved for him. And because he could not save him, he grieved at his death not for his own bereavement, but because he knew into what punishments a soul so impiously adulterous and parricidal was being snatched away. For earlier, when another son, who was innocent, lay dying—the son over whose sickness he had been afflicted—he rejoiced at his death.[^43]

## David, though he fell into adultery, was far from the intemperance of the lustful.

**21.** 31. And this appears especially from the moderation and temperance with which those men made use of women, in the fact that when this same king had unlawfully fallen upon one woman—carried away by a certain heat of his age and by the prosperity of temporal affairs, and having even ordered her husband to be killed—he was accused through the prophet.

When the prophet had come to him to convict him of his sin, he set before him a likeness about a poor man who had one ewe, whose neighbor, though he had many, at the arrival of his guest set out the poor man's single little ewe to be eaten instead. Roused against this man, David ordered him to be killed[^44] and the ewe to be repaid fourfold to the poor man—so that, not knowing it, he condemned himself, who had sinned knowingly. And when this was made plain to him and the vengeance announced by God, he washed away his sin by repenting.

Yet in this likeness only the seduction was signified, by the ewe of the poor neighbor. But about the husband of the woman who was killed—that is, about the poor man himself who had one ewe, who was slain—David was not questioned by the likeness, so that he pronounced the sentence of his own condemnation upon the adultery alone. From this it is understood how great was the temperance with which he had many women, since over the one in whom he exceeded the measure he was compelled to punish himself. But in this man there was not a permanence of this immoderate lust, but a passing; and for this reason the prophet who reproved him called that illicit appetite a guest.

For he did not say that he had set out the poor neighbor's ewe to be eaten for his king, but for his guest. But in his son Solomon this lust did not pass through as a guest; rather, it took possession as a kingdom. Of this Scripture was not silent, blaming him for having been a lover of women. His beginnings, nevertheless, had blazed with desire for wisdom; and when he had attained it by spiritual love, he lost it by carnal love.[^45]

## Many things suited to ancient customs are forbidden to the Christian.

**22.** 32. Therefore, although all or nearly all the deeds contained in the books of the Old Testament are to be taken not only literally but also figuratively, still, even those that the reader takes literally,[^46] if those who did them are praised, but the deeds themselves are abhorrent to the practice of good people who keep the divine precepts after the Lord's coming, let him refer the figure to understanding, but not transfer the deed itself to morals. For there are many things that were done dutifully in that time which now cannot be done except lustfully.

## A rule about the passages where the sins of great men are reported.

**23.** 33. But if anyone reads of the sins of great men, even though he may be able to notice and trace out in them some figure of things to come,[^47] let him nonetheless apply the literal sense of the deed to this use: that he never dare to boast of his own good deeds and despise the rest as sinners in comparison with his own righteousness, when he sees both the storms to be guarded against and the shipwrecks to be wept over in men so great.

For the sins of those men were written down for this very reason: that everywhere that apostolic saying might be dreaded, when he says, *Therefore let anyone who thinks that he stands take heed lest he fall*[^48]. For there is scarcely any page of the holy books on which it does not resound that *God resists the proud, but gives grace to the humble*[^49].

## Before all things the kind of expression must be considered.

**24.** 34. Above all, then, we must investigate whether the expression we are trying to understand is literal or figurative. For once we have found that it is figurative, by applying the rules about things which we set out in the first book it is easy to turn it over in every way until we arrive at the sense of truth—especially when the practice that has been strengthened by the exercise of piety is brought to bear. And we discover whether an expression is literal or figurative by attending to those things which have been said above.

**25.** 34. And when this has become clear, the words in which it is contained will be found to be drawn either from like things or from things touching upon some nearness to them.

## The same word does not signify the same thing everywhere.

**25.** 35. But since things resemble things in many ways, let us not suppose it to be laid down as a rule that what some thing has signified by likeness in one passage, we should believe it always signifies. For the Lord set leaven in a context of blame when he said, *Beware of the leaven of the Pharisees*[^50], and in a context of praise when he said, *The kingdom of heaven is like a woman who hid leaven in three measures of flour until it was all leavened*[^51].

## The observation of this variety has two forms . . .

**25.** 36. The observation of this variety, then, has two forms. For each several thing signifies one thing and another in such a way that things either contrary or merely diverse are signified. They signify contrary things when the same thing is set down by likeness now in a good and now in a bad sense, as in the case of leaven which we mentioned above.

Such too is the fact that the lion signifies Christ, where it is said, *The lion of the tribe of Judah has conquered*[^52], and it also signifies the devil, where it is written, *Your adversary the devil prowls around like a roaring lion, seeking someone to devour*[^53].

So too the serpent is in a good sense, *wise as serpents*[^54], but in a bad sense, *the serpent deceived Eve by his cunning*[^55].

Bread in a good sense: *I am the living bread that came down from heaven*[^56]; bread in a bad sense: *Eat hidden bread with pleasure*[^57]. And so with very many other things.

And these things which I have mentioned bear a meaning that is anything but doubtful, since for the sake of example only manifest things ought to have been mentioned.

But there are things about which it is uncertain in what sense they ought to be taken, as: *In the hand of the Lord there is a cup of unmixed wine full of mixture*[^58]. For it is uncertain whether it signifies the wrath of God not extending to the final punishment, that is, to the dregs; or rather the grace of the Scriptures passing over from the Jews to the Gentiles, because *he pours it out from this to that*, while there remain among the Jews the observances which they understand carnally, because *its dregs are not drained out*.

But when the same thing is set down with a signification not contrary but merely diverse, there is an example in the fact that water signifies both a people, as we read in *Revelation*,[^59] and the Holy Spirit, whence comes the saying, *Rivers of living water will flow from his belly*[^60]—and whatever else, according to the passages in which it is set down, water is understood to signify.

## . . . but sometimes several.

**25.** 37. So too other things, not singly but each of them, signify not only two diverse things but even sometimes many, according to the place in the sentence where they are found set down.

## Obscure passages are to be explained from clearer ones.

**26.** 37. But where things are set down more plainly, there we must learn how they are to be understood in obscure passages. For what is said to God, *Take hold of weapon and shield, and rise up to help me*[^61], cannot be better understood than from that passage where we read, *Lord, you have crowned us with the shield of your good will*[^62]—yet not in such a way that, wherever we read shield set down for some defense, we should take it to mean only the good will of God. For there is also said *the shield of faith, with which*, he says, *you can quench all the flaming arrows of the evil one*[^63]. Nor again, on that account, ought we in spiritual armor of this kind to assign the shield only to faith, since in another place the breastplate too is said to be of faith: *having put on*, he says, *the breastplate of faith and love*[^64].

## Nothing forbids the same passage from being understood in various ways.

**27.** 38. But when, from the same words of Scripture, not some one thing but two or more meanings are perceived, even if it is hidden what the one who wrote them meant, there is no danger, provided it can be shown from other passages of the Holy Scriptures that each of them agrees with the truth. The one who searches the divine utterances should, however, take pains to arrive at the will of the author through whom the Holy Spirit produced that Scripture—whether he attains this, or whether he carves out some other meaning from those words which does not run counter to right faith, having the support of some other passage of the divine utterances.

For that author, in the very words we wish to understand, perhaps saw that meaning too; and certainly the Spirit of God, who produced these things through him, foresaw without doubt that it would also occur to the reader or hearer—indeed, provided that it should occur, since it too rests upon the truth.[^65] For what could have been more generously and richly provided by God in the divine utterances than that the same words should be understood in several ways, which other words, no less divine, attest and cause to be approved?

## An uncertain passage is more safely clarified through other passages of Scripture than through reasoning.

**28.** 39. But where such a sense is drawn out whose uncertainty cannot be cleared up by sure testimonies of the Holy Scriptures, it remains for it to be made plain by giving a reason—even if the one whose words we are seeking to understand perhaps did not mean it. But this practice is dangerous; for it is far safer to walk through the divine Scriptures. When we wish to search these, darkened as they are by metaphorical words, either let something emerge from them that admits of no controversy, or, if it does admit of controversy, let it be settled from that same Scripture by finding and bringing forward its witnesses wherever they may be.

## The knowledge of tropes is necessary.

**29.** 40. But let the learned know that our authors made use of all the modes of expression which the grammarians call by the Greek name *tropes*, and did so more manifoldly and abundantly than those can suppose or believe who do not know them and have learned about these things in other authors. Yet those who do know these *tropes* recognize them in the Holy Letters, and by their knowledge are helped somewhat toward understanding them. But it is not fitting here to hand these things over to the ignorant, lest we should seem to be teaching the art of grammar. I do indeed advise that they be learned elsewhere, although I have already given that advice above—that is, in the second book, where I treated of the necessary knowledge of languages.[^66] For letters—from which grammar itself takes its name, since the Greeks call letters *grammata*—are at any rate signs of the sounds belonging to the articulate speech by which we talk.

Of these tropes not only examples, as of all of them, but even the names of some are read in the divine books, such as allegory, enigma,[^67] and parable. Yet almost all of these tropes, which are said to be known by the liberal art, are also found in the speech of those who have heard no grammarians and are content with the language the common people use. For who does not say, "May you flourish so"?[^68]—which trope is called *metaphor*. Who does not call a thing a fish pond, even one that has no fish and was not made for the sake of fish, and yet took its name from fish? This trope is called *catachresis*.

## On irony and antiphrasis.

**29.** 41. It would take long to go through the rest in this manner. For the speech of the common people reaches even to those tropes that are the more remarkable because they signify the contrary of what is said, such as the one called *irony* or *antiphrasis*.

But *irony* indicates by its delivery what it wishes to be understood, as when we say to a man doing evil, "You are doing fine things." *Antiphrasis*, on the other hand, signifies the contrary not by the voice of the one uttering it, but either has its own words whose origin is from the opposite—as a grove is called *lucus* because it gives the least light (*minime luceat*)—or else something is customarily said in such a way that it is uttered, though not from the opposite, as when we ask to receive what is not there and are answered, "It is overflowing"; or else by added words we bring it about that what we say is understood from the opposite, as if we should say, "Beware of him, for he is a good man."

And who does not say such things, untaught and altogether ignorant of what these tropes are or what they are called? The knowledge of them is necessary for resolving the ambiguities of the Scriptures because, when the sense is absurd if it is taken according to the literal meaning of the words, we must surely ask whether perhaps what we do not understand was said by this or that trope; and in this way very many things that lay hidden have been discovered.[^69]

## The rules of Tyconius the Donatist are weighed.

**30.** 42. A certain Tyconius, who wrote most unanswerably against the Donatists though he was himself a Donatist—and is found there too to be of a most absurd heart, in that he was unwilling to leave them on every point—made a book which he called *The Rules*, because in it he followed out certain seven rules by which, as by keys, the hidden things of the divine Scriptures might be unlocked. The first of these he sets down as *On the Lord and his body*; the second, *On the twofold body of the Lord*; the third, *On the promises and the Law*; the fourth, *On the species and the genus*; the fifth, *On the times*; the sixth, *On recapitulation*; the seventh, *On the devil and his body*.

These rules, indeed, when considered as he expounds them, help not a little toward penetrating the things that are hidden in the divine utterances. Yet not all the things that are written in such a way that they are not easily understood can be discovered by these rules, but by many other means, which he is so far from having embraced in this number seven that he himself expounds many obscure passages in which he applies none of these rules, since there is no need. For nothing of the sort is treated or asked there as he asks in the *Revelation* of John, how the angels of the seven Churches, to whom he is bidden to write, are to be understood. And he reasons in many ways and arrives at this, that we should understand the angels themselves to be the Churches.[^70] In this most copious discussion there is none of those rules, and yet a most obscure matter is being asked there. Let this be said by way of example, for it would be too long and too laborious to gather together all the things in the canonical Scriptures that are so obscure that none of those seven need be sought in them.

## The rules of Tyconius must be applied with great caution.

**30.** 43. But this man, when he commended these as rules, attributed so much to them as though, once they were well known and applied, we could understand everything we find obscurely set down in the law—that is, in the divine books. For he began that same book by saying: *I judged it necessary, before all the things that seem good to me, to write a little book of Rules and to fashion, as it were, keys and lamps for the secrets of the law. For there are certain mystical rules which hold the recesses of the whole law and make the treasures of truth, invisible to some, visible. If the system of these rules is received without envy, as we share it, all closed things will be laid open and obscure things made clear, so that anyone walking through the immense forest of prophecy, led by these rules along paths of light, as it were, may be kept from error*.

Here, if he had said, "For there are certain mystical rules which hold some recesses of the law," or at least, "which hold great recesses of the law"—not, as he said, *the recesses of the whole law*; and if he had not said, *all closed things will be laid open*, but "many closed things will be laid open"—he would have spoken truly, and would not, by giving to his so elaborate and useful work more than the matter itself demands, have sent its reader and student into a false hope.

I thought this should be said so that the book itself may both be read by the studious, because it helps very much toward understanding the Scriptures, and not have hoped of it more than it possesses. It must of course be read with caution, not only on account of certain matters in which he erred as a man, but especially on account of those things which he speaks as a Donatist heretic. But what these seven rules teach or advise, I will briefly show.

## On the first rule of Tyconius.

**31.** 44. The first is *On the Lord and his body*. In it, knowing that one person is sometimes intimated to us of head and body—that is, of Christ and the Church (for it was not said in vain to the faithful, *therefore you are Abraham's offspring*[^71], since there is one offspring of Abraham, which is Christ), let us not hesitate when there is a transition from the head to the body, or from the body to the head, and yet there is no departure from one and the same person. For one person speaks when it says, *as on a bridegroom he set a miter upon me, and as a bride he adorned me with an ornament*[^72]. And yet which of these two suits the head, which the body—that is, which suits Christ, which the Church—must of course be understood.

## On the second rule of Tyconius.

**32.** 45. The second is *On the twofold body of the Lord*, which indeed ought not to have been so called. For that is not really the Lord's body which will not be with him for eternity. But it ought to have been said, "On the true and intermingled body of the Lord," or "the true and the feigned," or something else; because hypocrites are to be said not to be with him not only for eternity but even now, although they appear to be in his Church. Hence this rule could also be named in this way, that it should be called "On the intermingled Church."

This rule requires a watchful interpreter, for at times Scripture, when it is already speaking to others, seems to speak as if to the very ones to whom it was speaking, or to speak about them when it is already speaking about others, as though the body of both were one, on account of their temporary intermingling and their common share in the sacraments. To this belongs the passage in the *Song of Songs*: *I am dark and beautiful, like the tents of Kedar, like the curtains of Solomon*[^73]. For it does not say, "I was dark like the tents of Kedar and I am beautiful like the curtains of Solomon," but said that it was both, on account of the temporal unity of good and bad fish within one net.[^74] For the tents of Kedar belong to Ishmael, who will not be heir together with the son of the free woman.[^75]

And so, when God says concerning the good part, *I will lead the blind in a way they do not know, and they will tread paths they do not know, and I will turn darkness into light for them and crooked things straight. These words I will do, and I will not forsake them*[^76]—he immediately says, concerning the other part, which has been intermingled as the bad, *but they themselves turned backward*[^77], although by these words others are now being meant.[^78]

But since they are now in one, it speaks as if about the very ones about whom it was speaking; yet they will not always be in one. For that is the very servant mentioned in the Gospel, whose lord, when he comes, *will divide him and put his portion with the hypocrites*[^79].

## On the third rule of Tyconius.

**33.** 46. The third rule is *On the promises and the Law*, which can be put another way as "On the Spirit and the Letter," as we called it when we wrote a book on this matter; it can also be put thus: "On grace and the commandment." But this seems to me a great question rather than a rule to be applied to resolving questions.

This is the question which the Pelagians, by failing to understand, either founded or enlarged their heresy. Tyconius labored at resolving it well, but not fully. For, disputing about faith and works, he said that works are given to us by God by the merit of faith, but that faith itself is from us in such a way that it is not from God for us.[^80] Nor did he attend to the Apostle saying, *peace to the brethren, and love with faith, from God the Father and the Lord Jesus Christ*[^81].

But he had not experienced this heresy which, arising in our own time, exercised us much in defending against it the grace of God which is through our Lord Jesus Christ; and—according to what the Apostle says, *there must be heresies, so that those who are approved may be made manifest among you*[^82]—it rendered us far more watchful and diligent, so that we noticed in the Holy Scriptures what escaped this Tyconius, who was less attentive and less anxious because he was without an enemy: namely, that faith itself too is a gift of him who *apportions to each one its measure*[^83].

From this saying it was said to certain people: *to you it has been granted for Christ's sake not only to believe in him, but also to suffer for him*[^84]. Hence who can doubt that both are God's gift, who faithfully and intelligently hears that both are granted? There are also many other testimonies by which this is shown, but we are not now dealing with that. We have, however, dealt with these things very often in one place and another.[^85]

## On the fourth rule of Tyconius.

**34.** 47. The fourth rule of Tyconius is *On the species and the genus*. For so he calls it, wishing the *species* to be understood as the part, and the *genus* as the whole of which that is a part which he names the species. As each individual city is of course a part of the whole body of nations,[^86] this he calls the species, and all the nations the genus. Nor is that subtlety of distinguishing to be applied here which is handed down by the dialecticians, who dispute most acutely about what difference there is between "part" and *species*.

The same reasoning holds if something of this kind is found in the divine utterances not about each individual city, but about each individual province or nation or kingdom. For not only, for example, about Jerusalem or about some city of the nations—whether Tyre or Babylon or any other—is something said in the Holy Scriptures that exceeds its own measure and suits rather all the nations; but even about Judea, about Egypt, about Assyria and any other nation in which there are very many cities, yet which is not the whole world but a part of it, something is said that passes beyond its measure and fits rather the whole of which this is a part—or, as he calls it, the genus of which this is a species.

Hence these terms have come even into the common people's knowledge, so that even the unlearned understand what is established specifically and what generally in any imperial decree. This happens also with men; thus the things said about Solomon exceed his measure and become clear rather when referred to Christ or to the Church, of which he is a part.

## The fourth rule of Tyconius is shown by examples.

**34.** 48. Nor is the species always exceeded; for often such things are said as suit most plainly either it too, or perhaps it only. But when there is a transition from the species to the genus, as if Scripture were still speaking of the species, there the reader's attention ought to be watchful, lest he seek in the species what he can better and more surely find in the genus.

For it is easy to understand what the prophet Ezekiel says: *The house of Israel dwelt in the land, and they defiled it by their way and by their idols and by their sins; their way became before my face according to the uncleanness of a menstruous woman. And I poured out my wrath upon them, and scattered them among the nations, and winnowed them into the lands; according to their ways and according to their sins I judged them*[^87]. It is easy, I say, to understand this about that house of Israel about which the Apostle says, *look at Israel according to the flesh*[^88], because the carnal people of Israel both did and suffered all these things.

The other things too which follow are understood to suit the same people. But when it begins to say, *and I will sanctify my great holy name, which has been defiled among the nations, which you have defiled in their midst; and the nations will know that I am the Lord*[^89], the one who reads ought now to be attentive to how the species is exceeded and the genus joined on. For it follows and says: *and while I am sanctified among you before their eyes, I will take you from the nations, and gather you out of all the lands, and bring you into your own land, and I will sprinkle you with clean water, and you will be cleansed from all your images, and I will cleanse you; and I will give you a new heart, and put a new spirit within you, and I will take away the heart of stone from your flesh, and give you a heart of flesh, and I will put my spirit within you, and bring it about that you walk in my statutes and keep and do my judgments; and you will dwell in the land which I gave to your fathers, and you will be my people and I will be your God, and I will cleanse you from all your uncleannesses*[^90].

That this was prophesied about the New Testament, to which belongs not only that one nation in its remnant—about which it is written elsewhere, *if the number of the children of Israel be as the sand of the sea, the remnant will be saved*[^91]—but also the other nations, which were promised to their fathers, who are ours too: this no one doubts who observes that the *washing of regeneration*[^92] is here promised, which we now see rendered to all the nations.[^93]

And that saying of the Apostle, when he was commending the grace of the New Testament so that it might stand out in comparison with the Old—*you are our letter, written not with ink but with the Spirit of the living God; not on tablets of stone, but on tablets of fleshly hearts*[^94]—he looks back upon and perceives to be drawn from this place where that prophet says, *and I will give you a new heart, and put a new spirit within you; and I will take away the heart of stone from your flesh, and give you a heart of flesh*[^95]. For by the heart of flesh, from which the Apostle says *on tablets of fleshly hearts*, he wished a feeling life to be distinguished from the heart of stone, and by a feeling life he signified an understanding one. Thus there comes to be a spiritual Israel, not of one nation, but of all the nations that were promised to the fathers in their offspring, *which is Christ*[^96].

## Further examples: the spiritual Israel and the carnal Israel.

**34.** 49. This spiritual Israel, then, is distinguished from that carnal Israel, which is of one nation, by the newness of grace, not by the nobility of fatherland, and by mind, not by descent.

But the prophetic loftiness, while it speaks of the one or to the one, passes over secretly to the other; and when it now speaks of this one or to this one, it still seems to speak of the other or to the other—not begrudging us the understanding of the Scriptures as if in hostility, but exercising it medicinally.

Hence too that saying, *and I will bring you into your own land*, and a little later, as if repeating the very same thing, *and you will dwell*, it says, *in the land which I gave to your fathers*, we ought to take not carnally, as the carnal Israel does, but spiritually, as the spiritual do.

For the Church *without spot or wrinkle*[^97], gathered out of all the nations and destined to reign forever with Christ, is itself the land of the blessed, the *land of the living*[^98]. It is to be understood as given to the fathers when it was promised to them by the sure and unchangeable will of God, since by the very firmness of the promise or predestination it has already been given—the thing which the fathers believed would be given in its own time; just as, writing to Timothy about that grace which is given to the saints, the Apostle says, *not according to our works, but according to his own purpose and grace, which was given to us in Christ Jesus before the eternal ages, but is now made manifest through the coming of our Savior*[^99].

He said grace was given when there were not yet any to whom it might be given, since in the disposition and predestination of God that had already been done which was to be in its own time—which he says was *made manifest*.

Although these things can also be understood of the land of the age to come, when there will be *a new heaven and a new earth*[^100], in which the unrighteous will not be able to dwell. And therefore it is rightly said to the pious that this is their land which will in no part belong to the impious, because it too has likewise been given, when it was made firm that it was to be given.[^101]

## The fifth rule of Tyconius is illustrated by examples.

**35.** 50. Tyconius sets down a fifth rule which he calls *On the times*, by which rule there can generally be discovered or conjectured the quantity of time lying hidden in the Holy Scriptures. He says this rule operates in two ways, either by the trope *synecdoche* or by mystical numbers. The trope *synecdoche* makes either the whole to be understood from a part or a part from the whole, as one Evangelist says that something happened after eight days which another says happened after six days, when on the mountain, with only three disciples present, *the face of the Lord shone like the sun and his garments like snow*[^102].

For both statements about the number of days could not be true unless the one who said *after eight days* is understood to have put the last part of the day on which Christ foretold that this would happen, and the first part of the day on which he showed it fulfilled, for two whole and entire days; while the one who said *after six days* reckoned only the entire and whole days in between.

By this mode of expression, in which the whole is signified from a part, that question too about the resurrection of Christ is resolved. For unless the last part of the day on which he suffered is taken for the whole day—that is, with the preceding night added—and the night in the last part of which he rose is taken for a whole day—with, of course, the dawning Lord's day added—they cannot be the *three days and three nights* during which he foretold he would be *in the heart of the earth*[^103].

## On mystical numbers.

**35.** 51. By mystical numbers he means those which divine Scripture commends more prominently, such as seven or ten or twelve and whatever others there are which the studious gladly recognize in their reading. For numbers of this kind are generally set down for the whole of time, as *seven times a day I will praise you*[^104] is nothing other than *his praise is always in my mouth*[^105]. They have the same force also when they are multiplied, whether by ten, as seventy and seven hundred (whence the seventy years of Jeremiah too can be taken spiritually for the whole of the time during which the Church is among aliens), or by themselves, as ten times ten is a hundred, and twelve times twelve is a hundred and forty-four, by which number the whole company of the saints is signified in the *Revelation*.[^106] Hence it appears that not questions of time alone are to be resolved by these numbers, but that their significations extend more widely and creep into many things. For that number in the *Revelation* does not pertain to times, but to men.

## On the sixth rule of Tyconius.

**36.** 52. Tyconius calls the sixth rule *Recapitulation*, discovered watchfully enough in the obscurity of the Scriptures. For some things are said as though they follow in order of time or are narrated in the continuation of events, when in fact the narrative secretly goes back to earlier things that had been passed over. Unless this is understood by this rule, one goes astray.

As in *Genesis*: *and the Lord God planted*, it says, *a garden in Eden toward the east, and there he put the man whom he had formed; and God brought forth besides, out of the ground, every tree fair and good for food*[^107]. So it seems to be said as though this happened after God had put the man, once made, in the garden; whereas, having briefly mentioned both—that is, that *God planted a garden and put there the man whom he had formed*—he returns by recapitulation and says what he had passed over, namely how the garden had been planted, because *God brought forth besides, out of the ground, every tree fair and good for food*.

Then, following on, he added: *and the tree of life in the midst of the garden, and the tree of the knowledge of good and evil*[^108]. Next the river by which the garden was watered is unfolded, *divided into four heads* of four rivers[^109]—all of which pertains to the establishing of the garden. And when he had finished this, he repeated what he had already said, and what in reality came after it, and says: *and the Lord God took the man whom he had fashioned, and put him in the garden*[^110], and so on. For after these things were done the man was put there, as the order itself now shows. These things were not done after the man was put there, as the earlier statement might be supposed to mean, unless the recapitulation is watchfully understood there, by which there is a return to the things that had been passed over.

## On the confusion of tongues and the dispersion of the nations.

**36.** 53. Likewise in that book, when the generations of the sons of Noah were being recounted, it was said: *these are the sons of Ham in their tribes, according to their tongues, in their regions and in their nations*[^111]. When the sons of Shem too had been enumerated, it is said: *these are the sons of Shem in their tribes, according to their tongues, in their regions and in their nations*[^112]. And about all of them it is added: *these are the tribes of the sons of Noah, according to their generations, according to their nations. From these the islands*[^113] of the nations were dispersed over the earth after the flood. And the whole earth was one lip and one voice for all[^114]. This, then, which is added—*and the whole earth was one lip and one voice for all*, that is, one tongue of all—seems to be said as though at that time, when they had already been dispersed over the earth even according to the islands of the nations, there was for all one common tongue. This without doubt conflicts with the earlier words, where it was said, *in their tribes, according to their tongues*. For the individual tribes which had made the individual nations would not be said to have had their own tongues already, when there was for all one common one.

And therefore by way of recapitulation it was added, *and the whole earth was one lip and one voice for all*, the narrative secretly going back to tell how it came about that out of the one tongue of all they were divided into many. And immediately the building of that tower is narrated, where this punishment of pride was inflicted on them by divine judgment, after which deed they were dispersed over the earth according to their tongues.

## On the warnings reported in Luke 17:29–32.

**36.** 54. This recapitulation occurs even more obscurely, as in the Gospel where the Lord says: *on the day that Lot went out from Sodom it rained fire from heaven and destroyed them all: so will be the day of the Son of man, when he will be revealed. In that hour, whoever is on the housetop, and his goods in the house, let him not come down to take them; and likewise whoever is in the field, let him not turn back. Let him remember Lot's wife*[^115].

Are these things to be observed only when the Lord has been revealed, that no one look back, that is, seek again the past life which he has renounced? And not rather at this present time, so that, when the Lord has been revealed, each may find the recompense for the things he has kept or scorned?

And yet, because it was said *in that hour*, these things are supposed to be observed only when the Lord has been revealed—unless the reader's sense is watchful to understand the recapitulation, helped by another Scripture which, in the time of the apostles themselves, already cried out, *children, it is the last hour*[^116]. The very time, then, in which the Gospel is being preached, until the Lord is revealed, is the hour in which these things must be observed, because the Lord's revelation itself also pertains to the same hour, which will be ended on the day of judgment.[^117]

## On the seventh rule of Tyconius.

**37.** 55. The seventh and last rule of Tyconius is *On the devil and his body*. For he too is the head of the impious, who are in a manner his body, destined to go with him into the punishment of eternal fire,[^118] just as Christ *is the head of the Church, which is his body*[^119], destined to be with him in the kingdom and in everlasting glory. As, then, in the first rule, which he calls *On the Lord and his body*, we must be watchful, when Scripture speaks of one and the same person, to understand what suits the head and what the body, so in this last rule too something is sometimes said of the devil which can be recognized not in himself but rather in his body, which he has not only in those who are most plainly *outside*, but also in those who, though they belong to him, are nevertheless for a time mingled with the Church, until each one departs from this life or the chaff is separated from the wheat by the final winnowing fan.[^120]

For what is written in Isaiah, *how has Lucifer fallen, who rose in the morning*[^121], and the rest, which under the figure of the king of Babylon were said of the same person, or to the same person, in the very texture of the discourse, are of course understood of the devil. And yet what is said there, *he who sends to all the nations is crushed to the earth*[^122], does not entirely suit the head himself. For although the devil sends his angels to all the nations, yet it is his body, not he himself, that is crushed to the earth—except insofar as he himself is in his body, which is crushed and becomes like *the dust which the wind drives from the face of the earth*[^123].

## The book is concluded: the kinds of expression, the necessity of prayer.

**37.** 56. But all these rules, except the one called *On the promises and the Law*, cause one thing to be understood from another, which is proper to figurative expression—a thing which extends more widely than, as it seems to me, can be wholly grasped by anyone. For wherever, as it were, one thing is said so that another may be understood, even if the name of the trope itself is not found in the art of speaking, it is figurative expression.

And when this occurs where it usually occurs, understanding follows without effort; but when it occurs where it usually does not, there is effort in understanding it—by some more, by some less, just as the gifts of God are more or less present in the talents of men, or aids are bestowed.

Accordingly, just as with literal words, about which we disputed above, where things are to be understood as they are said,[^124] so with metaphorical words, which make up figurative expressions, where one thing is to be understood from another—about which we have so far dealt sufficiently, as far as seemed good;

students of the venerable Letters must not only be admonished to know the kinds of expression in the Holy Scriptures, and to attend watchfully and hold in memory how something is customarily said in them, but also—what is chief and most necessary—to pray that they may understand. For in those very Letters of which they are students they read that *the Lord gives wisdom, and from his face come knowledge and understanding*[^125]; from whom they have received the very study itself, if it is endowed with piety.

But let this be said as enough about signs too, as far as words are concerned. It remains for us to treat, in the following volume, with whatever the Lord may grant, of the setting forth of the things we perceive.
