# Book IV

## The fourth book deals with the setting forth of things.

**1.** 1. This work of ours which is entitled *Teaching Christianity* I had, in the first distribution, divided into two parts, as it were. For after the preface, in which I answered those who were going to find fault with it, I said: *there are two things on which the whole treatment of the Scriptures rests: the way of discovering what is to be understood, and the way of setting forth what has been understood. Of discovering we will treat first, of setting forth afterward*. Since, then, we have already said much about discovering and have completed three volumes on this one part, with the Lord's help we shall say a few things about setting forth, so that, if it can be done, we may close everything in one book and end this whole work in four volumes.

## It is not the purpose of this work to hand down the precepts of rhetoric.

**1.** 2. First, then, by this prefatory remark I restrain the expectation of readers who perhaps suppose that I am going to give the precepts of rhetoric which I both learned and taught in the secular schools, and I warn them not to expect such things from me—not because they have no usefulness, but because, if they have any, it must be learned separately, should some good man perhaps have the leisure to learn even these things; it is not, however, to be sought from me, either in this work or in any other.

## It is fitting for the Christian teacher to make use of the faculty of rhetoric.

**2.** 3. For since by the art of rhetoric both truths and falsehoods are urged, who would dare to say that, against falsehood, truth ought to stand defenseless in the persons of its defenders—so that, namely, those who try to persuade people of false things should know how to make a hearer well-disposed, attentive, and teachable by their preface,[^1] while these defenders of truth should not?

Should the one party narrate falsehoods briefly, clearly, and plausibly, while the other narrates truths in such a way that it is tiresome to listen, impossible to understand, and, in the end, unappealing to believe?

Should the one party assail the truth and assert falsehood with deceptive arguments, while the other is able neither to defend truths nor to refute falsehoods?

Should the one party, moving and driving the minds of hearers into error, by their speaking terrify, sadden, gladden, and ardently exhort, while the other, on behalf of truth, drowses sluggish and cold?

Who is so foolish as to think this wise? Since, then, the faculty of eloquence is set in the midst, which avails very much for persuading whether to crooked or to right things, why is it not procured by the zeal of the good, so that it may do service to truth, if the wicked usurp it for obtaining their perverse and empty causes in the uses of iniquity and error?

## At what age and by what method the precepts of rhetoric can be learned.

**3.** 4. But whatever observations and precepts there are on this matter—by which, when there is added the most skillful habit, in many words and verbal ornaments, of a more practiced tongue, there comes about what is called fluency or eloquence—must be learned outside these our Letters, by those who can do so quickly, with a suitable space of time set apart for it, at a fit and convenient age. For even the very leaders of Roman eloquence were not ashamed to say that no one can ever fully learn this art unless he can learn it quickly.[^2] Whether this is true, what need is there to inquire? For even if these things could at last be learned in time by slower minds, we do not value them so highly as to wish the already mature, or even weighty, ages of men to be spent in learning them. It is enough that this be the concern of the young—and not of all of them whom we desire to be trained for the usefulness of the Church, but of those whom no more pressing necessity, which is without doubt to be put before this matter, has occupied.

For if a keen and fervent talent is present, eloquence clings more readily to those who read and hear the eloquent than to those who pursue the precepts of eloquence. Nor are there lacking ecclesiastical writings—even besides the canon wholesomely placed in the citadel of authority—by reading which a capable person, even if he is not aiming at it but is only intent on the things said there, is also imbued, while he is engaged in them, with the eloquence in which they are said; and this happens especially with the added exercise of writing or of dictating, and finally of speaking, what he thinks in accordance with the rule of piety and faith.

But if such a talent is lacking, those rhetorical precepts are not grasped; or, if they are grasped in however small a part by being drilled in with great labor, they are of no profit. Since indeed even those who have learned them and speak copiously and ornately cannot all, in order to speak according to them, think of those precepts while they speak, unless they are discussing them. Rather, I think there are scarcely any of them who can do both, both speak well and, in order to do so, think of those precepts of speaking while they speak.

For one must beware lest the things to be said slip from the mind while attention is given to saying them artfully. And yet in the sermons and discourses of the eloquent the precepts of eloquence are found fulfilled, of which those speakers did not think, either to be eloquent or while they were being eloquent—whether they had learned them or had not even touched upon them. For they fulfill those precepts because they are eloquent; they do not apply them in order to be eloquent.

## Children learn ways of speaking from the mouths of those who speak.

**3.** 5. Therefore, since out of infants there come speakers only by learning the speech of speakers, why can they not become eloquent with no art of eloquence handed down, but by reading and hearing the utterances of the eloquent and, as far as it is granted to attain, by imitating them? Indeed, we observe by examples themselves that this is so. For without rhetorical precepts we know very many who are more eloquent than very many who have learned them; but without having read and heard the discourses or utterances of the eloquent, no one.

For children would not need even the art of grammar itself, by which the correctness of speech is learned, if it were granted them to grow up and live among people who spoke correctly. For, knowing no names of faults, whatever faulty thing they heard from the mouth of anyone speaking, they would by their own sound habit censure and avoid, just as the city-bred censure country folk, even those who do not know their letters.

## The duty of the Christian teacher . . .

**4.** 6. The treater and teacher of the divine Scriptures, then, the defender of right faith and vanquisher of error, ought both to teach good things and to unteach evil things, and in this work of speech to win over the hostile, rouse the slack, and make known to those who do not know what is going on what they ought to expect.

But when he has either found or himself made his hearers well-disposed, attentive, and teachable, the rest is to be carried through as the case demands. If those who hear are to be taught, it must be done by narration—if indeed there is need of it—so that the matter in question may become known. But that things which are doubtful may become certain, reasoning must be employed, with proofs brought to bear.[^3]

But if those who hear are to be moved rather than taught, so that they may not be sluggish in acting upon what they already know and may give their assent to things which they confess to be true, there is need of greater forces of speaking. There entreaties and rebukes, stirrings and restraints, and whatever else avails to move minds, are necessary.

## . . . and of any educated person.

**4.** 7. And all these things which I have mentioned nearly all people never cease to do in the matters they carry out by speaking.

## It matters more that the Christian orator speak wisely than that he speak eloquently.

**5.** 7. But since some do these things dully, unattractively, and coldly, while others do them keenly, ornately, and forcefully, the man who ought to come to this work[^4] of which we are treating is one who can discuss or speak wisely, even if he cannot do so eloquently, so as to profit his hearers—even if less than he would profit if he could speak eloquently as well.

But the one who overflows with foolish eloquence is the more to be guarded against the more the hearer is delighted by him in matters which it is useless to hear, and supposes, because he hears him speak fluently, that he is also speaking truly. This opinion did not escape even those who thought the art of rhetoric should be taught. For they confessed that "wisdom without eloquence is of little profit to states, but eloquence without wisdom is generally a great harm, and never a profit."[^5]

If, then, those who handed down the precepts of eloquence were compelled by the prompting of truth to confess this in the very books in which they did so—not knowing the true, that is, the heavenly wisdom which *comes down from the Father of lights*[^6]—how much more ought we to think no otherwise, who are the children and ministers of this wisdom?

But a man speaks the more wisely or the less the more or less he has advanced in the Holy Scriptures—I do not mean in reading them much and committing them to memory, but in understanding them well and diligently searching out their meanings. For there are those who read them and neglect them: they read so as to retain, they neglect so as not to understand. Far to be preferred to these, without doubt, are those who retain their words less and see their heart with the eyes of their own heart. But better than both is the one who both can quote them when he wishes and understands them as he ought.

## The art of speaking is acquired by reading the books of orators.

For this man, then, who ought to speak wisely even what he cannot speak eloquently, it is most necessary to retain the words of the Scriptures.[^7]

For the poorer he perceives himself to be in his own resources, the richer he ought to be in these, so that what he says in his own words he may prove from theirs; and one who was the lesser in his own words may, by the testimony of great men, in a manner grow.[^8] For by proving, he gives delight who is less able to delight by speaking.

Furthermore, the one who wishes to speak not only wisely but also eloquently—since he will surely profit more if he can do both—I more gladly send to read or hear and, by exercise, to imitate the eloquent than I bid him spend his time on masters of the art of rhetoric; provided, however, that those who are read and heard are praised by truthful report as having spoken or speaking not only eloquently but also wisely.

For those who speak eloquently are heard with pleasure; those who speak wisely, with profit. On account of which Scripture does not say, "a multitude of the eloquent," but *a multitude of the wise is the health of the world*[^9].

But just as bitter things that are wholesome must often be taken, so harmful sweetness must always be avoided. Yet what is better than wholesome sweetness or sweet wholesomeness? For the more sweetness is sought there, the more easily does wholesomeness do its good. There are, then, ecclesiastical men who have treated the divine utterances not only wisely but also eloquently,[^10] for the reading of whom time is more likely to fail than they themselves to be lacking to those who study and have leisure.

## In the sacred authors eloquence surpasses profane eloquence.

**6.** 9. Here someone perhaps asks whether our authors, whose writings, divinely inspired, made for us a canon by their most wholesome authority, are to be called only wise or eloquent as well. This question, indeed, is most easily resolved in my own mind and in the minds of those who feel with me about what I say. For where I understand them, not only does nothing seem to me wiser, but also nothing more eloquent. And I dare to say that all who rightly understand what they say understand at the same time that they could not have spoken otherwise.

For just as there is a certain eloquence that better befits youthful age, and one that befits old age—and it is no longer to be called eloquence if it does not suit the person of the speaker—so there is a certain eloquence that befits men most worthy of the highest authority and plainly divine. This is what they spoke; and no other eloquence befits them, nor does theirs befit others. For it is suited to them; but others, the lowlier it seems, the more loftily it surpasses—not by windiness, but by solidity.

But where I do not understand them, their eloquence indeed appears less to me; yet I do not doubt that it is such as it is where I do understand. The very obscurity, too, of the divine and wholesome sayings had to be mingled with such eloquence, in which our understanding ought to make progress not only by discovery but also by exercise.[^11]

## The eloquence of the sacred authors is the fruit of wisdom.

**6.** 10. I could indeed, if there were leisure, show all the virtues and ornaments of eloquence—about which those people are puffed up who prefer their own tongue to the tongue of our authors not by its greatness but by its swelling—in the Sacred Letters of those whom divine providence provided for our instruction and for transferring us from this crooked age into the blessed age.[^12] But it is not those features that delight me, more than can be told, in that eloquence which these men have in common with the orators or poets of the Gentiles.

I rather marvel and am amazed at this, that they used that eloquence of ours[^13] by means of a certain other eloquence of their own, in such a way that it was neither lacking to them nor prominent in them, because it had neither to be disapproved by them nor to be paraded.

The one of these would happen if it were avoided, the other might be thought if it were easily recognized. And in whatever passages it is perhaps recognized by the learned, such matters are spoken that the words by which they are said seem not to have been applied by the speaker, but joined to the things themselves as if of their own accord—as though you were to understand wisdom proceeding from its own house, that is, from the breast of the wise man, and eloquence following like an inseparable handmaid, even uncalled.

## The teaching of rhetoric is illustrated from Romans 5:3–5.

**7.** 11. For who does not see what the Apostle wished to say, and how wisely he said it: *we glory in tribulations, knowing that tribulation produces patience, and patience trial, and trial hope; and hope does not disappoint, because the love of God has been poured out in our hearts through the Holy Spirit who has been given to us*[^14]? Here, if anyone—unskillfully skilled, so to speak—should contend that the Apostle followed the precepts of the art of eloquence, will he not be laughed at by Christians learned and unlearned alike? And yet here is recognized the figure which is called *climax* in Greek, but in Latin by some *gradatio* ("a stepping up"), since they were unwilling to say "ladder," when words or thoughts are linked one from another; as here we see patience linked from tribulation, trial from patience, hope from trial.

Another grace too is recognized: that after several clauses, each ended by a pause of the voice,[^15] which ours call "members" and "cuts," but the Greeks *cola* and *commata*, there follows the circuit or period, which they call *periodos*, whose members are held in suspense by the speaker's voice until it is ended by the last. For of those that precede the period, the first member is *since tribulation produces patience*, the second *and patience trial*, the third *and trial hope*. Then the period itself is joined on, which is carried through by three members, of which the first is *and hope does not disappoint*, the second *because the love of God has been poured out in our hearts*, the third *through the Holy Spirit who has been given to us*. But these and the like are handed down in the art of elocution. As, then, we do not say that the Apostle followed the precepts of eloquence, so we do not deny that eloquence followed his wisdom.

## A wonderful example of wisdom and eloquence in 2 Corinthians 11:16–30.

**7.** 12. Writing to the *Corinthians*, in the second epistle he refutes certain false apostles who were of the Jews and who disparaged him. And since he is compelled to praise himself, attributing to himself this seeming foolishness, how wisely he speaks, and how eloquently! But with wisdom as his companion, with eloquence as his guide—following the one, going before the other, and not spurning it as it follows.

*Again I say*, he says, *let no one think me foolish; but if you do, then accept me as foolish, that I too may boast a little. What I am saying I say not according to God, but as in foolishness, in this matter of boasting. Since indeed many boast according to the flesh, I too will boast. For you gladly bear with the foolish, being wise yourselves. For you tolerate it if anyone reduces you to slavery, if anyone devours you, if anyone takes from you, if anyone exalts himself, if anyone strikes you in the face. I speak according to dishonor, as though we had been weak*[^16].

*But in whatever anyone dares (I speak in foolishness), I too dare. Are they Hebrews? So am I. Are they Israelites? So am I. Are they the offspring of Abraham? So am I. Are they ministers of Christ? (I speak as a fool) I more so*[^17].

*In labors more abundantly, in prisons more frequently, in beatings beyond measure, in deaths often. From the Jews five times I received forty lashes less one. Three times I was beaten with rods, once I was stoned, three times I was shipwrecked; a night and a day I was in the deep of the sea; in journeys often, in dangers from rivers, in dangers from robbers, in dangers from my own race, in dangers from the Gentiles, in dangers in the city, in dangers in the wilderness, in dangers at sea, in dangers among false brethren; in toil and hardship, in watchings often, in hunger and thirst, in fastings often, in cold and nakedness; besides those things which are external, my daily pressure, the anxiety for all the Churches*[^18].

*Who is weak, and I am not weak? Who is made to stumble, and I am not inflamed? If I must boast, I will boast in the things that pertain to my weakness*[^19].

With how great wisdom these things were said, the watchful see; but with how great a river of eloquence they also concurred, even one who snores notices.

## The example of 2 Corinthians 11:16–30 is weighed.

**7.** 13. Now anyone who knows recognizes that those cuts which the Greeks call *commata*, and the members and periods, about which I spoke a little before, when interposed with a most becoming variety, produced that whole appearance of the discourse and, as it were, its very countenance, by which even the unlearned are delighted and moved. For from where we began to insert this passage, they are periods.

The first is the smallest, that is, of two members; for periods cannot have fewer than two members, though they can have more. So that first one is: *again I say, let no one think me foolish*.

There follows another of three members: *but if you do, then accept me as foolish, that I too may boast a little*.[^20]

The third, which follows, has four members: *what I am saying I say not according to God, but as in foolishness, in this matter of boasting*.

The fourth has two: *since indeed many boast according to the flesh, I too will boast*.

And the fifth has two: *for you gladly bear with the foolish, being wise yourselves*.

The sixth too is of two members: *for you tolerate it if anyone reduces you to slavery*.[^21]

There follow three cuts: *if anyone devours you, if anyone takes from you, if anyone exalts himself*.

Then three members: *if anyone strikes you in the face, I speak according to dishonor, as though we had been weak*.

A period of three members is added: *but in whatever anyone dares (I speak in foolishness), I too dare*.

From here on, individual cuts being set down by way of question, individual cuts likewise are rendered by way of answer, three to three: *are they Hebrews? So am I. Are they Israelites? So am I. Are they the offspring of Abraham? So am I*.

But a fourth cut being set down with a like question, he answers not with the opposition of another cut, but of a member: *are they ministers of Christ? (I speak as a fool) I more so*.

Now the four following cuts pour forth, the question being most becomingly removed: *in labors more abundantly, in prisons more frequently, in beatings beyond measure, in deaths often*.

Then a short period is interposed, since by a suspended delivery it must be marked off: *from the Jews five times*, so that this is one member, to which another is linked: *I received forty lashes less one*.

Thence there is a return to cuts, and three are set down: *three times I was beaten with rods, once I was stoned, three times I was shipwrecked*. A member follows: *a night and a day I was in the deep of the sea*.

Then fourteen cuts flow forth with a most becoming rush: *in journeys often, in dangers from rivers, in dangers from robbers, in dangers from my own race, in dangers from the Gentiles, in dangers in the city, in dangers in the wilderness, in dangers at sea, in dangers among false brethren; in toil and hardship, in watchings often, in hunger and thirst, in fastings often, in cold and nakedness*.

After these he interposes a period of three members: *besides those things which are external, my daily pressure, the anxiety for all the Churches*.

And to this he subjoins two members by way of questioning: *who is weak, and I am not weak? Who is made to stumble, and I am not inflamed?*

Finally, this whole passage, as if panting, is ended by a period of two members: *if I must boast, I will boast in the things that pertain to my weakness*.

But that after this rush, by interposing a little narrative, he in a manner rests and makes the hearer rest—what grace, what delight this has, cannot be sufficiently told. For he goes on, saying: *the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, who is blessed for ever, knows that I do not lie*[^22].

And then he narrates most briefly how he had been endangered and how he escaped.[^23]

## The sacred authors, though they possess eloquence, do not parade it.

**7.** 14. It would take long to pursue the rest, or to point out these things in other passages of the Holy Scriptures. What if I had wished to show even the figures of speech which are handed down by that art, at least in those passages of the Apostle's eloquence which I have mentioned?[^24] Would not serious people sooner think me excessive than any of the studious think me self-sufficient?[^25] When all these things are taught by masters, they are held to be a great matter, bought at a great price, sold with great boasting. And I fear that I myself smack of that boasting while I discuss these things thus. But an answer had to be given to ill-taught people who think our authors to be despised, not because they do not have the eloquence which these people love too much, but because they do not parade it.

## Besides Paul, many authors of the Scriptures are skilled in eloquence.

**7.** 15. But perhaps someone thinks that I have chosen the Apostle Paul as, so to speak, our eloquent man. For he seems, where he says, *though unskilled in speech, yet not in knowledge*[^26], to have spoken thus as if by conceding to his detractors, not as if acknowledging it true by confessing it. But if he had said, "unskilled indeed in speech, but not in knowledge," it could in no way be understood otherwise. Knowledge he plainly did not hesitate to profess, without which he could not have been the *teacher of the Gentiles*[^27].

Certainly, if we bring forward anything of his as an example of eloquence, we bring it of course from those Epistles which even his very detractors, who wished the speech of his presence to be thought contemptible, confessed to be *weighty and strong*[^28].

I see, then, that I must say something also about the eloquence of the prophets, where many things are covered over by figurative language. The more they seem to be veiled by metaphorical words, the sweeter they are when they have been laid open.[^29]

But in this place I ought to mention something of such a kind that I am not compelled to expound what is said, but only to point out how it is said. And I will do this preferably from the book of that prophet who says that he was a shepherd or herdsman, and was thence taken away by God and sent to prophesy to the people of God.[^30] Yet not according to the *Septuagint* translators,[^31] who, having also translated by the divine Spirit, seem on that account to have said some things otherwise, so that the reader's attention might be the more directed to searching out the spiritual sense (whence some things of theirs are also more obscure because they are more figurative)—but as they have been translated from the Hebrew into Latin speech by the priest Jerome, skilled in both languages.

## Amos rebukes the wanton with great force.

**7.** 16. When, therefore, he was rebuking the impious, the proud, the self-indulgent, and those most neglectful, on that account, of brotherly love, this prophet—a countryman, or sprung from a countryman—cried out, saying: *Woe to you who are wealthy in Zion, and who trust in the mountain of Samaria, you nobles, heads of the peoples, entering the house of Israel with pomp! Pass over to Calneh and see, and go from there to great Hamath, and go down to Gath of the Philistines, and to all their best kingdoms, whether their boundary is wider than your boundary. You who are set apart for the day of evil, and draw near to the throne of iniquity; who sleep on beds of ivory and are wanton on your couches; who eat the lamb from the flock and the calves from the midst of the herd; who sing to the sound of the harp. Like David they thought they had instruments of song, drinking wine in bowls, and anointed with the finest ointment; and they suffered nothing over the affliction of Joseph*[^32].

Would those who despise our prophets as untutored and ignorant of elocution, like learned and eloquent men, if anything of this kind had had to be said by them or against such people, have wished to speak otherwise—those of them, at least, who would not have wished to rave?

## The rules of eloquence are uncovered in Amos 6:1–2.

**7.** 17. For what is there in this eloquence that sober ears could desire more? First, the very invective—with what a roar was it dashed against senses as if asleep, so that they might wake up? *Woe to you who are wealthy in Zion, and who trust in the mountain of Samaria, you nobles, heads of the peoples, entering the house of Israel with pomp!*[^33] Then, to show them ungrateful for the benefits of God, who had given them the ample expanses of a kingdom, since they trusted in the mountain of Samaria, where of course idols were worshiped: *pass over*, he says, *to Calneh and see, and go from there to great Hamath, and go down to Gath of the Philistines, and to all their best kingdoms, whether their boundary is wider than your boundary*[^34]. At the same time, too, while these things are said, the speech is adorned with the names of places as if with lights: Zion, Samaria, Calneh, great Hamath, and Gath of the Philistines. Then the verbs joined to these places are most becomingly varied: *you are wealthy, you trust, pass over, go, go down*.

## The rules of eloquence in Amos 6:3–4.

**7.** 18. Next it is announced that the coming captivity under an unjust king draws near, when there is added: *you who are set apart for the day of evil, and draw near to the throne of iniquity*. Then the deserts of self-indulgence are subjoined: *who sleep on beds of ivory and are wanton on your couches; who eat the lamb from the flock and the calves from the midst of the herd*.

These six members produced three periods of two members each. For he did not say: "you who are set apart for the day of evil, you who draw near to the throne of iniquity, you who sleep on beds of ivory, you who are wanton on your couches, you who eat the lamb from the flock, you who eat the calves from the midst of the herd." If it were said thus, this too would indeed be beautiful, that all six members should run on from a single repeated pronoun and each be ended by the voice of the one delivering it.

But it was done more beautifully, that to the same pronoun two clauses each were joined, which unfolded three thoughts: one for the foretelling of captivity, *you who are set apart for the day of evil and draw near to the throne of iniquity*; another for lust, *you who sleep on beds of ivory and are wanton on your couches*; and a third pertaining to gluttony, *you who eat the lamb from the flock and the calves from the midst of the herd*—so that it is in the power of the one delivering it whether he ends each one, and there are six members, or whether he holds the first and third and fifth in suspense by his voice, and, linking the second to the first, the fourth to the third, the sixth to the fifth, most becomingly makes three periods of two members each: one by which the impending calamity, another by which the impure bed, the third by which the prodigal table is shown.

## The rules of eloquence in Amos 6:5–6.

**7.** 19. Then he gnaws at the self-indulgent pleasure of the ears. Here, when he had said, *who sing to the sound of the harp*—since music can be practiced wisely by the wise—with wonderful grace of speaking, the rush of the invective slackened, and now speaking not to them but about them, so as to admonish us to distinguish the music of the wise man from the music of the self-indulgent, he did not say, "you who sing to the sound of the harp, and like David think you have instruments of song," but, when he had said to them what the self-indulgent ought to hear—*who sing to the sound of the harp*—he in a manner indicated their lack of skill to others, adding: *like David they thought they had instruments of song, drinking wine in bowls, and anointed with the finest ointment*[^35]. These three are better delivered if, the two former members being held in suspense, they are ended by the third.

## The rules of eloquence in Amos 6:6.

**7.** 20. Now indeed that which is added to all these, *and they suffered nothing over the affliction of Joseph*—whether it be said continuously so as to be one member, or more becomingly held in suspense: *and they suffered nothing*, and after this division there be brought in *over the affliction of Joseph*, so that it is a period of two members;

with wonderful grace it was not said, "they suffered nothing over the affliction of a brother," but in place of "brother" was put *Joseph*, so that any brother whatever might be signified by the proper name of him whose fame among his brothers is illustrious, whether in the evils he paid out or in the good he repaid.[^36]

This trope, certainly, by which Joseph is made to be understood as any brother whatever, I do not know whether it is handed down by that art which we learned and taught.[^37] Yet how beautiful it is, and how it affects those who read and understand, there is no need to tell anyone, if he does not feel it himself.

## The sacred authors are found to be such as he who sent them willed.

**7.** 21. And many more things indeed which pertain to the precepts of eloquence can be found in this very passage which we have set down as an example. But it does not so much instruct a good hearer, if it is diligently analyzed, as it kindles him, if it is ardently delivered. For these things were not composed by human industry, but poured out by the divine mind both wisely and eloquently—wisdom not intent upon eloquence, but eloquence not departing from wisdom.

For if—as certain most eloquent and most acute men have been able to see and to say[^38]—the things that are learned as if by the oratorical art would not be observed and noted and reduced into this teaching, unless they had first been found in the talents of orators,[^39] what wonder if they are found also in those whom he sent who made the talents? Wherefore let us confess our canonical authors and teachers to be eloquent indeed, not only wise, with such eloquence as befitted persons of that kind.

## The obscurity of the sacred authors, though eloquent, is not to be imitated by Christian teachers.

**8.** 22. But even if we take some examples of elocution from those writings of theirs which are understood without difficulty, we ought by no means to think that we must imitate them in those things which they said with a useful and wholesome obscurity—in order, as it were, to exercise and refine the minds of readers, and to break through fastidiousness[^40] and sharpen the zeal of those wishing to learn, and also to veil[^41] the minds of the impious, whether that they might be converted to piety or shut out from the mysteries.

For they spoke in such a way that later men, who should rightly understand and expound them, might find in the Church of God a second grace—unlike, indeed,[^42] yet nevertheless following on.

Their expositors, therefore, ought not to speak as though they were setting themselves forth to be expounded with like authority, but in all their discourses they should labor first and foremost to be understood, with such perspicuity of speaking as they can, so that either he is very slow who does not understand, or the cause why what we say can be understood less or more slowly lies in the difficulty and subtlety of the matters which we wish to explain and show, not in our manner of speaking.

## Things hard to understand: before whom and how they are to be treated.

**9.** 23. For there are certain things which by their own force are not understood, or scarcely understood, however much and however plainly the one who speaks may turn them over in his speech. These are to be brought before the hearing of the people either rarely, if something presses, or never at all.

But in books, which are so written that they in a manner hold the reader to themselves when they are understood, but when they are not understood are no trouble to those unwilling to read, and in conversations with some people, this duty is not to be abandoned: that truths, however difficult to understand, which we ourselves have already grasped, we should, with whatever labor of discussion, bring to the understanding of others—if the desire of learning holds the hearer or fellow-talker, and there is no lack of mental capacity which can in some way receive what is intimated; the one who teaches taking no care with how much eloquence he teaches, but with how much clearness.

## The pursuit of clearness in speaking.

**10.** 24. The diligent appetite for this clearness sometimes neglects the more refined words and does not care what sounds well, but what well indicates and intimates that which it intends to show. Whence a certain author, when he was treating of such a kind of speech, said that there is in it a certain "diligent negligence."[^43]

Yet this negligence so withdraws ornament that it does not contract filth. Although in good teachers there is, or ought to be, so great a care of teaching that a word which cannot be Latin unless it is obscure or ambiguous, but in the common people's way is said so that ambiguity and obscurity are avoided, should be said not as the learned say it, but rather as the unlearned are accustomed to say it. For if our translators were not ashamed to say, *I will not gather their assemblies out of bloods*[^44], since they perceived it to be relevant to the matter that in that place this noun should be uttered in the plural, which in the Latin tongue is said only in the singular—the word "bloods," *sanguinibus*[^45]—why should a teacher of piety, speaking to the unskilled, be ashamed to say *ossum* rather than *os* for "bone,"[^46] lest that syllable be understood not from "bones" (*ossa*) but from "mouths" (*ora*), where African ears pass no judgment about the shortening or lengthening of vowels?

For what does correctness of speech profit, which the hearer's understanding does not follow, since there is no reason at all for speaking if those for whose sake we speak, that they may understand, do not understand what we say? The one who teaches, then, will avoid all words that do not teach; and if in place of them he can say other correct ones that are understood, he will rather choose that. But if he cannot, either because there are none or because at present they do not occur to him, he will use even less correct words, provided only that the matter itself is taught and learned correctly.

## Let the one who hears hear the truth, and understand what he hears.

**10.** 25. And this indeed not only in conversations—whether they are held with some one person or with several—but also, and much more, before the people when a sermon is delivered, we must insist on being understood; because in conversations each one has the power of asking, but where all are silent that one may be heard, and turn their faces, intent, upon him, there it is neither the custom nor the seemly thing for each to inquire about what he has not understood; and therefore the care of the speaker ought especially to come to the aid of the silent.

But a multitude eager to know is accustomed to signify by its own movement whether it has understood. Until it signifies this, the matter in hand must be turned over with a manifold variety of speaking—which those do not have in their power who deliver things prepared and retained by memory word for word.

But as soon as it has been established that the matter is understood, either the discourse must be ended or there must be a passing over to other things. For just as he is welcome who clears up what needs to be known, so he is burdensome who drives home what is already known—at least to those whose whole expectation was hanging on the resolving of the difficulty of the things being opened out. For things already known are said also for the sake of giving delight, where attention is paid not to them but to the manner in which they are said.

But if even the manner is already known and pleases the hearers, it makes almost no difference whether the one who delivers it is a speaker or a reader. For things that have been aptly written are accustomed to be read with pleasure not only by those to whom they first become known, but also to be reread, not without pleasure, by those to whom they are already known and whom forgetfulness has not yet erased them from memory, or to be gladly heard by both. But whatever each one has already forgotten, when he is reminded, he is taught.

But I am not now treating of the manner of giving delight; I am speaking of the manner in which those who desire to learn are to be taught. That is the best manner by which it comes about that the one who hears hears the truth and understands what he hears. When that end has been reached, then no further labor is to be spent on the matter itself, as though it had to be taught longer—but perhaps on commending it, that it may be fixed in the heart. And if this seems to be needed, it must be done so modestly that it does not arrive at weariness.

## Therefore one who tries to teach must speak clearly, yet not unpleasantly.

**11.** 26. This, in short, is eloquence in teaching, by which it comes about, through speaking, not that what was shuddered at becomes pleasing, or that what one was reluctant to do is done, but that what lay hidden appears. Yet if this is done unpleasantly, its fruit reaches only the few most studious, who desire to know the things that must be learned, however meanly and rudely they are said. And when they have attained these things, they feed delightfully on the truth itself; and it is the mark of good talents to love, in words, the truth, not the words.

For what does a golden key profit, if it cannot open what we wish? Or what harm does a wooden one do, if it can—since we seek nothing but that what is closed should lie open? But because those who eat and those who learn have a certain likeness to one another, on account of the fastidiousness of very many, even those very foods without which one cannot live must be seasoned.

## It is the orator's task to teach, to delight, to sway.

**12.** 27. A certain eloquent man said, then, and said truly, that the eloquent man ought to speak in such a way that he teaches, that he delights, that he sways. Then he added: *to teach belongs to necessity, to delight to charm, to sway to victory*[^47]. Of these three, the one placed first—that is, the necessity of teaching—is established in the things we say; the remaining two in the manner in which we say them.

The one who speaks, then, when he wishes to teach, let him not, so long as he is not understood, think that he has yet said what he wishes to the one whom he wishes to teach; because, even if he has said what he himself understands, he is not yet to be thought to have said it to one by whom he has not been understood; but if he has been understood, then in whatever manner he has spoken, he has said it.

But if he wishes also to delight the one to whom he speaks, or to sway him, he will not do it in whatever manner he speaks, but it matters how he speaks, in order to do it. And just as, in order that he may be held to listening, the hearer must be delighted, so, in order that he may be moved to act, he must be swayed.

And just as he is delighted if you speak pleasantly, so he is swayed if he loves what you promise, fears what you threaten, hates what you blame, embraces what you commend, grieves at what, as a thing to be grieved over, you set forth at length, rejoices when you proclaim something to be rejoiced over, has pity on those whom you set before his eyes by your speaking as objects of pity, flees those whom, by terrifying, you propose as men to be guarded against; and whatever else can be done by grand eloquence to move the minds of hearers, not that they may know what is to be done, but that they may do what they already know is to be done.

## Hearers must be taught before they are moved.

**12.** 28. But if they do not yet know, they must of course be taught before they are moved. And perhaps, once the things themselves are known, they will be so moved that there is no need for them to be moved any further by greater forces of eloquence. Yet when there is need, it must be done; and there is need when, though they know what is to be done, they do not do it. And on account of this, teaching belongs to necessity.

For people can both do and not do what they know. But who would say that they ought to do what they do not know? And therefore swaying does not belong to necessity, because it is not always needed, if the hearer assents merely to one who teaches or even delights him. But swaying belongs to victory, because it can happen that one is both taught and delighted and does not assent. And what will those two profit, if this third is lacking?

But neither does delighting belong to necessity, since, when by speaking true things are shown (which pertains to the duty of teaching), it is not brought about by eloquence, nor is attention paid to this, that either the things or the eloquence itself should delight; rather, true things, being made manifest through themselves, delight because they are true.

Hence even false things, when laid open and refuted, generally delight. For they delight not because they are false, but because it is true that they are false; and so the statement by which it is shown that this is true also delights.

## Minds are to be swayed in the end by speaking.

**13.** 29. But on account of those fastidious ones to whom the truth does not please if it is spoken in any other way than such that the speaker's discourse also pleases, no small place has been given to delight too in eloquence. Yet this, when added, is not enough for the hard, whom it has profited neither to have understood nor to have been delighted by the teacher's elocution.

For what do these two confer on the man who both confesses the truth and praises the eloquence, yet does not incline his assent—for the sake of which alone, when something is being urged, the speaker's attention keeps watch over the things that are said?

For if such things are taught as it suffices to believe or to know, to assent to them is nothing other than to confess that they are true. But when that is taught which is to be done, and is taught for this reason, that it may be done, in vain is it persuaded that what is said is true, in vain does the very manner in which it is said please, if it is not so learned that it is done.

It is fitting, therefore, that the ecclesiastical speaker, when he urges something that is to be done, not only teach in order to instruct and delight in order to hold, but also sway in order to conquer.

For that man remains to be swayed to consent by the grandeur of eloquence, in whom the truth demonstrated, with the added charm of expression, did not accomplish this up to his confession of it.

## The charm of expression must be procured in proportion to the argument.

**14.** 30. So much labor has been expended by people on this charm that so many and so great evils and base things—which were urged most eloquently by base and evil men, not so that one should consent to them, but for the sake of delight alone—are not only not to be done but even to be fled from and detested, and are nevertheless read again and again merely for the sake of the delight.

But may God turn away from his Church what the prophet Jeremiah recounts about the synagogue of the Jews, saying: *terror and horrible things have come to pass upon the land. The prophets prophesied iniquity, and the priests gave applause with their hands, and my people loved it so. And what will you do in the time to come?*[^48] O eloquence so much more terrible the purer it is, and so much more vehement the more solid it is! O truly an *axe cutting the rocks in pieces*! For God himself, through this very prophet, said that his word, which he made through the holy prophets, is like to this thing.[^49]

Far be it, then, far be it from us, that the priests should applaud those who say iniquitous things and the people of God should love it so. Far be it from us, I say, so great a madness; for what shall we do in the time to come? And let the things that are said certainly be less understood, less pleasing, less moving—yet let true things be said, and just things, not iniquitous ones, be gladly heard. Which would surely not happen unless they were said charmingly.

## Christian teaching calls one back from a redundancy of words.

**14.** 31. But among a serious people, of whom it was said to God, *among a serious people I will praise you*[^50], that charm is not delightful by which, although iniquitous things are not indeed said, yet slight and frail goods are adorned with a foamy parade of words, with which not even great and stable things would be becomingly and seriously adorned.

There is something of this kind in a letter of the most blessed Cyprian, which I think either happened, or was done on purpose, so that it might be known by posterity from what kind of language the soundness of Christian teaching has called the tongue back from that redundancy, and restrained it to a more serious and more modest eloquence—such as in his subsequent letters is safely loved, religiously sought after, but most difficult to attain. He says, then, in a certain place:

> *Let us seek this seat: the neighboring retreats grant seclusion, where, while the wandering tendrils of the vine-shoots creep in pendulous links along the reeds that bear them, they have made a leafy roof, a portico of vines*[^51].

These things are not said except with a marvelously overflowing fecundity of eloquence, but by their excessive profusion they displease seriousness. But those who love such things suppose that those who do not speak so, but utter themselves more chastely, cannot speak in that way—that they do not avoid such things by judgment. Wherefore this holy man both showed that he could speak so, because he did so somewhere, and that he was unwilling, since afterward he did so nowhere.

## God is to be prayed to by the ecclesiastical teacher before his sermon.

**15.** 32. This eloquent man of ours, then, when he says things just and holy and good—for he ought to say no others—acts, when he says these things, as much as he can, so that he may be heard with understanding, with pleasure, and with obedience.

And let him not doubt that he can do these things, if he can do them and insofar as he can, more by the piety of prayers than by the faculty of orators; so that, praying for himself and for those whom he is about to address, he may be a prayer before he is a speaker.[^52] When the very hour for speaking now draws near, before he puts forth the tongue that utters, let him lift up to God his thirsting soul, that he may belch forth what he has drunk, or pour out what he has been filled with.

For since, concerning each matter which is to be treated according to faith and love, there are many things that may be said, and many ways in which they may be said by those who know these things, who knows what it is expedient at the present time for us to say, or for it to be heard through us, except him who sees the hearts of all? And who brings it about that what is fitting, and in the way it is fitting, should be said by us, except him *in whose hand are both we and our words*[^53]?

And on account of this, let the one who both wishes to know and to teach learn indeed all the things that are to be taught, and procure a faculty of speaking such as befits an ecclesiastical man. But for the hour of the speaking itself, let him think that this rather befits a good mind, which the Lord says: *do not think how or what you are to speak; for it will be given you in that hour what you are to speak. For it is not you who speak, but the Spirit of your Father who speaks in you*[^54]. If, then, the Holy Spirit speaks in those who are handed over to persecutors for Christ's sake, why not also in those who hand Christ over to learners?

## Precepts of teaching are not given superfluously by man, even though God makes teachers.

**16.** 33. But whoever says that people are not to be given precepts about what or how they should teach, if the Holy Spirit makes the teachers, can say that we ought not to pray either, because the Lord says, *your Father knows what is necessary for you before you ask him*[^55]; or that the Apostle Paul ought not to have given precepts to *Timothy* and *Titus* about what or how they should give precepts to others. These three apostolic Epistles ought to be kept before the eyes of one on whom is laid the person of a teacher in the Church.

Is it not read in the *First to Timothy*: *announce these things and teach them*[^56]? And what these things are has been said above. Is it not there: *do not rebuke an elder, but entreat him as a father*[^57]?

Is it not said to him in the second: *hold the pattern of sound words which you heard from me*[^58]? Is it not there said to him: *be diligent, presenting yourself to God as an approved workman, not ashamed, rightly handling the word of truth*[^59]? There too is that: *preach the word, be urgent in season, out of season; reprove, entreat, rebuke, with all long-suffering and teaching*[^60].

And likewise to Titus, does he not say that a bishop ought to be one who perseveres according to the teaching of the faithful word, *so that he may be able both to exhort in sound teaching and to confute those who contradict*[^61]? There too he says: *but you, speak the things that befit sound teaching, that the old men be sober*[^62], and what follows. There too is that: *speak these things and exhort and rebuke with all authority. Let no one despise you. Admonish them to be subject to princes and powers*[^63], and the rest.

What, then, do we think? Does the Apostle hold an opinion against himself, who, while he says that teachers are made by the working of the Holy Spirit, himself gives them precepts about what and how they should teach? Or is it to be understood that even the duties of men ought not to cease in the very teaching of teachers, the Holy Spirit himself bestowing this; and yet that *neither he who plants is anything nor he who waters, but God who gives the increase*[^64]?

Hence, even when holy men, or even holy angels, are at work as ministers, no one rightly learns the things that pertain to living with God unless he is made by God teachable to God, to whom it is said in the Psalm: *teach me to do your will, for you are my God*[^65]. Hence the Apostle too says to Timothy himself—speaking, of course, as a teacher to a disciple: *but you, continue in the things which you have learned and which have been entrusted to you, knowing from whom you have learned them*[^66].

For just as the medicines of the body which are applied to people by people profit only those for whom God works health—who can heal even without them, while they cannot without him, and yet are applied; and if this is done dutifully, it is reckoned among the works of mercy or beneficence—so the aids of teaching, applied to the soul through a person, then profit when God works that they may profit, who was able to give the Gospel to a person even not from people nor through a person.

## To teaching, delighting, and swaying belong the three kinds of speaking.

**17.** 34. The one, therefore, who strives by speaking to persuade what is good, spurning none of those three—namely, that he teach, that he delight, that he sway—let him pray and act that, as we said above,[^67] he may be heard with understanding, with pleasure, and with obedience.

And when he does this aptly and fittingly, he can not undeservedly be called eloquent, even if the hearer's assent does not follow him.

For to these three—that is, that he teach, that he delight, that he sway—that author of Roman eloquence himself seems to have wished those other three also to pertain, when he likewise said: *he, then, will be eloquent who can say small things subduedly, moderate things temperately, great things grandly*[^68],

as if he were adding those other three too, and thus unfolding one and the same thought, saying: "he, then, will be eloquent who, in order to teach, can say small things subduedly; in order to delight, moderate things temperately; in order to sway, great things grandly."

## The ecclesiastical orator is always engaged in grand matter.

**18.** 35. These three things, as they were said by him, he could exhibit in forensic cases; but this is not so in ecclesiastical questions, in which the kind of discourse is engaged which we wish to form. For in those cases, those things are called small where the judgment concerns matters of money; those great where it concerns the safety and life of people. But those where none of these is to be judged, and nothing is done so that the hearer may act or decide, but only that he may be delighted, they called as if midway between the two, and on that account moderate, that is, restrained. For "moderate" (*modica*) takes its name from "measure" (*modus*), since we say "moderate" for "small" by misuse, not properly.[^69]

But in these matters of ours—since we ought to refer everything, especially what we say to the people from a higher place,[^70] to the salvation of people, not temporal but eternal, where eternal destruction too is to be guarded against—all the things we say are great, to such a degree that not even concerning those matters of money, whether to be acquired or to be lost, ought the things an ecclesiastical teacher says to seem small, whether that money be great or small.

For justice is not small, which surely we ought to keep even in a small matter of money, the Lord saying: *he who is faithful in the least is faithful also in much*[^71]. What is least, then, is least; but to be faithful in the least is great. For just as the principle of roundness—that is, that from the center point all lines should be drawn equal to the edge—is the same in a great disk as in a tiny coin, so where small things are done justly, the greatness of justice is not diminished.

## 1 Corinthians 6:1–9 is set forth as an example.

**18.** 36. About secular lawsuits, finally (and what are these but about money?), when the Apostle was speaking:

*Does any of you*, he says, *having a matter against another, dare to be judged before the unjust, and not before the saints? Or do you not know that the saints will judge the world? And if the world is judged among you, are you unworthy to judge the smallest matters? Do you not know that we will judge angels, to say nothing of secular affairs? If, then, you have secular judgments, set those who are contemptible in the Church to judge them. I say this to your shame*[^72].

*Is there not among you any wise man at all, who could judge between his brother? But brother goes to law with brother, and this before unbelievers. Now indeed it is altogether a fault that you have lawsuits among yourselves. Why do you not rather suffer wrong? Why are you not rather defrauded? But you do wrong and defraud, and this to brethren. Or do you not know that the unjust will not inherit the kingdom of God?*[^73]

What is it that the Apostle is so indignant about, so corrects, so reproaches, so rebukes, so threatens? What is it that he attests the feeling of his mind by so frequent and so harsh a change of voice? What is it, finally, that he speaks so grandly about the smallest matters? Did secular affairs deserve so much from him? Far from it! But he does this on account of justice, love, and piety, which, no sober mind doubting it, are great even in matters however tiny.

## Even when we commend the giving of a cup of cold water, we speak of a great matter.

**18.** 37. To be sure, if we were advising people how they ought to conduct those secular affairs themselves, whether for themselves or for their own, before ecclesiastical judges, we should rightly admonish them to conduct them subduedly, as small matters.[^74] But since we are discussing the eloquence of that man whom we wish to be the teacher of those things by which we are freed from eternal evils and arrive at eternal goods, wherever these things are dealt with—whether before the people or in private, whether to one or to many, whether to friends or to enemies, whether in continuous discourse or in conversation, whether in treatises or in books, whether in letters very long or very short—they are great.

Unless, perhaps, because a cup of cold water is a very small and very cheap thing, the Lord therefore said something very small and very cheap, that the one who gives it to his disciple *will not lose his reward*[^75]; or, indeed, when this teacher in the Church makes a sermon about it, he ought to think he is saying something small, and therefore that it must be said by him not temperately, not grandly, but subduedly. Did it not happen, when it fell to us to speak about this matter to the people, and God was present so that we spoke not unfittingly, that from that cold water, as it were, a kind of flame arose[^76] which kindled even the cold breasts of people to do works of mercy, in the hope of a heavenly reward?

## The different kinds of speaking are to be used on different occasions.

**19.** 38. And yet, although this teacher ought to be a speaker of great matters, he ought not always to speak them grandly, but subduedly when something is being taught, temperately when something is being blamed or praised. But when something is to be done, and we speak to those who ought to do it and yet are unwilling, then the things that are great must be said grandly, and in a manner suited to swaying minds. And sometimes about one and the same great matter it is said subduedly if it is taught, temperately if it is proclaimed,[^77] and grandly if a mind turned away from it is driven to be converted.

For what is greater than God himself? Is it therefore not learned? Or ought the one who teaches the unity of the Trinity to do it otherwise than by subdued discussion, so that a thing difficult to discern may, as far as is granted, be understood? Are ornaments sought here, and not proofs? Is the hearer here to be swayed in order to do something, and not rather to be instructed in order to learn?

Furthermore, when God is praised, whether for himself or for his works, what a face of beautiful and splendid expression arises for one who is able, as much as he is able, to praise him whom no one praises worthily, and whom no one in any way fails to praise! But if he is not worshiped, or if idols or demons or any creature whatever are worshiped along with him or even before him, how great this evil is, and how people are to be turned away from this evil, must of course be said grandly.

## Examples from the sacred Letters: first, of subdued expression.

**20.** 39. There is an example of subdued expression in the Apostle Paul—to mention something rather plain—where he says: *tell me, you who wish to be under the law, have you not heard the law? For it is written that Abraham had two sons, one by the slave girl and one by the free woman; but he who was of the slave girl was born according to the flesh, while he who was of the free woman was born through the promise. These things are by way of allegory. For these are the two covenants: one indeed from Mount Sinai, bearing children into slavery, which is Hagar. For Sinai is a mountain in Arabia, which is joined to the Jerusalem that now is, and is in slavery with her children. But the Jerusalem that is above is free, and she is our mother*[^78].

And likewise where he reasons and says: *brethren, I speak after the manner of men: yet no one annuls or adds to a man's testament once confirmed. The promises were spoken to Abraham and to his offspring. He does not say, "and to offsprings," as in the case of many, but as in one, "and to your offspring," which is Christ. But I say this: a testament confirmed by God the law, which came four hundred and thirty years after, does not invalidate so as to make the promises void. For if the inheritance is by the law, then it is no longer from the promise; but God granted it to Abraham through the promise*[^79].

And because it could occur to the hearer's thought, "Why, then, was the law given, if the inheritance is not from it?" he set this very objection to himself and said, as if asking, *what then is the law?* Then he answered: *it was added for the sake of transgressions, until the offspring should come to whom the promise was made, ordained through angels by the hand of a Mediator. Now a mediator is not a mediator of one, but God is one*[^80].

And here too occurred what he set before himself: *is the law, then, against the promises of God?* And he answered: *far from it!* and gave the reason, saying: *for if a law had been given which could give life, righteousness would altogether be from the law. But the Scripture has shut up all things under sin, that the promise by faith in Jesus Christ might be given to those who believe*[^81], and the rest, or whatever is of this kind.

It pertains, therefore, to the care of teaching not only to open closed things and to loose the knots of questions, but also, while this is being done, to meet other questions which perhaps may have arisen, lest through them what we say be disproved or refuted—provided, however, that the solution of those questions too occurs to us at the same time, lest we stir up what we cannot remove.

But it happens that, when other questions falling in upon a question, and others again falling in upon those that have fallen in, are treated thoroughly and resolved, the attention is stretched to such a length of reasoning that, unless the memory is very strong and vigorous, the disputant cannot return to the head from which he set out.

But it is very good that whatever can be objected, if it occurs to us, be refuted, lest it occur where there will be no one to answer, or occur to one present indeed but silent, and he go away less healed.

## Examples of temperate expression from the Pauline epistles.

**20.** 40. But in those apostolic words the expression is temperate: *do not rebuke an elder, but entreat him as a father, younger men as brothers, older women as mothers, younger women as sisters*[^82]. And in these: *I entreat you, therefore, brethren, by the mercy of God, to present your bodies as a living sacrifice, holy, pleasing to God*[^83]. And almost the whole passage of that exhortation has the temperate kind of elocution. Where those things are more beautiful in which proper things are becomingly rendered to proper things, as if due to them, such as: *having different gifts according to the grace that has been given to us: whether prophecy, according to the rule of faith; or ministry, in ministering; or one who teaches, in teaching; or one who exhorts, in exhortation; he who gives, in simplicity; he who presides, in carefulness; he who shows mercy, in cheerfulness. Let love be without pretense, hating evil, clinging to good, loving one another with the love of brotherhood, going before one another in mutual honor, not slothful in zeal, fervent in spirit, serving the Lord, rejoicing in hope, patient in tribulation, persevering in prayer, sharing in the needs of the saints, pursuing hospitality. Bless those who persecute you, bless and do not curse. Rejoice with those who rejoice, weep with those who weep, being of the same mind toward one another*[^84].

And how beautifully all these things, so poured out, are ended by a period of two members: *not minding high things, but consenting to lowly ones*[^85]! And somewhat later: *persevering in this very thing*, he says, *render to all their dues: to whom tribute, tribute; to whom toll, toll; to whom fear, fear; to whom honor, honor*[^86]. These things, poured forth member by member, are themselves also closed by a period which two members weave together: *owe no one anything, except to love one another*[^87]. And a little after: *the night has gone before*, he says, *but the day has drawn near. Let us therefore cast off the works of darkness and put on the armor of light. Let us walk becomingly as in the day, not in revelings and drunkenness, not in chamberings and wantonness, not in strife and rivalry; but put on the Lord Jesus Christ, and make no provision for the flesh in its desires*[^88].

But if anyone were to say it thus: "and make no provision in its desires for the flesh," he would no doubt soothe the ears with a more rhythmical close;[^89] but the more serious translator preferred to keep even the order of the words. But how this sounds in the Greek speech in which the Apostle spoke, let those more learned in that speech see for themselves; to me, however, what has been translated for us in the same order of words does not seem to run rhythmically even there.

## The ornament of expression which comes from rhythmical closes is rarely found in the Bible.

**20.** 41. To be sure, it must be confessed that this ornament of expression which comes from rhythmical closes is lacking to our authors. Whether this came about through the translators, or whether (which I rather think) they deliberately avoided these plausible devices, I do not dare to affirm, since I confess that I do not know. This, however, I do know: that if anyone skilled in this rhythm should arrange their closes by the law of those same rhythms—which is most easily done by changing certain words that have the same force in meaning, or by changing the order of those he finds—he will recognize that none of the things which he learned as if great in the schools of grammarians or rhetoricians was lacking to those divine men, and he will find many kinds of expression of such grace—which are indeed becoming in our language too, but most of all in their own—none of which is found in those writings over which these people are puffed up.

But care must be taken lest, while rhythm is added, weight be withdrawn from the divine and serious sentences.[^90] For that musical discipline, in which this rhythm is most fully learned, was so far from lacking to our prophets that the learned Jerome mentions the meters of some of them, in the Hebrew language at least.[^91] But, to keep their truth in the words, he did not translate these things over from there. For my part, however—to speak of my own feeling, which is of course better known to me than to others, and than that of others—just as in my own speech I do not omit these rhythmical closes, as far as I think can modestly be done, so in our authors this pleases me the more, that there I find them most rarely.

## On the grand kind of speaking.

**20.** 42. But the grand kind of speaking differs from this temperate kind chiefly in this, that it is not so much decked out with ornaments of words as it is violent with the affections of the mind. For it takes up even almost all those ornaments, but if it does not have them, it does not require them. For it is borne along by its own rush, and snatches up the beauty of elocution, if it occurs, by the force of the matters, not assuming it out of care for grace.

For it is enough for it, on account of the matter at hand, that fitting words be not chosen by the industry of the mouth, but follow the ardor of the breast.

For if a brave man be armed with weapons gilded and jeweled, most intent on the fight, he does indeed with those arms what he does, not because they are precious, but because they are arms; the same man is himself, and avails very much, even when *anger furnishes a weapon to him who gropes for one*[^92].

The Apostle is acting so that, for the sake of the Gospel ministry, all the evils of this time may be patiently borne, with the consolation of God's gifts. It is a great matter, and is dealt with grandly, nor are ornaments of speaking lacking: *behold*, he says, *now is the acceptable time, behold now is the day of salvation. Giving no offense to anyone, that the ministry be not blamed, but in all things commending ourselves as ministers of God: in much patience, in tribulations, in necessities, in distresses, in beatings, in prisons, in tumults, in labors, in watchings, in fastings, in chastity, in knowledge, in long-suffering, in kindness, in the Holy Spirit, in love unfeigned, in the word of truth, in the power of God; through the arms of righteousness on the right hand and on the left, through glory and dishonor, through ill repute and good repute; as deceivers and yet truthful, as unknown and yet recognized, as dying and behold we live, as chastened and not put to death, as sorrowful yet always rejoicing, as poor yet enriching many, as having nothing yet possessing all things*[^93]. See him still ardent: *our mouth is open to you, O Corinthians, our heart is enlarged*[^94], and the rest, which it would be long to pursue.

## An example of grand eloquence in Romans 8:28–39.

**20.** 43. Likewise, to the Romans he is acting so that the persecutions of this world may be conquered by love, with sure hope in the help of God. And he acts both grandly and ornately: *we know*, he says, *that for those who love God all things work together for good, for those who are called according to his purpose. Since those whom he foreknew, he also predestined to be conformed to the image of his Son, that he might be the firstborn among many brethren. And those whom he predestined, them he also called; and those whom he called, them he also justified; and those whom he justified, them he also glorified. What then shall we say to these things? If God is for us, who is against us? He who did not spare his own Son, but handed him over for us all, how has he not also with him given us all things? Who shall bring a charge against God's elect? Is it God who justifies? Who is it that condemns? Is it Christ Jesus who died, or rather who rose again, who is at the right hand of God, who also intercedes for us? Who shall separate us from the love of Christ? Tribulation? or distress? or persecution? or famine? or nakedness? or peril? or the sword? As it is written, 'for your sake we are put to death all the day long, we are accounted as sheep for the slaughter.' But in all these things we more than conquer through him who loved us. For I am certain that neither death nor life nor angel nor principality nor things present nor things to come nor power nor height nor depth nor any other creature will be able to separate us from the love of God, which is in Christ Jesus our Lord*[^95].

## Another example of grand eloquence in Galatians 4:10–20.

**20.** 44. But to the *Galatians*—although that whole *Epistle* is written in the subdued kind of speaking, except in the last parts where the expression is temperate—he nevertheless interposes a certain passage with such emotion of mind that, even without any such ornaments as are in those passages we just set down, it could not be said except grandly.

*You observe days*, he says, *and months and years and seasons. I fear for you, lest perhaps I have labored over you in vain. Be as I am, for I too am as you are. Brethren, I beg you, you have done me no wrong. You know that it was through weakness of the flesh that I preached the Gospel to you long ago, and you did not despise or spurn the trial to you in my flesh, but received me as an angel of God, as Christ Jesus. What, then, was your blessedness? I bear you witness that, if it had been possible, you would have plucked out your eyes and given them to me. Have I, then, become your enemy by preaching the truth to you? They are zealous for you, but not for good; they wish to shut you out, that you may be zealous for them. But it is good to be zealous in good always, and not only when I am present with you. My little children, with whom I am again in travail until Christ be formed in you. I could wish to be present with you now and to change my voice, for I am perplexed about you*[^96].

Were contrary words here rendered to contrary, or were any linked to one another by some gradation, or did cuts and members or periods sound forth? And yet the grand emotion, by which we feel the expression to grow fervent, did not on that account grow tepid.

## Cyprian uses the subdued kind of speaking.

**21.** 45. But these apostolic passages are so clear that they are also profound, and so written and committed to memory that they have need not only of a reader or hearer, but even of an expositor, if anyone, not content with the surface in them, seeks the depth. Wherefore let us look at these kinds of speaking in those who, by the reading of these authors, have advanced to the knowledge of divine and wholesome things and have ministered it to the Church.

The blessed Cyprian uses the subdued kind of speaking in that book where he disputes about the sacrament of the cup.[^97] For there a question is resolved in which it is asked whether the Lord's cup ought to have water alone or it mixed with wine. But for the sake of example something must be set down from it. After the beginning of the letter, then, now beginning to resolve the question proposed:

*Know, then, that we have been admonished that in offering the cup the Lord's tradition be observed, and that nothing be done by us other than what the Lord first did for us, namely that the cup which is offered in commemoration of him be offered mixed with wine. For since Christ says, 'I am the true vine,' the blood of Christ is assuredly not water, but wine. Nor can his blood, by which we have been redeemed and given life, be seen to be in the cup when wine is lacking to the cup, by which the blood of Christ is shown forth—which is proclaimed by the sacrament and testimony of all the Scriptures*[^98].

*For we find in Genesis that, with respect to the sacrament of Noah, this same thing ran ahead, and a figure of the Lord's passion existed there: that he drank wine, that he was made drunk, that he was made naked in his house, that he lay reclining with thighs bare and uncovered, that that nakedness of the father was noted by the middle son, but covered by the elder and the younger*[^99]*—and the rest, which it is not necessary to pursue, since it is enough to embrace this alone: that Noah, showing forth a type of the truth to come, drank not water but wine, and thus expressed the image of the Lord's passion*[^100].

*Likewise in the priest Melchizedek we see the Lord's sacrament prefigured, according as the divine Scripture testifies and says: 'and Melchizedek king of Salem brought forth bread and wine. And he was a priest of the Most High God, and he blessed Abraham.' But that Melchizedek bore a type of Christ the Holy Spirit declares in the Psalms, saying from the person of the Father to the Son: 'before the morning star I begot you. You are a priest forever according to the order of Melchizedek'*[^101].

These and the other things that follow in this letter keep the manner of subdued expression, which it is easy for readers to verify.

## Ambrose does the same.

**21.** 46. Saint Ambrose too, when he is dealing with a great matter concerning the Holy Spirit, in order to show him equal to the Father and the Son, nevertheless uses the subdued kind of speaking, since the matter undertaken requires not ornaments of words, nor the emotion of stirring for swaying minds, but proofs of things. Therefore, among other things, at the beginning of this work he says:[^102]

> *Gideon, moved by the oracle, when he had heard that, though the thousands of the peoples were failing, the Lord would free his people from their enemies by one man, offered a kid of the goats, whose flesh, according to the precept of the angel, and unleavened cakes, he placed upon a rock and drenched with broth. As soon as the angel of God touched these with the tip of the rod he was carrying, fire burst forth from the rock, and thus the sacrifice that was being offered was consumed*[^103].

> *By which sign it seems to be declared that that rock had a type of the body of Christ, because it is written: 'they drank from the rock that followed, and the rock was Christ.' This, of course, was referred not to his divinity but to his flesh, which flooded the hearts of the thirsting peoples with the perennial stream of his blood. Even then, therefore, it was declared in mystery that the Lord Jesus, crucified in his flesh, would abolish the sins of the whole world, and not only the offenses of deeds but also the desires of minds. For the flesh of the kid is referred to the fault of the deed, the broth to the enticements of desires, as it is written: 'for the people desired a most evil desire and said: who will feed us with flesh?' That the angel, then, extended the rod and touched the rock, from which fire went forth, showed that the flesh of the Lord, filled with the divine Spirit, would burn up all the sins of the human condition. Whence the Lord too says: 'I have come to cast fire upon the earth'*[^104];

and the rest, in which he chiefly bends his effort to teaching and proving the matter.

## On the temperate kind of eloquence in Cyprian.

**21.** 47. Of the temperate kind is that praise of virginity in Cyprian:

> *Now our discourse is to virgins, whose glory, the loftier it is, the greater also is the care for it. This is the flower of the ecclesiastical shoot, the grace and ornament of spiritual grace, a glad nature of praise and honor, a work whole and uncorrupted, the image of God answering to the holiness of the Lord, the more illustrious portion of the flock of Christ. The glorious fruitfulness of mother Church rejoices through them, and in them flourishes abundantly; and the more the glorious virginity adds to its number, the more the mother's joy grows*[^105].

And in another place, at the end of the letter:

> *'As we have borne,' he says, 'the image of him who is of the dust, so let us bear the image of him who is from heaven.' This image virginity bears, integrity bears, holiness bears, and truth bears; they bear it who are mindful of God's discipline, retaining justice with religion, steadfast in faith, humble in fear, brave for all endurance, gentle for bearing injuries, ready to do mercy, of one mind and concord in brotherly peace. All of which, O good virgins, you ought one by one to observe, to love, to fulfill—you who, having leisure for God and Christ, go before, with the greater and better part, toward the Lord to whom you have dedicated yourselves.*[^106] *You who are advanced in years, give instruction to the younger; you who are younger in years, offer ministry to your elders and encouragement to your equals; rouse one another with mutual exhortations, provoke one another to glory by rival proofs of virtue; endure bravely, go forward spiritually, arrive happily; only remember us then, when virginity begins to be honored in you*[^107].

## On the temperate kind of eloquence in Ambrose.

**21.** 48. Ambrose too, in the temperate and ornate kind of speaking, sets before professed virgins, as if under the form of an example, what they may imitate in their conduct, and says:

> *She was a virgin not only in body but also in mind, who adulterated her sincere affection by no cunning of deceit; humble of heart, serious in words, prudent of mind, sparing of speech, more eager in reading; placing her hope 'not in the uncertainty of riches,' but in the prayer of the poor; intent on work, modest in speech; accustomed to seek as the judge of her mind not man but God; to wound the face of no one, to wish well to all*[^108]*; to rise before her elders, not to envy her equals; to flee boasting, to follow reason, to love virtue.*

> *When did she, even by her look, wound her parents? When did she dissent from her kinsfolk? When did she disdain the lowly? When did she laugh at the weak? When did she shun the needy? She was careful to visit only those gatherings of men whom mercy would not blush at, nor modesty pass by. Nothing grim in her eyes, nothing forward in her words, nothing immodest in her act; no gesture too unrestrained, no gait too loose, no voice too petulant—so that the very appearance of her body was an image of her mind and a figure of her uprightness. For a good house ought to be recognized at the very entrance, and at the first approach should make plain that no darkness lurks within, as the light of a lamp set within shines forth without*[^109].

> *Why, then, should I recount her sparingness of food, her abundance of duties—the one having exceeded nature, the other having almost failed nature itself? In the one there were no times left off; in the other the days were doubled by fasting, and if ever the wish to refresh herself came on, the food was usually whatever lay to hand, such as would ward off death, not minister to delights*[^110], and the rest.

But I have set these things down as an example of this temperate kind because here he is not acting so that those who have not yet vowed virginity may vow it, but so that those who are already vowed may be of such a kind as they ought to be. For in order that the mind may undertake so great and such a resolution, it must of course be roused and kindled by the grand kind of speaking. But the martyr Cyprian wrote about the dress of virgins, not about taking up the resolution of virginity; whereas this bishop kindles them even to this by great eloquence.

## An example of grand expression from Cyprian.

**21.** 49. But from what both of them dealt with, I will recall examples of grand expression. For both inveighed against those women who color, or rather discolor, their appearance with pigments. The former of them, when he was treating of this, says among other things:

> *If some artist of painting had marked out the face and form and quality of someone's body with rival color, and, the likeness now marked and completed, another hand were laid upon it, so as to reform, as if more skilled, what was already shaped, already painted, it would seem a grave injury to the former artist, and a just indignation.*

> *Do you think you will carry off with impunity the boldness of so shameless a rashness, the offense against God the artist? For though you may not be unchaste and unclean toward men by your seductive paints, you are held a worse adulteress, since what is God's has been corrupted and violated.*

> *What you think is adorning you, what you think is grooming you, is an assault upon the divine work, a transgression against the truth.*

> *It is the voice of the admonishing Apostle: 'purge out the old leaven, that you may be a new lump, as you are unleavened. For Christ our Passover has been sacrificed. Therefore let us keep the feast, not with the old leaven, nor with the leaven of malice and wickedness, but with the unleavened bread of sincerity and truth.' Does sincerity and truth endure, when the things that are sincere are polluted, and true things are changed into a lie by the adulterating dyes of coloring drugs? Your Lord says, 'you cannot make one hair white or black', and you, to overcome the voice of your Lord, wish to be more powerful. With a bold attempt and sacrilegious contempt you dye your hair; with an evil foreboding of things to come, you take it as an omen that your hair is already flame-colored*[^111].

It would be long to insert everything that follows.

## An example of grand expression from Ambrose.

**21.** 50. But the latter, speaking against such women:

> *From this*, he says, *are born those incitements of vices, that they paint their faces with sought-after colors—while they fear to displease their husbands, and from the adultery of the face contrive the adultery of chastity. How great is this madness, to change the very likeness of nature, to seek a painting, and, while they dread their husband's judgment, to betray their own? For she pronounces first about herself who desires to change what she was born. Thus, while she is eager to please another, she first displeases herself.*

> *What truer judge of your deformity shall we seek, woman, than yourself, who are afraid to be seen? If you are beautiful, why do you hide yourself? If deformed, why do you lie that you are beautiful, when you will have the favor neither of your own conscience nor of another's error?*

> *For he loves another, you wish to please another; and you are angry if he loves another, who is taught to commit adultery in you. You are an evil mistress of your own injury.*

> *For even she who has suffered a pander shrinks from playing the bawd; and though a base woman, she yet sins not against another, but against herself. The crimes in adultery are almost more tolerable; for there chastity is adulterated, here nature*[^112].

It is sufficiently clear, as I judge, that women are vehemently driven, both to shame and to fear, by this eloquence, that they may not adulterate their own appearance with paints. Accordingly we recognize this kind of elocution to be neither subdued nor temperate, but altogether grand. And both in these two whom I wished to set forth out of all, and in other ecclesiastical men who say good things well—that is, as the matter demands, keenly, ornately, and ardently—these three kinds can be found through many of their writings or sayings, and can become ingrained in those who study by assiduous reading or hearing, with exercise also mixed in.

## Expression must be varied through all the kinds.

**22.** 51. Nor let anyone suppose it to be against the discipline to mix these kinds; rather, as far as can fittingly be done, expression must be varied through all the kinds. For when it is prolonged in one kind, it holds the hearer less. But when a passing is made from one to another, even if it is longer, the discourse proceeds more becomingly—although the single kinds also have their own varieties in the speech of the eloquent, by which they are not allowed to grow cold or tepid in the senses of those who hear. Nevertheless, the subdued alone can be borne longer more easily than the grand alone. For the stirring of the mind, the more it must be roused so that the hearer may assent to us, the less can it be held long in that state, once it has been roused as much as is enough.

And therefore care must be taken lest, while we wish to raise higher what is already raised, it fall away even from where it had been brought by rousing. But by interposing the things that are to be said more subduedly, there is a good return to the things that need to be said grandly, so that the rush of expression may alternate like the tide of the sea.

From which it comes about that the grand kind of speaking, if it is to be spoken at greater length, ought not to be alone, but to be varied by the interposition of the other kinds. Yet the whole expression is assigned to that kind whose abundance prevails.

## How the kinds of expression are to be intermingled.

**23.** 52. For it matters which kind is interposed in, or applied to, which kind, in fixed and necessary places. For in the grand kind too the beginnings ought always, or almost always, to be temperate; and it is in the power of the eloquent man that some things be said subduedly, even things that could be said grandly, so that the things said grandly may, by comparison with them, become grander, and be rendered, as if by their shadows, more luminous. But in whatever kind some knots of questions must be loosed, there is need of keenness, which the subdued kind properly claims for itself. And on account of this, that kind must be used in the other two kinds also, when these things fall to them:

just as, when something is to be praised or blamed, where neither the condemnation of anyone nor a liberation nor assent to any action is required, in whatever other kind it occurs, the temperate kind must be applied and interposed. In the grand kind, then, the other two find their places, and likewise in the subdued. But the temperate kind needs the subdued—not indeed always, yet sometimes—if, as I said, a question whose knot must be loosed comes up; or when some things that could be ornamented are on that account not ornamented, but said in subdued speech, so that they may give, as it were, certain cushions on which the ornaments may have a more eminent place. The grand kind, however, does not require temperate expression; for it is taken up to delight minds, not to move them.

## What the sublime kind of speaking accomplishes.

**24.** 53. To be sure, if a speaker is applauded rather frequently and vehemently, he is not on that account to be thought to be speaking grandly. For the keen points of the subdued kind and the ornaments of the temperate kind do this too. But the grand kind generally presses down the voices by its own weight, but presses out tears.[^113]

In short, when at Caesarea of Mauretania[^114] I was dissuading the people from a civil—or rather, more than civil—fight, which they called the "gang fight"; for not only fellow citizens, but even kinsmen, brothers, and finally parents and children, divided into two parties, used to fight one another with stones, in a customary way, for several days running, at a fixed time of year, and each killed whomever he could; I acted indeed grandly, as much as I was able, to tear and drive that so cruel and inveterate evil from their hearts and habits by speaking.

Yet I did not think I had accomplished anything when I heard them applauding, but when I saw them weeping. For by their applause they indicated that they were being taught and delighted, but by their tears that they were being swayed. And when I saw these, I believed that that monstrous custom, handed down from their fathers and grandfathers, and from their forebears far back, which besieged—or rather possessed—their breasts as an enemy, was conquered, before they showed it by the fact itself; and soon, the sermon ended, I turned their hearts and mouths to giving thanks to God. And behold, it is now nearly eight years or more, by the favor of Christ, since nothing of the kind has been attempted there.

There are also many other experiments by which we have learned that people have shown what the grandeur of a wise man's expression has done in them, not so much by shouting as by groaning, sometimes even by tears, and finally by a change of life.

## Some are moved by the subdued kind of speaking and changed for the good.

**24.** 54. By the subdued kind of speaking too very many have been changed, but so that they knew what they did not know, or believed what seemed to them incredible—not, however, so that they did what they already knew was to be done and were unwilling to do. For to sway hardness of this kind, one must speak grandly.

For praises and blames too, when they are spoken eloquently—since they are in the temperate kind—so affect some people that they are delighted not only by the eloquence in the praises and blames, but also themselves desire to live praiseworthily and to flee living blameworthily. But are all who are delighted changed, as in the grand kind all who are swayed act, and in the subdued kind all who are taught know, or believe to be true what they did not know?

## To what end the temperate kind of speaking should be referred.

**25.** 55. From which it is gathered that those two kinds, in what they intend to accomplish, are most necessary to those who wish to speak wisely and eloquently. But that which is brought about by the temperate kind—that is, that the eloquence itself may delight—is not to be employed for its own sake, but so that to things which are usefully and honorably said—if they need eloquence neither to teach nor to move, because they have hearers both knowing and favorable—assent may come somewhat more readily, from the very delight of the elocution, or cling more tenaciously.

For since the universal duty of eloquence, in whatever of these three kinds, is to speak aptly for persuasion, and the end is what you have intended to persuade by speaking, in whatever of these three kinds the eloquent man does indeed speak aptly for persuasion, but unless he persuades he does not arrive at the end of eloquence. Now he persuades in the subdued kind that what he says is true; he persuades in the grand kind that the things be done which are already known to be done and are not done; he persuades in the temperate kind that he speaks beautifully and ornately. What need have we of this end? Let those desire it who glory in their tongue and boast themselves in panegyrics and such discourses, where the hearer is neither to be taught nor moved to do anything, but only to be delighted.

But let us[^115] refer that end to another end—namely, that what we wish to accomplish when we speak grandly, this we may wish to accomplish by this kind also: that is, that the goods of conduct be loved, or its evils avoided, if people are not so estranged from this action that they seem to need to be urged to it by the grand kind of speaking; or, if they are already doing it, that they may do it more zealously and persevere firmly in it.

Thus it comes about that we use the ornament of the temperate kind too not boastfully, but prudently; not content with its end, by which the hearer is merely delighted, but rather acting so that even by it he may be helped toward the good which we wish to persuade.

## In each kind of speaking the orator ought to aim to be heard with understanding, with pleasure, and with obedience.

**26.** 56. Those three things, then, which we set down above—that the one who speaks wisely ought, if he wishes also to speak eloquently, so to act that he be heard with understanding, with pleasure, and with obedience[^116]—

are not to be so taken as though the single things are so assigned to those three kinds of speaking that to the subdued belongs being heard with understanding, to the temperate being heard with pleasure, and to the grand being heard with obedience; but rather so that he always aim at these three, and accomplish them as far as he can, even when he is engaged in any single one of those kinds.

For we do not wish that even what we say subduedly should be found tiresome; and on account of this we wish to be heard not only with understanding but also with pleasure.

And what do we accomplish by teaching what we say with divine testimonies, except that we may be heard with obedience—that is, that they may be believed, with the help of him to whom it was said: *your testimonies have been made exceedingly worthy of belief*[^117]?

And what does he desire but to be believed, who narrates something, even in subdued speech, to learners? And who would wish to hear him, unless he held the hearer by some charm too? For if he is not understood, who does not know that he can be heard neither with pleasure nor with obedience?

But the subdued expression itself, generally, while it resolves the most difficult questions and demonstrates them by an unexpected clarification; while it draws out and shows the keenest thoughts from I know not what hidden recesses, as it were, from which it was not hoped; while it convicts the adversary's error and teaches that what seemed to be said by him as unconquerable is false—

especially when there is present to it a certain grace not sought after but in a manner natural, and a certain rhythm of closes—not boastful, but as if necessary and, so to speak, arising from the very things themselves—rouses such applause that it is scarcely understood to be subdued.

For not because it neither advances ornamented nor armed, but engages as if naked, does it on that account fail to dash the adversary with its sinews and muscles, and to overthrow and destroy resisting falsehood with most powerful limbs.

But why are those who speak thus applauded frequently and much, except because truth so demonstrated, so defended, so unconquered, delights? And so in this subdued kind too, this teacher and speaker of ours ought to act so that he be heard not only with understanding but also with pleasure and with obedience.

## What the sacred orator must beware of in the use of the temperate kind.

**26.** 57. That eloquence too of the temperate kind, in the case of the eloquent ecclesiastic, is neither left unornamented nor unbecomingly ornamented. And it does not aim only at this, that it delight—which alone it professes among others—but even in the things it praises or blames, in seeking these or holding them more firmly, and in avoiding or spurning those, it surely wishes to be heard with obedience. But if it is not heard with understanding, it cannot be heard with pleasure either.

Accordingly those three things—that those who hear may understand, may be delighted, may obey—must be aimed at in this kind too, where delight holds the chief place.

## What the sacred orator must beware of in the use of the grander kind.

**26.** 58. But now, where it is needful to move and sway the hearer by the grand kind—which is needful then, when he confesses that what is said is said both truly and pleasantly, and yet is unwilling to do what is said—then, without doubt, one must speak grandly.

But who is moved if he does not know what is said? Or who is held to listen if he is not delighted? Hence in this kind too, where a hard heart must be swayed to obedience by the grandeur of expression, unless the one who speaks is heard both with understanding and with pleasure, he cannot be heard with obedience.

## He whose life answers to his speech is heard with more obedience.

**27.** 59. But, for being heard with obedience, the life of the speaker has greater weight than any grandeur of expression. For one who speaks wisely and eloquently, but lives wickedly, does indeed instruct many who are eager to learn, although *he is useless to his own soul*[^118], as it is written. Whence the Apostle says: *whether in pretense or in truth, let Christ be proclaimed*[^119].

But Christ is the truth,[^120] and yet the truth can be proclaimed even not in truth—that is, so that things which are right and true are preached with a crooked and deceitful heart. For thus *Jesus Christ is proclaimed by those who seek their own things, not the things of Jesus Christ*[^121].

But since good faithful people hear with obedience not any man whatever, but the Lord himself, who says, *do what they say; but what they do, do not do; for they say and do not do*[^122], on that account even those are heard with profit who do not act with profit. For they are eager to seek their own things, but they do not dare to teach their own things—from the higher place, that is, of the ecclesiastical seat which sound teaching has established.

On account of which the Lord himself, before he said about such people what I have mentioned, premised: *they have sat on the seat of Moses*[^123]. That seat, then—not theirs but Moses's—compelled them to say good things, even though they did not do good things. They acted their own things, then, in their own life; but the seat that was another's did not permit them to teach their own things.

## Those who do what they say are useful to more people.

**27.** 60. And so they profit many by saying the things they do not do, but they would profit far more by doing the things they say. For there are plenty who seek a defense of their own evil life from their very superiors and teachers, answering in their heart, or even, if they break out to this point with their mouth, saying: "What you command me, why do you not do it yourself?" Thus it comes about that they do not hear with obedience one who does not himself listen to himself, and they despise the word of God which is preached to them together with the preacher himself. Finally, the Apostle, writing to Timothy, when he had said, *let no one despise your youth*, added the means by which he should not be despised, and said: *but be a pattern to the faithful in speech, in conduct, in love, in faith, in chastity*[^124].

## One must be zealous for truth rather than for words. What it is to wrangle about words.

**28.** 61. Such a teacher, in order to be heard with obedience, speaks not impudently—not only subduedly and temperately, but even grandly—because he does not live contemptibly. For he so chooses the good life that he does not neglect a good reputation either, but *provides good things before God and men*[^125] as much as he can, fearing the one and taking thought for the others.

In his very speech, too, let him prefer to please by things rather than by words, and let him not think a thing better said unless it is more truly said; and let the teacher not be a slave to words, but words to the teacher.

For this is what the Apostle says: *not in wisdom of word, lest the cross of Christ be made void*[^126]. To this also pertains what he says to Timothy: *do not wrangle about words; for it is of no use, except for the subversion of the hearers*[^127].

For this was not said in order that, when adversaries assail the truth, we should say nothing for the truth. And where would be that which, when he showed what kind of person a bishop ought to be, he said among other things: *so that he may be able both to exhort in sound teaching and to confute those who contradict*[^128]? For to wrangle about words is not to care how error may be conquered by truth, but how your expression may be preferred to another's.

Furthermore, the one who does not wrangle about words, whether he speak subduedly or temperately or grandly, acts by words so that the truth may lie open, the truth may please, the truth may move; since not even love itself—which is the end of the precept and the fullness of the law[^129]—can in any way be right, if the things that are loved are not true but false.

But just as one whose body is beautiful and whose mind is deformed is more to be grieved over than if he had a deformed body too, so those who eloquently say things that are false are more to be pitied than if they said such things unbecomingly.

What is it, then, to speak not only eloquently but also wisely, except to apply words sufficient in the subdued kind, splendid in the temperate, vehement in the grand—yet to true things, which it is fitting to be heard? But the one who cannot do both, let him say wisely what he does not say eloquently, rather than say eloquently what he says foolishly.

**29.** 61. But if he cannot even do this, let him so conduct himself that he not only procures a reward for himself, but also affords an example to others, and that his manner of living be, as it were, his abundance of speaking.

## The churchman is not to be blamed who takes from a more skilled person a composed discourse to deliver to the people.

**29.** 62. There are indeed some who can deliver well, but cannot think out what they should deliver. But if they take from others something composed eloquently and wisely, commit it to memory, and deliver it to the people[^130]—if they bear that role, they do not act dishonestly.

For in this way too—which is surely useful—many become preachers of the truth and not many become masters, if all say the very same thing of the one true Master, and there are no schisms among them.[^131]

Nor are these people to be deterred by the voice of the prophet Jeremiah, through whom God reproves those who *steal his words, each one from his neighbor*[^132].

For those who steal take away what is another's; but the word of God is not another's to those who obey it; and rather it is he who speaks what is another's who, while he speaks well, lives badly. For whatever good things he says seem to be thought out by his own talent, but they are foreign to his own conduct. God therefore said that those steal his words who wish to seem good by speaking the things that are God's, while they are evil by doing the things that are their own.

Nor indeed do they themselves say the good things they say, if you attend diligently. For how do they say in words what they deny in deeds? For it is not in vain that the Apostle says about such people: *they confess that they know God, but by their deeds they deny him*[^133]. In one way, then, they themselves say it, and again in another way they do not themselves say it, since both are true which the Truth says. For speaking about such people, he says: *do what they say; but what they do, do not do*—that is, "what you hear from their mouth, do; what you see in their work, do not do"; *for they say*, he says, *and do not do*[^134]. Therefore, though they do not do it, they nevertheless say it. But in another place, reproving such people: *hypocrites*, he says, *how can you speak good things, when you are evil?*[^135] And on account of this, even the things they say, when they say good things, they themselves do not say—by denying, that is, in will and in work, what they say.

Hence it comes about that an eloquent and evil man may himself compose a discourse, by which the truth may be preached, to be delivered by another who is not eloquent but good. When this happens, the one hands on from himself what is another's, the other receives from another what is his own.

But when good faithful people lend this service to good faithful people, both say their own things, because God too is theirs, whose are the things they say; and they make their own the things they could not themselves compose, who live in an orderly way according to them.

## The preacher should send up a prayer to God beforehand.

**30.** 63. But whether one is about to speak at once before the people or before any persons whatever, or is about to dictate what is to be said before the people or to be read by those who are willing or able, let him pray that God may give a good discourse in his mouth. For if Queen Esther prayed, when she was about to speak before the king for the temporal safety of her people, that God would give a fitting discourse in her mouth,[^136] how much more ought one to pray that he may receive such a gift who labors in word and teaching for the eternal salvation of people?[^137]

But those who are about to say the things they have received from others, let them, even before they receive, pray for those from whom they receive, that there may be given to them what they wish to receive through them; and when they have received, let them pray both that they themselves may deliver it well and that those to whom they deliver it may take it in,

and for the prosperous outcome of the discourse let them give thanks to him from whom they do not doubt that they have received it, so that *he who glories* may *glory* in him[^138] in whose hand are both we and our words.

## Augustine acknowledges the length of the book and gives thanks to God.

**31.** 64. This book has turned out longer than I wished and than I had supposed. But to the reader or hearer to whom it is welcome, it is not long. And let the one who wishes to have it known, but to whom it is long, read it in parts. But let the one who is loath to know it not complain about its length.

I, however, give thanks to our God that in these four books I have discussed, with whatever small faculty I could, not what kind of person I myself am, to whom many things are lacking, but what kind he ought to be who strives to labor in sound—that is, Christian—teaching, not only for himself but also for others.
