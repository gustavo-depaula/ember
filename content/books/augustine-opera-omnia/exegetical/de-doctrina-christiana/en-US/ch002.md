# Prologue

## Augustine’s purpose in writing this work.

**1.** There are certain precepts for treating the Scriptures which, as I see it, can be handed on to those who study them without disadvantage, so that they may make progress not only by reading others who have opened up what is hidden in the divine writings, but also by themselves opening it up to others. I have undertaken to hand these on to those who are willing and able to learn, if our Lord and God does not deny me, as I write, what he is accustomed to suggest to me when I think about this subject.

Before I begin, however, it seems to me that I should reply to those who are going to find fault with this work, or who would find fault with it if I did not first appease them. And if some still find fault even after this, at least they will not influence others or call them back from a useful pursuit to the sloth of ignorance—others whom they could have influenced had they not found them forewarned and prepared.

## Augustine enumerates his critics.

**2.** For some will find fault with this work of ours because they do not understand the things we are going to teach. Others, however, when they have wished to make use of what they have understood and have tried to treat the divine Scriptures according to these precepts but have not been able to open up and explain what they desire, will think that I have labored in vain; and because they themselves are not helped by this work, they will judge that no one can be helped by it.

There is a third kind of critic: those who either treat the divine Scriptures well in truth or think that they treat them well. Since, without having read any observations of the kind I have now undertaken to hand on, they either see or suppose that they have attained the ability to expound the holy books, they will cry out that these precepts are necessary for no one, but rather that everything which is praiseworthily opened up out of the obscurities of those writings can come about by a divine gift.

## He answers his critics.

**3.** Replying briefly to all of these, to those who do not understand what we write I say this: that I am not to be blamed because they do not understand these things. It is as though they wished to see the moon, whether old or new, or some star that is hardly bright, which I pointed out with outstretched finger, and the keenness of their eyes was not even sufficient to see my finger itself; they ought not on that account to be angry with me. As for those who, even when these precepts have been known and grasped, are still unable to discern what is obscure in the divine Scriptures, let them suppose that they can indeed see my finger, but cannot see the stars it is stretched out to point to. Let both these and those, then, cease to find fault with me, and let them pray that the light of their eyes may be granted them from above. For even if I can move my limb to point something out, I cannot also kindle the eyes by which either my very pointing or even the thing I wish to point out may be perceived.

**4.** As for those who exult in a divine gift and boast that, without precepts of the kind I have now undertaken to hand on, they understand and treat the holy books, and who therefore think that I have wished to write what is superfluous—their agitation must be soothed in this way: that, although they rightly rejoice in a great gift of God, they should nevertheless remember that they too learned even their letters through human beings,

and that they ought not on that account to be sneered at by the holy and perfect Egyptian monk Antony,[^1] who is reported, without any knowledge of letters, both to have held the divine Scriptures in memory by hearing them and to have understood them by prudent reflection; or by that Christian barbarian slave, about whom we have recently heard from most weighty men, most worthy of belief,[^2]

who received the knowledge of letters themselves, with no human being teaching him, by praying that they might be revealed to him, obtaining it by three days of prayer, so that he could even read through a manuscript handed to him, as those present looked on in amazement.

**5.** Or if anyone thinks these things are false, I will not contend stubbornly. For surely, since we are dealing with Christians who rejoice that they know the holy Scriptures without any human guide and who, if that is so, rejoice in a true and no slight good, they must necessarily grant that each one of us learned his own tongue from the very beginning of childhood by the habit of hearing it, and acquired some other tongue—Greek or Hebrew or any of the rest—either likewise by hearing or through a human teacher.

Now then, if it please them, let us advise all the brethren not to teach their little ones these things, since in a single moment of time the apostles, filled with the coming of the Holy Spirit, spoke in the tongues of all nations;[^3] or else let anyone to whom such things have not happened not think himself a Christian, or let him doubt that he has received the Holy Spirit.

On the contrary, let a person learn without pride even what must be learned through another human being; and let the one through whom another is taught hand on without pride and without envy what he has received.[^4] Let us not tempt the one in whom we have come to believe,[^5] lest, deceived by such cunning and perversity of the enemy, we become unwilling even to go to the churches to hear and learn the Gospel itself, or to read the manuscript, or to listen to a person reading and preaching, and instead wait to be caught up *even to the third heaven, whether in the body or out of the body*, as the Apostle says, and there to hear *unspeakable words that are not lawful for a man to utter*, or there to see the Lord Jesus Christ and to hear the Gospel from him rather than from human beings.

## Examples are cited that confirm the usefulness of the effort.

**6.** Let us beware of such most proud and most dangerous temptations, and let us rather consider that even the apostle Paul himself, although laid low and instructed by a divine and heavenly voice, was nevertheless sent to a man so that he might receive the sacraments and be joined to the Church;[^6] and that the centurion Cornelius, although an angel announced to him that his prayers had been heard and his almsgiving regarded, was nevertheless handed over to Peter to be instructed, through whom he might not only receive the sacraments but also hear what was to be believed, what to be hoped for, and what to be loved.[^7] And all this could certainly have been done through an angel, but the human condition would have been debased if God had seemed unwilling to minister his word to human beings through human beings.

For how would what was said be true—*for the temple of God is holy, which you are*—if God gave his responses from a human temple[^8] and yet wished everything that human beings must learn to be handed on from heaven and to resound through angels? Then, too, love itself, which binds human beings to one another in the knot of unity, would have no avenue for pouring minds together and, as it were, mingling them with one another, if human beings learned nothing through human beings.

**7.** And surely, in the case of that eunuch who was reading the prophet Isaiah and did not understand him, the Apostle neither sent him to an angel nor had what he did not understand explained to him through an angel, nor was it revealed to him from above in his mind without the ministry of a human being. Rather, by divine prompting Philip was sent to him—Philip, who knew the prophet Isaiah—and he sat with him and opened up in human words and language what was hidden in that Scripture.[^9]

Did not God speak with Moses, and yet Moses received counsel for ruling and administering so great a people from his father-in-law—a foreigner, that is—being most provident and least proud?[^10] For that man knew that, from whatever soul true counsel might proceed, it was to be attributed not to that soul but to him who is the truth, the unchangeable God.[^11]

**8.** Finally, whoever boasts that, instructed by no precepts, he understands by a divine gift whatever is obscure in the Scriptures, believes well indeed, and it is true, that this ability of his does not, as it were, exist from himself but has been given from above; for in this way he seeks the glory of God and not his own.

But when he reads and understands with no human being expounding to him, why does he himself aspire to expound to others, and not rather send them back to God, so that they too may understand not through a human being but with him teaching within? But no doubt he is afraid that he may hear from the Lord: *You wicked servant, you should have given my money to the bankers*.

Just as these people, then, disclose to others what they understand, whether by speaking or by writing, so I too, if I disclose not only the things I understand but also the things to be observed in understanding them, ought certainly not to be blamed by them. And yet no one ought to have anything as though it were his very own, except perhaps a lie. For everything true is from him who says: *I am the truth*. For what do we have that we have not received? But if we have received it, why do we boast as though we had not received it?[^12]

**9.** Whoever reads aloud to listeners certainly pronounces the letters that he recognizes; but whoever teaches the letters themselves does so that others too may know how to read; yet each of them passes on what he has received.

So too, whoever expounds to listeners what he understands in the Scriptures pronounces, as it were, the letters he recognizes, performing the office of a reader. But whoever teaches how it is to be understood is like one who hands on the letters—that is, who teaches how to read;

so that, just as the one who knows how to read does not need another reader, when he has found a manuscript, to hear from him what is written there, so the one who has received the precepts we are trying to hand on, when he has found something obscure in the books, holds certain rules as though they were letters and does not require another interpreter through whom what is hidden may be uncovered for him, but, having traced out certain clues, arrives by himself at the hidden meaning without any error, or at least does not fall into the absurdity of a perverse opinion.

Therefore, although it can be sufficiently apparent in the work itself that no one rightly contradicts this dutiful labor of ours, nevertheless, if it seems fitting that an answer be given in a prologue of this kind to any who may object, such is the beginning that has occurred to us for this path that we wish to enter upon in this book.
