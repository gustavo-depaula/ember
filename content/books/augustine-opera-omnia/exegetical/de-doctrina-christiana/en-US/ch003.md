# Book I

## The treatment of Scripture rests on discovery and presentation, and is to be undertaken with God’s help.

**1.** 1. There are two things on which all treatment of the Scriptures rests: the way of discovering what is to be understood, and the way of presenting what has been understood. We shall discuss discovery first, presentation afterward.[^1]

It is a great and arduous work, and if it is difficult to sustain, I fear it may be rash to undertake—so indeed it would be if we were presuming upon ourselves. But as it is, since the hope of carrying through this work lies in him from whom we already hold much that has been handed to us in our thinking about this matter, there is no need to fear that he will cease to give the rest, once we have begun to spend what has been given.

For everything that is not diminished by being given is not yet possessed as it ought to be possessed, so long as it is kept and not given. For he says: *To one who has, it will be given*. He will give, then, to those who have—that is, to those who use with kindness what they have received; he will fill up and heap high what he has given.

Those five loaves, and those seven, were loaves before they began to be given to the hungry; and once that began to happen, they filled baskets and hampers when so many thousands of people had been satisfied.[^2] Just as that bread increased while it was being broken, so the things that the Lord has already provided for undertaking this work will, once they have begun to be dispensed, be multiplied at his very prompting, so that in this ministry of ours we may not only suffer no lack, but rejoice in a wonderful abundance.

## What things are, what signs are.

**2.** 2. All teaching is either of things or of signs, but things are learned through signs. Properly, however, I have here called things those which are not employed to signify something—such as wood, stone, cattle, and other things of this kind; but not that wood which we read Moses cast into the bitter waters so that they might lose their bitterness, nor that stone which Jacob had placed at his head, nor that beast which Abraham offered up in place of his son.[^3] For these are things in such a way that they are also signs of other things. But there are other signs whose whole use is in signifying, such as words. For no one uses words except for the sake of signifying something.

From this it is understood what I call signs: namely, those things which are employed to signify something. For this reason every sign is also some thing, since what is no thing is altogether nothing. But not every thing is also a sign. And so, in this division of things and signs, when we speak of things, we shall speak in such a way that, even if some of them can be employed to signify, this will not impede the arrangement by which we discuss things first and signs afterward; and let us hold firmly in mind that what is now to be considered in things is what they are, not what else, besides themselves, they may also signify.

## The division of things.

**3.** 3. Some things, then, are to be enjoyed, others to be used, and others that both enjoy and use. Those which are to be enjoyed make us happy; by those which are to be used we are helped and, as it were, supported as we strive toward happiness, so that we may attain and cling to those things which make us happy.

But we ourselves, who both enjoy and use, are set between the two; and if we wish to enjoy the things that are to be used, our course is impeded and sometimes even diverted, so that we are either delayed or even called back from obtaining the things that are to be enjoyed, held back by love of lower things.

## What it is to enjoy and to use.

**4.** 4. For to enjoy is to cling with love to something for its own sake. To use, however, is to refer what has come into use to obtaining what you love, provided that it is something to be loved. For an illicit use should rather be called abuse or misuse.

How, then, if we were travelers in a foreign land who could not live happily except in our homeland, and being wretched in that wandering and longing to put an end to our wretchedness, we wished to return to our homeland, we would need vehicles, whether by land or by sea, which we would have to use so that we might be able to reach the homeland, which was to be enjoyed;

but if the pleasantness of the journey and the very riding in the vehicles delighted us, and we turned to enjoying the things we ought to have used, we would be unwilling to finish the journey quickly, and, entangled in a perverse sweetness, we would be estranged from the homeland whose sweetness would make us happy:

so, traveling in this life of mortality *away from the Lord*, if we wish to return to the homeland where we can be happy, we must use this world, not enjoy it, so that *the invisible things of God may be seen and understood through the things that have been made*—that is, so that from corporeal and temporal things we may grasp things eternal and spiritual.

## God the Trinity, the thing to be enjoyed.

**5.** 5. The things, therefore, that are to be enjoyed are the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit, and the same Trinity—one supreme thing, common to all who enjoy it; if indeed it is a thing and not the cause of all things, if indeed it is even a cause. For a name that befits such great excellence cannot easily be found, except that this Trinity is better called the one God *from whom are all things, through whom are all things, in whom are all things*. Thus the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit, and each one of these, is God; and all together are one God; and each one of these is a full substance, and all together are one substance.

The Father is neither the Son nor the Holy Spirit; the Son is neither the Father nor the Holy Spirit; the Holy Spirit is neither the Father nor the Son; but the Father is only the Father, the Son only the Son, and the Holy Spirit only the Holy Spirit. The three have the same eternity, the same unchangeableness, the same majesty, the same power. In the Father is unity, in the Son equality, in the Holy Spirit the concord of unity and equality. And these three are all one because of the Father, all equal because of the Son, all bound together because of the Holy Spirit.

## God ineffable—in what sense.

**6.** 6. Have we said anything, have we sounded anything worthy of God? Indeed, I feel that I have wished to do nothing but speak; yet if I have spoken, it is not what I wished to say. How do I know this, except because God is ineffable; but what has been said by me, if it were ineffable, would not have been said?

And for this reason God is not even to be called ineffable, because when this too is said, something is said. And a kind of contradiction in words arises, since if that is ineffable which cannot be spoken, then what can be called even "ineffable" is not ineffable. This contradiction in words is rather to be avoided by silence than resolved by speech.

And yet God, although nothing can be said of him worthily, has accepted the homage of the human voice, and has willed us to rejoice in his praise by means of our words. For this is why he is called God. For he is not in truth recognized in the noise of these two syllables, but nevertheless, when that sound has touched their ears, it moves all who know the Latin language[^4] to think of a certain most excellent and immortal nature.

## All understand God to be that than which nothing is better.

**7.** 7. For when that one God of gods is thought of—even by those who both suspect and call upon and worship other gods, whether in heaven or on earth—he is thought of in such a way that the thought tries to reach something than which nothing is better or more sublime.

To be sure, since they are moved by different goods—some by those that pertain to the senses of the body, some by those that pertain to the understanding of the mind—those who are given over to the senses of the body suppose that either the sky itself, or what they see most brilliant in the sky, or the world itself, is the God of gods.

Or, if they strive to go beyond the world, they imagine something luminous and, by an empty supposition, set it up as either infinite or in whatever shape seems best; or they think of the figure of a human body, if they prefer it to the rest.

But if they do not think there is one God of gods, but rather many or innumerable gods of equal rank, even these too they hold fashioned in their mind according to whatever bodily feature seems to each to be most excellent.

Those, however, who proceed by understanding to see what God is, prefer him to all natures visible and corporeal, and indeed to all intelligible and spiritual ones, and to all that is changeable.

Yet all[^5] strive eagerly, as in a contest, on behalf of the excellence of God, and no one can be found who believes God to be that than which something is better. And so all agree that God is that which they set above all other things.

## Since God is unchangeable wisdom, he is to be set above all things.

**8.** 8. And since all who think about God think of something living, only those can hold views about God that are not absurd and unworthy who think of life itself. And whatever bodily form occurs to them, they determine that it either lives or does not live by life, and they prefer the living to the nonliving; and they understand that this very living bodily form, however brightly it may shine with light, however greatly it may stand out in magnitude, however much it may be adorned with beauty, is one thing, and the life by which it is animated another; and they prefer that life, with an incomparable dignity, to the mass which is animated and given life by it.

Then they proceed to examine life itself, and if they find it animating without sensation, as in trees, they set above it the sentient life, as in cattle; and above this, again, the intelligent life, as in human beings. When they see that this too is still changeable, they are compelled to set above it some unchangeable life as well—namely, that life which is not at one time foolish and at another wise, but is rather Wisdom itself. For a wise mind—that is, one that has attained wisdom—was not wise before it attained it; but Wisdom itself never was unwise, nor can it ever be. And if they did not see this, they would by no means with full confidence set unchangeably wise life above changeable life. For they see that the very rule of truth by which they proclaim that life to be better is unchangeable; and they see it nowhere except above their own nature, since they see that they themselves are changeable.

## Unchangeable wisdom is to be preferred to the changeable.

**9.** 9. For no one is so impudently foolish as to say: "How do you know that unchangeably wise life is to be preferred to changeable life?" For the very thing he asks about—how I know it—is available to all in common and unchangeably for contemplation.[^6] And whoever does not see this is like a blind man in the sun, who gains nothing from the brilliance of so clear and present a light poured upon the very seats of his eyes. But whoever sees it and shrinks from it has a mind whose keenness of sight has been weakened by the habit of carnal shadows. By the contrary winds, as it were, of depraved habits, then, human beings are driven back from their very homeland, pursuing things later and lower than that which they confess to be better and more excellent.

## The mind must be purified in order to see God.

**10.** 10. Therefore, since we are to enjoy fully that truth which lives unchangeably, and in it God the Trinity, the author and founder of the universe, takes thought for the things he has founded, the mind must be purified so that it may be able both to perceive that light and to cling to what it has perceived. Let us regard this purification as a kind of walking, a kind of voyage, to the homeland. For we do not move by spatial places to him who is everywhere present, but by good zeal and good habits.[^7]

## Wisdom has become for us the way.

**11.** 11. This we could not do unless Wisdom itself had deigned to accommodate itself even to such great weakness of ours and had offered us an example of living, in no other way than in a man, since we too are human beings. But because, when we come to it, we act wisely, while it, when it came to us, was thought by proud men to have acted as if foolishly; and because, when we come to it, we grow strong, while it, when it came to us, was regarded as if weak. But *what is foolish of God is wiser than men, and what is weak of God is stronger than men*. Since, therefore, it is itself the homeland, it also made itself the way for us to the homeland.[^8]

**12.** 11. And although to the sound and pure inner eye it is everywhere present, it deigned to appear to the bodily eyes as well of those who have that inner eye weak and unclean. *For since, in the wisdom of God, the world could not through wisdom know God, it pleased God to save believers through the foolishness of preaching*.

## The wisdom of God is laid open through the foolishness of preaching.

**12.** 12. He is said to have come to us, then, not by traversing spatial distances, but by appearing in mortal flesh to mortals. He came there, therefore, where he was, because *he was in this world, and the world was made through him*.[^9] But because human beings, conformed to this world through the desire of enjoying the creature in place of the Creator and "most fittingly" called by the name of the world,[^10] did not recognize it, the Evangelist therefore said: *and the world did not know it*. And so, in the wisdom of God, the world could not through wisdom know God. Why, then, did it come, when it was here, except because it pleased God to save believers through the foolishness of preaching?

## The Word was made flesh.

**13.** 12. How did it come, except that *the Word was made flesh and dwelt among us*?[^11] Just as, when we speak, in order that what we hold in mind may glide into the mind of the listener through the ears of flesh, the word we bear in our heart becomes sound and is called speech; and yet our thought is not turned into that same sound, but, remaining whole in itself, takes on the form of a voice by which it may make its way to the ears, without any blemish of change in itself: so the Word of God, though not changed, was nevertheless made flesh so that it might dwell among us.

## The wisdom of God is wonderful in healing the human being.

**14.** 13. Now just as treatment is the way to health, so this treatment took up the task of healing and restoring sinners. And as physicians, when they bind up wounds, do so not haphazardly but fittingly, so that a certain beauty too may attend the usefulness of the bandage, so the medicine of Wisdom, by taking up a human being, was accommodated to our wounds, healing some by contraries and some by likenesses. Just as one who tends a wound of the body applies certain contrary things—cold to hot, say, or moist to dry, or anything else of this kind—and also applies certain like things, such as fitting a round cloth to a round wound, or an oblong one to an oblong wound, and adapts the very bandage not the same to all limbs, but like to like: so the Wisdom of God, healing the human being, presented itself for the healing—itself the physician, itself the medicine.

Since, therefore, the human being fell through pride, he applied humility for the healing. We were deceived by the wisdom of the serpent; we are set free by the foolishness of God. And just as that was called wisdom but was in fact foolishness in those who despise God, so this which is called foolishness is wisdom in those who conquer the devil. We made bad use of immortality so that we died; Christ made good use of mortality so that we might live. Through the corrupted mind of a woman the disease entered; through the intact body of a woman salvation came forth. To the same class of contraries it belongs that our vices, too, are healed by the example of his virtues.

Now the like things, applied as bandages, as it were, to our limbs and wounds are these: that, having been deceived through a woman, we are set free through one born of a woman; that, being human, he set free human beings; being mortal, mortals; by death, the dead. Many other things too, both from contraries and from likenesses, reveal the instruction of the Christian medicine to those who consider them more carefully—those whom the necessity of carrying through the work in hand does not hurry along.

## Faith is supported by Christ’s Resurrection and ascension, and roused by his judgment.

**15.** 14. Now indeed the belief in the Lord’s Resurrection from the dead and his ascension into heaven supports our faith with great hope. For he showed clearly how willingly he laid down his life for us, who had it so within his power to take it up again.[^12] With how great a confidence, then, does the hope of believers console itself, when it considers how great a one suffered such great things for those who did not yet believe!

But when he is awaited from heaven as judge of the living and the dead, he strikes great fear into the negligent, so that they may turn themselves to diligence and may long for him by doing good rather than dread him by doing evil.

But with what words can it be told, or by what thought grasped, what reward he will give in the end—since, for the consolation of this journey, he has given so much of his Spirit, by which we may hold, amid the adversities of this life, such great confidence and love of him whom we do not yet see, and gifts proper to each for the building up of his Church,[^13] so that what he has shown must be done we may do not only without murmuring but even with delight!

## The Church is the body and the spouse of Christ.

**16.** 15. For the Church is his body, as the apostolic teaching commends,[^14] which is also called his spouse. His body, then, with its many members performing different offices,[^15] he binds together with the knot of unity and love as with health. And at this present time he exercises and purifies it by certain medicinal hardships, so that, having rescued it from this world, he may join to himself forever as his spouse the *Church, having no spot or wrinkle or anything of the kind*.

## By forgiving sins, Christ opens the way to the homeland.

**17.** 16. Furthermore, since we are on the way—and this way is not one of places but of dispositions, which the malice of past sins was blocking off like a kind of thorny hedge—what more generous and merciful thing could he do, who willed to lay himself down beneath us as the way by which we might return,[^16] than to forgive all sins to those who turn, and, crucified for us, to tear up the heavily fixed barriers of our return?[^17]

## The keys of the kingdom are handed over to the Church.

**18.** 17. These keys, then, he gave to his Church, so that whatever it loosed on earth should be loosed also in heaven, and whatever it bound on earth should be bound also in heaven[^18]—namely, that whoever in his Church did not believe his sins were forgiven him should not have them forgiven, but whoever did believe and, corrected, turned away from them should, being established in the bosom of that same Church, be healed by that same faith and correction. For whoever does not believe that his sins can be forgiven him becomes worse by despairing, as though nothing remained for him better than to be evil, since he is faithless about the fruit of his own conversion.

## The death and resurrection of the body and of the mind.

**19.** 18. Now just as there is a certain death of the mind, the abandonment of one’s former life and habits, which comes about by repenting, so too there is a death of the body, the dissolution of its former animation. And just as the mind after repentance, by which it has destroyed its lost former habits, is reformed for the better, so too the body, after this death which we all owe by the bond of sin, is to be believed and hoped to be changed for the better at the time of resurrection,

so that flesh and blood may not possess the kingdom of heaven—which cannot be—but rather *this corruptible thing may put on incorruption, and this mortal thing may put on immortality*, and, causing no hardship because it will suffer no want, may be animated by the happy and perfect soul in the highest repose.

## Those who are reborn not to life but to punishments.

**20.** 19. But the person whose mind does not die to this world and does not begin to be conformed to the truth is dragged by the death of the body into a graver death; and he comes back to life not for a change into a heavenly condition, but to pay penalties.

## Again, on the resurrection of the body.

**21.** 19. This, then, is what faith holds, and this is how the matter is to be believed to stand: that neither the human mind nor the human body undergoes total destruction, but that the impious rise to incalculable punishments and the pious to eternal life.[^19]

## God, not the human being, is to be enjoyed.

**22.** 20. In all these things, then, those alone are to be enjoyed which we have called eternal and unchangeable; the rest are to be used so that we may be able to arrive at the full enjoyment of those. And so we, who both enjoy and use other things, are ourselves things of a kind. For the human being is a great thing, made *to the image and likeness of God*, not insofar as he is enclosed in a mortal body, but insofar as by the honor of a rational soul he excels the beasts. And so there is a great question, whether human beings ought to enjoy one another, or use one another, or both.

For we have been given the precept to love one another,[^20] but the question is whether a human being is to be loved by another for his own sake or for the sake of something else. For if for his own sake, we enjoy him; if for the sake of something else, we use him.

But it seems to me that he is to be loved for the sake of something else. For that which is to be loved for its own sake is that in which the happy life consists; and even if we do not yet have the reality of it, the hope of it nonetheless consoles us at this present time. But *cursed is the one who places his hope in man*.

## The whole impulse of love runs toward God.

**22.** 21. But no one ought to enjoy even himself, if you observe carefully, because he ought not to love himself for his own sake either, but for the sake of him who is to be enjoyed. For a person is at his best when his whole life makes for the unchangeable life[^21] and clings to it with his whole affection. But if he loves himself for his own sake, he does not refer himself to God, but, turned to himself, he is not turning to anything unchangeable. And therefore he then enjoys himself with some defect, since he is better when he clings wholly and is bound fast to the unchangeable good than when he relaxes from it even to himself.

If, then, you ought to love yourself not for your own sake, but for the sake of him in whom is the most rightful end of your love, let no other person be angry if you love him too for the sake of God.[^22] For this is the rule of love divinely established: *you shall love*, he says, *your neighbor as yourself*, but *God with your whole heart, with your whole soul, with your whole mind*, so that you may direct all your thoughts and all your life and all your understanding toward him from whom you have those very things that you direct toward him.

And when he says *with your whole heart, your whole soul, your whole mind*, he leaves no part of our life that ought to be free and, as it were, to give room so that one might wish to enjoy some other thing; but whatever else comes into the mind as a thing to be loved, let it be swept along to where the whole impulse of love runs.

Whoever, therefore, rightly loves his neighbor ought to bring it about with him that he too love God with his whole heart, his whole soul, his whole mind. For loving him thus as himself, he refers the whole love of himself and of his neighbor to that love of God which suffers no little stream to be led off from itself, by whose diversion it might be diminished.

## Distinguish how you love each thing.

**23.** 22. But not all things that are to be used are to be loved, but only those which either, by a certain fellowship, are referred together with us to God, such as a human being or an angel, or, being referred to us, stand in need of the kindness of God through us, such as the body. For surely the martyrs did not love the wickedness of their persecutors, which they nevertheless used to merit God.

Since, then, there are four things to be loved—one that is above us, a second that is ourselves, a third that is alongside us, and a fourth that is below us—about the second and the fourth no precepts had to be given. For however far a human being may fall away from the truth,[^23] there remains in him love of himself and love of his body. For the mind, fleeing from the unchangeable light that rules over all, acts so as to reign over itself and its own body, and therefore it cannot but love both itself and its body.

## On the order of love.

**23.** 23. But he thinks he has attained something great if he has been able to dominate even his fellows—that is, other human beings. For it is innate in a vicious mind to crave the more, and to claim for itself as though owed, what is properly owed to God alone. Such love of self is better called hatred. For it is unjust, since he wishes what is below him to serve him while he himself refuses to serve his superior; and it has been most rightly said: *but he who loves iniquity hates his own soul*.

And therefore the mind becomes weak and is tormented by the mortal body. For it must necessarily love it and be weighed down by its corruption.[^24] For the immortality and incorruption of the body arises from the health of the mind, and the health of the mind is to cling most firmly to the higher, that is, to the unchangeable God. But when it also desires to dominate those who are by nature its equals—that is, human beings—this is altogether intolerable pride.

## No one hates his own flesh, not even those who chastise it.

**24.** 24. No one, then, hates himself. And on this point indeed there has been no dispute with any sect. But neither does anyone hate his own body. For what the Apostle says is true: *no one ever hated his own flesh*. And as for what some say, that they would prefer to be altogether without a body, they are altogether mistaken. For it is not their body that they hate, but its corruptions and weight.

And so it is not no body at all, but an incorrupt and most swift body, that they wish to have; but they think such a thing would be no body at all, because they think the soul is something of that kind.[^25]

But as for the fact that by a certain self-restraint and by labors they seem, as it were, to persecute their own bodies: those who do this rightly do not act so as not to have a body, but so as to have it subdued and ready for necessary works. For they aim, by a certain laborious soldiering of the body itself, to extinguish the lusts that make bad use of the body—that is, the soul’s habits and inclinations toward enjoying lower things. For they do not destroy themselves, but take care of their own health.

## Spirit and flesh war against each other.

**24.** 25. But those who do this perversely wage war, as if naturally, upon their own body as an enemy. In this they are misled by what they read: *the flesh lusts against the spirit, and the spirit against the flesh; for these are opposed to one another*. For this was said because of the untamed carnal habit, against which the spirit lusts—not so as to destroy the body, but so that, its concupiscence (that is, its bad habit) having been tamed, it may make the body subdued to the spirit, as the natural order requires.

For because after the resurrection it will be the case that the body, in every way subject to the spirit with the highest repose, will thrive immortally, this too is to be practiced in this life, so that the carnal habit may be changed for the better and may not resist the spirit by disordered motions.

Until this comes about, *the flesh lusts against the spirit, and the spirit against the flesh*—the spirit resisting not through hatred but through sovereignty, since it rather wishes what it loves to be subject to the better; and the flesh resisting not through hatred but through the bond of habit, which, grown old even from the line of our parents, has taken root by the law of nature. The spirit, then, in taming the flesh, acts so as to break the perverse covenants, as it were, of bad habit, and so that the peace of good habit may come about.

Yet not even those who, depraved by a false opinion, detest their own bodies would be ready to lose one eye, even without the sensation of pain, even if as much power of seeing remained in the other as there had been in the two, unless some thing that ought to be preferred pressed upon them. By this and similar evidence it is shown clearly enough, to those who seek the truth without obstinacy, how certain is the Apostle’s judgment where he says: *for no one ever hated his own flesh*. He added also: *but nourishes and cherishes it, as Christ also does the Church*.

## Even if something is loved more than the body, the body is nevertheless not hated.

**25.** 26. A measure of loving, then, is to be prescribed to a person—that is, how he should love himself so as to benefit himself; for to doubt that he loves himself and wishes to benefit himself is the mark of a madman. He must also be taught how to love his own body, so as to provide for it in an orderly and prudent way. For that he loves his own body too, and wishes to keep it safe and whole, is equally manifest. And so a person can love something more than the safety and wholeness of his own body.

For many are found to have taken upon themselves both pains and the voluntary loss of certain members, in order to obtain other things which they loved more. No one, therefore, is to be said not to love the safety and soundness of his own body just because he loves something more. For the miser too, although he loves money, nevertheless buys himself bread; and in doing so he gives the money he loves so much and longs to increase, but he does so because he values more highly the safety of his own body, which is sustained by that bread. It is superfluous to dispute longer about a most manifest matter, which, however, the error of the impious often compels us to do.[^26]

## In the precept about loving God and neighbor, love of oneself is contained.

**26.** 27. Therefore, since there is no need of a precept that one should love himself and his own body—that is, since we love what we are and what is below us but belongs to us by an unshaken law of nature, which has been promulgated even to the beasts (for the beasts too love themselves and their bodies)—it remained that we should receive precepts both about that which is above us and about that which is alongside us. *You shall love*, he says, *the Lord your God with your whole heart and with your whole soul and with your whole mind, and you shall love your neighbor as yourself. On these two precepts the whole Law and all the Prophets depend*. The end of the precept, therefore, is love, and that twofold—namely, of God and of neighbor.

But if you understand yourself as a whole—that is, your mind and your body—and your neighbor as a whole—that is, his mind and his body (for a human being consists of mind and body)—no kind of thing to be loved has been left out in these two precepts. For since the love of God comes first and the prescribed measure of that love appears, in such a way that everything else flows together into it, nothing seems to have been said about your love of yourself. But when it was said, *you shall love your neighbor as yourself*, your love of yourself was at the same time not left out.

## God is to be loved for his own sake, the human being for the sake of God.

**27.** 28. Now that person lives justly and holily who is an undistorted appraiser of things. And he is the one who has an ordered love, so that he neither loves what is not to be loved, nor fails to love what is to be loved, nor loves more what is to be loved less, nor loves equally what is to be loved either less or more. Every sinner, insofar as he is a sinner, is not to be loved, and every human being, insofar as he is a human being, is to be loved for the sake of God, but God for his own sake. And if God is to be loved more than any human being, each one ought to love God more than himself. Likewise another human being is to be loved more than our own body, because all these things are to be loved for the sake of God, and another human being can enjoy God together with us, which the body cannot, since the body lives through the soul by which we enjoy God.

## Whom you should help, when you cannot help everyone.

**28.** 29. But all are to be loved equally. Yet since you cannot do good to all, you must especially provide for those who, by the opportunities of place and time or any other circumstances, are bound to you more closely as though by a kind of lot. For just as, if you had an abundance of something that you ought to give to one who did not have it, and it could not be given to two, if two were to meet you, neither of whom surpassed the other either in need or in any tie of relationship toward you, you would do nothing more just than to choose by lot to whom should be given what could not be given to both: so among the human beings for all of whom you cannot provide, it must be treated as a matter of lot, according as each is able to cling to you more closely in time.

## We should wish and act so that all may love God.

**29.** 30. Of all those who can enjoy God together with us, we love some whom we ourselves help, some by whom we are helped, some whose help we both need and whose need we relieve, and some to whom we neither confer any benefit nor look to have any conferred on us by them. Nevertheless, we ought to wish that all may love God together with us, and the whole of what we either do for them or are helped in by them is to be referred to that one end.

For if, in the theaters of vice, one who loves some actor and enjoys his art as a great or even supreme good loves all who love that actor along with him—not for their own sake, but for the sake of the one whom they love together; and the more fervent he is in his love of him, the more he acts in whatever ways he can so that the actor may be loved by more people, and the more he longs to show him to others,

and whomever he sees rather cool toward him he rouses, as much as he can, by praising the actor; but if he finds someone opposing him, he vehemently hates in that person the hatred of his beloved, and by whatever means he can he presses to remove it:

what is it fitting for us to do in the fellowship of the love of God, to enjoy whom fully is to live happily; and from whom all who love him have both their being and the fact that they love him; about whom we have no fear that he could displease anyone once he is known; and who wishes to be loved, not so that anything may be conferred on him, but so that an eternal reward may be conferred on those who love him—that is, himself, the very one whom they love?

From this it follows that we love even our enemies. For we do not fear them, since they cannot take from us what we love, but we rather pity them, because the more they hate us, the more they are separated from him whom we love. And if they turn to him, they must necessarily love both him as the good that makes happy, and us as fellows in so great a good.

## Our neighbors are all human beings and the angels themselves.

**30.** 31. But at this point a certain question arises about the angels. For they too are happy by enjoying the very one whom we also desire to enjoy; and the more we enjoy him in this life, whether *through a mirror or in an enigma*, the more bearably we endure our pilgrimage and the more ardently we long to end it. But whether the love of the angels also pertains to those two precepts can be asked, not unreasonably. For that the one who gave the precept that we love our neighbor excepted no human being, both the Lord himself shows in the Gospel and the apostle Paul. For the man to whom he had set forth those two precepts and had said that on them depended the whole Law and all the Prophets, when he questioned him, saying: *And who is my neighbor*?[^27] he set before him a certain man who, going down from Jerusalem to Jericho, had fallen among robbers and, gravely wounded by them, had been left wounded and half-dead.[^28] And he taught that no one was neighbor to him except the one who showed mercy toward refreshing and tending him, so that the very one who had asked the question, when questioned himself, confessed this. To him the Lord said: *go and you also do likewise*, so that, namely, we may understand that one to be a neighbor to whom the duty of mercy either is to be shown, if he is in need, or would be shown, if he were in need.

From this it now follows that the one by whom this duty is to be shown to us in turn is also our neighbor. For the name "neighbor" is relative to something, and no one can be a neighbor except to a neighbor. But that no one has been excepted to whom the duty of mercy is to be denied—who does not see this, since it has been extended even to enemies, the same Lord saying: *love your enemies, do good to those who hate you*?

## Love is the fullness of the law.

**30.** 32. So too the apostle Paul teaches when he says: *for, you shall not commit adultery, you shall not commit murder, you shall not steal, you shall not covet, and if there is any other commandment, it is summed up in this saying: You shall love your neighbor as yourself. Love does the neighbor no wrong*.[^29] Whoever, therefore, thinks that the Apostle gave the precept not about every human being is compelled to confess what is most absurd and most wicked: that it seemed to the Apostle to be no sin if someone committed adultery with the wife of a non-Christian or of an enemy, or killed such a one, or coveted his property. But if it is the mark of a madman to say this, it is manifest that every human being is to be reckoned a neighbor, because evil is to be done to no one.

## Let us be caught up by love of the invisible things.

**30.** 33. Now indeed, if either the one to whom the duty of mercy is to be shown, or the one by whom it is to be shown to us, is rightly called a neighbor, it is manifest that by this precept by which we are commanded to love our neighbor the holy angels too are included, by whom such great duties of mercy are bestowed upon us as it is easy to observe in many passages of the divine Scriptures. From this our God and Lord himself willed to be called our neighbor. For the Lord Jesus Christ signifies himself to be the one who came to the aid of the man lying half-dead on the road, afflicted and abandoned by the robbers.[^30] And the Prophet says in prayer: *as a neighbor, as our brother, so was I pleasing*.

But because the divine substance is more excellent and above our nature, the precept by which we love God has been distinguished from the love of neighbor.[^31] For he shows mercy to us on account of his own goodness, but we to one another on account of his; that is, he has mercy on us so that we may enjoy him fully, but we have mercy on one another so that we may enjoy him.

## God does not enjoy us, but uses us.

**31.** 34. For this reason it still seems doubtful, when we say that we enjoy fully that thing which we love for its own sake, and that we are to enjoy only that thing by which we are made happy, while the rest are to be used. For God loves us, and the divine Scripture much commends his love toward us to us.

How, then, does he love us? So as to use us, or so as to enjoy us? But if he enjoys us, he is in need of our good, which no sane person would say. For all our good is either himself or from himself. And to whom is it obscure or doubtful that the light does not need the brightness of those things which it has itself illuminated?

The Prophet also says most plainly: *I said to the Lord: You are my Lord, since you have no need of my goods*. Therefore he does not enjoy us, but uses us. For if he neither enjoys nor uses us, I do not find how he loves us.

## God is our reward.

**32.** 35. But neither does he use us as we use things; for we refer the things we use to this end, that we may enjoy fully the goodness of God; God, however, refers his use of us to his own goodness.[^32] For because he is good, we exist; and insofar as we exist, we are good. Furthermore, because he is also just, we are not evil with impunity; and insofar as we are evil, to that extent we also exist less. For he supremely and primarily exists who is altogether unchangeable, and who could say most fully: *I am who I am*, and: *you shall say to them: He who is has sent me to you*—so that the other things that exist both cannot exist except from him, and are good to the extent that they have received that they may exist.

That use, then, which is called God’s, by which he uses us, is referred not to his benefit but to ours, and only to his goodness. But when we have mercy on someone and provide for him, we do this for his benefit indeed and have that in view; yet somehow our own benefit also follows, since God does not leave without reward the mercy we expend on the needy. And this is the highest reward: that we may enjoy him fully, and that all of us who enjoy him may also enjoy one another fully in him.

## Let us not place the hope of our happiness in creatures.

**33.** 36. For if we do this in ourselves, we remain on the way and place the hope of our happiness in a human being or in an angel. This both a proud human being and a proud angel claim for themselves, and they rejoice to have the hope of others fixed in them. But a holy human being and a holy angel, even when we are weary and desire to rest and remain in them, rather refresh us—either with the provision they have received for our sake, or with that which they have received for their own sake, but received nonetheless. And so, having refreshed us, they compel us to go on to him by enjoying whom we are alike made happy.

For the Apostle too cries out: *Was Paul crucified for you? Or were you baptized in the name of Paul*? and: *neither is he who plants anything, nor he who waters, but God who gives the increase*. And an angel warns a man who is worshiping him to worship rather the one under whom, as Lord, he himself is also a fellow servant.[^33]

## A human being is to be enjoyed in God and for the sake of God.

**33.** 37. But when you enjoy a human being in God, you enjoy God rather than the human being. For you enjoy the one by whom you are made happy, and you will rejoice to have come to him in whom you place your hope of coming. Hence Paul says to Philemon: *so, brother, let me enjoy you in the Lord*. For if he had not added *in the Lord*, but had said only *let me enjoy you*, he would have placed in him the hope of his happiness.

And yet "to enjoy" is said very nearly the same as "to use with delight."[^34] For when what is loved is present, it necessarily brings delight with it. And if you pass through this delight and refer it to that in which one must abide, you use it, and you are said to enjoy it improperly, not properly. But if you cling to it and abide in it, placing in it the end of your joy, then you are to be said to enjoy it truly and properly. And this must not be done except in that Trinity, that is, the supreme and unchangeable good.

## The first way to God is Christ.

**34.** 38. See how, although Truth itself, and the Word through whom all things were made, had become flesh so that it might dwell among us,[^35] the Apostle nevertheless says: *and even if we have known Christ according to the flesh, yet now we know him so no longer*. For he who willed to offer himself not only as the possession to those who arrive, but also as the way to those who are coming to the beginning of the ways, willed to take on flesh.

Hence too is that saying: *the Lord created me in the beginning of his ways*, so that from there those who wished to come might begin. The Apostle, therefore, although he was still walking on the way and was following God who called toward the prize of the heavenly calling, yet, forgetting the things that are behind and stretching out toward the things that are ahead, had already passed the beginning of the ways—that is, he no longer needed that from which the journey must nevertheless be begun and undertaken by all who desire to reach the truth and to abide in eternal life.

For he says thus: *I am the way and the truth and the life*—that is, "through me one comes, to me one arrives, in me one abides." For when one comes to him, one comes also to the Father, since through one who is equal the one to whom he is equal is recognized;[^36] the Holy Spirit binding and, as it were, gluing us fast, so that we may be able to abide in the supreme and unchangeable good.

From this it is understood how no thing ought to hold us on the way, since not even the Lord himself, insofar as he deigned to be our way, willed to hold us, but rather that we should pass on, lest we cling weakly to temporal things—even though taken up and accomplished by him for our salvation—but rather run eagerly through them, so that we may deserve to be carried forward and brought through to him who freed our nature from temporal things and placed it at the right hand of the Father.

## The economy of human salvation is shown.

**35.** 39. Of all the things, then, that have been said since we began to treat of things, this is the sum: that it be understood that *the fullness and the end of the Law* and of all the divine Scriptures *is love* of the thing that is to be enjoyed, and of the thing that can enjoy that thing together with us, since there is no need of a precept that each should love himself.

So that we might know and be able to do this, the whole temporal dispensation was carried out for our salvation by divine providence, which we ought to use, not with an abiding love and delight, as it were, but rather with a passing one, as toward a way, as toward vehicles or any other instruments, or whatever can be said more fittingly; so that we may love the things by which we are carried for the sake of that toward which we are carried.

## The interpretation of Scripture should not be deceptive but useful for building up love.

**36.** 40. Whoever, then, thinks that he has understood the divine Scriptures, or any part of them, in such a way that by that understanding he does not build up that twofold love of God and neighbor, has not yet understood.[^37] But whoever has drawn from them such an opinion as is useful for building up this love, and yet has not said what the one he reads is shown to have meant in that place, is not perniciously deceived, nor does he lie at all. For in one who lies there is the will to say what is false, and therefore we find many who are willing to lie, but no one who is willing to be deceived.

Since, then, a person does the one thing knowingly but suffers the other unknowingly, it is sufficiently apparent that, in one and the same matter, the one who is deceived is better than the one who lies, since it is better to suffer injustice than to do it.[^38] But everyone who lies does injustice; and if a lie seems useful to anyone at some time, injustice can seem useful at some time. For no one who lies keeps faith in what he lies about. For he certainly wishes that the one to whom he lies should have faith in him, which faith he nevertheless does not keep toward him by lying. But every violator of faith is unjust. Either, therefore, injustice is sometimes useful—which cannot be—or a lie is always useless.[^39]

## Above all, the mind of the sacred author is to be sought.

**36.** 41. But whoever understands something different in the Scriptures from what the one who wrote them meant is deceived, though they do not lie. Yet, as I had begun to say, if he is deceived by an opinion that builds up love, which *is the end of the precept*, he is deceived in the same way as someone who, leaving the road by mistake, nevertheless makes his way across a field to the place to which that road also leads.

He must nevertheless be corrected, and it must be shown to him how much more useful it is not to leave the road, lest by the habit of going astray he be compelled to go even crosswise or in the wrong direction.

## Holy Scripture, if read devoutly, nourishes faith, hope, and love.

**37.** 41. For by rashly asserting what the one he reads did not mean, he often runs into other things that he cannot weave together with that opinion. And if he agrees that these are true and certain, then what he had thought cannot be true; and somehow it comes about in him that, by loving his own opinion, he begins to be more offended at the Scripture than at himself. And if he allows this evil to creep on, he will be overturned by it. *For we walk by faith, not by sight*; but faith will totter if the authority of the divine Scriptures wavers; and further, with faith tottering, love itself also grows faint. For if anyone falls from faith, he must also fall from love. For he cannot love what he does not believe to exist.

Furthermore, if he both believes and loves, by acting well and obeying the precepts of good morals he brings it about that he also hopes that he will come to that which he loves. And so these are the three by which all knowledge and prophecy are sustained: faith, hope, love.[^40]

## Love remains forever.

**38.** 42. But faith will give way to the sight that we shall behold, and hope will give way to the very happiness to which we shall come; love, however, even as these pass away, will rather be increased. For if by believing we love what we do not yet see, how much more when we have begun to see it? And if by hoping we love that to which we have not yet come, how much more when we have come to it?

For between things temporal and things eternal there is this difference: that a temporal thing is loved more before it is possessed, but grows cheap once it has arrived; for it does not satisfy the soul, whose true and certain seat is eternity. But the eternal is loved more ardently when attained than when desired. For no one who desires it is permitted to esteem it more than it actually is, so that it would grow cheap to him when he found it less; but however much each one coming to it could esteem it, he will find more upon arriving.

## The interpreter of the Scriptures ought to be equipped with faith, hope, and love.

**39.** 43. A person, therefore, supported by faith and hope and love, and holding fast to them unshaken, does not need the Scriptures except for instructing others. And so many live by these three even in solitude without books. Hence in them, I think, that has already been fulfilled which was said: *whether prophecies will be done away, or tongues will cease, or knowledge will be done away*.

Yet by these as by a kind of scaffolding so great a structure of faith and hope and love has risen up in them that, holding something perfect, they do not seek the things that are in part[^41]—perfect, that is, as far as is possible in this life. For in comparison with the future life, this life of no just and holy person is perfect. Therefore *there remain*, he says, *faith, hope, love, these three. But the greatest of these is love*, because even when one has come to the eternal things, with those two passing away, love will remain, fuller and more certain.

## What kind of reader Scripture requires.

**40.** 44. Therefore, when anyone has recognized that *the end of the precept is love from a pure heart and a good conscience and faith unfeigned*, and is going to refer the whole understanding of the divine Scriptures to those three, let him approach the treatment of those books with confidence.

For when he said *love*, he added *from a pure heart*, so that nothing else might be loved than that which is to be loved.

And he joined *a good conscience* on account of hope. For he despairs of coming to that which he believes and loves who has within him the prick of a bad conscience.

Third, he says *and faith unfeigned*. For if our faith is free of falsehood, then we both do not love what is not to be loved, and by living rightly we hope so that our hope may in no way be disappointed. For this reason I have wished to say about the matters that pertain to faith as much as I judged sufficient for the present, since on other matters many things have already been said, whether by others or by ourselves, in other volumes.

Let this, then, be the measure of this book. The rest, concerning signs, we shall discuss as far as the Lord shall grant.
