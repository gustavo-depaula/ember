# Book I: The Precepts That Pertain to the Forming of Life

## The First Part of the Sermon Given by the Lord on the Mount Is Expounded (1, 1 - 5, 15)

## The Perfect Measure of the Christian Life Is Described in the Lord's Sermon

**1. 1.** If anyone considers piously and soberly the sermon which our Lord Jesus Christ spoke on the mount, as we read in the Gospel according to Matthew, I think he will find in it, as far as concerns the best moral conduct, the perfect measure of the Christian life. This we dare to promise not rashly, but inferring it from the very words of the same Lord; for the sermon is so concluded that it appears that in it are all the precepts that pertain to the forming of life. For he speaks thus: *everyone, therefore, who hears these words of mine and does them, I will liken him to a wise man who built his house upon the rock. The rain fell, the floods came, the winds blew and beat upon that house, and it did not fall; for it was founded upon the rock. And everyone who hears these words of mine and does not do them, I will liken him to a foolish man who built his house upon the sand. The rain fell, the floods came, the winds blew and beat upon that house, and it fell; and great was its ruin*[^1]*.* Since, then, he did not say, *who hears my words* only, but added the words, *who hears these words of mine*, he sufficiently signified, I think, that these words which he spoke on the mount so perfectly instruct the life of those who are willing to live according to them that they are rightly compared to one building upon the rock. I have said this so that it may appear that this sermon is perfect in all the precepts by which the Christian life is formed. For about this passage there will be a more careful treatment in its own place.

## What the Mountain Signifies

**1. 2.** The beginning of this sermon, then, is taken up thus: *and when he saw the great crowds, he went up onto the mountain; and when he had sat down, his disciples came to him. And opening his mouth he taught them, saying*[^2]. If it is asked what *the mountain* signifies, it is rightly understood to signify the greater precepts of righteousness, because the precepts given to the Jews were lesser.

Yet one and the same God, through his holy prophets and servants, according to a most well-ordered distribution of the times, gave lesser precepts to a people that still needed to be bound by fear, and through his Son gave greater precepts to a people that it was now fitting should be set free by love. But when lesser things are given to the lesser and greater things to the greater, they are given by him who alone knows how to offer to the human race the medicine suited to its times.

Nor is it any wonder that greater precepts are given for the sake of the kingdom of heaven, and lesser precepts were given for the sake of an earthly kingdom, by the same one God who made heaven and earth. Of this righteousness, then, which is greater, it is said through the prophet, *your righteousness is like the mountains of God*[^3], and this is well signified by the fact that it is taught on the mountain by the one master[^4] alone fit to teach such great matters.

And he teaches sitting, which pertains to the dignity of a master. And his disciples come to him, so that those who drew near to him in mind by fulfilling his precepts might be nearer to him also in body for hearing his words. *He opened his mouth and taught them, saying*[^5]. This circumlocution by which it is written, and opening his mouth, perhaps by its very delay suggests that the sermon to come would be somewhat longer; unless perhaps it is not without meaning that he is now said to have opened his mouth, since he himself was accustomed in the Old Law to open the mouths of the prophets.

## Blessed Are the Poor in Spirit

**1. 3.** What, then, does he say? *Blessed are the poor in spirit, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven*[^6]. We read it written of the craving for temporal things, *all is vanity and presumption of spirit*[^7]; and presumption of spirit signifies audacity and pride. Commonly, too, the proud are said to have great spirits, and rightly, since spirit is also called wind, whence it is written, *fire, hail, snow, ice, the wind of the storm*[^8]. And who does not know that the proud are called puffed up, as though distended with wind? Whence comes also that saying of the apostle, *knowledge puffs up, love builds up*[^9]. Therefore the poor in spirit are here rightly understood as the humble and those who fear God, that is, those who do not have a puffed-up spirit. Nor should blessedness have begun from anywhere else at all, since it is destined to arrive at the highest wisdom. *But the beginning of wisdom is the fear of the Lord*[^10], since, on the contrary, *the beginning of all sin is pride* is written[^11]. Let the proud, then, crave and love the kingdoms of the earth; but *blessed are the poor in spirit, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven*[^12]*.*

## Blessed Are the Meek

**2. 4.** *Blessed are the meek, for they shall possess the land by inheritance*[^13]—that land, I believe, of which it is said in the Psalms, *you are my hope, my portion in the land of the living*[^14]. For it signifies a certain solidity and stability of the everlasting inheritance, where the soul, through good affection, rests as in its own place, just as the body rests in the land, and is there nourished with its own food, just as the body is from the land. This is the rest and the life of the saints. But the meek are those who yield to wrongs and do not resist evil, but overcome evil with good[^15]. Let the unmeek, then, quarrel and contend for earthly and temporal things; but *blessed are the meek, for they shall possess the land by inheritance*[^16], from which they cannot be driven out.

## Blessed Are Those Who Mourn

**2. 5.** *Blessed are those who mourn, for they shall be comforted*[^17]. Mourning is sadness over the loss of loved things. But those who have turned to God lose the things they used to embrace as dear in this world; for they no longer rejoice in those things in which they rejoiced before, and until the love of eternal things is formed in them, they are wounded by a certain sadness. They will be comforted, then, by the Holy Spirit, who is for this reason chiefly named the Paraclete, that is, the Comforter, so that, losing temporal joy, they may fully enjoy eternal joy.

## Blessed Are Those Who Hunger for Righteousness

**2. 6.** *Blessed are those who hunger and thirst for righteousness, for they shall be satisfied*[^18]*.* Now he calls these the lovers of the true and unshaken good. They shall be satisfied, then, with that food of which the Lord himself says, *my food is to do the will of my Father*[^19], which is righteousness, and with that water of which whoever drinks, as he likewise says, *it will become in him a spring of water welling up to eternal life*[^20].

## Blessed Are the Merciful

**2. 7.** *Blessed are the merciful, for they shall obtain mercy*[^21]. He says that those are blessed who come to the aid of the wretched, since it is repaid to them in such a way that they are freed from wretchedness.

## Blessed Are the Pure in Heart

**2. 8.** *Blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God*[^22]. How foolish, then, are those who seek God with these outward eyes, when he is seen with the heart, as it is written elsewhere, *and in simplicity of heart seek him!*[^23] For this is a pure heart, which is a simple heart. And just as this light cannot be seen except by pure eyes, so neither is God seen unless that by which he can be seen is pure.

## Blessed Are the Peacemakers

**2. 9.** *Blessed are the peacemakers, for they shall be called sons of God*[^24]*.* In peace there is perfection, where nothing is in conflict; and therefore the peacemakers are sons of God, since nothing resists God, and surely sons ought to bear the likeness of their Father. But the peacemakers are at peace within themselves, who, ordering and subjecting all the movements of their mind to reason, that is, to mind and spirit, and having their carnal desires subdued, become the kingdom of God, in which all things are so ordered that what is foremost and most excellent in a man commands the rest, which do not resist and which we have in common with the beasts,

and that very thing which is most excellent in a man, that is, mind and reason, is subjected to a still mightier one, which is Truth itself, the only-begotten Son of God. For he cannot command what is below him unless he subjects himself to what is above him.

And this is the peace that is given on earth to men of good will[^25], this the life of the consummate and perfect wise man. From a kingdom of this sort, most peaceful and most well-ordered, the prince of this world has been cast out[^26], who lords it over the perverse and disordered. With this peace established and made firm within, whatever persecutions he who has been cast out stirs up from without, he increases the glory that is according to God, weakening nothing in that building, but, as his engines fail, making known how great is the firmness raised up within. Therefore there follows, *blessed are those who suffer persecution for the sake of righteousness, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven*[^27]*.*

## All These Eight Sentences

**3. 10.** Now there are all these eight sentences. For now, addressing the rest, he speaks to those who were present, saying, *blessed shall you be when they revile you and persecute you*[^28]. The earlier sentences he set out in general terms; for he did not say, "*blessed are the poor in spirit*, for yours is the kingdom of heaven," but, *for theirs*, he says, *is the kingdom of heaven*[^29]; nor, "*blessed are the meek*, for you shall possess the land," but, *for they shall possess the land*[^30]; and so with the rest, down to the eighth sentence, where he says, *blessed are those who suffer persecution for the sake of righteousness, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven*[^31].

From there he now begins to speak addressing those present, since both the things said above pertained also to those who were present and hearing, and these things afterward, which seem to be said specifically to those present, pertain also to those who were absent or were to come later. Therefore this number of the sentences is to be considered carefully.

For blessedness begins from humility: *blessed are the poor in spirit*, that is, the not puffed up, while the soul, fearing God, submits itself to the divine authority, lest after this life it go on to punishments, even if perchance in this life it seems blessed to itself.

From there it comes to the knowledge of the Divine Scriptures, where it must show itself meek through piety, lest it dare to find fault with what seems absurd to the unskilled, and through obstinate disputes be made unteachable.

From there it now begins to know by what bonds of this world it is held through carnal habit and sins. And so in this third step, in which there is knowledge, the loss of the highest good is mourned, because one clings to the lowest things.

In the fourth step there is labor, where one strives vehemently so that the mind may tear itself away from those things to which it is bound by a deadly sweetness. Here, then, righteousness is hungered and thirsted for, and fortitude is very necessary, because what is held with delight is not relinquished without sorrow.

In the fifth step, to those persevering in labor counsel for escape is given, because unless one is helped by a higher power, one is in no way fit of oneself to extricate oneself from such great entanglements of miseries. But it is a just counsel that he who wishes to be helped by a more powerful one should help the weaker in that wherein he himself is more powerful. And so, *blessed are the merciful, for they shall obtain mercy*.

In the sixth step is purity of heart, arising from a good conscience of good works, able to contemplate that highest good which can be discerned only by a pure and serene understanding.

Finally is the seventh, wisdom itself, that is, the contemplation of truth, making the whole man peaceful and taking on the likeness of God, which is concluded thus: *blessed are the peacemakers, for they shall be called sons of God*.

The eighth returns, as it were, to the head, because it shows and proves what is consummated and perfect.

And so in the first and in the eighth the kingdom of heaven is named: *blessed are the poor in spirit, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven*, and, *blessed are those who suffer persecution for the sake of righteousness, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven*, when it is now said, *who shall separate us from the love of Christ? Shall tribulation, or distress, or persecution, or famine, or nakedness, or peril, or the sword?*[^32] There are, then, seven that perfect; for the eighth makes clear and demonstrates what is perfect, so that through these steps the rest too may be perfected, beginning again, as it were, from the head.

## The Sevenfold Activity of the Holy Spirit Seems to Correspond to These Steps and Sentences

**4. 11.** It seems to me, then, that the sevenfold activity of the Holy Spirit too, of which Isaiah speaks[^33], corresponds to these steps and sentences. But the order differs: for there the enumeration began with the more excellent, here with the lower; for there it begins with wisdom and ends with the fear of God, but *the fear of God is the beginning of wisdom*[^34]. Therefore, if we count step by step as though ascending, first there is the fear of God, second piety, third knowledge, fourth fortitude, fifth counsel, sixth understanding, seventh wisdom.

The fear of God corresponds to the humble, of whom it is said here, *blessed are the poor in spirit*, that is, the not puffed up, not proud, to whom the apostle says, *do not be high-minded, but fear*[^35], that is, do not be exalted.

Piety corresponds to the meek. For he who seeks piously honors Holy Scripture and does not censure what he does not yet understand, and therefore does not resist, which is to be meek; whence it is said here, *blessed are the meek*.

Knowledge corresponds to those who mourn, who have now learned in the Scriptures by what evils they are held bound—evils that, not knowing them, they craved as though they were good and useful—of whom it is said here, *blessed are those who mourn*.

Fortitude corresponds to those who hunger and thirst. For they labor, desiring joy in the true goods and longing to turn their love away from earthly and bodily things, of whom it is said here, *blessed are those who hunger and thirst for righteousness.*

Counsel corresponds to the merciful. For this is the one remedy for escaping such great evils: that we forgive as we wish to be forgiven, and help others in what we can, as we ourselves desire to be helped in what we cannot, of whom it is said here, *blessed are the merciful*.

Understanding corresponds to the pure in heart, as to an eye that has been purified, by which can be discerned *what* the bodily *eye has not seen, nor the ear heard, nor has it entered into the heart of man*[^36], of whom it is said here, *blessed are the pure in heart*. Wisdom corresponds to the peacemakers, in whom all things are now set in order and no movement is in rebellion against reason, but all things obey the spirit of a man, since he himself also obeys God, of whom it is said here, *blessed are the peacemakers*.

## One Reward Is Named in Various Ways for the Several Steps

**4. 12.** Now one reward, which is the kingdom of heaven, is named in various ways for the several steps. In the first, as was fitting, the kingdom of heaven is set down, which is the perfect and highest wisdom of the rational soul. And so it was said, *blessed are the poor in spirit, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven*, as though it were said, *the fear of the Lord is the beginning of wisdom*.

To the meek an inheritance is given, as a Father's testament to those who seek with piety: *blessed are the meek, for they shall possess the land by inheritance*.

To those who mourn, consolation, as to those who know what they have lost and in what they have been submerged: *blessed are those who mourn, for they shall be comforted*;

to those who hunger and thirst, satisfaction, as refreshment to those who labor and contend bravely for salvation: *blessed are those who hunger and thirst for righteousness, for they shall be satisfied*;

to the merciful, mercy, as to those using true and excellent counsel, so that what they show to the weaker may be shown to them by a more powerful one: *blessed are the merciful, for they shall obtain mercy*;

to the pure in heart, the power of seeing God, as to those bearing a pure eye for understanding eternal things: *blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God*;

to the peacemakers, the likeness of God, as to those perfectly wise and formed to the image of God through the regeneration of the renewed man: *blessed are the peacemakers, for they shall be called sons of God*.

And these things indeed can be fulfilled in this life, as we believe they were fulfilled in the apostles; for that wholesale change into angelic form, which is promised after this life, cannot be set forth in any words.

*Blessed*, therefore, *are those who suffer persecution for the sake of righteousness, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven.* This eighth sentence, which returns to the head and declares the perfect man, is signified perhaps both by the circumcision on the eighth day in the Old Testament, and by the Lord's resurrection after the Sabbath, which is certainly the eighth and likewise the first day, and by the celebration of the octave days which we celebrate in the regeneration of the new man, and by the very number of Pentecost. For when seven is multiplied by seven, by which forty-nine is made, an eighth is added, as it were, so that fifty may be completed and there may be, as it were, a return to the head. On that day the Holy Spirit was sent, by which we are led into the kingdom of heaven and receive the inheritance and are comforted and are fed and obtain mercy and are made pure and are made peaceful. And so, being made perfect, we endure all the troubles brought upon us from without for the sake of truth and righteousness.

## Our Blessedness Is Within

**5. 13.** *Blessed shall you be*, he says, *when they revile you and persecute you and say every evil against you, lying, for my sake. Rejoice and exult, for your reward is great in heaven*[^37]. Let whoever seeks the delights of this world and an abundance of temporal things under the Christian name take note that our blessedness is within, just as it is said of the soul of the Church, by the mouth of the prophet, *all the beauty of the king's daughter is within*[^38]. For from without are promised revilings and persecutions and slanders, of which nonetheless there is a great reward in heaven, which is felt in the heart of those who endure, of those who can now say, *we glory in tribulations, knowing that tribulation works patience, and patience trial, and trial hope; and hope does not disappoint, because the love of God is poured out in our hearts through the Holy Spirit who has been given to us*[^39]. For to suffer these things is not fruitful, but to endure these things for Christ's name not only with an even mind but even with exultation. For many heretics, deceiving souls under the Christian name, suffer many such things; but they are for this reason excluded from this reward, because it was not said only, *blessed are those who suffer persecution*, but it was added, *for the sake of righteousness*. But where there is not sound faith, there cannot be righteousness, because *the just man lives by faith*[^40]. Nor let schismatics promise themselves anything from this reward, because likewise where there is no love, there cannot be righteousness; for *love of neighbor does no evil*[^41], and if they had it, they would not tear apart the body of Christ, which is the Church[^42].

What the Difference Is Between to Revile and to Speak Evil

**5. 14.** Now it can be asked what difference there is between his saying, *when they revile you*, and, *they will say every evil against you*, since "to revile" is the same as "to speak evil." But a reviling is cast in one way with insult in the presence of him who is reviled, as it was said to our Lord, *do we not say truly that you are a Samaritan and have a demon?*[^43], and in another way when the reputation of one absent is injured, as it is likewise written of him, *some said, "he is a prophet"; others said, "no, but he leads the people astray*[^44]." But to persecute is to inflict force or to attack with snares. This those did who betrayed him and who crucified him.

Indeed, that this too was not set down bare, so that it would be said, *and they will say every evil against you*, but that there was added, *lying*, and added also, *for my sake*—this was added, I think, on account of those who wish to glory over persecutions and over the disgrace of their reputation, and therefore to say that Christ pertains to them because many evil things are said about them, even though true things are said, when they are said about their error. And if sometimes some false things too are bandied about, which generally happens through the rashness of men, they nonetheless do not suffer these things for Christ's sake. For he does not follow Christ who is called a Christian otherwise than according to the true faith and Catholic discipline.

## Our Reward Will Be in Heaven

**5. 15.** *Rejoice*, he says, *and exult, for your reward is great in heaven*[^45]. I do not think the *heavens* here mean the upper parts of this visible world—for our reward, which ought to be unshaken and eternal, is not to be placed in things that roll and pass away—but I think *in heaven* means in the spiritual firmaments, where everlasting righteousness dwells[^46]. In comparison with these, the wicked soul is called earth, to which, when it sinned, it was said, *you are earth, and into the earth you shall go*[^47]. Of these heavens the apostle says, *for our citizenship is in heaven*[^48]. They feel this reward already, then, who rejoice in spiritual goods; but it will be perfected in every part when this mortal too has put on immortality[^49]. *For so*, he says, *they persecuted the prophets also, who were before you*[^50]. Now he has set down persecution in general, both in revilings and in the tearing of reputation. And he has well exhorted by an example, because those who speak the truth are wont to suffer persecution. Nor for this reason, however, did the ancient prophets, from fear of persecution, fail in the preaching of the truth.

## Blessed Are the Poor in Spirit, Blessed Are the Humble,  — Because They Are in Concord with Their Brothers (6, 16 - 10, 28)

## You Are the Salt of the Earth

**6. 16.** Most rightly, then, there follows, *you are the salt of the earth*[^51], showing that those are to be judged foolish who, whether pursuing an abundance of temporal goods or fearing want, lose eternal things, which can neither be given nor taken away by men.

And so, *if the salt loses its savor, with what shall it be salted?*—that is, if you, through whom the peoples are in a manner to be seasoned, should from fear of temporal persecutions lose the kingdoms of heaven, who will be the men through whom error may be taken away from you, when God has chosen you, through whom he takes away the error of the rest?

Therefore, *salt that has lost its savor is good for nothing, except to be thrown out and trodden underfoot by men*. It is not, then, the one who suffers persecution who is trodden underfoot by men, but the one who, by fearing persecution, loses his savor. For one cannot be trodden underfoot unless he is lower; but he is not lower who, although in body he endures much on earth, is nonetheless fixed in heart upon heaven.

## You Are the Light of the World

**6. 17.** *You are the light of the world*[^52]. As above he said, *the salt of the earth*, so now he says, *the light of the world*. For neither above is that earth to be understood which we tread with bodily feet, but men, who dwell on the earth, or even sinners, for the seasoning and the suppressing of whose rottenness the Lord sent the apostolic salt. And here too the world must be understood not as heaven and earth, but as men who are in the world or love the world, for the enlightening of whom the apostles were sent. *A city set upon a mountain cannot be hidden*, that is, founded upon a notable and great righteousness, which the very mountain on which the Lord discourses also signifies. *Nor do they light a lamp and put it under a bushel, but upon a lampstand*[^53]. What do we think? Was it said, *under a bushel*, so that only the hiding of the lamp is to be understood, as though he said, "no one lights a lamp and hides it"? Or does the bushel also signify something, so that to put the lamp under a bushel is this: to make the conveniences of the body higher than the preaching of the truth, so that for this reason one does not preach the truth, while he fears lest he suffer some trouble in bodily and temporal things?

And it is well called *a bushel*: whether on account of the repayment by measure, because each one receives the things he has done in the body—*that there*, says the apostle, *each one may receive the things he has done in the body*[^54], and as though of this bushel of the body it is said in another place, *for with what measure you have measured, it shall be measured back to you*[^55]—;

or because temporal goods, which are carried out through the body, are both begun and pass away within a fixed measure of days, which perhaps the *bushel* signifies. But eternal and spiritual things are confined by no such limit; *for God does not give the Spirit by measure*[^56].

Under a bushel, then, anyone puts his lamp who obscures and covers the light of good doctrine with temporal conveniences, but upon a lampstand he who subjects his body to the ministry of God, so that the preaching of the truth is higher and the service of the body lower.

Yet through that very service of the body, let the loftier doctrine shine, which through bodily offices, that is, through the voice and the tongue and the other movements of the body, is conveyed to learners in good works.

Upon a lampstand, then, he puts his lamp, when the apostle says, *I do not so fight as one beating the air, but I chastise my body and bring it into subjection, lest perhaps, while preaching to others, I myself should be found reprobate*[^57].

But as for what he says, *that it may give light to all who are in the house*[^58], I think the *house* means the dwelling of men, that is, the world itself, on account of what he said above, *you are the light of the world*[^59]. Or if anyone wishes to take the *house* as the Church, neither is this absurd.

## Let a Man Set His Goal Through Good Works So as to Please God

**7. 18.** *So let your light shine*, he says, *before men, that they may see your good deeds and glorify your Father who is in heaven*[^60]. If he said only, *so let your light shine before men, that they may see your good deeds*, he would seem to have set the goal in the praises of men, which hypocrites seek, and those who strive after honors and chase the emptiest glory.

Against whom it is said, *if I were still pleasing men, I would not be a servant of Christ*[^61], and through the prophet, *those who please men have been put to shame, because God has brought them to nothing*, and again, *God breaks the bones of those who please men*[^62]; and again the apostle, *let us not become greedy for empty glory*[^63]; and he again, *but let a man prove himself, and then he will have glory in himself and not in another*[^64].

He did not say only, *that they may see your good deeds*, then, but added, *and glorify your Father who is in heaven*, so that this very thing—that a man pleases men through good works—should not set its goal there, in pleasing men, but should refer this to the praise of God, and for this reason please men, that in him God may be glorified. For this is to the advantage of those who praise, that they honor not the man but God, just as the Lord showed in the man himself who was being carried, when the crowds marveled at his powers over the paralytic who was healed, as it is written in the Gospel, *they feared and glorified God, who gave such power to men*[^65]. The apostle Paul, imitating him, says, *but they only kept hearing that he who once persecuted us now preaches the faith he once laid waste, and they glorified God in me*[^66]*.*

## I Have Not Come to Abolish the Law but to Fulfill It

**7. 19.** After he has exhorted his hearers, then, to prepare themselves to endure all things for truth and righteousness, and not to hide the good they were to receive, but to learn it with such benevolence that they might teach others, referring their good works not to their own praise but to the glory of God, he now begins to form them and to teach them what they should teach, as though they were asking and saying, "Behold, we are willing both to endure all things for your name and not to hide your teaching. But what is this very thing that you forbid to be hidden? And for what do you bid that all things be endured? Are you going to say other things against what is written in the Law?" No, he says. *For do not think that I have come to abolish the Law or the prophets; I have not come to abolish but to fulfill*[^67].

## In the Law We Are Bidden to Carry Even the Least Things Through to Their Effect

**8. 20.** In this sentence there is a twofold sense; it must be treated according to each. For he who says, *I have not come to abolish the Law but to fulfill*, either says it by adding what it lacks or by doing what it contains.

Let us first consider the former, which I set down first. For he who adds what it lacks does not abolish what he finds, but rather confirms it by perfecting it. And so he goes on and says, *amen I say to you, until heaven and earth pass away, not one iota or one tittle shall pass from the Law, until all is accomplished*[^68]. For while even those things are accomplished which are added for perfection, much more are those things accomplished which were set down first for a beginning. And as for what he says, *not one iota or one tittle shall pass from the Law*, nothing else can be understood except a forceful expression of perfection, since it is demonstrated by the individual letters, among which letters the iota is smaller than the rest, because it is made by a single stroke, while the tittle is some little part even of the iota itself, at the top. By these words he shows that in the Law even the least things are carried through to their effect.

Then he adds, *for whoever shall abolish one of these least commandments and shall so teach men, shall be called least in the kingdom of heaven*[^69]. The least commandments, then, are signified by the one iota and the one tittle. *Whoever*, therefore, *shall abolish and shall so teach*, that is, according to what he abolishes, not according to what he finds and reads, *shall be called least in the kingdom of heaven*; and perhaps for this reason he will not be in the kingdom of heaven, where none but the great can be. *But whoever shall do and shall so teach, he shall be called great in the kingdom of heaven*. *But whoever shall do* is this: but whoever shall not abolish and shall so teach, according to what he does not abolish. But that he *shall be called great in the kingdom of heaven*, it follows that he will also be in the kingdom of heaven, into which the great are admitted. For to this pertains what follows.

## Let Our Righteousness Abound Beyond That of the Scribes and Pharisees

**9. 21.** *For I say to you that, unless your righteousness shall abound more than that of the scribes and Pharisees, you shall not enter the kingdom of heaven*[^70]; that is, unless you fulfill not only those least precepts of the Law which begin a man, but also these which are added by me, who *have not come to abolish the Law but to fulfill*, *you shall not enter the kingdom of heaven*.

But you say to me, "If, when he was speaking above of those least commandments, he said that whoever should abolish one of them and teach according to his abolition is called least in the kingdom of heaven, but that whoever should do them and so teach is called great, and that for this reason he will already be in the kingdom of heaven because he is great, what need is there to add to the least precepts of the Law, if one can already be in the kingdom of heaven because whoever does them and so teaches is great?" Therefore that sentence is to be taken thus: *but whoever shall do and shall so teach, he shall be called great in the kingdom of heaven*, that is, not according to those least things, but according to those which I am going to say. And what are these? That your righteousness, he says, may abound beyond that of the scribes and Pharisees, because *unless it abound, you shall not enter the kingdom of heaven*.

Therefore he who shall abolish those least things and so teach shall be called least; but he who shall do those least things and so teach is not yet to be held great and fit for the kingdom of heaven, yet not so least as the one who abolishes them. But that he may be great and suited to that kingdom, he ought to do and to teach as Christ now teaches, that is, so that his righteousness abounds beyond that of the scribes and Pharisees.

The righteousness of the Pharisees is that they do not kill; the righteousness of those who are to enter the kingdom of God is that they are not angry without cause. It is the least thing, then, not to kill; and he who abolishes that shall be called least in the kingdom of heaven. But he who fulfills it, so as not to kill, will not at once be great and fit for the kingdom of heaven, yet he has climbed some step. He will be perfected, however, if he is not even angry without cause; and if he perfects this, he will be much farther removed from murder. Therefore he who teaches that we should not be angry does not abolish the Law that we should not kill, but rather fulfills it, so that both outwardly, in not killing, and in the heart, in not being angry, we may guard innocence.

## What the Difference Is Between One Liable to Judgment, One Liable to the Council, and One Liable to the Fire of Gehenna

**9. 22.** *You have heard*, then, he says, *that it was said to the ancients, "you shall not kill; but whoever shall kill shall be liable to judgment." But I say to you that everyone who is angry with his brother without cause shall be liable to judgment; and whoever shall say to his brother, "raca," shall be liable to the council; and whoever shall say, "you fool," shall be liable to the fire of Gehenna*[^71]. What is the difference between one liable to judgment, one liable to the council, and one liable to the fire of Gehenna? For this last sounds most grave and points to certain steps made from the lighter to the graver, until one comes to the fire of Gehenna.

And therefore, if it is lighter to be liable to judgment than to be liable to the council, and likewise lighter to be liable to the council than to be liable to the fire of Gehenna, it follows that it must be understood as lighter to be angry with one's brother without cause than to say, *raca*, and again lighter to say, *raca*, than to say, *you fool*. For the liability itself would not have steps unless the sins too were recalled step by step.

## "Raca," an Obscure Word Signifying the Disposition of an Agitated Mind

**9. 23.** Now one obscure word is set down here, because it is neither Greek nor Latin: *raca*; the rest are in common use in our language. Some have wished to draw the interpretation of this word from the Greek, supposing that *raca* means "ragged," since in Greek "rag" is "rakos." Yet when these are asked what "ragged" is in Greek, they do not answer *raca*. Then the Latin translator could, where he put *raca*, have put "ragged," and not used a word that is both nonexistent in the Latin language and unusual in the Greek. More probable, then, is what I heard from a certain Hebrew, when I asked him about it. For he said it is a word signifying nothing in particular, but expressing the movement of an indignant mind. Grammarians call these interjections—particles of speech signifying the disposition of an agitated mind, as when one in pain says "alas," or one who is angry says "hah." These words are proper to their respective languages and are not easily transferred into another language. This is certainly the reason that compelled both the Greek translator and the Latin to set down the word itself, since they could not find how to translate it.

## The Three Liabilities: Judgment, the Council, the Fire of Gehenna

**9. 24.** There are, then, steps in these sins, so that first one is angry and retains that movement conceived in the heart. Now if the agitation itself wrings from him a cry that signifies nothing in particular, but by its very outburst testifies to that movement of mind by which the one with whom he is angry is struck, this is certainly more than if the rising anger were pressed down in silence. But if not only the cry of one indignant is heard, but also a word that now marks and brands a definite reproach of the one against whom it is uttered, who would doubt that this is more than if only the sound of indignation were emitted? And so in the first there is one thing, that is, anger alone; in the second, two, both anger and the cry that signifies anger; in the third, three, both anger and the cry that signifies anger and, in the cry itself, the expression of a definite reproach.

See now also the three liabilities: judgment, the council, the fire of Gehenna! For in a judgment there is still room given for a defense.

But in the council, although there too there is usually a judgment, yet because the very distinction compels us to admit that there is some difference in this place, the pronouncing of the sentence seems to pertain to the council, when the question is no longer dealt with together with the accused himself, whether he is to be condemned, but those who judge confer among themselves by what punishment one who is agreed to be condemned ought to be condemned.

But the fire of Gehenna has neither a doubtful condemnation, like the judgment, nor the punishment of one condemned, like the council; for in Gehenna both the condemnation and the punishment of the condemned are certain.

There seem to be, then, certain steps in sins and in liability. But in what ways they are invisibly applied to the deserts of souls, who can say? Therefore one must hear how great the difference is between the righteousness of the Pharisees and the greater righteousness which leads into the kingdom of heaven,

namely, that, although it is graver to kill than to inflict abuse with a word, there killing makes one liable to judgment, but here anger makes one liable to judgment, which is the lightest of those three sins; because there they conducted the case of murder among men, but here all things are committed to the divine judgment, where the end of the condemned is the fire of Gehenna. But whoever shall say that murder is punished with a graver penalty under the greater righteousness, if abuse is punished by the fire of Gehenna, compels us to understand that there are differences of Gehennas.

## In the Three Sentences an Implied Supplying of Words Must Be Noted

**9. 25.** Indeed, in these three sentences an implied supplying of words must be noted. For the first sentence has all the necessary words, so that nothing is supplied: *whoever is angry*, he says, *with his brother without cause shall be liable to judgment*. But in the second, when he says, *but whoever shall say to his brother, "raca*," there is supplied, "without cause," and so it is joined, *shall be liable to the council*. Now in the third, where he says, *but whoever shall say, "you fool*[^72]," two things are supplied: both "to his brother" and "without cause."

This is the basis on which it is defended that the apostle calls the Galatians fools, whom he also names brothers; for he does not do it *without cause*. But here "brother" is to be supplied, because of an enemy it is said afterward how he too is to be treated under the greater righteousness.

## We Are Bidden to Leave Our Gift Before the Altar and Go and Be Reconciled to Our Brother

**10. 26.** Then there follows here, *if, therefore, you offer your gift at the altar and there remember that your brother has something against you, leave your gift there before the altar, and go first to be reconciled to your brother; and then, coming, offer your gift*[^73]. From this it certainly appears that it was of a brother that the foregoing was said, since the sentence that follows is connected by such a conjunction that it bears witness to the foregoing. For he does not say, "But if you offer your gift at the altar," but he says, *if, therefore, you offer your gift at the altar*. For if it is not permitted to be angry with one's brother without cause, or to say, *raca*, or to say, *you fool*, much less is it permitted to hold something in the mind, so that indignation turns into hatred. To this pertains also what is said in another place, *let not the sun go down upon your anger*[^74]. We are bidden, then, when about to bring our gift to the altar, if we remember that our brother has something against us, to leave our gift before the altar and go and be reconciled to our brother, and then to come and offer the gift.

But if this is taken literally, perhaps someone may believe that it ought to be done thus only if the brother is present; for it cannot be put off any longer, since you are bidden to leave your gift before the altar. If, then, something of this sort should come to mind concerning one who is absent and, as may happen, even set across the sea, it is absurd to believe that the gift is to be left before the altar, which you would offer to God after wandering over lands and seas. And therefore we are altogether compelled to take refuge within, in spiritual things, so that what was said can be understood without absurdity.

## By the Altar We May Spiritually Understand Faith Itself, in the Inner Temple of God

**10. 27.** By the altar, then, we may spiritually understand faith itself, in the inner temple of God, whose sign is the visible altar. For whatever gift we offer to God—whether prophecy or teaching or prayer or hymn or psalm, and if any other such of the spiritual gifts comes to mind—it cannot be accepted by God unless it is supported by sincerity of faith and is, as it were, set firmly and immovably upon it, so that what we say can be whole and unimpaired. For many heretics, not having an altar, that is, true faith, have spoken blasphemies in place of praise, weighed down, that is, by earthly opinions, casting their vow as it were upon the ground. But the intention of the one offering ought also to be sound.

And therefore, when we are about to offer something of this sort in our heart, that is, in the inner temple of God—for *the temple of God is holy*[^75], says the apostle, *which you are*; and, *that Christ may dwell in the inner man through faith in your hearts*[^76]—if it comes to mind that our brother has something against us, that is, if we have injured him in something, for then he himself has something against us; for we have something against him if he has injured us, where there is no need to go for reconciliation, for you will not ask pardon of one who did you a wrong, but will only forgive, as you desire to be forgiven by the Lord for what you yourself have committed. We must go for reconciliation, then, when it comes to mind that we have perhaps injured our brother in something,

and we must go not with the feet of the body but with the movements of the mind, so that you prostrate yourself with humble affection before your brother, to whom you have run with dear thought, in the sight of him to whom you are about to offer your gift. For thus, even if he is present, you will be able to soothe him with no feigned mind and recall him to favor by asking pardon, if you have first done this before God, going to him not with a sluggish movement of the body but with a most swift affection of love. And then, coming—that is, recalling your intention to what you had begun to do—you will offer your gift.

## We Must Go for Reconciliation If We Have Perhaps Injured Our Brother

**10. 28.** But who does this—either not to be angry with his brother without cause, or not to say *raca* without cause, or not to call him *a fool* without cause, all of which is committed most proudly, or, if he should perhaps have fallen into any of these, to beg pardon with a suppliant mind, which is the one remedy—who does this unless he is not puffed up with the spirit of empty boasting? *Blessed*, therefore, *are the poor in spirit, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven*[^77]. Now let us see what follows!

## The Joy of the Divine Rewards for the Well-Deserving Servant (11, 29-32)

## Be Well-Disposed Toward Your Adversary

**11. 29.** *Be*, he says, *well-disposed toward your adversary quickly, while you are on the way with him, lest perhaps the adversary hand you over to the judge, and the judge hand you over to the officer, and you be thrown into prison. Amen I say to you, you will not go out from there until you pay the last farthing*[^78].

By the *judge* I understand: *for the Father judges no one, but has given all judgment to the Son*[^79]. By the *officer* I understand: *and the angels*, it says, *ministered to him*[^80]; and we believe that he will come with his angels to judge the living and the dead[^81]. By the *prison* I understand the punishments, namely, of the darkness, which in another place he calls *outer*[^82], I believe for this reason: that within, in the mind itself, or even, if anything more hidden can be conceived, is the joy of the divine rewards, of which it is said to the well-deserving servant, *enter into the joy of your Lord*[^83]; just as also in this ordering of the commonwealth one who is thrust into prison is sent out from the chamber or from the praetorium of the judge.

## On Paying the Last Farthing

**11. 30.** But as for paying the last farthing, it can not absurdly be understood either as set down for this, that nothing is left unpunished—just as in speaking we also say, "down to the dregs," when we wish to express something so thoroughly exacted that nothing is left—or so that by the name of the last *farthing* earthly sins are signified. For the fourth part of the distinct members of this world, and that the last, is found to be earth, if you begin from heaven, count the air as second, the water as third, the earth as fourth. It can fittingly seem, then, to have been said, *until you pay the last farthing*[^84], "until you expiate your earthly sins"; for the sinner heard this too, *you are earth, and into the earth you shall go*[^85].

But as for what was said, *until you pay*, I wonder whether it does not signify that punishment which is called eternal. For from where will that debt be paid, where there is no longer given any room for repenting and living more rightly? For perhaps *until you pay* is placed here just as in that passage where it was said, *sit at my right hand, until I put all your enemies under your feet*[^86]; for he will not, when the enemies have been put under his feet, cease to sit at the right hand; or that saying of the apostle, *for he must reign, until he puts all his enemies under his feet*[^87]; for he will not, when they have been put there, cease to reign. As, then, he is there understood, of whom it was said, *he must reign until he puts his enemies under his feet*, to be going to reign always, since they will always be under his feet, so here it can be taken that he, of whom it was said, *you will not go out from there until you pay the last farthing*, is never going to go out, because he will always be paying the last farthing, while he expiates with everlasting punishments the earthly sins. Nor would I say this in such a way that I seem to have removed a more careful treatment of the punishments of sins, how in the Scriptures they are called eternal; though in whatever way they may be, they are to be avoided rather than known.

## Let Us See Who the Adversary Is

**11. 31.** But let us now see who this adversary is, toward whom we are bidden to be well-disposed quickly, while we are with him on the way! For he is either the devil, or a man, or the flesh, or God, or his precept. But I do not see how we are bidden to be well-disposed toward the devil, that is, in concord or in agreement with him; for some have interpreted what is set down in Greek, *eunoon*, as *in concord*, others as *agreeing*. But neither are we bidden to show benevolence toward the devil—for where there is benevolence, there is friendship—nor would anyone say that friendship is to be made with the devil; nor is it of advantage to be in concord with him, against whom, by renouncing him once for all, we have declared war, and by whose conquest we shall be crowned; nor ought we now to agree with him, with whom, if we had never agreed, we would never have fallen into these miseries.

But concerning a man, although we are bidden to have peace with all, so far as is in us[^88], where certainly benevolence and concord and agreement can be understood, I nonetheless do not see how I should take it that we are handed over to a judge by a man, where I understand Christ as the judge, *before* whose *tribunal all must be presented*, as the apostle says[^89]. How, then, will one who will be presented equally before the judge hand another over to the judge?

Or if anyone is handed over to the judge for this reason, that he has injured a man, even though the one injured does not hand him over, it is much more conveniently taken that one is handed over to the judge by the Law itself, against which he acted when he injured the man. For even if someone has harmed a man by killing him, there will now be no time in which he may be in concord with him, since he is no longer with him on the way, that is, in this life. Nor, however, will he for that reason fail to be healed by repenting, and by taking refuge, with the sacrifice of a contrite heart, in the mercy of him who forgives the sins of those who turn to him, and who rejoices more over one penitent than over ninety-nine just[^90].

But toward the flesh I see much less how we are bidden to be well-disposed, or in concord, or in agreement. For sinners rather love their flesh and are in concord with it and agree with it; but those who subject it to servitude do not themselves agree with it, but force it to agree with them.

## He Agrees with Divine Scripture Who Reads or Hears It Piously, Granting It the Highest Authority

**11. 32.** Perhaps, then, we are bidden to agree with God and to be well-disposed toward him, so that we may be reconciled to him from whom by sinning we have turned away, so that he can be called our adversary.

For those whom he resists he is rightly called the adversary of; for *God resists the proud, but gives grace to the humble*[^91]; and *the beginning of all sin is pride, but the beginning of man's pride is to apostatize from God*[^92]; and the apostle says, *for if, while we were enemies, we were reconciled to God through the death of his Son, much more, being reconciled, shall we be saved in his life*[^93]. From which it can be understood that no nature is an enemy to God, since the very ones who were enemies are reconciled.

Whoever, then, on this way—that is, in this life—is not reconciled to God through the death of his Son, will be handed over to the judge by him, because *the Father judges no one, but has given all judgment to the Son*[^94]. And so the other things follow that are written in this passage, of which we have already treated.

There is one thing only that makes a difficulty for this understanding: how it can rightly be said that we are on the way with God, if in this place he himself is to be taken as the adversary of the impious, to whom we are bidden to be reconciled quickly—unless perhaps, because he himself is everywhere, we too, when we are on this way, are certainly with him. *For if I ascend into heaven*, it says, *you are there; if I descend into hell, you are present; if I take my wings straight ahead and dwell in the uttermost parts of the sea, even there your hand will lead me down, and your right hand will bring me*[^95].

Or if it is not pleasing to say that the impious are with God, although God is nowhere not present—just as we do not say that the blind are with the light, even though the light surrounds their eyes—one thing remains: that here we understand the adversary to be the precept of God. For what so opposes those who wish to sin as the precept of God, that is, his Law and the Divine Scripture which has been given to us for this life, so that it may be with us on the way, which we ought not to contradict, lest it hand us over to the judge, but with which we ought to agree quickly? For no one knows when he may depart from this life.

But who agrees with Divine Scripture except one who reads or hears it piously, granting it the highest authority, so that what he understands he does not hate because he perceives it to be opposed to his sins, but rather loves his own correction and rejoices that his diseases are not spared until they are healed; and what sounds to him either obscure or absurd, he does not from it stir up contests of contradictions, but prays that he may understand, yet remembers that benevolence and reverence are to be shown to so great an authority? But who does this except one who has come to open and learn his Father's testament not with the threatening of lawsuits, but meek with piety? *Blessed*, therefore, *are the meek, for they shall possess the land by inheritance*[^96]. Let us see what follows!

## Blessed Are Those Who Mourn, for They Shall Be Comforted. — Blessed Is He Who Does Not Commit Adultery in His Heart (12, 33-36)

## The Lesser Righteousness Is Not to Commit Adultery by Bodily Union, the Greater Righteousness of the Kingdom of God Is Not to Commit Adultery in the Heart

**12. 33.** *You have heard that it was said, "you shall not commit adultery." But I say to you that everyone who looks at a woman to lust for her has already committed adultery with her in his heart*[^97]. The lesser righteousness, then, is not to commit adultery by bodily union, but the greater righteousness of the kingdom of God is not to commit adultery in the heart. But whoever does not commit adultery in the heart guards much more easily against committing it in the body. He confirmed the former, then, who commanded the latter; for he did not come to abolish the Law but to fulfill it[^98].

Indeed, it must be considered that he did not say, "everyone who lusts for a woman," but, *who looks at a woman to lust*, that is, who looks with this end and this mind, that he may lust for her; which is no longer to be tickled by the delight of the flesh, but plainly to consent to lust, in such a way that the unlawful appetite is not reined in but, if the opportunity is given, is satisfied.

## Suggestion, Persuasion, Delight, Consent

**12. 34.** For there are three things by which sin is completed: by suggestion, by delight, by consent. Suggestion comes about either through memory or through the senses of the body, when we see or hear or smell or taste or touch something. And if it delights to enjoy it, the unlawful delight must be reined in. As when we fast and, at the sight of food, the appetite of the palate rises, it comes about only through delight; yet to this we do not consent, and we restrain it by the right of ruling reason. But if consent has been given, the sin will be complete, known to God in our heart, even if by the deed it does not become known to men.

These steps, then, are as follows: as though the suggestion comes from the serpent, that is, from a slippery and rolling—that is, temporal—movement of bodies; for even if any such phantasms are turned over within the soul, they have been drawn from the body from without; and if any hidden movement of the body, apart from those five senses, touches the soul, it too is temporal and slippery. And therefore, the more hiddenly it glides in, so as to touch thought, the more fittingly it is compared to a serpent.

These three, then, as I had begun to say, are like that deed which is written in *Genesis*, so that, as it were, from the serpent comes the suggestion and a certain persuasion, but in the carnal appetite, as in Eve, the delight, and in reason, as in the man, the consent[^99]. When these have been carried through, the man is driven, as it were, out of paradise, that is, out of the most blessed light of righteousness, into death.

Most justly, indeed. For he who persuades does not compel. And all natures, in their own order and their own grades, are beautiful, but from the higher things, in which the rational mind is set in order, one must not decline to the lower. Nor is anyone compelled to do this; and therefore, if he does it, he is punished by the just Law of God; for he does not commit it unwillingly.

Nevertheless, delight, before habit, is either nothing or so slight as to be almost nothing; to consent to it is a great sin, when it is unlawful. But when anyone has consented, he commits sin in the heart. And if it has even gone forward into a deed, the desire seems to be satisfied and extinguished. But afterward, when the suggestion is repeated, a greater delight is kindled, which nonetheless is still much less than that which, by repeated deeds, turns into habit. For to conquer this is most difficult. And yet even habit itself, if one does not abandon oneself and does not shrink back from the Christian warfare, one will overcome under that Leader and Helper, and so, into the former peace and order, both the man is subjected to Christ and the woman to the man[^100].

## Of Sin Itself There Are Three Differences: In the Heart, In the Deed, In Habit

**12. 35.** As, then, sin is reached by three steps—suggestion, delight, consent—so of sin itself there are three differences: in the heart, in the deed, in habit, as it were three deaths: one as though in the house, that is, when in the heart consent is given to lust; another now brought forth, as though outside the gate, when the assent goes forward into a deed; the third, when by the force of evil habit the mind is pressed down as though by an earthy mass, as though now stinking in the tomb. That the Lord raised these three kinds of dead, whoever reads the Gospel recognizes. And perhaps he considers what differences the very voice of the one raising them has, when in one place he says, *girl, arise!*[^101], in another, *young man, I say to you, arise!*[^102], in another, *he groaned in spirit and wept and again groaned*, and then afterward *cried out with a loud voice, "Lazarus, come forth!"*[^103]

## Every Evil Desire Is Rightly Called Fornication

**12. 36.** Therefore, by the name of *those who commit adultery*, who are mentioned in this passage, every carnal and lustful desire must be understood. For since Scripture so constantly calls idolatry fornication, and the apostle Paul calls greed by the name of idolatry[^104], who would doubt that every evil desire is rightly called fornication, when the soul, neglecting the higher law by which it is ruled, is corrupted by the shameful pleasure of lower natures, prostituted as though for hire? And therefore whoever feels carnal delight rebelling against his right will through the habit of sins, by whose untamed violence he is dragged into captivity, let him recall as much as he can what kind of peace he has lost by sinning, and cry out, *wretched man that I am! Who will free me from the body of this death? The grace of God through Jesus Christ our Lord*[^105]. For thus, when he cries out that he is wretched, by mourning he implores the help of the Comforter. Nor is the knowledge of one's wretchedness a small approach to blessedness; and therefore *blessed* also *are those who mourn, for they shall be comforted*[^106].

## Blessed Are Those Who Hunger and Thirst for Righteousness, — for They Shall Be Satisfied (13, 37 - 18, 54)

## Here Indeed There Is Need of Great Fortitude to Cut Off the Members

**13. 37.** Then there follows, and he says, *but if your right eye scandalizes you, pluck it out and cast it from you! For it is expedient for you that one of your members should perish, rather than that your whole body should go into Gehenna*[^107]. Here indeed there is need of great fortitude to cut off the members. For whatever it is that the eye signifies, it is without doubt something that is vehemently loved. For those too who vehemently wish to express their love are wont to say, "I love him as my eyes," or even, "more than my eyes." But that *right* is added perhaps serves to increase the force of the love. For although these bodily eyes are commonly directed to seeing and, if both are directed, can see equally, men nonetheless fear more to lose the right one. So that this is the sense: "Whatever it is that you so love that you hold it as a right eye, *if it scandalizes you*, that is, if it is a hindrance to you toward true blessedness, *pluck it out and cast it from you! For it is expedient for you that one of those things should perish*, which you so love that they cling to you as members, *rather than that your whole body should go into Gehenna*."

## As Contemplation Is Rightly Understood in the Eye, So Action in the Hand

**13. 38.** But since there follows about the right hand and it is said likewise, *if your right hand scandalizes you, cut it off and cast it from you! For it is expedient for you that one of your members should perish, rather than that your whole body should go into Gehenna*[^108], it compels us to ask more carefully what he meant by the *eye*. In which question nothing occurs to me more fitting than a most beloved friend. For this is certainly what we can rightly call a member, what we vehemently love, and this a counselor, because the eye is, as it were, one who points out the way; and in divine matters, because it is the right one, so that the left may be a beloved counselor indeed, but in earthly matters pertaining to the necessity of the body; about which, if it scandalizes, it would be superfluous to speak, since not even the right one is to be spared. But in divine matters a counselor scandalizes if, under the name of religion and doctrine, he tries to lead one into some pernicious heresy. Therefore by the right hand too is taken a beloved helper and minister in divine works—for as contemplation is rightly understood in the eye, so action in the hand—so that by the left hand may be understood works that are necessary for this life and the body.

## The Lord, So That a Wife Should Not Easily Be Divorced, Made an Exception Only of the Cause of Fornication

**14. 39.** *And it was said, "whoever divorces his wife, let him give her a writ of repudiation*[^109]." This is the lesser righteousness of the Pharisees, to which what the Lord says is not contrary: *but I say to you, "whoever divorces his wife, except for the cause of fornication, makes her commit adultery, and whoever marries her who has been set free from her husband commits adultery*[^110]."

For he who commanded that a writ of repudiation be given did not command this, that a wife be divorced; but, *he who divorces*, he says, *let him give her a writ of repudiation*, so that the thought of the writ might temper the rash anger of the one divorcing his wife. He, then, who sought a delay in divorcing signified, as much as he could to hard men, that he was unwilling for the separation.

And therefore the Lord himself, in another place, when asked about this, answered thus: *Moses did this on account of your hardness*[^111]. For however hard one might be who wished to divorce his wife, when he considered that, the writ of repudiation having been given, she could now without danger marry another, he would easily be appeased.

The Lord, then, to confirm this, that a wife should not easily be divorced, made an exception only of the cause of fornication; but all other troubles whatsoever, if any should arise, he bids to be bravely endured for the sake of marital fidelity and chastity. And he calls an adulterer also the man who marries her who has been set free from her husband. Of which matter the apostle Paul shows the limit, saying that it must be observed as long as her husband lives; but when he is dead, he gives permission to marry[^112]. For this rule he himself held, and in it he showed not his own counsel, as in some admonitions, but the precept of the Lord commanding, when he says, *but to those who are in marriage I command, not I but the Lord, that the wife not depart from her husband; but if she has departed, that she remain unmarried or be reconciled to her husband; and let not the husband divorce his wife*[^113];

in a like form, I believe, so that if he divorces her he should not marry another, or should be reconciled to his wife. For it can happen that he divorces his wife for the cause of fornication, which the Lord wished to be excepted. But now if it is not granted to her to marry another while the husband from whom she departed lives, nor to him to marry another while the wife whom he divorced lives, much less is it permitted to commit unlawful acts of unchastity with anyone at all.

Indeed, those marriages are to be judged more blessed which, whether children have been begotten, or even this earthly offspring has been despised, have been able to keep continence between themselves by equal consent, because neither is anything done against that precept by which the Lord forbids a spouse to be divorced—for he does not divorce her who lives with her not carnally but spiritually—and that is kept which is said through the apostle, *it remains that those who have wives be as though not having them*[^114].

## He Who Does Not Hate These Things That Are to Pass Away Does Not Yet Love That Eternal Life

**15. 40.** That is more wont to trouble the mind of the little ones, who nonetheless already long to live according to the precepts of Christ, which the Lord himself says in another place: *whoever comes to me and does not hate his father and mother and wife and children and brothers and sisters, and his own soul besides, cannot be my disciple*[^115]. For to those who understand less it can seem contrary that here he forbids a wife to be divorced, except for the cause of fornication, but elsewhere denies that anyone can be his disciple who does not hate his wife.

For if he said this on account of intercourse, he would not also place father and mother and brothers in the same condition. But how true it is that *the kingdom of heaven suffers violence, and those who do violence seize it!*[^116] For how great a violence is needed that a man should love his enemies, and hate father and mother and wife and children and brothers! For he who calls to the kingdom of heaven commands both. And how these are not contrary to each other, it is easy to show under his guidance; but, when understood, it is difficult to fulfill—though even this, with that same one helping, is most easy.

For the eternal kingdom, to which he has deigned to call his disciples, whom he also names brothers[^117], does not have temporal relationships of this sort. For *there is neither Jew nor Greek, neither male nor female, neither slave nor free, but Christ is all and in all*[^118]. And the Lord himself says, *for in the resurrection they will neither marry nor be given in marriage, but will be like the angels in heaven*[^119]. It is necessary, then, that whoever wishes already here to meditate on the life of that kingdom should hate not the men themselves, but those temporal relationships by which this life that is to pass away is supported, which is carried on by being born and dying. He who does not hate this does not yet love that life where there will be no condition of being born and dying, which joins earthly marriages.

## Let Us Love What Can Be Brought with Us to Those Kingdoms

**15. 41.** And so if I should ask some good Christian who nonetheless has a wife, although he still begets children with her, whether he would wish to have a wife in that kingdom—mindful, of course, of the promises of God and of that life where this corruptible will put on incorruption and this mortal will put on immortality[^120]—he will at once, held back by a great or at least some love, answer with execration that he vehemently does not wish it. Again, if I should ask whether he would wish his wife, after the resurrection, the angelic change which is promised to the saints having been received, to live there with him, he will answer that he wishes it as vehemently as he does not wish the other. So the good Christian is found to love in one woman the creature of God, whom he desires to be reformed and renewed, but to hate the corruptible and mortal union and coupling, that is, to love in her what is human, to hate what is wife. So too he loves an enemy, not insofar as he is an enemy, but insofar as he is a man, so that he wishes for him what he wishes for himself, that is, that, corrected and renewed, he may come to the kingdom of heaven.

This is to be understood also of father and of mother and of the other ties of blood[^121], so that in them we hate what the human race has been allotted by being born and dying, but love what can be brought with us to those kingdoms, where no one says "my father," but all to the one God, *our Father*[^122]; nor "my mother," but to all that Jerusalem is our mother[^123]; nor "my brother," but of all, from among all, our brother[^124]. But the marriage, when together we have been brought into one with him who freed us from the prostitution of this world by the shedding of his blood, will be as it were of one spouse[^125]. It is necessary, then, that the disciple of Christ hate the things that pass away, in those whom he desires to come with him to the things that will always remain; and he will hate these things in them the more, the more he loves them.

## What Is Most Excellent and Sublime in the Marriage of Christians

**15. 42.** A Christian, therefore, can live in concord with his spouse, whether supplying with her carnal need, which the apostle says is by way of concession, not of command[^126], or for the propagation of children, which can now in some degree be praiseworthy, or in a brotherly fellowship without any commixture of bodies, having a wife as though not having one[^127]—which is most excellent and sublime in the marriage of Christians—so that nonetheless he hates in her the name of temporal necessity and loves the hope of everlasting blessedness. For we hate, without doubt, what we wish at some time not to be, just as this very life of the present time, which, if we did not hate it as temporal, we would not desire the life to come, which is not subject to time. For it is for the sake of this life that the soul is set down, of which it was said there, *he who does not hate his own soul besides cannot be my disciple*[^128]. For this life that corruptible food is necessary, of which the Lord himself says, *is not the soul more than food?*[^129], that is, this life, for which food is necessary[^130]. And what he says, that he lays down his soul for his sheep, he certainly means this life, when he declares that he will die for us.

## The Form Is Equal in the Right of Marriage Between Man and Woman

**16. 43.** Here another question arises: "Since the Lord permits a wife to be divorced for the cause of fornication, how far is fornication to be understood in this place: whether as far as all understand it, that is, that we believe the fornication spoken of is that committed in acts of unchastity, or in the way the Scriptures are wont to call fornication, as was said above, every unlawful corruption, such as idolatry or greed, and, from this, now every transgression of the Law on account of unlawful desire." But let us consult the apostle, lest we say anything rashly! *To those who are in marriage*, he says, *I command, not I but the Lord, that the wife not depart from her husband; but if she has departed, that she remain unmarried or be reconciled to her husband*[^131]. For it can happen that she departs for the cause for which the Lord permitted. Or if it is permitted to a woman to divorce her husband even apart from the cause of fornication, and not permitted to the man, what shall we answer about what he says afterward, *and let not the husband divorce his wife*[^132]? Why did he not add, *except for the cause of fornication*, which the Lord permits, unless because he wishes a like form to be understood, so that if he divorces her—which is permitted for the cause of fornication—he should remain without a wife or be reconciled to his wife?

For the man would not wrongly be reconciled to that woman whom, when no one had dared to stone her, the Lord said to, *go, see that henceforth you do not sin*[^133]. For even he who says, it is not permitted to divorce a wife except for the cause of fornication, compels one to retain the wife if there has not been the cause of fornication; but if there has been, he does not compel one to divorce her, but permits it;

just as it is said: it is not permitted to a woman to marry another except when her husband is dead; if she marries before the husband's death, she is guilty; if she does not marry after the husband's death, she is not guilty; for she was not commanded to marry, but permitted[^134].

If, then, the form is equal in this right of marriage between man and woman, so much so that the same apostle said not only of the woman, *the woman does not have power over her own body, but the man*, but also did not keep silent about the man, saying, *likewise also the man does not have power over his own body, but the woman*[^135]—if, then, the form is alike, it must not be understood that it is permitted to a woman to divorce her husband except for the cause of fornication, just as it is for the man.

## In the Apostle's Counsel One Thing Is Commanded, Another Advised, Another Conceded

**16. 44.** And so it must be considered how far we ought to understand fornication, and the apostle must be consulted, as we had begun. For there follows, and he says, *but to the rest I say, not the Lord*[^136]. Here first it must be seen to which *rest*; for he was speaking above, in the person of the Lord, to those who are in marriage, but now in his own person he speaks to the rest. So perhaps to those who are not in marriage. But this does not follow; for he adds thus: *if any brother has an unbelieving wife, and she consents to dwell with him, let him not divorce her*. So even now he speaks to those who are in marriage.

What, then, does he mean by saying, *to the rest*, except that above he was speaking to those who were so joined that they were equally in the faith of Christ, but now he speaks *to the rest*, that is, to those who are so joined that they are not both believers? But what does he say to them? *If any brother has an unbelieving wife, and she consents to dwell with him, let him not divorce her; and if any woman has an unbelieving husband, and he consents to dwell with her, let her not divorce her husband*[^137]. If, then, he does not command in the person of the Lord, but advises in his own person, this is a good thing, so that if anyone does otherwise, he is not a transgressor of a precept.

Just as a little later he says of virgins that he does not have a precept of the Lord, but gives counsel; and so he praises virginity in such a way that she who wishes may seize it, yet not so that, if she does not do so, she is judged to have acted against a precept[^138]. For one thing is commanded, another advised, another conceded.

The woman is commanded *not to depart from her husband; but if she has departed, to remain unmarried or be reconciled to her husband*. To do otherwise, then, is not permitted. But the believing husband is advised, if he has an unbelieving wife who consents to dwell with him, not to divorce her. It is permitted, then, even to divorce her, because it is not a precept of the Lord not to divorce her, but the counsel of the apostle; just as a virgin is advised not to marry, but if she marries, she will indeed not hold to the counsel, but she will not act against a precept. Yet it is conceded, when he says, *but this I say by way of concession, not of command*[^139]. Therefore, if it is permitted that an unbelieving spouse be divorced, although it is better not to divorce, and yet according to the precept of the Lord it is not permitted that a spouse be divorced except for the cause of fornication, unbelief itself is also fornication.

## Some Unbelievers Can Come to the Faith Through Believing Spouses

**16. 45.** For what do you say, O apostle? Certainly that a believing husband should not divorce an unbelieving wife who consents to dwell with him. So it is, he says. Since, then, the Lord too commands this, that a husband not divorce his wife except for the cause of fornication, why do you say here, *I say, not the Lord*[^140]? Because, namely, the idolatry which unbelievers follow, and any harmful superstition, is fornication.

But the Lord permitted a wife to be divorced for the cause of fornication; yet because he permitted and did not command it, he gave room for the apostle to advise that whoever wishes should not divorce an unbelieving wife, so that perhaps in this way she may become a believer. *For the unbelieving husband*, he says, *is sanctified in the wife, and the unbelieving wife is sanctified in the brother*[^141].

I believe it had already come about that some women came to the faith through believing husbands and some men through believing wives; and although not naming names, he nonetheless exhorted by examples to confirm his counsel.

Then there follows, *otherwise your children would be unclean, but now they are holy*[^142]. For there were already little Christians who, whether by the agency of one of the parents or with the consent of both, had been sanctified. This would not happen if, with one believing, the marriage were dissolved, and the unbelief of the spouse were not borne until the opportunity for believing. This, then, is his counsel, to whom, I believe, it was said, *whatever you spend beyond, on my return I will repay you*[^143].

## Greed Is Fornication

**16. 46.** Furthermore, if unbelief is fornication, and idolatry is unbelief, and greed is idolatry, it must not be doubted that greed too is fornication. Who, then, can now rightly separate any unlawful desire from the class of fornication, if greed is fornication? From which it is understood that on account of unlawful desires—not only those committed in acts of unchastity with other men's or women's spouses, but any whatsoever that make the soul, misusing the body, stray from the law of God and corrupt it perniciously and shamefully—both a husband can without crime divorce his wife and a wife her husband, because the Lord makes the cause of fornication an exception. Which fornication, as was considered above, we are compelled to understand generally and universally.

## Whoever, Whether Man or Woman, Wishes to Cast Off a Spouse for the Cause of Fornication Ought First to Be Purified of Fornication

**16. 47.** But when he says, *except for the cause of fornication*, he did not say whose fornication, the man's or the woman's. For it is granted not only to divorce a wife who fornicates, but whoever divorces also that wife by whom he himself is compelled to fornicate divorces her certainly for the cause of fornication. For instance, if a wife compels someone to sacrifice to idols, he who divorces such a one divorces her for the cause of fornication, not only hers but also his own: hers because she fornicates, his own lest he fornicate.

But nothing is more unjust than to divorce a wife for the cause of fornication, if he himself too is convicted of fornicating. For that saying occurs: *for in that wherein you judge another, you condemn yourself, for you do the same things that you judge*[^144]. Therefore whoever wishes to cast off his wife for the cause of fornication ought first to be purified of fornication; which I would say likewise of the woman too.

## As He Who Marries Commits Adultery, So Too She Whom He Marries

**16. 48.** But as for what he says, *whoever marries her who has been set free from her husband commits adultery*[^145], it can be asked whether, as he who marries her commits adultery, so too she whom he marries. For she too is bidden to remain unmarried or be reconciled to her husband; but if she has departed, he says, from her husband[^146]. Now there is a great difference whether she divorces or is divorced. For if she herself has divorced her husband and married another, she seems, from a desire of changing her marriage, to have left her former husband, which is without doubt an adulterous thought.

But if she is divorced by her husband with whom she desired to be, he indeed who marries her commits adultery, according to the Lord's sentence; but whether she too is held by such a crime is uncertain. Although it can much less be found how, when a man and a woman are joined together by equal consent, one of them should be an adulterer and the other not.

To this is added that, if he commits adultery by marrying her who has been set free from her husband, even though she has not divorced but has been divorced, she herself makes him commit adultery, which the Lord no less forbids. From which it is gathered that, whether she has been divorced or has divorced, she ought to remain unmarried or be reconciled to her husband.

## It Is Asked Whether, with the Wife's Permission, a Husband May Lie with Another, Who Is Neither Another Man's Wife Nor Separated from Her Husband

**16. 49.** Again it is asked whether, if with his wife's permission—she being either barren or unwilling to endure intercourse—a husband takes to himself another, not another man's wife nor separated from her husband, he can be without the crime of fornication. And in the history of the Old Testament indeed an example is found[^147].

But now the precepts are greater, to which the human race arrived through that step. Those things are to be treated for distinguishing the ages of the dispensation of divine providence, which came to the aid of the human race in a most orderly way, but not to be appropriated as rules for living.

But nonetheless, whether what the apostle says, *the woman does not have power over her own body, but the man; likewise also the man does not have power over his own body, but the woman*[^148], can avail so far that, with the wife's permission, who has power over the marital body, a husband may lie with another, who is neither another man's wife nor separated from her husband? But it must not be so supposed, lest a woman too should seem able to do this with the husband's permission, which the judgment of all excludes.

## What Is Reported to Have Happened at Antioch Some Fifty Years Ago, in the Times of Constantius

**16. 50.** Although certain cases can arise where even a wife, with her husband's consent, would seem bound to do this for her husband's sake, as is reported to have happened at Antioch some fifty years ago, in the times of Constantius. For Acindynus, then prefect, who was also consul, when he was demanding from a certain debtor of the treasury a pound of gold, moved by I know not what—which is generally dangerous in those holding such powers, to whom anything is permitted, or rather is thought to be permitted—threatened, swearing and vehemently affirming that, if on the fixed day he had appointed the said gold were not paid, he would be killed.

And so, since the man was held in harsh custody and could not free himself from that debt, the dreaded day began to loom and draw near. And he happened to have a most beautiful wife, but with no money by which she could help her husband. When a certain rich man was inflamed by this woman's beauty, and had learned that her husband was set in that peril, he sent to her, promising that for one night, if she would be joined to him, he would give a pound of gold.

Then she, knowing that she did not have power over her own body but her husband did, carried the matter to him, saying that she was ready to do it for her husband, if only he, the master of the conjugal body, to whom all that chastity was owed, should wish, as of his own property, for the sake of his life, that it be done.

He gave thanks and ordered that it be done, judging the intercourse in no way adulterous, since no lust and the great love of her husband, with himself willing and commanding it, demanded it.

The woman came to the villa of that rich man. The shameless man did what he wished. But she gave her body only to her husband, who desired not to lie with her, as usual, but to live.

She received the gold. But he who gave it withdrew by fraud what he had given, and substituted a similar packet with earth. When the woman, now set in her own home, found this, she sprang out into public to cry out, by that same love of her husband, what she had done, by which she had been compelled to do it.

She appealed to the prefect, confessed everything, showed what fraud she had suffered. Then indeed the prefect, first pronouncing himself guilty, because it had come to this by his threats—as though giving sentence against another—decreed that a pound of gold from Acindynus's goods be brought into the treasury, but that woman be made mistress of that land from which, in place of gold, she had received earth.

I argue nothing from this in any direction. Let each be allowed to judge what he will; for the story is not drawn from divine authorities. But nonetheless, the deed having been narrated, human judgment does not so reject what was committed in that woman by her husband's command as we shuddered before, when the matter itself was set down without any example. But in this passage of the Gospel nothing is more forcefully to be considered than that the evil of fornication is so great that, although marriages are bound to each other by so great a bond, this one cause of dissolution is excepted. But what fornication is has already been treated.

## How Difficult It Is Both to Extinguish the Habit of Swearing and Never Rashly to Do What Necessity Sometimes Compels Us to Do

**17. 51.** *Again*, he says, *you have heard that it was said to the ancients: you shall not swear falsely, but you shall render to the Lord your oath. But I say to you not to swear at all, neither by heaven, because it is the throne of God, nor by earth, because it is the footstool of his feet, nor by Jerusalem, because it is the city of the great king, nor shall you swear by your head, because you cannot make one hair white or black. But let your speech be: yes, yes; no, no. But what is more than this is from evil*[^149]. The righteousness of the Pharisees is not to swear falsely. This he confirms who forbids swearing, which pertains to the righteousness of the kingdom of heaven. For as he who does not speak cannot speak falsely, so he who does not swear cannot swear falsely. But nonetheless, since he swears who calls God to witness, this passage must be carefully considered, lest the apostle seem to have acted against the precept of the Lord, who often swore in this way, when he says, *but the things I write to you, behold, before God, that I do not lie*[^150], and again, *the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, who is blessed forever, knows that I do not lie*[^151]. Such also is this: *for God is my witness, whom I serve in my spirit in the Gospel of his Son, how without ceasing I always make mention of you in my prayers*[^152]—unless perhaps someone says that an oath is to be reckoned only when it is sworn by something through which one swears, so that he did not swear, because he did not say, "by God," but said, *God is witness*. It is ridiculous to think this.

Yet on account of the contentious or the very slow, lest anyone think there is some difference, let him know that the apostle swore also in this way, saying, *I die daily, by your glory*[^153]. And lest anyone suppose this was said as though it were said, "your glory makes me die daily"—as it is said, "by his teaching he was made learned," that is, by his teaching it came about that he was perfectly taught—the Greek copies decide the matter, in which is written *ne tan hymeteran kauchesin*, which is said only by one swearing.

So the Lord is understood to have commanded that one not swear, lest anyone should crave an oath as a good thing, and by the constancy of swearing slip through habit into perjury. Therefore he who understands that an oath is to be reckoned not among good things but among necessary things, let him rein himself in as much as he can, so that he does not use it except from necessity, when he sees that men are sluggish to believe what it is useful for them to believe, unless they are made firm by an oath. To this, then, pertains what is said thus: *but let your speech be: yes, yes; no, no*. This is good and to be sought.

*But what is more than this is from evil*[^154]; that is, if you are compelled to swear, know that it comes from the necessity of the weakness of those to whom you are urging something. Which weakness is certainly an evil, from which we daily beg to be freed, when we say, *deliver us from evil*[^155]. And so he did not say, "but what is more than this is evil"; for you do not do evil, who use well an oath, which, though not good, is nonetheless necessary, to persuade another of what you usefully urge; but it is from the evil of that one, by whose weakness you are compelled to swear. But no one knows, except one who has experienced it, how difficult it is both to extinguish the habit of swearing and never rashly to do what necessity sometimes compels us to do.

## He Who Swears Owes His Oath to the Lord

**17. 52.** But it can be asked, since it was said, *but I say to you not to swear at all*, why it was added, *neither by heaven, because it is the throne of God*, and the rest, down to what was said, *nor by your head*[^156]. I believe for this reason: that the Jews did not think themselves bound by an oath if they swore by these things; and since they had heard, *but you shall render to the Lord your oath*[^157], they did not think they owed the Lord an oath if they swore by heaven or earth or Jerusalem or by their head; which came about not by the fault of the one giving the precept, but by their misunderstanding. And so the Lord teaches that nothing among God's creatures is so cheap that anyone should think he may swear falsely by it, since from the highest down to the lowest they were created and ruled by divine providence, beginning from the throne of God down to a white or black hair.

*Neither by heaven*, he says, *because it is the throne of God, nor by earth, because it is the footstool of his feet*[^158], that is, when you swear by heaven or earth, do not think you do not owe the Lord your oath, because you are convicted of swearing by him whose throne heaven is[^159], and whose footstool earth is. *Nor by Jerusalem, because it is the city of the great king*[^160]; better than if he said "mine," though he is understood to have said this. And since he himself is certainly the Lord, he owes his oath to the Lord who swears by Jerusalem. *Nor shall you swear by your head*[^161]. For what could anyone think pertained more to himself than his own head? But how is it ours, when we do not have the power of making one hair white or black? Therefore whoever wishes to swear even by his own head owes his oath to God, who ineffably holds all things and is everywhere present. And from this the rest too are understood, all of which certainly could not be said, as that saying which we mentioned, said by the apostle, *I die daily, by your glory*. To show that he owed this oath to the Lord, he added, *which I have in Christ Jesus*[^162].

## Spiritually, the Name of Heaven Signifies Holy Souls and That of Earth Sinful Ones

**17. 53.** Nevertheless, on account of the carnal I say: it must not be supposed that what was said—that heaven is the throne of God and earth the footstool of his feet—means that God has limbs so placed in heaven and on earth as we have when we sit; but that seat signifies judgment.

And since in this whole body of the world heaven has the greatest beauty and earth the least, as though the divine power were more present to surpassing beauty, but orders the least in the outermost and lowest places, it is said to sit in heaven and to tread the earth.

But spiritually the name of *heaven* signifies holy souls and that of *earth* sinful ones. And since the spiritual man judges all things, but is himself judged by no one[^163], he is fittingly called the *seat of God*. But the sinner, to whom it was said, *you are earth, and into the earth you shall go*[^164], because through justice, which renders to merits what is worthy, he is ordered among the lowest and, because he was unwilling to remain in the Law, is punished under the Law, is fittingly taken as the footstool of God's feet.

## Let the Faithful Soul Cut Off the Members That Hinder the Kingdom of Heaven and Not Be Broken by Sorrow!

**18. 54.** But now, to conclude this summary too, what more laborious and toilsome thing can be said or thought, on which the faithful soul exercises all the sinews of its industry, than the overcoming of vicious habit? Let it cut off the members that hinder the kingdom of heaven and not be broken by sorrow! Let it endure in conjugal fidelity all things which, however most troublesome they are, nonetheless do not have the crime of unlawful corruption, that is, of fornication! As, for instance, if anyone has a wife, whether barren, or deformed in body, or feeble in her limbs, or blind, or deaf, or lame, or whatever else, or worn out by diseases and pains and infirmities, and whatever, fornication excepted, can be conceived as vehemently horrible, let him endure it for the sake of fidelity and fellowship.

And not only let him not cast off such a one, but even if he has no wife, let him not marry her who has been set free from her husband, beautiful, healthy, rich, fruitful. And if it is not permitted to do this, let him judge that it is much less permitted to him to approach any other unlawful intercourse; and let him so flee fornication that he draws himself away from all shameful corruption. Let him speak the truth and commend it not by frequent oaths but by uprightness of conduct!

Taking refuge in the citadel of the Christian warfare, let him cast down, as from a higher place, the innumerable hosts of all evil habits rebelling against him, of which, so that all might be understood, a few have been mentioned. But who would dare to undertake such great labors except one who so burns with love of righteousness that, as though most vehemently inflamed with hunger and thirst, and reckoning that he has no life until he is satisfied with it, he does violence to the kingdom of heaven? For he cannot otherwise be strong to endure all the things that lovers of this world think laborious and arduous and altogether difficult in cutting off habits. *Blessed*, therefore, *are those who hunger and thirst for righteousness, for they shall be satisfied*[^165].

## Blessed Is He Who Bears the Weakness of Others — and Helps Them as Much as He Can (18, 55 - 23, 80)

## The Meek and the Merciful Seem to Be One, but They Differ in Resisting

**18. 55.** Nevertheless, in these labors, when anyone suffers difficulty and, making his way through hard and rough things, surrounded by various temptations and seeing the masses of his past life rising up on this side and that, fears that he cannot fulfill what he has undertaken, let him seize upon counsel, that he may deserve help! But what other counsel is there than that he should bear the weakness of others, and help, as much as he can, the one for whom he desires that his own may be relieved by God?

And so let us consequently look at the precepts of mercy! The meek and the merciful seem to be one. But this is the difference: that the meek man, of whom there was treatment above, out of piety does not contradict the divine sentences which are brought forth against his sins, nor those words of God which he does not yet understand, but bestows no benefit on the one whom he does not contradict or resist; but the merciful man so does not resist that he does it for the correction of the one whom by resisting he would make worse.

## It is perfect peace to be utterly unwilling for such vengeance.

**19. 56.** The Lord goes on, then, and says, *you have heard that it was said, "an eye for an eye and a tooth for a tooth." But I say to you not to resist evil; but if anyone strikes you on your right cheek, offer him the other also; and to him who wishes to contend with you in judgment and take away your tunic, let go to him your cloak also; and whoever shall compel you to go a mile, go with him another two. Give to everyone who asks you, and from him who wishes to borrow from you do not turn away*[^166]. The lesser righteousness of the Pharisees is not to exceed measure in vengeance, so that no one repays more than he received; and this is a great step.

For no one is easily found who, having received a blow, is willing to return only a blow, and, having heard one word from one reviling him, is content to return one word of equal weight; but either, disturbed by anger, he takes vengeance immoderately, or, because he thinks it just that the one who injured first should be more grievously injured than the one injured who had not injured.

The Law in great part reined in such a mind, in which it is written, *an eye for an eye and a tooth for a tooth*[^167], by which names measure is signified, so that the vengeance may not exceed the injury. And this is the beginning of peace; but perfect peace is to be utterly unwilling for such vengeance.

## A Transition, According to the Distribution of the Times, from the Highest Discord to the Highest Concord

**19. 57.** Between that first thing, then, which is beyond the Law, that a greater evil be returned for a lesser evil, and this which the Lord says for the perfecting of his disciples, that no evil be returned for evil, a certain middle place is held, that only as much be returned as was received, through which a transition was made, according to the distribution of the times, from the highest discord to the highest concord.

Whoever, then, inflicts evil first, in eagerness to injure and harm, see how far he is from the one who, even when injured, does not repay! But whoever has first done evil to no one, yet, being injured, repays more grievously, whether in will or in deed, has withdrawn somewhat from the highest iniquity and advanced toward the highest justice, and yet does not yet hold to what the law given through Moses commanded.

He, then, who returns only as much as he received, already concedes something, for the one who harms does not deserve so great a punishment as the one who, injured by him, suffered though innocent. This justice, then, begun—not severe but merciful—he perfects who came to fulfill the Law, not to abolish it[^168].

He left, then, two further steps that lie between to be understood, and chose rather to speak of the very highest summit of mercy. For there is still something for him to do who does not fulfill that greatness of the precept which pertains to the kingdom of heaven, so that he returns not only no more but less, as, for two blows, one, or, for a plucked-out eye, cut off an ear.

Ascending from this, he who returns nothing at all draws near to the Lord's precept, yet is not yet there. For it still seems too little to the Lord if for the evil you have received you return no evil, unless you are also prepared to receive more. Therefore he does not say, "But I say to you not to return evil for evil," although this too is a great precept, but he says, "not to resist evil, so that you not only do not repay what was inflicted on you, but also do not resist, so that nothing else be inflicted." For this is what he also consequently sets forth: *but if anyone strikes you on your right cheek, offer him the other also*[^169]. For he does not say, "if anyone strikes you, do not strike him," but, "be prepared for the one still striking."

That this pertains to mercy, those especially feel who serve those whom they greatly love, as their sick children or any of their most beloved, or little ones, or those who are frenzied, from whom they often suffer many things and, if their welfare requires it, offer themselves even to suffer more, until the weakness of age or of disease passes. Those, then, whom the Lord, the physician of souls, was instructing for the care of their neighbors, what else could he teach them than that they should bear with an even mind the infirmities of those for whose welfare they wished to provide? For all wickedness comes from weakness of mind, since nothing is more innocent than one who is perfect in virtue.

## What the Right Cheek Means

**19. 58.** But it can be asked what the right cheek means. For so it is found in the Greek copies, to which greater trust is to be given. For many Latin ones have only "cheek," not also "right."

But the face is that by which each one is recognized. And we read in the apostle, *for you endure it if anyone reduces you to slavery, if anyone devours you, if anyone takes from you, if anyone exalts himself, if anyone strikes you in the face*[^170]; then immediately he adds, *I speak according to dishonor*, to set forth what it is to be struck in the face, that is, to be despised and looked down upon. Which indeed the apostle says not so that they should not put up with those men, but so that he himself rather, who loved them as himself, should wish to be spent for them[^171]. But since the face cannot be called right and left, and yet there can be nobility both according to God and according to this world, it is assigned as though to a right cheek and a left, so that, in whatever disciple of Christ that which is Christian is despised, he should be much more prepared for it to be despised in himself, if he has any honors of this world.

As the same apostle, when men persecuted in him the Christian name, if he kept silent about the dignity which he had in the world, would not offer the other cheek to those striking the right. For in saying, *I am a Roman citizen*[^172], he was not prepared for that to be despised in him—which he held as the least thing—by those who in him had despised so precious and saving a name.

For did he therefore afterward any less endure the chains which it was not permitted to impose on Roman citizens, or did he wish to accuse anyone of this wrong? And if some spared him on account of the name of Roman citizenship, he did not for that reason fail to offer what they might strike, since by his patience he desired to correct from so great a perversity those whom he saw honoring in him the left parts rather than the right.

For only this is to be considered: with what mind he did all things, how benevolently and gently toward those from whom he suffered these things. For even when struck with the palm at the high priest's command, what he seemed to say insultingly, when he said, *God will strike you, you whitewashed wall*[^173], sounds to those who understand less like a reproach, but to those who understand it is a prophecy. For a whitewashed wall is hypocrisy, that is, pretense putting forward priestly dignity, and under this name, as under a white covering, hiding the inner, as it were muddy, shamefulness.

For what belonged to humility he wonderfully guarded, when it was said to him, *do you revile the high priest?*, he answered, *I did not know, brothers, that he is the high priest; for it is written, "you shall not revile the prince of your people*[^174]." Here he showed with how great a tranquillity he had said what he seemed to have said in anger, in that he answered so quickly, so gently—which cannot be done by the indignant and disturbed. And in this very thing he spoke the truth to those who understand: *I did not know that he is the high priest*, as though he said, "I knew another high priest, for whose name I endure these things, whom it is not lawful to revile, and whom you revile, when in me you hate nothing but his name."

So, then, one ought not to boast of these things in pretense, but to be, in the very heart, prepared for all things, so that one can sing that prophetic verse, *my heart is ready, O God, my heart is ready*[^175]. For many have known how to offer the other cheek, but do not know how to love the one by whom they are struck. But the Lord himself, who certainly first fulfilled the precepts he taught, did not offer the other cheek to the priest's officer who struck him on the cheek, but rather said, *if I have spoken evil, reproach me for the evil; but if well, why do you strike me?*[^176] Yet he was not for that reason unprepared in heart not only to be struck on the other cheek for the salvation of all, but even to be crucified in his whole body.

## The Lord's Precept Is to Be Observed in All Things That by Some Right We Call Temporally Ours

**19. 59.** Therefore that too which follows, *and to him who wishes to contend with you in judgment and take away your tunic, let go to him your cloak also*[^177], is rightly understood as a precept for the preparation of the heart, not for the display of the deed. But what was said of the tunic and the cloak is to be done not in these alone, but in all things that by some right we call temporally ours. For if this is commanded concerning necessities, how much more is it fitting to despise superfluous things! Nevertheless, those things which I called ours are to be included in that kind which the Lord himself prescribes, saying, *if anyone wishes to contend with you in judgment and take away your tunic*. Let all those things, then, be understood concerning which one can be contended with against us in judgment, in such a way that they pass from our right into the right of the one who contends or for whom he contends, such as clothing, a house, a farm, a beast of burden, and, generally, all property.

Whether this is to be taken also of slaves is a great question. For a Christian ought not to possess a slave as he does a horse or silver, although it can happen that a horse is worth a greater price than a slave, and much more some object of gold or silver. But if that slave is brought up or governed more rightly and honorably and more suitably for the worship of God by you, his master, than he can be by the one who desires to take him away, I do not know whether anyone would dare to say that he ought to be despised like a garment. For one man ought to love another as himself, since by the Lord of all, as the things that follow show, he is commanded even to love his enemies.

## The Name "Cloak" Signifies More Than the Name "Tunic"

**19. 60.** Indeed, it must be noted that every tunic is a garment, but not every garment is a tunic. The name *garment*, then, signifies more than the name *tunic*. And therefore I think it was said thus, *and to him who wishes to contend with you in judgment and take away your tunic, let go to him your cloak also*; as though he said, "to him who wishes to take away your tunic, let go also any other clothing you have." Therefore some have interpreted as *cloak* what is set down in Greek as *himation*.

## Let Us Remember That We Fulfill Perfect Righteousness by Mercifully Bearing the Infirmities of Those Whom We Wish to Be Made Whole

**19. 61.** *And whoever shall compel you*, he says, *to go a mile, go with him another two*[^178], and this certainly not so much that you may act with your feet, as that you may be prepared in mind. For in the Christian history itself, in which there is authority, you will find nothing of the sort done by the saints or by the Lord himself, when, in the man whom he deigned to take up, he offered us an example of living; though nonetheless in almost all places you will find them to have been prepared to bear with an even mind whatever was wickedly heaped upon them. But do we think it was said for the sake of the word, *go with him another two*? Or did he wish three to be completed, by which number perfection is signified, so that each one may remember, when he does this, that he is fulfilling perfect righteousness by mercifully bearing the infirmities of those whom he wishes to be made whole? It can seem for this reason that he conveyed these precepts also by three examples, of which the first is, if anyone strikes you on the cheek; the second, if anyone wishes to take away your tunic; the third, if anyone compels you a mile, in which third example the single is doubled, so that the triple is completed.

If this number in this place does not, as was said, signify perfection, let it be taken thus: that in commanding, beginning as though with the more tolerable, he gradually increased, until he came down to the bearing of another double.

For first he wished the other cheek to be offered, when the right had been struck, so that you may be prepared to bear less than you bore—for whatever the right signifies is certainly dearer than what the left signifies, and he who has borne something in the dearer matter, if he also bears it in the cheaper, bears less—;

then to him who wishes to take away the tunic he bids the cloak also to be let go, which is either equal or not much more, yet not double; third, concerning the mile, to which he says two miles are to be added, he bids you bear up to another double; so signifying that, whether a wicked man wishes to be somewhat less against you than he already was, or equally, or more, it is to be borne with an even mind.

## Another's Infirmity Is to Be Borne Mercifully

**20. 62.** In these kinds of three examples I see indeed that no kind of injury has been passed over. For all things in which we suffer some wickedness are divided into two kinds; one of which is that which cannot be restored, the other that which can. But in that which cannot be restored, the solace of vengeance is wont to be sought. For what does it profit that, having been struck, you strike back? Is what was injured in the body therefore restored to wholeness? But a swollen mind desires such poultices; the sound and firm mind, however, these things do not help, but rather it judges that another's infirmity is to be borne mercifully, than that its own—which is none—is to be soothed by another's suffering.

## Nor Is That Vengeance Forbidden Here Which Avails for Correction

**20. 63.** Nor is that vengeance forbidden here which avails for correction. For it too pertains to mercy, nor does it impede that resolve by which one is prepared to bear more from the one whom he wishes to be corrected. But for exercising this vengeance no one is fit except one who has overcome, by the greatness of love, the hatred with which those are wont to burn who desire to avenge themselves. For it is not to be feared that parents should seem to hate a little son when he is whipped by them while sinning, lest he sin further.

And certainly the perfection of love itself is set before us in the imitation of God the Father[^179], when in the following it is said, *love your enemies, do good to those who hate you, and pray for those who persecute you*[^180]. And yet of him it is said through the prophet, *for whom the Lord loves he chastens, and he scourges every son whom he receives*[^181]*.* The Lord also says, *the servant who does not know his Lord's will and does things worthy of blows shall be beaten with few; but the servant who knows his Lord's will and does things worthy of blows shall be beaten with many*[^182].

Nothing else is sought, then, but that he too take vengeance to whom by the order of things the power has been given, and that he take it with that will with which a father takes it on a little son, whom on account of his age he cannot yet hate. For here a most apt example is given, by which it may sufficiently appear that sin can be avenged out of love rather than left unpunished, so that he wishes the one on whom he takes vengeance to be not wretched through punishment but blessed through correction; yet prepared, if need be, to bear with an even mind more things inflicted by the one whom he wishes to be corrected, whether he has over him the power of coercing or has it not.

## We Shall Take Note That Out of Love We Desire Correction, Not Out of Hatred Vengeance

**20. 64.** But great and holy men, who already knew very well that this death, which dissolves the soul from the body, is not to be dreaded, nonetheless, according to the mind of those who feared it, punished certain sins with death, so that both a useful fear might be struck into the living, and to those who were punished with death not death itself might do harm but the sin, which could be increased if they lived. Those did not judge rashly to whom God had granted such judgment.

Hence it is that Elijah put many to death, both by his own hand and by fire obtained from God, when many other great and divine men too, by the same spirit, in consulting for human affairs, acted not rashly.

When the disciples had given this example of Elijah to the Lord, recalling what had been done by him[^183], that he too might give them the power of asking fire from heaven to consume those who would not offer them lodging, the Lord reproved in them not the example of the holy prophet but the ignorance of taking vengeance, which was still in those untrained, noting that they desired not out of love correction but out of hatred vengeance[^184].

And so, after he had taught them what it was to love one's neighbor as oneself[^185], the Holy Spirit also having been poured in, whom, ten days being completed after his ascension, he sent from above as he had promised[^186], such acts of vengeance were not lacking, although much more rarely than in the Old Testament. For there, for the greater part, those serving were pressed down by fear, but here those who were free were chiefly nourished by love. For at the words of the apostle Peter, Ananias and his wife, as we read in the *Acts of the Apostles*, fell down lifeless, and were not raised up but buried[^187].

## What Is Written in the Noncanonical Books About the Apostle Thomas Being Unjustly Struck

**20. 65.** But if the heretics who are opposed to the Old Testament are unwilling to believe this book, let them look at the apostle Paul, whom they read with us, saying of a certain sinner whom he handed over to Satan for the destruction of the flesh, *that the spirit may be saved*[^188]. And if they are unwilling here to understand death—for perhaps it is uncertain—let them confess that some vengeance was done by the apostle through Satan, which that added phrase makes clear he did not out of hatred but out of love: *that the spirit may be saved*.

Or in those books to which they themselves attribute great authority, let them note what we say, where it is written that the apostle Thomas called down upon a certain man, by whom he had been struck with the palm, the punishment of a most atrocious death, yet commended his soul, that in the world to come he might be spared. When he had been killed by a lion, a dog brought his hand, torn from the rest of his body, to the tables at which the apostle was feasting[^189]. Although it is permitted to us not to believe this writing—for it is not in the Catholic canon—nonetheless those very men both read it and honor it as most incorrupt and most true, who, by I know not what blindness, most fiercely rage against the bodily acts of vengeance that are in the Old Testament, being altogether ignorant with what mind and in what distribution of the times they were done.

## There Is Another Kind of Injuries Which Can Be Restored to Wholeness

**20. 66.** In this kind of injuries, then, which is expiated by vengeance, this measure will be held by Christians: that, an injury having been received, hatred does not arise, but out of mercy for the weakness the mind is prepared to suffer more; nor does it neglect the correction which it can use whether by counsel or by authority or by power.

There is another kind of injuries which can be restored to wholeness, of which there are two species: one pertains to money, the other to labor. Therefore of the former the examples about the tunic and the cloak were set down, of the latter about the compulsion of one mile and two miles; because both a garment can be returned, and one whom you have helped by labor can himself, if there is need, help you in turn.

Unless perhaps it is rather to be distinguished thus: that the first thing, set down about the struck cheek, signifies all things that are so heaped on by the wicked that they cannot be restored except by vengeance; the second, set down about the garment, signifies all things that can be restored without vengeance, and therefore perhaps it was added, *who wishes to contend with you in judgment*[^190], because what is taken away through a judgment is not thought to be taken away by that force to which vengeance is owed;

but the third is made up of both, so that it can be restored both without vengeance and with vengeance. For he who violently exacts undue labor without any judgment, as does one who wickedly compels a man and forces himself to be unlawfully helped by an unwilling person, can both pay the penalty of his wickedness and return the labor, if he who put up with the wicked man demands it back.

In all these kinds of injuries, then, the Lord teaches that the mind of a Christian ought to be most patient and most merciful and most prepared to bear more.

## It Is Too Little Not to Harm, Unless You Also Bestow a Benefit as Much as You Can

**20. 67.** But since it is too little not to harm, unless you also bestow a benefit as much as you can, he consequently adds and says, *give to everyone who asks you, and from him who wishes to borrow from you do not turn away*[^191]. *To everyone who asks*, he says, not, "everything to the one who asks," so that you may give what you can give honorably and justly. For what if he asks for money by which he may try to oppress an innocent person? What if, lastly, he asks for an act of unchastity? But, not to pursue many things, which are innumerable, that surely is to be given which harms neither you nor another, as far as can be known or believed by a man.

And to him to whom you justly deny what he asks, the justice itself is to be made known, so that you do not send him away empty. So you will give to everyone who asks you, although you will not always give what he asks. And sometimes you will give something better, when you have corrected the one asking for unjust things.

## Either We Give Benevolently What We Give, or We Lend to One Who Will Repay

**20. 68.** But as for what he says, *from him who wishes to borrow from you do not turn away*, it is to be referred to the mind: *for God loves a cheerful giver*[^192]. But everyone who receives borrows, even if he himself is not going to repay. For since God restores more to the merciful, everyone who bestows a benefit lends at interest. Or if one is not pleased to take as a borrower except one who receives intending to repay, it is to be understood that the Lord embraced those two kinds of bestowing. For either we give benevolently what we give, or we lend to one who will repay. And generally men who, with the divine reward set before them, are prepared to give, become sluggish to give what is asked as a loan, as though they would receive nothing from God, since the thing that is given is repaid by the one who receives it. Rightly, then, does the divine authority exhort us to this kind of bestowing a benefit, saying, *and from him who wishes to borrow from you do not turn away*, that is, do not for this reason alienate your will from the one who asks, because both your money will be idle and God will not repay you, when the man has repaid. But when you do this from the precept of God, with him who commands these things it cannot be without fruit.

## We Are Commanded to Love Even Our Enemies and Persecutors

**21. 69.** Then he adds and says, *you have heard that it was said, "you shall love your neighbor and hate your enemy." But I say to you: love your enemies, do good to those who hate you, and pray for those who persecute you, that you may be sons of your Father who is in heaven, who bids his sun to rise upon the good and the evil, and rains upon the just and the unjust. For if you love those who love you, what reward will you have? Do not even the tax collectors do this? And if you greet only your brothers, what more do you do? Do not even the heathen do this very thing? Be therefore perfect, as your Father who is in heaven is perfect*[^193]. Without that love, by which we are commanded to love even our enemies and persecutors, who can fulfill the things that were said above? But the perfection of mercy, by which most help is given to a laboring soul, cannot be extended beyond the love of an enemy. And therefore it is closed thus: *be therefore perfect, as your Father who is in heaven is perfect*[^194]; yet in such a way that God is understood as perfect in the way that God is, and the soul as perfect in the way that a soul is.

## The Voice of the Law Is to Be Taken Not as That of One Commanding the Just but of One Permitting the Weak

**21. 70.** That there is nonetheless some step in the righteousness of the Pharisees, which pertains to the old Law, is understood from this: that many men hate even those by whom they are loved, as dissolute sons hate their parents who restrain their dissoluteness. He climbs some step, then, who loves his neighbor, although he still hates his enemy. But by the command of him who came to fulfill the Law, not to abolish it[^195], he will perfect benevolence and kindness, when he has carried it as far as the love of an enemy. For that step, although it is something, is nonetheless so small that it can be held in common even with the tax collectors. Nor is what was said in the Law, *you shall hate your enemy*[^196], to be taken as the voice of one commanding the just, but of one permitting the weak.

## A Question Arises, Because Other Scriptures Seem Contrary to the Lord's Precept

**21. 71.** Here indeed there arises a question by no means to be passed over: that to this precept of the Lord, by which he exhorts us to love our enemies, to do good to those who hate us, and to pray for those who persecute us, many other parts of the Scriptures seem, to those considering less carefully and soberly, to be contrary, because in the prophets too are found many imprecations against enemies, which are thought to be curses, such as this, *let their table become a snare*[^197], and the rest that are said there; and that, *let his children become orphans and his wife a widow*[^198]; and the other things said, whether above or below in the same Psalm, in the person of Judas through the prophet. Many other things are found everywhere in the Scriptures which seem to be contrary both to this precept of the Lord and to that apostolic one in which he says, *bless and do not curse*[^199], since of the Lord too it is written that he cursed the cities which did not receive his word[^200], and the said apostle said of a certain man, *the Lord will repay him according to his works*[^201].

## The Lord Did Not Wish the Future by Malevolence but Discerned It by Divinity

**21. 72.** But these things are easily resolved, because, as the prophet by an imprecation foretold what was to be, not by the wish of one desiring but by the spirit of one foreseeing, so too the Lord, so too the apostle; although in their words too there is not found what they desired but what they foretold. For when the Lord says, *woe to you, Capernaum*, it sounds nothing else than that some evil would befall it deservedly for its unbelief, which the Lord did not wish by malevolence but discerned by divinity.

And the apostle does not say, "may the Lord repay," but, *the Lord will repay him according to his works*, which is the word of one foretelling, not of one imprecating; just as also of that hypocrisy of the Jews, of which it has already been said, whose overthrow he discerned to be impending, he said, *the Lord will strike you, you whitewashed wall*[^202].

But the prophets are especially wont to foretell things to come under the figure of one imprecating, just as under the figure of past time they often sang of things that were to come, as is that, *why did the nations rage, and the peoples plot vain things?* For he did not say, "why will the nations rage, and the peoples plot vain things?"[^203], since he did not remember these things as already done, but foresaw them as to come.

Such also is that, *they divided my garments among themselves, and over my garment they cast lots*[^204]. For here too he did not say, "they will divide my garments among themselves, and over my garment they will cast lots." Yet no one quibbles about these words, except one who does not perceive that this variety of figures in speaking takes nothing from the truth of things, and adds very much to the dispositions of minds.

## We Confess That There Are Some Sins in Brothers Graver Than the Persecution of Enemies

**22. 73.** But those things press this question more, which the apostle John says: *if anyone knows that his brother sins a sin not unto death, he shall ask, and God will give him life, to the one who sins not unto death; but there is a sin unto death, I do not say that one should pray for that*[^205]. For he openly shows that there are certain brothers for whom we are not commanded to pray, although the Lord bids us pray even for our persecutors[^206]. Nor can this question be resolved unless we confess that there are some sins in brothers which are graver than the persecution of enemies.

But that Christian brothers are signified can be proved by many documents of the Divine Scriptures. Most manifest, however, is that which the apostle sets down thus: *for the unbelieving husband is sanctified in the wife, and the unbelieving wife is sanctified in the brother*[^207]. For he did not add, "our," but considered it manifest, since by the name *brother* he wished a Christian to be understood who had an unbelieving wife. And therefore a little after he says, *but if the unbeliever departs, let him depart; for a brother or sister is not subjected to slavery in such cases*[^208].

A sin of a brother unto death, then, I think it is, when, after the acknowledgment of God through the grace of our Lord Jesus Christ, someone attacks the brotherhood and, against the very grace by which he was reconciled to God, is driven by the firebrands of envy; but a sin not unto death, if anyone has not alienated his love from his brother, but, through some weakness of mind, has not rendered the offices owed to brotherhood.

Therefore the Lord too on the cross says, *Father, forgive them, for they do not know what they do*[^209]. For, not yet made partakers of the grace of the Holy Spirit, they had not entered the fellowship of the holy brotherhood. And blessed Stephen in the *Acts of the Apostles* prays for those by whom he is stoned[^210], because they had not yet believed in Christ, nor were they fighting against that common grace.

And the apostle Paul for this reason, I believe, does not pray for Alexander, because he was already a brother and had sinned unto death, that is, by attacking the brotherhood out of envy. But for those who had not broken off their love, but had succumbed through fear, he prays that they be forgiven. For he says thus: *Alexander the coppersmith did me many evils; the Lord will repay him according to his works. Avoid him too, for he greatly withstood our words*. Then he adds those for whom he prays, saying thus: *at my first defense no one stood by me, but all forsook me; may it not be charged against them*[^211].

## It Makes a Great Difference to What Kind of Repentance God Grants Pardon

**22. 74.** This difference of sins distinguishes Judas who betrayed[^212] from Peter who denied[^213], not because pardon is not to be granted to one who repents—lest we go against that sentence of the Lord by which he commanded that pardon must always be granted to a brother who asks[^214], so that the brother may forgive him—but because the stain of that sin is so great that it cannot undergo the humility of beseeching, even if by an evil conscience it is compelled both to acknowledge and to declare its sin. For when Judas had said, *I have sinned in that I betrayed innocent blood*, he nonetheless more easily ran in despair to the noose[^215] than in humility besought pardon.

Therefore it makes a great difference to what kind of repentance God grants pardon. For many much more quickly confess that they have sinned and are so angry with themselves that they would vehemently wish not to have sinned, yet nonetheless do not lay down their mind to humbling and crushing the heart and imploring pardon. And this disposition of mind, on account of the magnitude of the sin, must be believed to belong to them already from their condemnation.

## The Lord Says That the Sin Against the Holy Spirit Is Forgiven Neither in This Age Nor in the Age to Come

**22. 75.** And this is perhaps to sin against the Holy Spirit, that is, through malice and envy to attack brotherly love after the grace of the Holy Spirit has been received, which sin the Lord says is forgiven neither in this age nor in the age to come[^216]. Whence it can be asked whether the Jews sinned against the Holy Spirit when they said that the Lord cast out demons by Beelzebub, the prince of demons: whether we should take this as said against the Lord himself, because he says of himself in another place, *if they have called the master of the house Beelzebub, how much more those of his household?*[^217] Or whether, since they had said it out of great envy, ungrateful for benefits so present, although they were not yet Christians, they are nonetheless to be believed to have sinned against the Holy Spirit on account of the very magnitude of the envy.

For this is not gathered from the Lord's words. For although in the same place he said, *but whoever shall speak a wicked word against the Son of man, it shall be forgiven him; but whoever shall speak against the Holy Spirit, it shall not be forgiven him, neither in this age nor in the age to come*[^218], nonetheless it can seem that he warned them for this, that they should come to grace and, after grace is received, not sin as they now sinned. For now they have spoken a wicked word against the Son of man, and it can be forgiven them, if they are converted and believe in him and receive the Holy Spirit; which having been received, if they should wish to envy the brotherhood and attack the grace they received, it is not forgiven them, neither in this age nor in the age to come. For if he held them so condemned that no hope remained for them, he would not have judged them still to be warned, when he added, saying, *either make the tree good and its fruit good, or make the tree bad and its fruit bad*[^219].

## But For Whom You Do Not Pray, Neither Do You Pray Against Him

**22. 76.** And so let it be taken thus, that enemies are to be loved, and good is to be done to those who hate us, and we are to pray for those who persecute us, in such a way that, for certain sins even of brothers, it is understood that it is not commanded that we pray, lest through our ignorance the Divine Scripture should seem—which cannot be—to disagree with itself. But whether, as for certain people one is not to pray, so also one is to pray against some, has already appeared to some sufficiently. For it was said generally, *bless and do not curse*[^220]; and that, *rendering to no one evil for evil*[^221]. But for whom you do not pray, neither do you pray against him. For you can see his certain punishment and his utterly despaired-of salvation, and you do not for this reason fail to pray for him because you hate him, but because you perceive that you can profit nothing, and you do not wish your prayer to be repelled by the most just judge.

But what do we make of those against whom we find prayer made by the saints, not that they might be corrected—for in that way prayer is rather made for them—but for that final condemnation; not as against the Lord's betrayer through the prophet[^222]—for that, as was said, was a prediction of things to come, not a wish for punishment—nor as by the apostle against Alexander[^223]—for of that too enough has now been said—but as in the *Apocalypse* of John we read the martyrs praying that they be avenged[^224], when that first martyr prayed that his stoners be forgiven[^225]?

## This Is the Sincere Vengeance of the Martyrs, Full of Justice and Mercy: That the Reign of Sin Be Overthrown

**22. 77.** But one ought not to be moved by this. For who would dare to affirm, when those holy white-robed ones asked that they be avenged, whether they asked against the men themselves or against the reign of sin? For this is the sincere vengeance of the martyrs, full of justice and mercy: that the reign of sin be overthrown, by whose reigning they suffered so much. To the overthrow of which the apostle strives, saying, *let not sin, therefore, reign in your mortal body*[^226]. But the reign of sin is destroyed and overthrown partly by the correction of men, so that the flesh is subjected to the spirit, partly by the condemnation of those who persevere in sin, so that they are so ordered that they cannot be troublesome to the just reigning with Christ.

Look at the apostle Paul! Does he not seem to you to avenge in himself the martyr Stephen, when he says, *I do not so fight as one beating the air, but I chastise my body and bring it into subjection*[^227]? For he certainly cast down and weakened and brought into ordered subjection in himself that from which he had persecuted Stephen and the other Christians.

Who, then, convicts the holy martyrs of not having begged such vengeance of their own from the Lord, since they could even gladly have desired the end of this world, in which they endured such ruin, for their own vengeance? And those who pray for this both pray for their enemies who are curable, and do not pray against those who have willed to be incurable, because God too, in punishing them, is not a malevolent torturer but a most just orderer. Without any doubt, then, let us love our enemies, do good to those who hate us, and pray for those who persecute us.

## We Are Made Sons, Therefore, by Spiritual Regeneration and Are Adopted into the Kingdom of God

**23. 78.** But what is consequently set down, *that you may be sons of your Father who is in heaven*[^228], is to be understood by that rule by which John too says, *he gave them power to become sons of God*[^229]. For there is one who is naturally the Son, who knows not how to sin at all; but we, the power having been received, are made sons, insofar as we fulfill the things that are commanded by him. Whence the apostolic teaching calls "adoption" that by which we are called to the eternal inheritance, so that we can be coheirs of Christ[^230]. We are made sons, therefore, by spiritual regeneration and are adopted into the kingdom of God, not as strangers but as made and created—that is, founded—by him, so that there is one benefit by which he made us to be through his omnipotence, when before we were nothing, and another by which he adopted us, so that with him, as sons, we might enjoy eternal life according to our participation. And so he does not say, "do these things, because you are sons," but, "do these things, that *you may be sons*."

## That Spiritual Sun Rises Only on the Good, and That Spiritual Rain Waters Only the Good

**23. 79.** But when he calls us to this through the Only-begotten himself, he calls us to his own likeness. For he, as he consequently says, *makes his sun rise upon the good and the evil and rains upon the just and the unjust*[^231]. Whether you take his sun not as that one visible to fleshly eyes, but as that wisdom of which it is said, *for it is the brightness of eternal light*; of which it is likewise said, *the sun of righteousness has risen for me*[^232]; and again, *but for you who fear the name of the Lord the sun of righteousness shall rise*[^233], so that you take the rain too as the watering of the teaching of truth, because Christ appeared to the good and the evil and was preached to the good and the evil.

Or whether you prefer to take it as that sun, displayed to the bodily eyes not only of men but also of cattle, and that rain by which are produced the fruits that have been given for the refreshment of the body. Which I judge to be more probably understood thus: that that spiritual sun rises only on the good and holy, because this very thing is what the wicked lament in that book which is inscribed the *Wisdom* of Solomon, *and the sun did not rise for us*[^234]; and that that spiritual rain waters only the good, because the evil were signified by the vineyard of which it was said, *I will command my clouds not to rain upon it*[^235].

But whether you understand the one or the other, it comes about by the great goodness of God, which is commanded to be imitated by us, if we wish to be sons of God. For how great a solace of this life this visible light and bodily rain bring, who is so ungrateful as not to feel? Which solace we see shown in common both to the just in this life and to sinners. But he does not say, *who makes the sun rise upon the good and the evil*, but added, *his*, that is, the one which he himself made and established, and from no one took anything in order to make it, as in *Genesis* is written of all the luminaries[^236]—he who can properly call his own all the things which he created from nothing, so that from this we might be admonished with how great liberality we ought, by his precept, to bestow on our enemies the things which we did not create but received from his gifts.

## Blessed Are Those Who Are Fully and Perfectly Merciful, for They Shall Obtain Mercy

**23. 80.** But who can be even prepared to bear injuries from the weak, so far as it profits their welfare, and to prefer to endure more of another's iniquity than to return what he has endured; to give to everyone who asks something of him, either what he asks, if it is had and if it can rightly be given, or good counsel or a benevolent mind, and not to turn away from one who wishes to borrow; to love enemies, to do good to those who hate him, to pray for those who persecute him? Who, then, does these things except one who is fully and perfectly merciful? By which one counsel wretchedness is avoided, with the help of him who says, *I desire mercy more than sacrifice*[^237]. *Blessed*, therefore, *are the merciful, for they shall obtain mercy*[^238]. But I think it now fitting that here the reader, wearied by so long a volume, take a little breath, and refresh himself for the rest to be considered in another book.
