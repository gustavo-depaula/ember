# Revisions I

## Chapter 19 (18): The Lord's Sermon on the Mount, in Two Books

**1.** During the same period I wrote two volumes *On the Lord's Sermon on the Mount* according to Matthew. In the first of them, on account of what is written, *blessed are the peacemakers, for they shall be called sons of God*[^1]*,* I said that *wisdom befits the peacemakers, in whom all things are now set in order and no movement is in rebellion against reason, but all things obey the spirit of a man, since he himself also obeys God*[^2]. This rightly raises the question of how I could have said it. For it cannot happen to anyone in this life that the law warring against *the law of the mind*[^3] should be wholly absent from the members, since even if the spirit of a man so resisted it that he slipped into no consent to it, it would nonetheless still war against him. So what was said, that *there is no movement in rebellion against reason*, can rightly be taken of those who are now, as peacemakers, by subduing the desires of the flesh, working toward that fullest peace which is to be reached one day.

**2.** Accordingly, in another place, when, repeating the same Gospel sentence, I had said, *blessed are the peacemakers, for they shall be called sons of God,* I added the words, *and these things can indeed be fulfilled in this life, as we believe they were fulfilled in the apostles*[^4]*;* this must be taken not as though we suppose that, in the apostles while they lived here, no movement of the flesh warred against the spirit, but rather that these things can be fulfilled here only so far as we believe they were fulfilled in the apostles, that is, in that measure of human perfection which is possible in this life.

For it was not said: these things can be fulfilled in this life, for we believe they were fulfilled in the apostles, but it was said, *as we believe they were fulfilled in the apostles,* so that they may be fulfilled just as they were fulfilled in them, that is, with a certain perfection of which this life is capable, not as those things are to be fulfilled in that fullest peace which we hope for, when it will be said, *O death, where is your strife?*[^5]

**3.** In another place, the testimony I inserted, *for God does not give the Spirit by measure*[^6]*,* I had not yet understood is more truly taken as said properly of Christ. For if the Spirit were not given to other men by measure, Elisha would not have asked for double what was in Elijah[^7].

Likewise, when I was expounding what is written, *not one iota or one tittle shall pass from the Law until all is accomplished*[^8]*,* I said that *nothing else can be understood by it except a forceful expression of perfection*[^9]*.* Here it is rightly asked whether this perfection can be understood in such a way that it remains true that no one still using the free exercise of his will lives here without sin.

For by whom can the Law be fulfilled down to a single tittle, except by him through whom all the divine commandments are accomplished? But among those same commandments is also the one we are bidden to say, *forgive us our debts as we also forgive our debtors*[^10], a prayer that the whole Church says until the end of the age. Therefore all the commandments are reckoned accomplished, since whatever is not done is forgiven.

**4.** As for what the Lord says, *whoever shall loose one of these least commandments and shall so teach*[^11], and so on, down to the place where he says, *unless your righteousness shall abound more than that of the scribes and Pharisees, you shall not enter the kingdom of heaven*[^12]; I expounded this much better and more fittingly in other, later sermons of mine, which it would take too long to repeat here. But the sense reached there comes to this, that the righteousness of those who both say and do is greater than that of the scribes and Pharisees. For of the scribes and Pharisees the Lord himself says elsewhere, *for they say and do not do*[^13].

We also understood better afterward[^14] what is written, *whoever is angry with his brother*[^15]. For the Greek manuscripts do not have *without cause,* as it is placed here, although the sense is the same. For we said that one must consider what it is to be angry with one's brother, since he who is angry at his brother's sin is not angry with his brother. He, then, who is angry with his brother and not with the sin is angry without cause.

**5.** Likewise, what I said, that *this is to be understood of father and mother and the other ties of blood, so that in them we hate what the human race has been allotted by being born and dying*[^16]*,* sounds as though these relationships would not exist if no one died, no sin of human nature having gone before—a sense I have already rejected above[^17]. For there would surely be kinships and affinities even if, with no original sin existing, the human race grew and multiplied without death. And on this account the question why the Lord commanded that enemies be loved[^18], while in another place he commands that even parents and children be held in hatred[^19], must be resolved otherwise—not as it is resolved here, but as we have often resolved it later[^20], that is, so that we love our enemies to win them for the kingdom of God, and hate in our kin whatever hinders them from the kingdom of God.

**6.** Likewise, on the precept by which a wife is forbidden to be divorced except for fornication[^21], I argued here very scrupulously[^22]. But what fornication the Lord wishes to be understood, on account of which it is permitted to divorce a wife—whether the one condemned in acts of unchastity, or the one of which it is said, *you have destroyed everyone who fornicates away from you*[^23], in which the former too is certainly included (for he indeed fornicates away from the Lord who, *taking the members of Christ, makes them members of a harlot*[^24])—must be pondered and inquired into again and again.

And in a matter so great and so difficult to discern, I do not wish the reader to think that this discussion of ours should suffice for him; let him also read other things, whether ours that were written later, or the better-considered and better-treated works of others[^25]; or let him, if he can, examine with a more watchful and more discerning mind the things that may here rightly raise questions. Not that every sin is fornication (for God does not destroy every sinner, since he daily hears his saints saying, *forgive us our debts*[^26], while he destroys *everyone who fornicates away from him*), but how this fornication is to be understood and delimited, and whether on account of it too it is permitted to divorce a wife, is a most hidden question.

That it is permitted on account of the fornication committed in acts of unchastity, however, there is no question. And where I said, *this was permitted, not commanded,* I did not attend to another Scripture saying, *he who keeps an adulteress is foolish and impious*[^27]. Yet I would not say that that woman was to be reckoned an adulteress even after she heard from the Lord, *neither will I condemn you; go, and now sin no more*[^28], if she heard this obediently.

**7.** In another place, the sin of a brother unto death, of which the apostle John says, *I do not say that one should pray for that*[^29]*,* I defined by saying, *I think the sin of a brother unto death is when, after the acknowledgment of God through the grace of our Lord Jesus Christ, someone attacks the brotherhood and, against the very grace by which he was reconciled to God, is driven by the firebrands of envy*[^30]*.* This indeed I did not affirm, since I said that I thought it; but still it should have been added: "if he should end this life in such a wicked perversity of mind," since one must by no means despair of any most wicked person while he is established in this life, nor is prayer imprudently made for one of whom there is no despair.

**8.** Likewise in the second book I said, *it will be permitted to no one to be ignorant of the kingdom of God, when his Only-begotten shall come from heaven not only intelligibly but also visibly in the Lordly man, "to judge the living and the dead*[^31]*."* But I do not see whether he is rightly called the Lordly man who is *the mediator of God and men, the man Christ Jesus*[^32], since he is certainly Lord. Yet who in his holy household cannot be called a Lordly man? And that I said this I had read among certain Catholic treatise-writers of the Divine Oracles[^33]. But wherever I said this, I wish I had not said it[^34]. For afterward I saw that it should not be said, although it can be defended on some ground.

Likewise, what I said, *for hardly anyone's conscience can hate God*[^35]*,* I do not see should have been said; for there are many of whom it is written, *the pride of those who hate you*[^36].

**9.** In another place, in what I said, *that the Lord said, "sufficient for the day is its own evil," because the very necessity of taking food will press upon us, which necessity I think is therefore called evil because it is penal for us, for it belongs to this frailty which we have deserved by sinning*[^37]*,* I did not attend to the fact that bodily nourishment was given to the first human beings too in paradise, before they deserved this penalty of death by sinning. For they were immortal in a body not yet spiritual but animal, in such a way that nonetheless in that kind of immortality they made use of bodily nourishment.

Likewise, what I said, *that God chose for himself a "glorious Church not having spot or wrinkle"*[^38]*,* I did not say because it is now in every part already such, though there would be no doubt that it was chosen to this end, that it may be such when *Christ, who is* its *life, shall appear*[^39], for *then* it too will appear with him in glory, on account of which glory it is called a *glorious Church.*

Likewise, as for what the Lord says, *ask and you shall receive, seek and you shall find, knock and it shall be opened to you*[^40], I thought it worth expounding laboriously how these three differ from one another[^41]; but far better are they all referred to the most insistent petition. For he shows this where he concluded them all with the same word, saying, *how much more will your Father who is in heaven give good things to those who ask him*[^42]—for he did not say, "to those who ask and seek and knock."

This work begins thus: *the sermon which the Lord spoke.*
