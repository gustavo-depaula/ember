# Book II: On the Beatitudes by Which God Is Seen

## On Purity of Heart in Prayer, Almsgiving, — Fasting, and in Good Works (1, 1 - 22, 76)

## Purity of Heart

**1. 1.** Mercy, to the treatment of which the first book received its limit, is followed by the cleansing of the heart, whence this one takes its beginning. But the cleansing of the heart is, as it were, of the eye by which God is seen; and there ought to be as great a care to have it single as the dignity of that thing demands which can be beheld by such an eye. But for this eye, cleansed in great part, it is difficult for some filth not to creep in from those things which are wont to accompany our good actions, such as human praise.

For if indeed it is pernicious not to live rightly, yet to live rightly and not to wish to be praised—what else is this than to be an enemy to human affairs, which are certainly the more wretched the less the right life of men is pleasing? If, then, among those with whom you live they do not praise you living rightly, they are in error;

but if they praise you, you are in peril, unless you have a heart so single and pure that you do not do the things you do rightly for the sake of the praises of men, and rejoice more in those who rightly praise—to whom what is good is pleasing—than in yourself, except that you live rightly even if no one were to praise you; and you understand that your praise is then useful to those who praise, if they honor not you yourself in your good life but God, whose most holy temple is whoever lives well, so that that may be fulfilled which David says, *in the Lord my soul shall be praised; let the meek hear and rejoice*[^1]*.*

It pertains, therefore, to a pure eye not to look, in doing rightly, at the praises of men, and to refer to them what you do rightly, that is, to do something rightly for the purpose of pleasing men. For thus it will even be pleasing to feign good, if nothing is attended to except that a man may praise—who, since he cannot see the heart, can also praise false things. And those who do this, that is, who feign goodness, are of a double heart. He, then, does not have a single heart, that is, a pure heart, except one who transcends human praises and looks to him alone, when he lives rightly, and strives to please him who alone is the inspector of conscience. Whatever proceeds from the purity of such a conscience is the more praiseworthy the less it desires human praises.

## Where to Place the Goal of Our Resolve

**1. 2.** *Beware, therefore*, he says, *of doing your righteousness before men, to be seen by them*; that is: Beware of living justly with this mind, and of setting your good there, that men may see you! *Otherwise you will not have a reward with your Father who is in heaven*[^2]; not if you are seen by men, but if you live justly for this reason, that you may be seen by men. For where then will be what was said at the beginning of this sermon, *you are the light of the world*? *A city set upon a mountain cannot be hidden. Nor do they light a lamp and put it under a bushel, but upon a lampstand, that it may give light to all who are in the house. So let your light shine before men, that they may see your good deeds*. But he did not set the goal there, for he added, *and glorify your Father who is in heaven*[^3]. But here, because he censures this—if the goal of right deeds is there, that is, if we do right for this reason, that we may only be seen by men—after he said, *beware of doing your righteousness before men, to be seen by them*[^4], he added nothing. In which it appears that he did not forbid this, that what is right be done before men, but that what is right be done before men for this reason, that we may be seen by them, that is, that we look to this and set the goal of our resolve there.

## Paul Said That He Did Not Please Men

**1. 3.** For the apostle too says, *if I were still pleasing men, I would not be a servant of Christ*[^5], although in another place he says, *please all men in all things, just as I too please all men in all things*[^6]. Those who do not understand this think it contrary, since he said that he did not please men, because he did not do right for this reason, that he might please men, but that he might please God,

to whose love he wished to turn the hearts of men by that very thing in which he pleased men. And so he both rightly said that he did not please men, because in that very thing he looked to pleasing God, and rightly commanded that men be pleased, not that this be sought as the reward of right deeds; but because he could not please God who did not offer himself as a model for imitation to those whom he wished to be saved; but no one can in any way imitate one who has not pleased him.

As, then, he would not speak absurdly who said, "in this work by which I seek a ship I do not seek a ship but my homeland," so the apostle would fittingly say, "in this work by which I please men I please not men but God, because I do not seek this, but refer it to this, that those whom I wish to be saved may imitate me." As he says of the offering that is made to the saints, *not that I seek the gift, but I seek the fruit*[^7]; that is, "as for my seeking your gift, I do not seek this but your fruit." For by this sign it could appear how much they had advanced toward God, since they did gladly what was sought from them not for the sake of joy over the gifts but for the sake of the communion of love.

## Let Us Not Seek Human Praise

**1. 4.** And yet, even when he adds and says, *otherwise you will not have a reward with your Father who is in heaven*[^8], he shows nothing else than that we ought to beware of seeking human praise as the reward of our works, that is, of thinking ourselves to be made blessed by them.

## Whoever Wishes to Seem What He Is Not Is a Hypocrite

**2. 5.** *When, therefore, you give alms*, he says, *do not sound a trumpet before you, as the hypocrites do in the synagogues and in the streets, that they may be glorified by men*[^9]. Do not, he says, wish to become known as the hypocrites do. But it is manifest that hypocrites do not carry in the heart that also which they hold out to the eyes of men. For hypocrites are dissemblers, like those who voice the persons of others, as in stage plays. For he who acts the parts of Agamemnon in a tragedy, for example, or of some other person belonging to the history or fable that is being acted, is not truly himself, but feigns him and is called a hypocrite.

So in the Church, or in all human life, whoever wishes to seem what he is not is a hypocrite. For he feigns a just man and does not exhibit one, because he places the whole fruit in the praise of men, which even dissemblers can receive, while they deceive those to whom they seem good and by whom they are praised.

But such men receive from God, the inspector of the heart, no reward except the punishment of their deceit. *But from men*, he says, *they have received their reward*[^10]. And most rightly will it be said to them, *depart from me, you workers of deceit*[^11]; for you had my name, but you did not do my works. They, then, have received their reward who give alms for no other reason than to be glorified by men; not if they are glorified by men, but if they do it for this reason, that they may be glorified, as was treated above. For human praise ought not to be sought by one doing right, but ought to follow one doing right, so that those may profit who can also imitate what they praise, not that one here should think that those whom he praises profit him anything.

## An Enemy Ought Not to Know When We Give Alms

**2. 6.** *But when you give alms, let not your left hand know what your right hand does*[^12]. If you understand the left hand to mean unbelievers, it will seem that there is no fault in wishing to please the faithful, since nonetheless we are no less forbidden to set the fruit and goal of a good work in the praise of any men whatsoever. But as for what pertains to this, that those to whom your right deeds are pleasing may imitate you, it is to be shown not only to the faithful but also to unbelievers, so that, in praising our good works, they may honor God and come to salvation.

But if you think the left hand to be an enemy, so that your enemy may not know when you give alms, why did the Lord himself mercifully heal men with the hostile Jews standing around? Why did the apostle Peter, having healed the man whom he pitied, crippled, at the Beautiful Gate, endure even the wraths of enemies against himself and against the other disciples of Christ[^13]? Then, if an enemy ought not to know when we give alms, how shall we do it with the enemy himself, so that we may fulfill that precept, *if your enemy is hungry, feed him; if he is thirsty, give him drink*[^14]?

## The Absurd Opinion of Those Who Say That by the Name of the Left Hand a Wife Is Signified

**2. 7.** There is wont to be a third opinion of the carnal, so absurd and laughable that I would not mention it, had I not found by experience that not a few are held in that error who say that by the name of the *left hand* a wife is signified; so that, since in household matters women are wont to be more tenacious of money, it may be hidden from them, on account of domestic quarrels, when their husbands mercifully spend something on the needy. As though indeed only men were Christians, and this precept were not given also to women. To what left hand, then, is a woman bidden to hide her work of mercy? Or will the husband too be the left hand of the woman? Which is most absurdly said.

Or if anyone thinks they are mutually left hands to each other, if something of the household property is paid out by one against the will of the other, such a marriage will not be Christian. But it must be that whoever of them wishes to give alms from the precept of God, whomever he has as an opponent, that opponent is an enemy to the precept of God and therefore is to be reckoned among unbelievers. But it is commanded concerning such, that by his good conversation and conduct a believing husband may win his wife, or a believing woman her husband[^15]. Therefore they ought not to hide their good works from each other, by which they are to be invited by one another, so that one may be able to invite the other to the communion of the Christian faith.

Nor are thefts to be committed in order to earn God's favor. But if something is to be hidden, as long as another's weakness cannot bear it with an even mind—which nonetheless is done neither unjustly nor unlawfully—it nonetheless easily appears, from the consideration of the whole passage, that the left hand is not now signified by this, in which passage it will at the same time be found what he calls the left hand.

## The Left Hand Seems to Signify the Very Delight in Praise

**2. 8.** *Beware*, he says, *of doing your righteousness before men, to be seen by them; otherwise you will not have a reward with your Father who is in heaven*[^16]. Here he named righteousness in general, then pursues it in particular. For the work that is done through almsgiving is some part of righteousness, and therefore he connects it by saying, *when, therefore, you give alms, do not sound a trumpet before you, as the hypocrites do in the synagogues and in the streets, that they may be glorified by men*[^17]. This looks back to what he said above, *beware of doing your righteousness before men, to be seen by them*. But what follows, *amen I say to you, they have received their reward*, looks back to what he set down above, *otherwise you will not have a reward with your Father who is in heaven*.

Then there follows, *but when you give alms*. When he says, *but you*, what else does he say than, "not as they do"? What, then, does he bid me? *But when you*, he says, *give alms, let not your left hand know what your right hand does*[^18]. So they do it in such a way that their left hand knows what their right hand does. What, then, was censured in them, this you are forbidden to do. But in them this was censured, that they do it in such a way as to seek the praises of men. Therefore nothing more consistently seems to signify the left hand than the very delight in praise. But the right hand signifies the intention of fulfilling the divine precepts. And so, when the appetite for human praise mingles itself with the conscience of one giving alms, the left hand becomes conscious of the work of the right. *Let not*, therefore, *your left hand know what your right hand does*, that is, "let not the appetite for human praise mingle itself with your conscience, when in giving alms you strive to fulfill the divine precept."

## Let Your Almsgiving Be in Secret

**2. 9.** *That your almsgiving may be in secret*[^19]. What is *in secret* except in the good conscience itself, which cannot be shown to human eyes nor opened by words? Since indeed many lie about many things. Therefore, if the right hand acts within, in secret, to the left hand pertain all the outward things that are visible and temporal. Let your almsgiving, then, be in the conscience itself, where many give alms with a good will, even if they do not have money or anything else that is to be bestowed on the needy. But many do it outwardly and do not do it within, who, whether from ambition or for the sake of some temporal thing, wish to seem merciful, in whom the left hand alone is to be reckoned to work. Likewise others hold, as it were, a middle place between the two, so that they both give alms with the intention that is toward God, and yet there inserts itself into this excellent will some desire also of praise or of some frail and temporal thing. But our Lord much more vehemently forbids the left hand alone to work in us, when he forbids it even to mingle with the works of the right hand, namely, so that not only do we beware of giving alms from the desire of temporal things alone, but neither do we so look to God in this work that the appetite for outward conveniences mingles or joins itself. For the matter concerns the cleansing of the heart, which, unless it is single, will not be pure. But how will it be single if it serves two masters[^20], and does not purify its gaze by the one intention of eternal things, but clouds it with the love also of mortal and frail things? *Let*, therefore, *your almsgiving be in secret, and your Father, who sees in secret, will repay you*[^21]. Most rightly indeed and most truly. For if you expect a reward from him who alone is the inspector of conscience, let the conscience itself suffice you for earning the reward! Many Latin copies have it thus: *and your Father, who sees in secret, will repay you openly*. But because in the Greek, which are earlier, we do not find *openly*, we did not think anything was to be discussed from this.

## Do Not Act in Your Prayers So as to Be Seen by Men

**3. 10.** *And when you pray*, he says, *you shall not be as the hypocrites, who love to stand in the synagogues and at the corners of the streets to pray, that they may be seen by men*[^22]. Here too it is not wrong to be seen by men, but to do these things for this reason, that you may be seen by men. And the same things are said so often to no purpose, since there is now one rule to be kept, by which it is recognized that it is not to be dreaded or fled if men know these things, but if they are done with this mind, that the fruit of pleasing men be sought in them. The Lord himself keeps the same words, when he likewise adds, *amen I say to you, they have received their reward*, hereby showing that he forbids this, that the reward be sought in which fools rejoice when they are praised by men.

## The Door Must Be Shut, So That Spiritual Prayer May Be Directed to the Father

**3. 11.** *But you, when you pray*, he says, *enter into your inner chambers*[^23]. What are those inner chambers but the very hearts, which are also signified in the *Psalm*, where it is said, *the things you say in your hearts, be pricked with remorse also in your chambers*[^24]? *And shutting the doors, pray*, he says, *to your Father in secret*. It is too little to enter into the inner chambers, if the door lies open to the importunate, through which door the things that are outside wickedly thrust themselves in and seek out our inner parts. But we have said that all temporal and visible things are *outside*, which through the door, that is, through the carnal sense, penetrate our thoughts and, with a crowd of empty phantasms, clamor against those praying. The door, then, must be shut, that is, the carnal sense must be resisted, so that spiritual prayer may be directed to the Father, which is made in the inmost parts of the heart, where the Father is prayed to in secret.

*And your Father*, he says, *who sees in secret, will repay you*[^25]. And this had to be concluded with such a clause. For now he does not admonish *that* we pray, but *how* we pray; nor above *that* we give alms, but with what mind we do it[^26], since he gives a precept about the cleansing of the heart, which nothing cleanses except one and single intention toward eternal life, with the sole and pure love of wisdom.

## It Is the Way of the Gentiles to Think Themselves Heard for Their Much Speaking

**3. 12.** *But when you pray, do not*, he says, *be of many words, as the heathen are, for they think that in their much speaking they will be heard*[^27]. As it is the way of hypocrites to offer themselves to be watched in prayer, whose fruit is to please men, so it is the way of the heathen, that is, of the gentiles, to think themselves heard for their much speaking. And indeed all much speaking comes from the gentiles, who give effort rather to exercising the tongue than to cleansing the mind. And this kind of trifling zeal they try to transfer even to the bending of God by prayer, supposing that, like a human judge, he is brought by words to a verdict. *Do not, therefore, be like them*, says the one true master; *for your Father knows what is necessary for you before you ask him*[^28].

For if many words are brought forth for this, that one ignorant may be instructed and taught, what need is there of them for the knower of all things, to whom all things that are, by the very fact that they are, speak and show themselves as made? And the things that are to come are not hidden from his skill and wisdom, in which both the things that have passed and all the things that are to pass are present and not passing.

## We Ought to Deal with God in Pure Love and with Single Affection

**3. 13.** But since, although few, he too is going to say words, by which he may teach us to pray, it can be asked why there is need even of these few words for him who knows all things before they are done, and knows, as was said, what is necessary for us before we ask him. Here it is first answered that we ought to deal with God not by words, that we may obtain what we wish, but by the things we bear in mind and by the intention of thought, with pure love and single affection; but that our Lord taught us the things themselves by words, so that, committed to memory, we may recall them at the time of praying.

## God Is Always Ready to Give; but We Are Not Always Ready to Receive

**3. 14.** But again it can be asked—whether one is to pray by things or by words—what need there is of prayer itself, if God already knows what is necessary for us, except that the very intention of prayer makes our heart serene and cleanses it, and makes it more capable of receiving the divine gifts, which are poured into us spiritually. For God does not hear us through the ambition of our prayers, who is always ready to give us his light, not visible but intelligible and spiritual; but we are not always ready to receive, since we are inclined toward other things and darkened by the desire of temporal things.

There comes about, then, in prayer a turning of the heart to him who is always ready to give, if we receive what he has given, and in that turning a cleansing of the inner eye, when the things that were temporally desired are shut out, so that the gaze of the single heart can bear the single light, divinely shining without any setting or change, and not only bear it but also remain in it, not only without trouble but even with ineffable joy, by which the truly and sincerely blessed life is perfected.

## What the Lord Commanded Us to Pray, Through Whom We Both Learn What to Pray and Obtain What We Pray For

**4. 15.** But now it must be considered what he commanded us to pray, through whom we both learn what to pray and obtain what we pray for. *So, therefore, pray*, he says: "*Our Father who are in heaven, hallowed be your name; your kingdom come; your will be done, as in heaven, so on earth. Give us this day our daily bread; and forgive us our debts, as we also forgive our debtors; and lead us not into temptation, but deliver us from evil*[^29]."

Since in every prayer the benevolence of him whom we beseech is to be won, and then what we beseech is to be said, benevolence is wont to be won by the praise of him to whom the prayer is directed, and this is wont to be placed at the beginning of the prayer. In which our Lord bade us say nothing else than, *Our Father who are in heaven*. Many things have been said in praise of God, which anyone will be able to consider, variously and widely diffused throughout all the Holy Scriptures, when he reads; yet nowhere is it found commanded to the people Israel that they should say, *Our Father*, or pray to God the Father; but the Lord was made known to them as to servants, that is, to those still living according to the flesh.

But I say this with reference to when they received the commands of the Law, which they were bidden to observe; for the prophets often show that the same Lord God could also have been their Father, if they had not strayed from his commands, as is that, *I have begotten sons and exalted them; but they have spurned me*[^30]; and that, *I said, "you are gods and sons of the Most High; but you shall die like men, and shall fall like one of the princes*[^31]"; and that, *if I am Lord, where is my fear? And if I am Father, where is my honor?*[^32], and very many others, where the Jews are reproached because by sinning they were unwilling to be sons—except those things that are said in prophecy of the future Christian people, that it was to have God as Father, according to that Gospel saying, *he gave them power to become sons of God*[^33]. But the apostle Paul says, *as long as the heir is a little one, he differs in nothing from a servant*[^34]; and he records that we have received the spirit of adoption, *in which we cry, "Abba, Father*[^35]."

## We Have Received a Great Gift, That We Are Allowed to Say "Our Father" to God

**4. 16.** And since the fact that we are called to the eternal inheritance, so that we may be coheirs of Christ and come into the adoption of sons[^36], is not of our merits but of the grace of God, we place this very grace at the beginning of the prayer, when we say, *Our Father*. By which name both love is stirred up—for what ought to be dearer to sons than a father?—and a suppliant affection, when men say to God, *Our Father*, and a certain presumption of obtaining the things we are about to ask, since, before we asked anything, we have received so great a gift, that we are allowed to say, *Our Father*, to God. For what would he not now give to sons who ask, since he has already given this very thing before, that they should be sons? Lastly, how great a care touches the mind, that he who says, *Our Father*, may not be unworthy of so great a Father? For if some commoner were permitted by a senator of advanced age to call him father, he would without doubt tremble and would not easily dare it, considering the lowliness of his birth and the lack of his means and the cheapness of his plebeian person; how much more, then, must one tremble to call God Father, if there is so great a defilement and so great filth in one's conduct that God much more justly expels it from his union than that senator the poverty of any beggar—since indeed that man despises in the beggar that to which he himself can come down through the frailty of human affairs, but God never falls into filthy conduct? And thanks to his mercy, who demands this of us, that he be our Father, which can be procured at no cost but by good will alone! Here too the rich, or those noble by birth according to the world, are admonished, when they have become Christians, not to be proud against the poor and the ignoble, since together they say to God, *Our Father*, which they cannot truly and piously say unless they recognize that they are brothers.

## It Is Rightly Said, "Who Are in Heaven": Who Are in the Saints

**5. 17.** Let the new people, then, called to the eternal inheritance, use the voice of the New Testament and say, *Our Father who are in heaven*[^37], that is, in the saints and the just; for God is not contained by spaces of places. But the heavens are indeed the most excellent bodies of the world, yet bodies, which cannot be except in a place. But if God's place is believed to be in the heavens, as in the upper parts of the world, the birds are of better merit, whose life is nearer to God. But it is not written: The Lord is near to lofty men or to those who dwell on the mountains, but it is written, *the Lord is near to those who are crushed in heart*[^38], which pertains more to humility. But as the sinner was called "earth," when it was said to him, *you are earth, and into the earth you shall go*[^39], so, on the contrary, the just man can be called "heaven." For to the just it is said, *for the temple of God is holy, which you are*[^40]. Therefore, if God dwells in his temple, and the saints are his temple, it is rightly said, *who are in heaven*: who are in the saints.

And this comparison is most apt, so that there may seem to be as great a difference spiritually between the just and sinners as there is bodily between heaven and earth.

## God Is Everywhere Present, Not by Spaces of Places, but by the Power of His Majesty

**5. 18.** For the signifying of which thing, when we stand for prayer, we turn toward the east, whence the sky rises; not as though God dwelt there, as if he had deserted the other parts of the world, who is everywhere present, not by spaces of places, but by the power of his majesty; but so that the mind may be admonished to turn to a more excellent nature, that is, to God, when his own body, which is earthly, is turned toward a more excellent body, that is, toward a celestial body. It is fitting also to the grades of religion, and is of much advantage, that in the senses of all, both little ones and great, there should be a good opinion of God.

And therefore those who are still given to visible beauties and cannot conceive anything incorporeal, since they must prefer heaven to earth, their opinion is more tolerable if they believe God, whom they still conceive bodily, to be in heaven rather than on earth; so that, when at some time they have recognized that the dignity of the soul surpasses even a celestial body, they may seek him in the soul rather than in a body, even a celestial one, and, when they have recognized how great a distance there is between the souls of sinners and of the just, just as they did not dare, when they still thought carnally[^41], to place him on earth but in heaven, so afterward, with a better faith or understanding, they may seek him more in the souls of the just than in those of sinners.

Rightly, then, is what was said, *Our Father who are in heaven*[^42], understood to be said to be in the hearts of the just, as in his holy temple. At the same time also, so that he who prays may wish him whom he invokes to dwell in himself too; and when he aims at this, let him hold to righteousness, by which gift God is invited to inhabit the mind.

## Hallowed Be Your Name

**5. 19.** Now let us see what things are to be asked! For it has been said who it is that is asked and where he dwells. But the first of all the things that are asked is this: *hallowed be your name*[^43]. Which is not asked as though God's name were not holy, but that it may be held holy by men, that is, that God may so become known to them that they think nothing holier, which they more fear to offend. For not because it was said, *God is known in Judea, in Israel his name is great*[^44], is it so to be understood, as though elsewhere God were lesser, elsewhere greater; but his name is great there where it is named according to the greatness of his majesty. So his name is said to be holy there where it is named with veneration and the fear of offending. And this is what is now being done, while the Gospel, by still becoming known through the various nations, commends the name of the one God through the administration of his Son.

## Your Kingdom Come

**6. 20.** Then there follows, *your kingdom come*[^45]. As the Lord himself teaches in the Gospel, the day of judgment will then be, when the Gospel has been preached in all nations; which matter pertains to the hallowing of God's name. For here too it was not so said, *your kingdom come*, as though God did not now reign. But perhaps someone may say that *come* was said with reference to the earth; as though indeed he himself did not even now reign on earth, and had not always reigned in it from the foundation of the world. *Come*, then, is to be taken as: let it be made manifest to men. For just as even present light is absent to the blind and to those who close their eyes, so the kingdom of God, although it never departs from the earth, is nonetheless absent to those who are ignorant of it. But it will be permitted to no one to be ignorant of the kingdom of God, when his Only-begotten shall come from heaven, not only intelligibly but also visibly in the Lordly man, to judge the living and the dead.

After which judgment, that is, when the distinction and separation of the just from the unjust has been made, God will so dwell in the just that there will be no need for anyone to be taught through a man, but all, as it is written, will be *taught by God*[^46]. Then the blessed life will be perfected in every part in the saints forever, just as now the celestial angels, most holy and most blessed, are wise and blessed by the illumination of God alone, because the Lord promised this too to his own: *in the resurrection they will be*, he says, *like the angels in heaven*[^47].

## Your Will Be Done . . .

**6. 21.** And therefore, after that petition in which we say, *your kingdom come*, there follows, *your will be done, as in heaven, so on earth*[^48], that is, as your will is in the angels, who are in heaven, so that they cleave to you in every way and fully enjoy you, no error clouding their wisdom, no wretchedness impeding their blessedness, so let it be done in your saints, who are on earth, and were made of earth as concerns the body, and, although they are to be taken up into a heavenly dwelling and change, are nonetheless of earth. To this looks also that proclamation of the angels, *Glory to God in the highest, and on earth peace to men of good will*[^49], so that, when our good will has gone before, which follows the one who calls, the will of God may be perfected in us, just as it is in the celestial angels, so that no adversity may resist our blessedness, which is peace.

Likewise, *your will be done* is rightly understood: let your precepts be obeyed, *as in heaven, so on earth*, that is, as by the angels, so by men. For that the will of God is done when his precepts are obeyed, the Lord himself says, when he says, *my food is to do the will of him who sent me*[^50], and often, *I have not come to do my own will, but the will of him who sent me*[^51]; and when he says, *behold my mother and my brothers. And whoever does the will of God, he is to me brother and mother and sister*[^52]. Those, then, who do the will of God—in them certainly the will of God is done, not because they themselves bring it about that God wills, but because they do what he wills, that is, they do according to his will.

## . . . As in Heaven, So on Earth

**6. 22.** There is also that understanding: *your will be done, as in heaven, so on earth*[^53]: as in the saints and the just, so also in sinners. Which can be taken in two ways: either that we may pray also for our enemies—for what else are those to be reckoned against whose will the Christian and Catholic name is increased?—so that it is said, *your will be done, as in heaven, so on earth*, as though it were said: Let sinners do your will, as the just do, so that they may be converted to you; or thus, *your will be done, as in heaven, so on earth*, so that to each may be assigned his own; which will happen at the last judgment, so that to the just may be repaid reward, to sinners condemnation, when the lambs will be separated from the goats[^54].

## Heaven and Earth Are Fittingly Understood as Spirit and Flesh . . .

**6. 23.** There is also that not absurd, indeed most fitting understanding for our faith and hope, that we take heaven and earth as spirit and flesh. And since the apostle says, *with my mind I serve the law of God, but with my flesh the law of sin*[^55], we see the will of God done in the mind, that is, in the spirit. But when death has been swallowed up in victory, and this mortal has put on immortality, which will happen at the resurrection of the flesh and that change which is promised to the just according to the preaching of the same apostle[^56], the will of God will be done on earth too as in heaven; that is, so that as the spirit does not resist God, following and doing his will, so also the body may not resist the spirit or the soul, which now is vexed by the weakness of the body and is prone to carnal habit.

This will belong to the highest peace in eternal life, so that not only the willing but also the perfecting of the good may be present to us. *For now to will*, he says, *is present to me, but to perfect the good is not*[^57], because not yet on earth as in heaven, that is, not yet in the flesh as in the spirit, is the will of God done. For even in our wretchedness the will of God is done, when we suffer through the flesh those things that are owed to us by the right of mortality, which our nature deserved by sinning. But this is to be prayed for, that as in heaven so on earth the will of God may be done, that is, that as we are delighted together with the law of God according to the inner man, so also, the change of the body having been made, no part of us may oppose this our delight with earthly pains or pleasures.

## . . . As Man and Woman

**6. 24.** Nor does this shrink from the truth, that we take *your will be done, as in heaven, so on earth*[^58]: as in the Lord Jesus Christ himself, so also in the Church, as in the man who fulfilled the Father's will, so also in the woman who is betrothed to him. For heaven and earth are fittingly understood as, in a manner, man and woman, since the earth is made fruitful by the heaven making it fertile.

## Give Us This Day Our Daily Bread

**7. 25.** The fourth petition is, *give us this day our daily bread*[^59]. The daily bread is said either for all those things that sustain the necessity of this life, of which, when he gave a precept, he said, *do not be anxious about tomorrow*[^60], so that for this reason it was added, *give us this day*; or for the sacrament of the body of Christ, which we receive daily; or for the spiritual food, of which the same Lord says, *work for the food that does not perish*[^61], and that, *I am the bread of life, who came down from heaven*[^62]. But which of these three is more probable can be considered. For perhaps someone may be moved as to why we should pray for obtaining the things that are necessary for this life, such as food and clothing, when the Lord himself says, *do not be anxious about what you may eat or what you may put on*[^63]. Or can anyone be unanxious about that thing for the obtaining of which he prays, when prayer is to be directed with so great an intention of mind that to this is referred all that which was said about shutting the inner chambers[^64], and that which he says, *seek first the kingdom of God, and all these things shall be added to you*[^65]? For he certainly did not say, "*seek first the kingdom of God*, then seek these things," but, *all these things*, he says, *shall be added to you*, namely even to those not seeking them. But how anyone can rightly be said not to seek what he most intently beseeches God to receive, I do not know whether it can be found.

## The Sacrament of the Lord's Body Is Called Daily Bread

**7. 26.** But concerning the sacrament of the Lord's body, let those not raise a question who, very many in the eastern parts, do not daily communicate at the Lord's supper, since this bread is called *daily*! So let those be silent, and neither defend their practice by ecclesiastical authority itself—since they do these things without scandal, and are neither forbidden to do so by those who preside over the churches, nor condemned for not complying! Whence it is proved that this is not understood in those parts as *daily bread*; for those who do not receive of it daily would be charged with the crime of a great sin. But, as was said, that we may discuss nothing about these in any direction, this at least ought to occur to those considering it, that we have received from the Lord a rule of praying, which we ought not to transgress either by adding anything or by passing over anything. Since this is so, who is there who would dare to say that we ought to pray the Lord's Prayer only once, or at any rate, even if a second or third time, yet only up to that hour at which we communicate at the body of the Lord, but afterward not so to pray through the remaining parts of the day? For we shall no longer be able to say, *give us this day*, what we have already received. Or will anyone be able to compel us to celebrate that sacrament even in the last part of the day?

## The Word of God Is Called Daily Bread

**7. 27.** It remains, then, that we take the daily bread as spiritual, namely the divine precepts, which we ought daily to meditate on and to work at. For of them the Lord says, *work for the food that does not perish*[^66]. But this food is now called daily, as long as this temporal life is carried on through days departing and succeeding. And indeed, as long as the affection of the mind alternates now toward higher, now toward lower things, that is, now toward spiritual, now toward carnal things, like one who is sometimes fed with food, sometimes suffers hunger, daily bread is necessary, by which the hungry may be refreshed and the failing raised up. And so, just as our body in this life—before that change, of course—is refreshed with food, because it feels loss, so too the mind, since through temporal affections it suffers, as it were, loss of its intention toward God, is refreshed with the food of the precepts.

But *give us this day*[^67] was said *as long as it is called today*, that is, in this temporal life. For so we shall be satisfied with spiritual food after this life forever, that it will not then be called *daily bread*, because there the rolling of time, which makes day succeed day, whence it is called *daily*, will be none. But as it was said, *today, if you hear his voice*[^68], which the apostle interprets in the *epistle* that is *to the Hebrews*, *as long as it is called today*[^69], so here too it is to be taken, *give us this day*.

But if anyone wishes to take this sentence also of the necessary food of the body or of the sacrament of the Lord's body, all three must be taken together, namely that we ask at once for daily bread both necessary for the body and the consecrated visible bread and the invisible bread of the word of God.

## Forgive Us, as We Also Forgive

**8. 28.** There follows the fifth petition, *and forgive us our debts, as we also forgive our debtors*[^70]. That *debts* means "sins" is manifest, either from that which the same Lord says, *you will not go out from there until you pay the last farthing*[^71], or from that he called debtors those who were reported to him as killed, whether by the fall of a tower, or whose blood Pilate mingled with their sacrifice[^72]. For he said that men thought those were debtors beyond measure, that is, sinners, and he added, *amen I say to you, unless you repent, you will likewise perish*[^73].

Here, then, no one is pressed to forgive money to his debtors, but whatever another has sinned against him. For we are bidden to forgive money rather by that precept which was said above, *if anyone wishes to take away your tunic and contend with you in judgment, let go to him your cloak also*[^74]. Nor is it there necessary to forgive the debt to every money-debtor, but to him who is unwilling to repay, so far that he even wishes to go to law. *But the servant of the Lord*, as the apostle says, *ought not to go to law*[^75].

He, then, who, whether of his own accord or when appealed to, is unwilling to repay the money owed, is to be forgiven. For he will be unwilling to repay for two reasons: either because he does not have it, or because he is greedy and covetous of another's property. But both pertain to want; for the former is want of household property, the latter of mind. Whoever, therefore, forgives the debt to such a one, forgives one in want and works a Christian work, that rule remaining, that in mind he be prepared to lose what is owed to him. For if he acts modestly and gently in every way, so that it be restored to him, aiming not so much at the profit of the money as at correcting the man—for whom it is without doubt pernicious to have the means of repaying and not to repay—he will not only not sin but will even profit very much, lest the other, while wishing to make profit of another's money, suffer loss of faith. Which is so much graver that there is no comparison.

From which it is understood that in this fifth petition too, in which we say, *forgive us our debts, as we also forgive our debtors*[^76], it is not said indeed of money, but of all the things in which anyone sins against us, and through this of money too. For he sins against you who refuses to repay you the money owed, when he has the means of repaying. And if you do not forgive this sin, you will not be able to say, *forgive us, as we also forgive*; but if you pardon it, you see that he too is admonished about forgiving money, who is bidden to pray with such a prayer.

## All Sins Are to Be Forgiven If We Wish Ours to Be Forgiven Us by the Father

**8. 29.** This indeed can be discussed: that, since we say, *forgive us, as we also forgive*, we are then convicted of having acted against this rule, if we do not forgive those who ask pardon, since we too, asking pardon, wish to be forgiven by the most kind Father. But again, by that precept by which we are bidden to pray for our enemies[^77], we are not bidden to pray for those who ask pardon. For now those who are such are not enemies. But in no way could anyone truly say that he prays for one whom he has not pardoned. Therefore it must be confessed that all sins committed against us are to be forgiven, if we wish ours, which we commit, to be forgiven us by the Father. For about vengeance enough has been discussed above, I think.

## Lead Us Not into Temptation

**9. 30.** The sixth petition is, *and lead us not into temptation*[^78]. Some manuscripts have *bring us not*, which I think is of the same value; for from the one Greek word that was said, *eisenenkes*, both have been translated. But many in praying say thus, *do not allow us to be brought into temptation*, setting forth, namely, in what way *lead* was said. For God does not by himself lead in, but allows him to be led in whom by a most hidden order and by deserts he has deserted by his help. Often, too, for manifest causes, he judges worthy to be deserted and allowed to be led into temptation the one whom he deserts. But it is one thing to be led into temptation, another to be tempted. For without temptation no one can be proved, whether to himself, as it is written, *he who has not been tempted, what does he know?*[^79], or to another, as the apostle says, *and your temptation in my flesh you did not despise*[^80]. For from this he himself recognized them firm, in that by those tribulations which had happened to the apostle according to the flesh they were not turned aside from love. For we are known to God even before all temptations, who knows all things before they are done.

## God Tempts Us to Make Us Know

**9. 31.** Therefore what is written, *the Lord your God tempts you, that he may know whether you love him*[^81], is set down by that manner of speaking, *that he may know*, in place of, "that he may make you know"; just as we call a day glad, because it makes glad, and cold sluggish, because it makes sluggish, and innumerable things of this kind, which are found whether in the custom of speaking or in the discourse of the learned or in the Holy Scriptures.

Not understanding this, the heretics who oppose the Old Testament think that he, of whom it was said, *the Lord your God tempts you*, is to be marked, as it were, with the fault of ignorance—as though in the Gospel it were not written of the Lord, *but this he said tempting him; for he himself knew what he was about to do*[^82]. For if he knew the heart of the one whom he tempted, what is it that he wished to see by tempting? But surely that was done so that he who was tempted might become known to himself, and might condemn his own despair, the crowds having been satisfied with the Lord's bread, which he had supposed they did not have to eat[^83].

## Against the Manichees, Who Do Not See How Satan Could Speak with God

**9. 32.** Here, then, it is not prayed that we be not tempted, but that we be not led into temptation; as if someone for whom it is necessary to be tried by fire should pray not that he not be touched by the fire, but that he not be burned up. For *the furnace proves the vessels of the potter, and the temptation of tribulation just men*[^84]. Joseph, then, was tempted by the enticement of unchastity, but was not led into temptation[^85]. Susanna was tempted, nor was she herself led or brought into temptation[^86]; and many others of either sex, but Job especially.

When those heretics, enemies of the Old Testament, wished with sacrilegious mouth to mock his admirable stability in God his Lord, they bandy about, before the rest, that Satan asked that he be tempted[^87]. For they seek from inexperienced men, who are by no means able to understand such things, how Satan could speak with God, not seeing—for they cannot, since they are blinded by superstition and contention—that God does not occupy a space of place by the mass of a body, and so be in one place and not in another, or at any rate have one part here and another elsewhere, but is present everywhere by his majesty, not divided into parts but everywhere perfect. And if they look carnally at what was said, *heaven is my throne, and earth the footstool of my feet*[^88], to which place the Lord too bears witness, saying, *do not swear, neither by heaven, because it is the throne of God, nor by earth, because it is the footstool of his feet*[^89], what wonder if the devil, set on earth, stood before the feet of God and spoke something in his presence[^90]? For when are they able to understand that there is no soul, however perverse, which nonetheless can in any way reason, in whose conscience God does not speak? For who wrote the natural law in the hearts of men except God? Of which Law the apostle says, *for when the gentiles, who do not have the Law, naturally do the things that are of the Law, these, not having the Law, are a law to themselves; who show the work of the Law written in their hearts, their conscience bearing witness, and their thoughts among themselves accusing or even excusing them, on the day when God will judge the hidden things of men*[^91].

Therefore, if every rational soul, even one blinded by desire, nonetheless, when it thinks and reasons, whatever is true in that reasoning is to be attributed not to it but to the very light of truth, by which it is illumined, even if faintly according to its capacity, so that it perceives something true in reasoning,

what wonder if the soul of the devil, perverse with depraved desire, is nonetheless reported to have heard, by the voice of God himself, that is, by the voice of truth itself, whatever true thing it thought about the just man when it wished to tempt him[^92], but whatever was false is attributed to that desire from which it received the name of devil?

Although also through bodily and visible creation God has often spoken, whether to the good or to the evil, as Lord and ruler of all and orderer according to the merits of each thing: as through the angels, who appeared also to the sight of men, and through the prophets, saying, "thus says the Lord." What wonder, then, if, although not in the very thought, yet at least through some creature suited to such a work, God is said to have spoken with the devil?

## Certainly the Lord Himself Says This in the Gospel, to Which These Heretics, Willing or Unwilling, Submit Their Necks

**9. 33.** Nor let them think it to be of dignity and, as it were, a merit of righteousness that God spoke with him, since he spoke with an angelic soul, although foolish and covetous, just as if he spoke with a human soul foolish and covetous. Or let them themselves say how God spoke with that rich man, whose most foolish covetousness he wished to reprove, saying, *fool, this night your soul is demanded of you; these things that you have prepared, whose will they be?*[^93] Certainly the Lord himself says this in the Gospel, to which these heretics, willing or unwilling, submit their necks.

But if they are moved by this, that Satan asks of God that the just man be tempted, I do not set forth why it was done, but I compel them to set forth why it was said in the Gospel by the Lord himself to the disciples, *behold, Satan asks to sift you like wheat*[^94], and he says to Peter, *but I have prayed that your faith may not fail*[^95]. But when they set this forth to me, they at the same time set forth to themselves that which they ask of me. But if they are unable to set this forth, let them not dare to find fault rashly, in any book, with what they read in the Gospel without offense.

## There Are Also Human Temptations

**9. 34.** Temptations, then, are brought about through Satan not by his power but by the permission of the Lord, upon men, either to punish them for their sins or, by the mercy of God, to prove and exercise them. And it makes a great difference into what kind of temptation each one falls. For Judas, who sold the Lord[^96], did not fall into such a temptation as that into which Peter fell, when, terrified, he denied the Lord[^97]. There are also human temptations, I believe, when anyone with a good mind, yet according to human frailty, slips in some counsel, or is irritated against a brother out of zeal for correcting him, yet a little more than Christian tranquillity demands. Of these the apostle says, *let no temptation take hold of you but a human one*, while he likewise says, *God is faithful, who will not let you be tempted beyond what you can bear, but will with the temptation also make a way out, that you may be able to endure*[^98]. In which sentence he sufficiently shows that we ought to pray not that we be not tempted, but that we be not led into temptation. For we are led in if such temptations happen as we cannot bear. But since the dangerous temptations, into which it is pernicious to be brought and led, arise either from prosperous temporal things or from adverse ones, no one is broken by the trouble of adverse things who is not captivated by the delight of prosperous ones.

## But Deliver Us from Evil

**9. 35.** The last and seventh petition is, *but deliver us from evil*[^99]. For it is to be prayed that not only we be not led into the evil we lack, which is asked in the sixth place, but that we be also delivered from that into which we have already been led. When this has been done, nothing will remain to be dreaded, nor will any temptation at all be to be feared. Which nonetheless, in this life, as long as we carry about this mortality, into which we have been led by the serpent's persuasion[^100], is not to be hoped to be able to come about; but nonetheless it is to be hoped that at some time it will be, and this is the hope which is not seen. Of which, when the apostle spoke, he said, *but hope that is seen is not hope*[^101]. But nonetheless the wisdom which has been granted in this life too to the faithful servants of God is not to be despaired of. And it is this: that what, by the Lord's revelation, we have understood is to be fled, we flee with most cautious watchfulness, and what, by the Lord's revelation, we have understood is to be sought, we seek with most ardent love. For so, the remaining burden of this mortality laid down by death itself, in every part of the man, at the opportune time, blessedness will be perfected, which was begun in this life, and to the grasping and obtaining of which now all effort is at some time expended.

## The Obtainings of the First Three Petitions Take Their Beginning in This Life and Will Remain Forever

**10. 36.** But the distinction of these seven petitions is to be considered and commended. For since our life is now carried on temporally, while eternal life is hoped for, and since eternal things are prior in dignity, although, temporal things being first carried on, one passes over to those, the obtainings of the first three petitions, although they take their beginning in this life which is carried on in this world—for both the hallowing of God's name began to be carried on from the very coming of the Lord in humility; and the coming of his kingdom, in which he is to come in glory, will be made manifest not when the world is already ended but at the end of the world; and the perfection of his will, as in heaven so on earth, whether you understand heaven and earth as the just and sinners, or as spirit and flesh, or as the Lord and the Church, or all together, will be completed by the very perfection of our blessedness and therefore by the ending of the world—yet all three will remain forever. For both the hallowing of God's name is everlasting, and of his kingdom there is no end, and to our perfected blessedness eternal life is promised. These three, then, consummated and heaped up, will abide forever in that life which is promised to us.

## The Remaining Four Things We Ask Seem to Pertain to This Temporal Life

**10. 37.** But the remaining four things we ask seem to me to pertain to this temporal life. Of which the first is, *give us this day our daily bread*[^102]. For by this very thing, that it was called *daily bread*—whether spiritual is signified, or this visible bread in the sacrament or in food—it pertains to this time, which he called *today*; not because spiritual food is not everlasting, but because this, which is called *daily* in the Scriptures, is presented to the soul whether in the sound of speech or by any temporal signs, all of which will certainly not be then, when all will be taught by God and will drink in the ineffable light of truth itself, not signifying it by movements of bodies, but drawing it in by purity of mind. For perhaps for this reason it was called bread and not drink, because bread, by breaking and chewing, is turned into nourishment, as the Scriptures, by opening and discussing, feed the soul, while drink, when prepared as it is, passes into the body, so that at this time truth is bread, when it is called *daily bread*, but then drink, when there will be need of no labor of disputing and discoursing, as of breaking and chewing, but only of the drinking in of sincere and clear truth. And sins are now forgiven us and we now forgive, which is the second of these remaining four petitions; but then there will be no pardon of sins, because no sins. And temptations infest this temporal life; but they will not be when that is perfected which was said, *you will hide them in the secret of your face*[^103]. And the evil from which we wish to be freed, and the very deliverance from evil, certainly pertain to this life, which by the justice of God we deserved as mortal, and from which by his mercy we are freed.

## The Sevenfold Number of These Petitions Seems to Correspond to That Sevenfold Number of the Gifts of the Holy Spirit

**11. 38.** It seems to me also that this sevenfold number of these petitions corresponds to that sevenfold number from which this whole sermon flowed. For if it is the fear of God by which the poor in spirit are blessed, since theirs is the kingdom of heaven, let us ask that God's name be hallowed among men by a chaste fear remaining forever and ever[^104]. If it is piety by which the meek are blessed, because they shall possess the land by inheritance, let us ask that his kingdom come, whether into ourselves, that we may grow meek and not resist him, or from heaven to earth in the glory of the Lord's coming, in which we shall rejoice and be praised, as he says, *come, blessed of my Father, receive the kingdom which has been prepared for you from the origin of the world*[^105]. For *in the Lord*, says the prophet, *my soul shall be praised; let the meek hear and rejoice*[^106].

If it is knowledge by which those who mourn are blessed, since they shall be comforted, let us pray that his *will be done, as in heaven, so on earth*, because when the body, as earth, has consented to the spirit, as heaven, with the highest and entire peace, we shall not mourn; for there is no other mourning of this time except when these things conflict against each other and compel us to say, *I see another law in my members warring against the law of my mind*[^107], and to testify to our mourning with a tearful voice, *wretched man that I am, who will free me from the body of this death?*[^108] If it is fortitude by which those who hunger and thirst for righteousness are blessed, because they shall be satisfied, let us pray that our daily bread be given us this day, by which, supported and sustained, we may be able to come to that fullest satisfaction.

If it is counsel by which the merciful are blessed, since they shall obtain mercy, let us forgive the debts of our debtors, and pray that ours be forgiven us. If it is understanding by which the pure in heart are blessed, since they shall see God, let us pray not to be led into temptation, lest we have a double heart by not seeking the single good, to which we may refer all that we do, but pursuing temporal and eternal things at once. For temptations from those things which seem grave and calamitous to men have no power over us, if those have no power which befall through the allurements of the things that men think good and to be rejoiced in.

If it is wisdom by which the peacemakers are blessed, since they shall be called sons of God, let us pray that we be delivered from evil; for that very deliverance will make us free, that is, sons of God, so that in the spirit of adoption we may cry, *Abba, Father!*

## That Which Pertains to the Forgiveness of Sins Is Especially to Be Commended

**11. 39.** Indeed, it must not be passed over negligently that, of all these sentences in which the Lord commanded us to pray, he judged that one especially to be commended which pertains to the forgiveness of sins, in which he wished us to be merciful, which is the one counsel for escaping miseries. For in no other sentence do we so pray that we, as it were, make a pact with God; for we say, *forgive us, as we also forgive*. In which pact, if we lie, there is no fruit of the whole prayer. For he says thus: *for if you forgive men their sins, your Father who is in heaven will also forgive you. But if you do not forgive men, neither will your Father forgive you your offenses*[^109].

## The Precept of Fasting, Pertaining to the Same Cleansing of the Heart

**12. 40.** There follows the precept about fasting, pertaining to the same cleansing of the heart of which we are now treating. For in this work too one must beware lest some ostentation and appetite for human praise creep in, which doubles the heart and does not allow it to be pure and single for the understanding of God. *But when you fast*, he says, *do not become sad like the hypocrites; for they disfigure their faces, that they may appear to men to be fasting. Amen I say to you, they have received their reward. But you, when you fast, anoint your heads and wash your faces, that you may not appear to men to be fasting, but to your Father who is in secret; and your Father, who sees in secret, will repay you*[^110]. It is manifest that by these precepts all our intention is directed to inner joys, lest, seeking the reward outwardly, we be conformed to this world, and lose the promise of a blessedness so much more solid and firm as it is more inward, by which God chose us *to be conformed to the image of his Son*.

## Boasting Can Be Not Only in the Splendor of Bodily Things but Even in Mournful Filth Itself

**12. 41.** But in this passage it must especially be noted that boasting can be not only in the splendor and pomp of bodily things but even in mournful filth itself, and the more dangerous the more it deceives under the name of the service of God. He, therefore, who shines forth with immoderate care of the body and dress or with the splendor of other things is easily convicted of being a follower, in the things themselves, of the pomps of the world, and deceives no one with a deceitful image of holiness; but he who, in the profession of Christianity, by unusual squalor and filth makes the eyes of men intent upon himself, since he does this by will and does not suffer it by necessity, can be judged by his other works whether he does this from contempt of superfluous care or from some ambition, because the Lord too commanded that wolves under a sheep's skin be guarded against.

But *by their fruits*, he says, *you shall know them*[^111]. For when, by certain temptations, those very things begin to be withdrawn or denied to them which by that veil they have either obtained or desire to obtain, then it must appear whether it is a wolf in a sheep's skin or a sheep in its own. For a Christian ought not, for this reason, to soothe the sight of men with superfluous adornment, because dissemblers too often usurp that sparing and necessary habit, to deceive the incautious—just as those sheep ought not to lay aside their skins, if at some time wolves cover themselves with them.

## We Are Commanded to Be, with Heads Anointed, When We Fast

**12. 42.** It is wont to be asked, then, what it is that he says, *but you, when you fast, anoint your heads, and wash your faces, that you may not appear to men to be fasting*[^112]. For no one would rightly command that, although we wash our faces by daily custom, we ought also, with heads anointed, to be when we fast. And if all confess this to be most disgraceful, it must be understood that this precept of anointing the head and washing the face pertains to the inner man. To anoint the head, then, pertains to gladness, but to wash the face to cleanness; and therefore he anoints his head who rejoices inwardly in mind and reason.

For we rightly take this as the head, which is preeminent in the soul and by which it is manifest that the rest of the man is ruled and governed. And he does this who does not seek gladness outwardly, so as to rejoice carnally over the praises of men. For the flesh, which ought to be subject, is in no way the head of the whole of human nature. *No one*, indeed, *ever hated his own flesh*[^113], as the apostle says, when he gives a precept about loving one's wife; but the head of the woman is the man, of which man the head is Christ[^114].

Let him, then, rejoice inwardly in his fasting by this very thing, that by so fasting he turns himself away from the pleasure of the world so as to be subject to Christ, who, according to this precept, desires him to have an anointed head. For thus he will also wash his face, that is, cleanse his heart, with which he is to see God, with no veil interposed because of the weakness contracted from filth, but firm and stable, since pure and single. *Wash yourselves*, he says, *be clean: take away wickednesses from your souls and from before my eyes*[^115]. From these filths, then, our face is to be washed, by which the sight of God is offended. *For we, with unveiled face beholding the glory of the Lord, shall be transformed into the same image*[^116].

## Love from a Pure Heart and a Good Conscience and Faith Unfeigned

**12. 43.** Often, too, the thought of things necessary to this life wounds and defiles our inner eye, and generally doubles the heart, so that the things we seem to do rightly with men we do not do with that heart which the Lord commanded, that is, not because we love them, but because we wish to obtain from them some advantage on account of the necessity of the present life. But we ought to do good to them for the sake of their eternal salvation, not for the sake of our temporal advantage. Let God, then, incline our heart to his testimonies and not to gain[^117]! *For the end of the precept is love from a pure heart and a good conscience and faith unfeigned*[^118]. But he who, on account of his own need from this life, consults for a brother, does not consult out of love, because he consults not for him whom he ought to love as himself, but for himself—or rather not even for himself, since from this he makes for himself a double heart, by which he is hindered from seeing God, in which seeing alone is sure and perpetual blessedness.

## But If the Heart Is in Heaven, It Will Be Pure, Because Whatever Things Are Heavenly Are Pure

**13. 44.** Rightly, then, there follows, and he commands, who insists on the cleansing of our heart, saying, *do not lay up for yourselves treasures on earth, where moth and rust destroy, and where thieves dig through and steal; but lay up for yourselves treasures in heaven, where neither moth nor rust destroys, and where thieves do not dig through nor steal. For where your treasure is, there your heart will be also*[^119]. Therefore, if the heart is on earth, that is, if anyone does something with this heart, that he may obtain earthly advantage, how will that be pure which is rolled in the earth? But if it is in heaven, it will be pure, because whatever things are heavenly are pure. For something becomes filthy when it is mingled with a lower nature, although in its own kind not filthy, because even from pure silver gold becomes filthy if it is mingled with it. So too our mind becomes filthy by the desire of earthly things, although the earth itself in its own kind and order is pure.

But I would not take heaven in this place as bodily, because every body is to be reckoned as earth. For he ought to despise the whole world who lays up treasure for himself in heaven, in that heaven, then, of which it was said, *the heaven of heaven to the Lord*[^120], that is, in the spiritual firmament. For we ought not to set and place our treasure and our heart in that which will pass away, but in that which always remains; but *heaven and earth will pass away*[^121].

## Here We Ought to Take the Eye as the Very Intention with Which We Do Whatever We Do

**13. 45.** And here he makes it manifest that he commands all these things about the cleansing of the heart, when he says, *the lamp of your body is your eye. If, therefore, your eye is single, your whole body will be full of light; but if your eye is wicked, your whole body will be dark. If, then, the light that is in you is darkness, how great is the darkness?*[^122] Which place is to be understood thus: that we may know that all our works are then pure and pleasing in the sight of God if they are done with a single heart, that is, with an intention toward the things above, with that end of love, because also *the fullness of the Law is love*[^123]. Here, then, we ought to take the eye as the very intention with which we do whatever we do. And if it is pure and right, and looking to that which is to be looked at, all our works, which we do according to it, must of necessity be good. All which works he called the whole body, because the apostle too calls certain works our members, which he disapproves and commands to be mortified, saying, *mortify, therefore, your members which are upon the earth: fornication, uncleanness, greed*, and the rest of such[^124].

## Not What Each One Does, but with What Mind He Does It, Is to Be Considered

**13. 46.** Therefore not what each one does, but with what mind he does it, is to be considered. For this is the light in us, because this is manifest to us, that we do with a good mind what we do: *for everything that is made manifest is light*[^125]. For the very deeds that proceed from us toward the fellowship of men have an uncertain outcome, and therefore he called them *darkness*. For I do not know, when I hold out money to one needy and asking, what he is going to do with it or suffer on account of it; and it can happen that he either does some evil with it or suffers some evil on account of it, which I, when I gave it, did not wish to happen nor gave with that mind. And so if I did with a good mind what was known to me when I did it, and is therefore called light, my deed too is illumined, whatever outcome it has had. Which outcome, since it is uncertain and unknown, is called darkness.

But if I did it with an evil mind, the very light is darkness. For it is called light, because each one knows with what mind he acts, even when he acts with an evil mind. But that very light is darkness, because the single intention is not directed to the things above, but is bent down to lower things and, with a double heart, makes, as it were, a shadow.

*If, then, the light that is in you is darkness, how great is the darkness?*[^126], that is: "if the very intention of the heart with which you do what you do, which is known to you, is defiled by the appetite for earthly and temporal things and is blinded, how much more is the deed itself, whose outcome is uncertain, filthy and dark, because even if something turns out well for someone which you do not with a right and pure intention, how you did it is imputed to you, not how it turned out for him."

## No One Can Serve Two Masters

**14. 47.** But what follows, and he says, *no one can serve two masters*, is to be referred to this same intention, which he consequently sets forth, saying, *for either he will hate the one and love the other, or he will bear with the one and despise the other*. Which words are to be carefully considered. For who the two masters are he then shows, when he says, *you cannot serve God and mammon*[^127]. *Mammon* is said to be called "riches" among the Hebrews[^128]. The Punic name agrees too; for "gain" in Punic is called *mammon*. But he who serves mammon serves him who, set over these earthly things by the desert of his perversity, is called by the Lord the magistrate of this world[^129]. *Either*, then, *a man will hate* this one *and love the other*, that is, God, *or he will bear with the one and despise the other*. For he bears a hard and pernicious master, whoever serves mammon; for, entangled by his own desire, he is made subject to the devil, and does not love him—for who is there who loves the devil?—but nonetheless bears with him. As in some greater household, one who is joined to another's maidservant on account of his own desire bears a hard servitude, even if he does not love him whose maidservant he loves.

## Let Us Disentangle the Right Intention of the Heart from All Duplicity

**14. 48.** *But the other he will despise*, he said, not, "he will hate." For hardly anyone's conscience can hate God, but he despises him, that is, does not fear him, when he is, as it were, secure about his goodness. From this negligence and pernicious security the Holy Spirit calls us back, when he says through the prophet, *son, do not add sin upon sin and say, "the mercy of God is great," not knowing that the patience of God invites you to repentance*[^130]. For whose mercy can be recalled so great as his who forgives all sins to those who turn to him and makes the wild olive a partaker of the fatness of the olive? And whose severity so great as his who did not spare the natural branches, but broke them off on account of unbelief[^131]? But whoever wishes to love God and to beware of offending him, let him not think that he can serve two masters, and let him disentangle the right intention of his heart from all duplicity! For so *he will think of the Lord in goodness, and in simplicity of heart will seek him.*

## The Intention of the Heart in Heaven Does Not Seek Superfluous Things

**15. 49.** *Therefore*, he says, *I say to you, do not be anxious in your soul about what you may eat, nor for your body about what you may put on*[^132], lest perhaps, although superfluous things are no longer sought, the heart be doubled on account of the necessities themselves, and our intention be twisted toward acquiring these, when we do something as though out of mercy, that is, so that, when we wish to seem to consult for someone, we look there to our own gain rather than to his advantage, and therefore do not seem to ourselves to sin, because the things we wish to obtain are not superfluous but necessary.

But the Lord admonishes us to remember that God has given us much more—that he made and composed us of soul and body—than is food and clothing, the care of which he does not wish to double our heart. *Is not the soul*, he says, *more than food*, that you may understand that he who gave the soul will much more easily give food, *and the body than clothing*[^133], that is, it is more, that you may likewise understand that he who gave the body will much more easily give clothing.

## The Soul Is Here Set Down for This Life

**15. 50.** In which place it is wont to be asked whether this food pertains to the soul, since the soul is incorporeal, but this food is corporeal. But let us know that the soul is here set down for this life, the support of which is this corporeal nourishment. According to this signification was said also that, *he who loves his soul shall lose it*[^134]. Which, unless we take it of this life, which must be lost for the kingdom of God, as it was made clear the martyrs were able to do, this precept will be contrary to that sentence in which it was said, *what does it profit a man if he gains the whole world but suffers the loss of his soul?*[^135]

## To Leave to God the Care of Clothing the Body Too!

**15. 51.** *Look*, he says, *at the birds of heaven, for they do not sow nor reap nor gather into barns; and your heavenly Father feeds them. Are you not worth more than they*[^136], that is, are you not worth more dearly? Because certainly a rational animal, such as man is, is set higher in the nature of things than irrational ones, such as birds are. *But which of you*, he says, *by being anxious can add one cubit to his stature? And about clothing why are you anxious?*[^137], that is: By whose power and dominion it was brought about that your body was carried to this stature, by his providence it can also be clothed. But that it was not brought about by your care that your body came to this stature can be understood from this, that if you should care and wish to add one cubit to this stature, you cannot. To him, then, leave the care of clothing the body too, by whose care you see it brought about that you have a body of so great a stature.

## God, Who Is Good and Just, So Much the More Cares for Those Who Beseech Him

**15. 52.** But a proof had to be given about clothing too, as it was given about food. And so there follows, and he says, *consider the lilies of the field, how they grow; they do not labor nor spin. But I say to you that not even Solomon in all his glory was clothed like one of these. But if God so clothes the grass of the field, which today is and tomorrow is thrown into the oven, how much more you, O you of little faith?*[^138] But these proofs are not to be discussed as allegories, so that we ask what the birds of heaven or the lilies of the field signify; for they are set down so that from lesser things greater might be made persuasive. As is that about the judge who neither feared God nor reverenced man, and yet often yielded to the widow appealing to him, to consider her case, not on account of piety or humanity, but lest he suffer weariness[^139]. For in no way does that unjust judge allegorically bear the person of God; but nonetheless how much God, who is good and just, cares for those who beseech him, the Lord wished to be inferred from this, that even an unjust man cannot despise those who beat upon him with constant prayers, even for the avoiding of weariness.

## The Kingdom and Righteousness of God Is Our Good, and This Is to Be Sought, and the End Is to Be Set There

**16. 53.** *Therefore do not*, he says, *be anxious, saying, "what shall we eat, or what shall we drink, or with what shall we be clothed?" For all these things the gentiles seek. For your Father knows that you have need of all these. Seek first the kingdom and righteousness of God, and all these things shall be added to you*[^140]. Here he most manifestly shows that these things are not to be sought as such goods of ours, that for their sake we ought to do good, if we do anything, but that they are nonetheless necessary. For what difference there is between the good, which is to be sought, and the necessary, which is to be taken, he declared by this sentence, when he said, *seek first the kingdom and righteousness of God, and all these things shall be added to you*. The kingdom and righteousness of God, then, is our good, and this is to be sought, and the end is to be set there, for the sake of which we do all that we do. But because in this life we are soldiers, that we may be able to come to that kingdom, which life cannot be carried on without these necessities: *these shall be added to you*, he says, but you, *seek first the kingdom of God and righteousness*! For when he said that *first*, he signified that this is to be sought afterward, not in time but in dignity: the former as our good, the latter as our necessary thing, but necessary for the sake of that good.

## Paul, Preaching the Gospel Not Compelled but Willing, Set His Reward in the Gospel Itself and in the Kingdom of God

**16. 54.** For we ought not, for example, to preach the Gospel in order that we may eat, but to eat in order that we may preach the Gospel. For if we preach the Gospel for this reason, that we may eat, we hold the Gospel cheaper than food; and our good will now be in eating, but the necessary in preaching the Gospel. Which the apostle also forbids, when he says that it is indeed permitted to him and granted by the Lord that those who announce the Gospel should live by the Gospel, that is, should have from the Gospel the things necessary for this life, but that he nonetheless did not abuse this power[^141].

For there were many who desired to have an occasion of acquiring and selling the Gospel, from whom the apostle, wishing to cut it off, sustained his living by his own hands[^142]. For of these he says in another place, *that I may cut off the occasion from those who seek an occasion*[^143]. Although even if, like the other and good apostles, by the Lord's permission he lived by the Gospel, he would not set in that living the end of preaching the Gospel, but would rather place the end of his living itself in the Gospel, that is, as I said above, he would not preach the Gospel in order to come to food and whatever other things are necessary, but would take these in order to fulfill that, lest he preach the Gospel not willing but by necessity.

For this he disapproves, when he says, *do you not know that those who work in the temple eat the things of the temple, that those who serve at the altar share with the altar? So also the Lord ordained that those who announce the Gospel should live by the Gospel. But I have used none of these things*[^144]. Hence he shows that it was permitted, not commanded; otherwise he will be held to have acted against the precept of the Lord.

Then there follows, and he says, *but I have not written these things that they should so be done with me. For it is good for me rather to die than that anyone should make my glory empty*[^145]. He said this because he had already determined, on account of certain ones seeking an occasion, to provide his living with his own hands[^146]. *For if I preach the Gospel*, he says, *it is no glory to me*[^147], that is: If I preach the Gospel, that things should so be done with me, that is, if I preach the Gospel for this reason, that I may come to those things and set the end of the Gospel in food and drink and clothing.

But why is it no glory to him? *For necessity*, he says, *lies upon me*, that is: that I preach the Gospel for this reason, because I have nothing to live on, or that I may acquire temporal fruit from the preaching of eternal things. For so there will now be necessity in the Gospel, not will. *For woe will be to me*, he says, *if I do not preach the Gospel*[^148]. But how ought he to preach the Gospel? Namely, so that in the Gospel itself and in the kingdom of God he sets his reward. For so he can preach the Gospel not compelled but willing.

For *if I do this willingly*, he says, *I have a reward; but if unwillingly, a stewardship has been entrusted to me*[^149]—that is: if, compelled by want of the things necessary for this temporal life, I preach the Gospel, then others will have through me the reward of the Gospel, namely those who, while I preach it, love the Gospel itself; but I will not have it, because I do not love the Gospel itself but its price, set in those temporal things.

And it is wrong for this to come about—that anyone should minister the Gospel not as a son but as a slave to whom *a stewardship has been entrusted*, so as to dispense it as something belonging to another and take nothing from it except his food, which is given from outside not as a share in the kingdom but as the support of a wretched servitude. Yet in another place he also calls himself a steward[^150]. For even a slave adopted into the number of sons can faithfully dispense to his fellow sharers that very thing in which he has earned the lot of a coheir. But here, where he says *but if unwillingly, a stewardship has been entrusted to me*[^151], he meant us to understand such a steward as dispenses what belongs to another and receives nothing from it himself.

## Let us seek temporal things in order that we may have the kingdom of God.

**16. 55.** Therefore whatever thing is sought for the sake of something else is without doubt inferior to that for the sake of which it is sought. And so that first thing is the reason you seek this thing, not this thing the reason you seek that. Accordingly, if we seek the Gospel and the kingdom of God for the sake of food, we make food prior and the kingdom of God posterior, so that, if food were not lacking, we would not seek the kingdom of God. This, then, is to seek food first and afterward the kingdom of God—that is, to put the latter in the prior place and the former in the posterior. But if we seek food in order that we may have the kingdom of God, we do what was said: *seek first the kingdom and the righteousness of God, and all these things shall be added to you*[^152].

## Let us seek first the kingdom and the righteousness of God.

**17. 56.** For those who seek first the kingdom and the righteousness of God—that is, who set this before all other things, so that we seek the rest for the sake of this—there ought to be no underlying anxiety that those things may be lacking which are necessary for this life on account of the kingdom of God. For he said above: *your Father knows that you need all these things*.

And therefore, when he had said *seek first the kingdom and the righteousness of God*, he did not say, "then seek these things, although they are necessary," but said *all these things shall be added to you*[^153]—that is, they will follow, if you seek those, without any hindrance on your part, lest in seeking these you be turned away from that, or lest you set up two ends, so as to desire both the kingdom of God for its own sake and these necessary things; rather, desire these for the sake of that. So they will not be lacking to you, because you cannot serve two masters[^154]. Now whoever desires both the kingdom of God as a great good and these temporal things is trying to serve two masters. But he will not be able to have a single eye and to serve the one Lord God unless whatever else there is—if it is necessary—he takes up for the sake of this one thing, that is, for the sake of the kingdom of God.

Now just as all who serve as soldiers receive their rations and pay, so all who preach the Gospel receive food and clothing. But not all serve as soldiers for the safety of the commonwealth; some do so for what they receive. So too not all minister to God for the safety of the Church; some do so for these temporal things, which they obtain as rations and pay, or else both for this and for that. But it has already been said above: *you cannot serve two masters*. Therefore with a single heart, solely for the sake of the kingdom of God, we ought to do good to all, and not, in this activity, to think of a temporal reward—either by itself or together with the kingdom of God.

Under the name of all these temporal things he set "tomorrow," saying: *do not therefore be anxious about tomorrow*[^155]. For a day is not called "tomorrow" except in time, where the future succeeds the past. Therefore, when we do something good, let us think not of temporal but of eternal things! Then that work will be good and perfect. *For tomorrow*, he says, *will be anxious for itself*—that is, so that, when it is fitting, you may take food or drink or clothing, namely when necessity itself begins to press. For these things will be at hand, because our Father knows that we need all these things[^156]. *For sufficient*, he says, *to the day is its own evil*[^157]—that is, "it is enough that necessity itself will press you to take these things," which I think is for this reason called "evil," because it is punitive for us; for it belongs to this frailty and mortality which we earned by sinning. To this penalty, then, of temporal necessity do not add anything heavier, so that you not only suffer want of these things but even serve God as a soldier in order to satisfy that want.

## We must be careful not to judge that a servant of God acts against the Lord's precept when he provides that these necessary things may not be lacking, either to himself or to those whose care has been committed to him.

**17. 57.** But in this place we must be very careful that, when we see some servant of God provide that these necessary things may not be lacking either to himself or to those whose care has been committed to him, we do not judge him to be acting against the Lord's precept and to be anxious about tomorrow. For even the Lord himself, whom the angels served[^158], nevertheless, for the sake of example, lest anyone afterward suffer scandal, when he had noticed that one of his servants procured these necessary things, deigned to keep a purse with money in it, from which whatever was needed might be supplied for necessary uses. The keeper and the thief of this purse, as it is written, was Judas, who betrayed him[^159].

So too the apostle Paul can seem to have thought about tomorrow when he said: *now concerning the collection for the saints, as I directed the churches of Galatia, so you also are to do. On the first day of the week each of you is to put something aside, storing up as he has been prospered, so that the collections need not be made when I come. And when I arrive, I will send those whom you approve by letter to carry your gift to Jerusalem. And if it is fitting that I go too, they shall go with me. I will visit you after passing through Macedonia, for I am passing through Macedonia. Perhaps I will stay with you or even spend the winter, so that you may send me on my way wherever I go. For I do not want to see you now in passing; for I hope to stay some time with you, if the Lord permits. But I will remain at Ephesus until Pentecost*[^160]. Likewise in the *Acts of the Apostles* it is written that the things necessary for sustenance were procured in advance because of an imminent famine. For we read as follows: *in those days prophets came down from Jerusalem to Antioch, and there was great rejoicing. And when we were gathered together, one of them named Agabus stood up and foretold through the Spirit that there would be a great famine over all the world, which also took place under Claudius. And the disciples, as each one prospered, each of them determined to send relief to the brethren, the elders living in Judea; and they did so, sending it by the hand of Barnabas and Saul*[^161]. And from the fact that, when the same apostle Paul was sailing, supplies that were offered were put on board, this provision of food seems to have been procured not for one day only[^162].

And from the fact that the same apostle writes *let the thief no longer steal, but rather let him labor, doing honest work with his hands, so that he may have something to give to him who is in need*[^163], it seems to those who understand badly that he does not keep the Lord's precept who says *look at the birds of the air, for they neither sow nor reap nor gather into barns*[^164], and *consider the lilies of the field, how they grow; they neither toil nor spin*[^165], since he commanded these men to labor, working with their hands, so as to have something they could even give to others. And from the fact that he often says of himself that he worked with his own hands[^166] so as not to burden anyone[^167], and that it is written of him that he joined himself to Aquila because of their common trade, so that they might work together to earn their living[^168], he seems not to have imitated the birds of the air and the lilies of the field.

From these and similar passages of the Scriptures it is sufficiently clear that our Lord does not disapprove if someone procures these things in the human manner, but only if someone serves God as a soldier for the sake of these things, so that in his works he looks not to the kingdom of God but to the acquisition of these things.

## To provide necessary things, as aids, is the part of that physician to whom we have once for all entrusted our whole selves.

**17. 58.** To this rule, then, the whole precept is reduced: that even in the provision of these things we think of the kingdom of God, but in the service of the kingdom of God we do not think of these things. For in this way, even if at some time they are lacking—as God for the most part allows for our training—they not only do not weaken our resolve but even strengthen it, tested and proven. *For we glory*, he says, *in tribulations, knowing that tribulation produces patience, and patience trial, and trial hope, and hope does not disappoint, because the love of God has been poured out in our hearts through the Holy Spirit who has been given to us*[^169]. And in recalling his own tribulations and labors, the same apostle recalls that he labored not only in prisons and shipwrecks and many troubles of this kind, but even in hunger and thirst, in cold and nakedness[^170].

When we read this, let us not suppose that the Lord's promises wavered, so that the apostle suffered hunger and thirst and nakedness while seeking the kingdom and righteousness of God, when it has been said to us *seek first the kingdom and the righteousness of God, and all these things shall be added to you*[^171]—since indeed that physician, to whom we have once for all entrusted our whole selves, knows these things as aids, and from him we have the promise of the present life and the life to come; he knows when to add them and when to withdraw them, as he judges to be good for us;

and he governs and directs us, to be consoled and trained in this life, and after this life to be established and strengthened in everlasting rest. For even a man, when he often withholds fodder from his beast of burden, does not thereby abandon his care of it, but rather does this by way of caring for it.

## God alone judges the hidden depths of the heart.

**18. 59.** And since, when these things are either procured for the future or, when there is no reason to spend them, kept in reserve, it is uncertain with what intention this is done—since it can be done with a single heart and can also be done with a double one—he has fittingly added here: *do not judge, lest you be judged. For with the judgment you pronounce you will be judged, and with the measure you use it will be measured back to you*[^172]. In this place I think nothing else is being enjoined on us than that we should interpret in the better sense those deeds about which it is doubtful with what intention they are done. For what is written, *by their fruits you shall know them*[^173], was said of manifest things, which cannot be done with a good intention—such as fornication or blasphemies or thefts or drunkenness and any others of the sort—about which we are permitted to judge, since the apostle says: *for what have I to do with judging those who are outside? Is it not those who are inside whom you judge?*[^174]

But as to the kind of food, since any human foods whatever can be taken indifferently, with a good intention and a single heart, without the vice of disordered desire, the same apostle forbids those who ate meat and drank wine to be judged by those who abstained from such foods. *Let him who eats*, he says, *not despise him who abstains, and let him who abstains not judge him who eats*. There too he says: *who are you to judge the servant of another? It is before his own master that he stands or falls*[^175].

For about such things, which can be done with a good and single and great intention—although they can also be done with a bad one—those men, being human, wanted to pass sentence in the hidden things of the heart, about which God alone judges.

## Let us therefore judge manifest things; but the judgment of hidden things let us leave to God.

**18. 60.** To this belongs also what he says in another place: *do not pronounce judgment before the time, until the Lord comes, who will bring to light the things hidden in darkness and will disclose the purposes of the heart. And then each one will receive his commendation from God*[^176]. There are, then, certain intermediate deeds, of which we do not know with what intention they are done, because they can be done both with a good and with an evil one; about these it is rash to judge, especially so as to condemn. But the time will come for these to be judged, when the Lord will bring to light the things hidden in darkness and will disclose the purposes of the heart. Likewise in another place the same apostle says: *the sins of some men are conspicuous, going before them to judgment, but the sins of others appear later*[^177]. He calls those "conspicuous" about which it is clear with what intention they are done; these go before to judgment—that is, because, if judgment follows them, it is not rash. But those things follow later which are hidden, because these too will not stay concealed in their own time. The same is to be understood of good deeds also. For he goes on thus: *so too good deeds are conspicuous; and even those that are otherwise cannot be hidden*[^178]. Let us therefore judge manifest things; but the judgment of hidden things let us leave to God, since these too cannot be hidden, whether evil or good, when the time comes for them to be made manifest.

## There are two cases in which we ought to guard against rash judgment.

**18. 61.** Now there are two cases in which we ought to guard against rash judgment: when it is uncertain with what intention something has been done, and when it is uncertain what sort of person he will be who now appears either bad or good. If, then, someone, for example, complaining of his stomach, did not want to fast, and you, not believing this, attributed it to the vice of gluttony, you will judge rashly. Likewise, if you came to know manifest gluttony and drunkenness, and rebuked it as though that man could never be corrected and changed, you will nonetheless judge rashly. Let us not, therefore, rebuke those things about which we do not know with what intention they are done; nor let us so rebuke things that are manifest as to despair of their cure, and we will avoid the judgment of which it is now said: *do not judge, lest you be judged*[^179].

## The very rashness by which you punish another God must of necessity punish in you.

**18. 62.** But it may be a difficulty that he says: *for with the judgment you pronounce you will be judged, and with the measure you use it will be measured back to you*[^180]. For surely, if we judge with a rash judgment, will God too judge us rashly? Or surely, if we have measured with an unjust measure, is there with God too an unjust measure by which it will be measured back to us? For by the name "measure" I think the judgment itself is signified. In no way does God either judge rashly or repay anyone with an unjust measure. But this was said because the very rashness by which you punish another must of necessity punish you. Unless perhaps we are to suppose that injustice does some harm to the one against whom it proceeds, but none to the one from whom it proceeds. On the contrary, often it does no harm to the one who suffers the wrong, but it must of necessity harm the one who does it. For what harm did the injustice of their persecutors do to the martyrs? Yet it did the greatest harm to the persecutors themselves. For even if some of them were later set right, nevertheless at the time when they were persecuting, their malice was blinding them. So too rash judgment for the most part does no harm to the one who is rashly judged, but the rashness itself must of necessity harm the one who judges rashly.

I think that saying too belongs to this rule: *all who take the sword will perish by the sword*[^181]. For how many strike with the sword and yet do not perish by the sword—as Peter himself did not! But lest anyone suppose that he escaped such a penalty through the forgiveness of his sins—though nothing is more absurd than to suppose that the penalty of the sword, which did not befall Peter, could have been greater than that of the cross, which did befall him—what then will he say of the robbers who were crucified with the Lord, since the one who earned forgiveness earned it after he was crucified, while the other did not earn it at all[^182]? Or had they perhaps crucified all whom they had killed, and for that reason earned to suffer this themselves? It is ridiculous to suppose this. What else, then, was said by *for all who strike with the sword will perish by the sword*, except that the soul dies by that very sin, whatever it has committed?

## How far anger is removed from hatred.

**19. 63.** And since in this place the Lord warns us against rash and unjust judgment—for he wills that with a single heart, intent on the one God, we do whatever we do; and many things it is uncertain with what heart they are done, about which it is rash to judge, and those especially judge rashly about uncertain matters and readily rebuke who love rather to revile and condemn than to amend and correct, which vice belongs either to pride or to envy—he consequently adds and says: *why do you see the speck in your brother's eye, but do not see the beam in your own eye?*[^183], so that, if for example that man sinned through anger, you rebuke him through hatred. Now as far as the speck is from the beam, so far, as it were, is anger removed from hatred. For hatred is anger grown old, anger which by its very age has gained such strength that it deserves to be called a beam. But it can happen that, if you are angry with a man, you may wish him to be corrected; whereas if you hate a man, you cannot wish to correct him.

## To correct vices is the duty of good and well-disposed men.

**19. 64.** *For how do you say to your brother, "Let me cast the speck out of your eye," and behold, there is a beam in your own eye? Hypocrite, first cast the beam out of your own eye, and then you will see clearly to cast the speck out of your brother's eye*[^184]—that is: "first drive hatred away from yourself, and then you will be able to amend the one whom you love." And he says well, *hypocrite*. For to accuse vices is the duty of good and well-disposed men; and when bad men do this, they play a part that is not their own, like hypocrites who hide under a mask what they are and display in their mask what they are not. By the name "hypocrites," then, you may understand dissemblers. And the kind of dissemblers is truly much to be guarded against and is troublesome—those who, while they undertake their accusations of all vices out of hatred and spite, want even to be seen as advisers. And therefore we must watch piously and carefully, so that when necessity compels us to rebuke or chide someone, we first consider whether the vice is such as we never had or such as we have now been freed from. And if we never had it, let us consider that we too are human and could have had it; but if we did have it and no longer have it, let our common weakness touch our memory, so that not hatred but mercy may go before that rebuke or chiding, so that—whether it avails for the correction of the one for whose sake we do it, or for his perversion (for the outcome is uncertain)—we ourselves may at least be secure in the singleness of our eye. But if, considering ourselves, we find that we are in the same vice as the one we were preparing to rebuke, let us not rebuke or chide, but rather groan together with him; and let us invite him not to obey us but to strive together with us!

## Paul acted not out of dissimulation but out of love.

**19. 65.** For that too which the apostle says—*to the Jews I became as a Jew, that I might win the Jews; to those under the Law as one under the Law, though I myself am not under the Law, that I might win those who were under the Law; to those outside the Law as one outside the Law, though I am not outside the Law of God but am under the Law of Christ, that I might win those who are outside the Law. To the weak I became weak, that I might win the weak. I have become all things to all, that I might win all*[^185]—he certainly did not do out of dissimulation, as some want it to be understood, so that their own detestable dissimulation may be fortified by the authority of so great an example; rather, he did this out of love, by which he regarded as his own the weakness of the one he wished to help.

For he prepares the ground for this by saying: *for though I am free from all, I have made myself a slave to all, that I might win the more*[^186]. And that you may understand this to be done not out of dissimulation but out of love, by which we have compassion on weak persons as though we were they, he gives this warning in another place: *you were called to freedom, brethren; only do not use your freedom as an opportunity for the flesh, but through love be servants of one another*[^187]. This cannot be done unless each one holds another's weakness as though it were his own, so as to bear it with equanimity, until the one whose salvation he cares for is freed from it.

## Rebukes are to be employed rarely, so that we may serve not ourselves but God.

**19. 66.** Rebukes, then, are to be employed rarely and only under great necessity, yet in such a way that even in these very rebukes we press for God to be served, not ourselves. For he is the end, so that we do nothing with a double heart, removing from our own eye the beam of envy or malice or dissimulation, that we may see clearly to cast the speck out of our brother's eye. For we shall see it with the eyes of a dove[^188]—such eyes as are praised in the bride of Christ, whom God chose for himself as a glorious Church having no spot or wrinkle, that is, pure and single[^189].

## To disclose those things which they to whom they are disclosed cannot bear always does harm.

**20. 67.** But since the name of singleness can deceive some who desire to obey God's precepts, so that they suppose it to be sometimes a vice to conceal what is true, just as it is sometimes a vice to speak what is false, and in this way, by disclosing those things which they to whom they are disclosed cannot bear, they do more harm than if they concealed them altogether and always, he most rightly adds: *do not give what is holy to the dogs, and do not throw your pearls before swine, lest they trample them under their feet and turn to attack you*[^190]. For the Lord too, although he lied in nothing, nevertheless showed that he concealed certain true things when he said: *I have yet many things to say to you, but you cannot bear them now*[^191]. And the apostle Paul: *I could not*, he says, *address you as spiritual but as carnal. As to little ones in Christ, I gave you milk to drink, not solid food; for you were not able, but neither even now are you able, for you are still carnal*[^192].

## We must carefully inquire what is holy, what are the pearls, what are the dogs, what are the swine.

**20. 68.** Now in this precept, by which we are forbidden to give what is holy to the dogs and to throw our pearls before swine, we must carefully inquire what is *holy*, what are *pearls*, what are *dogs*, what are *swine*. The *holy* is that which it is wrong to violate and corrupt; and the attempt and the will to commit such a crime is held guilty, even though that holy thing remains by nature inviolable and incorruptible. *Pearls* are whatever spiritual things are to be esteemed as great; and because they lie hidden in secret, they are, as it were, dug up from the deep and found in the coverings of allegories as though in opened shells. So one may understand that one and the same thing can be called both *holy* and a *pearl*—*holy* from the fact that it ought not to be corrupted, a *pearl* from the fact that it ought not to be despised. Now anyone tries to corrupt what he does not want to remain whole; but he despises what he holds cheap and reckons to be, as it were, beneath himself, and therefore whatever is despised is said to be trampled upon. Wherefore *dogs*, because they leap forward to tear apart; and what they tear apart they do not allow to remain whole. *Do not*, he says, *give what is holy to the dogs*[^193], because, even though it cannot be torn apart and corrupted and remains whole and inviolable, nevertheless one must consider what those people intend who resist sharply and most hostilely, and who, so far as in them lies, try, if it could be done, to destroy the truth. *Swine*, however, although they do not attack with the bite as *dogs* do, nevertheless defile by trampling here and there. *Do not*, therefore, he says, *throw your pearls before swine, lest they trample them under their feet and turn to attack you*[^194]. Therefore I think it is not inappropriate that *dogs* are put for assailants of the truth, and *swine* for despisers of it.

## We must be careful not to disclose anything to one who does not grasp it; for what is disclosed he can either attack or neglect.

**20. 69.** But as for what he says, *turn to attack you*, he does not say "tear apart the pearls themselves." For by trampling them, even when they turn back to hear something further, they nevertheless tear apart the one from whom they have already trampled the pearls that were thrown. For you will not easily find what could be pleasing to one who has trampled pearls—that is, who has despised divine discoveries gained with great labor. But how the one who teaches such people is not torn apart by their indignation and fretting, I do not see. Now each animal is unclean, both the *dog* and the *swine*. We must be careful, therefore, not to disclose anything to one who does not grasp it; for it is better that he seek what is closed than that what is disclosed he should either attack or neglect. Nor is any other cause found why things that are manifest and great are not received, except hatred or contempt; on account of the one men are called *dogs*, on account of the other *swine*. Yet all this uncleanness is conceived from love of temporal things—that is, from love of this world, which we are commanded to renounce, so that we may be able to be clean. Whoever, then, desires to have a single and clean heart ought not to think himself guilty if he conceals something which the one from whom he conceals it cannot grasp.

Nor is it to be supposed from this that it is permitted to lie; for it does not follow that, when what is true is concealed, what is false is spoken. We must therefore act first to remove the impediments by which it comes about that he does not grasp it; for surely, if he does not grasp because of his filth, he must be cleansed either by word or by deed, as far as we are able.

## On useful matters we must sometimes answer the very thing we are asked, as the Lord did.

**20. 70.** But as for the fact that our Lord is found to have said certain things which many who were present did not receive, whether by resisting or by despising, he is not to be thought to have given *what is holy* to *dogs* or to have thrown *pearls* before *swine*; for he did not give them to those who could not grasp them, but to those who could and who were present at the same time, who ought not to be neglected on account of the uncleanness of the others. And when his tempters questioned him, he answered them in such a way that they had nothing to contradict, although they wasted away with their own poisons rather than being satisfied with his food; yet others who could grasp it heard many useful things on the occasion of those men.

I have said this lest perhaps anyone, when he has been unable to answer a questioner, should think himself excused by this saying if he says that he does not want to give *what is holy to the dogs* or to throw *pearls before swine*. For he who knows what to answer ought to answer, if only for the sake of others, who are seized by despair if they believe that the question put cannot be solved—and this of useful matters pertaining to the instruction of salvation.

For there are many things which can be inquired into by idle people—superfluous and empty and for the most part harmful—about which nevertheless something must be said: but this very thing must be disclosed and explained, namely why such things ought not to be inquired into. On useful matters, however, we must sometimes answer the very thing we are asked, as the Lord did when the Sadducees questioned him about the woman who had seven husbands, whose of them she would be in the resurrection to come. For he answered that *in the resurrection they neither marry nor are given in marriage, but are like the angels in heaven*[^195].

But sometimes the one who asks is to be asked something else, which nevertheless, if he states it, will let him answer himself as to what he asked; and if he refuses to state it, it will not seem unjust to those present if he too does not hear an answer to what he asked. For those also who questioned him, tempting him, whether tribute ought to be paid, were asked something else—namely, whose image the coin bore that was produced by them; and because they said what they were asked—namely, that the coin bore Caesar's image—they in a way answered themselves as to what they had asked the Lord. And so from their answer he drew this conclusion: *render therefore to Caesar what is Caesar's, and to God what is God's*[^196].

And when the chief priests and the elders of the people questioned him by what authority he did those things, he questioned them about the baptism of John; and when they were unwilling to say what they saw was being said against them, yet did not dare to say anything bad about John because of the bystanders, he said: *neither will I tell you by what authority I do these things*[^197]—which appeared most just to the bystanders. For they said they did not know what they did not fail to know but were unwilling to say. And in truth it was just that those who wanted answered to them what they had asked should first themselves do what they demanded should be done toward them; and if they had done this, they would certainly have answered themselves. For they themselves had sent to John, asking who he was—or rather, priests and Levites had been sent, thinking that he himself was the Christ, when he denied that he was and bore witness about the Lord[^198]. And if they had been willing to confess about that testimony, they would themselves have taught themselves by what authority the Christ did those things, which they had asked about as though they did not know, in order to find an opening for slander.

## Asking pertains to obtaining health of mind; seeking, to finding the truth.

**21. 71.** Since, then, it had been enjoined that *what is holy* not be given to *dogs* and that *pearls* not be thrown before *swine*, the hearer could meet this and say—aware of his own ignorance and weakness, and hearing it enjoined on him that he not give what he perceived he had not yet himself received—he could, then, meet this and say: "what holy thing do you forbid me to give to dogs, and what pearls do you forbid me to throw before swine, when I do not yet see that I have them?"

Most opportunely he added, saying: *ask, and it will be given you; seek, and you will find; knock, and it will be opened to you. For everyone who asks receives, and he who seeks finds, and to him who knocks it will be opened*[^199]. Asking pertains to obtaining health and strength of mind, so that we may be able to fulfill what is enjoined; but seeking pertains to finding the truth. For since the blessed life is completed by action and by knowledge, action requires the capacity of strength, and contemplation requires the manifestation of things. The first of these, then, is to be asked for, the second to be sought, so that the one may be given and the other found. But knowledge in this life is of the way rather than of the possession itself. Yet when anyone has found the true way, he will arrive at the possession itself, which nevertheless will be opened to him who knocks.

## Asking, seeking, knocking, made plain by an illustration.

**21. 72.** So that these three things—that is, *asking, seeking, knocking*—may be made plain, let us set down, by way of an illustration, someone who cannot walk because his feet are weak. First, then, he must be healed and strengthened for walking, and to this belongs what he said, *ask*. But what does it profit him that he can now walk or even run, if he wanders by byways? The second thing, then, is that he find the way that leads where he wishes to arrive. And when he has held to it and traveled it, if he finds the very place where he wishes to dwell closed, it will have profited him nothing to have been able to walk and to have walked and arrived, unless it is opened; to this belongs what was said, *knock*.

## If evil men give good things, how much we ought to hope that God will give good things to us when we ask, and that we cannot be deceived.

**21. 73.** Now he gave great hope, he who does not deceive in promising; for he says: *everyone who asks receives, and he who seeks finds, and to him who knocks it will be opened*[^200]. Therefore perseverance is needed, so that we may receive what we ask, and find what we seek, and have opened to us what we knock at.

Just as he spoke of the birds of the air and of the lilies of the field[^201], so that we might not despair of food and clothing being at hand for us, so that our hope might rise from lesser things to greater, so too in this place he says: *or what man of you, if his son asks him for bread, will hand him a stone? Or if he asks for a fish, will hand him a serpent? If you then, who are evil, know how to give good gifts to your children, how much more will your Father who is in heaven give good things to those who ask him!*[^202]

How do evil men give good things? But he called "evil" those who still love this world, and sinners. The good things, however, which they give are to be called good according to their own way of thinking, because they regard these as good. And yet in the nature of things these are also good, but temporal and pertaining to this weak life. And whoever, being evil, gives them does not give of his own; for *the earth is the Lord's, and its fullness*[^203]—*who made heaven and earth and the sea and all that is in them*[^204]. How much, then, we ought to hope that God will give good things to us when we ask, and that we cannot be deceived so as to receive one thing for another when we ask of him, since even we, when we are evil, know how to give what we are asked! For we do not deceive our children; and whatever good things we give, we give not of our own but of his.

## Whatever you wish that men should do to you, do so to them.

**22. 74.** Now firmness and a certain vigor for walking along the way of wisdom is established in good morals, which are brought all the way to cleansing and singleness of heart, about which, speaking now for some time, he concludes thus: *all things, therefore, whatever you wish that men should do for you, do so to them; for this is the Law and the prophets*[^205]. In the Greek copies we find it thus: *all things, therefore, whatever you wish that men should do for you, do so to them*. But I think "good" was added by the Latins to make the meaning plain. For there occurred the difficulty that, if anyone should wish something shameful to be done toward him and should apply this saying to it—as if someone should wish to be challenged to drink immoderately and gorge himself with cups, and should do this first to the one by whom he desires it to be done to himself—it is ridiculous to suppose that this man has fulfilled this saying. Since, then, this raised a difficulty, as I think, one word was added to make the matter plain, so that after it was said *all things, therefore, whatever you wish that men should do for you*, "good" was added. But if it is lacking in the Greek copies, those too should be emended. But who would dare this? It is to be understood, then, that the saying is full and altogether complete, even if this word is not added. For what is said, *whatever you wish*, ought to be taken as said not in the customary and loose way but properly. For there is no "willing" except in good things; for in evil and shameful deeds it is properly called desire, not will. Not that the Scriptures always speak so, but where it is fitting, they keep so strictly to the proper word that they allow nothing else to be understood.

## By this one saying he has embraced both of those precepts.

**22. 75.** But this precept seems to pertain to the love of neighbor and not also to the love of God, since in another place he says that there are two precepts on which *the whole Law depends and* all *the prophets*[^206]. For if he had said, "all things whatever you wish to be done to you, these do you also do," by this one saying he would have embraced both of those precepts; for it would readily be said that each one wishes to be loved both by God and by men. And so, when this was enjoined on him—that what he wished to be done to himself he should do—this surely would be enjoined: that he should love both God and men. But when it is said more expressly of men, *all things, therefore, whatever you wish that men should do for you, do so to them*[^207], nothing else seems to be said than: *you shall love your neighbor as yourself*.

But we must not carelessly fail to attend to what he added here: *for this is the Law and the prophets*[^208]. But in those two precepts he did not merely say *the Law depends and the prophets*, but added *the whole Law and* all *the prophets*, which stands for the whole of prophecy. Since he did not add this here, he has reserved a place for the other precept, which pertains to the love of God. But here, since he is pursuing the precepts of a single heart, and toward those persons especially it is to be feared that anyone might have a double heart, namely those from whom the heart can be concealed—that is, toward men—this very thing had to be enjoined. For there is scarcely anyone who would wish anyone to deal with him with a double heart. But this cannot be done—that is, that a man bestow something on a man with a single heart—unless he so bestows it that he expects no temporal advantage from him, and acts with that intention which we have sufficiently treated above, when we were speaking of the single eye.

## The eye, cleansed and made single, will be fit for seeing God.

**22. 76.** The eye, then, cleansed and made single, will be fit and suited to behold and contemplate its own interior light. For this eye is the eye of the heart. And such an eye that man has who does not set the end of his good works—so that they may be truly good works—in pleasing men, but, even if it happens that he pleases them, refers this rather to their salvation and to the glory of God, not to his own empty boasting; nor does he work any good thing for the salvation of his neighbor in order to procure from it the things necessary for getting through this life; nor does he rashly condemn the mind and will of a man in a deed in which it does not appear with what mind and will it was done; and whatever services he renders to a man, he renders with that intention with which he wishes them rendered to himself—that is, so as not to expect from him any temporal advantage. So the heart will be single and clean, in which God is sought. *Blessed*, therefore, *are the clean of heart, for they shall see God*[^209].

## Blessed is he who hears the precepts of the Lord and does them (23, 77—27, 87)

## The narrow way that leads to life, and the strait gate by which it is entered.

**23. 77.** But because this belongs to few, he now begins to speak of investigating and possessing wisdom, which is the tree of life[^210]. And surely, to investigate and possess it—that is, to contemplate it—such an eye has been led through all the foregoing, by which the narrow way and the strait gate may now be seen. So what he next says—*enter by the strait gate; for wide is the gate and broad the way that leads to destruction, and many are those who enter by it. How strait is the gate and narrow the way that leads to life, and few are those who find it*[^211]—he does not say because the Lord's yoke is harsh or his burden heavy, but because few are willing to bring their labors to an end, believing too little him who cries out: *come to me, all you who labor, and I will refresh you. Take my yoke and learn from me, for I am gentle and humble of heart; for my yoke is easy and my burden is light*[^212]—and from this point this sermon took its beginning, from the humble and gentle of heart[^213]—since many refuse that easy yoke and light burden, and few take it up; and so the way that leads to life is narrow, and the gate by which it is entered is strait.

## A tree is known by its fruits.

**24. 78.** Here, then, those especially are to be guarded against who promise the wisdom and knowledge of truth which they do not have, such as heretics, who for the most part commend themselves by their fewness. And therefore, when he had said that there are few who find the strait gate and the narrow way, lest those men put themselves forward under the name of fewness, he at once added: *beware of false prophets, who come to you in sheep's clothing, but inwardly are ravenous wolves*[^214]. But these do not deceive the single eye, which knows how to discern a tree by its fruits; for he says: *by their fruits you shall know them. Then he adds comparisons: are grapes gathered from thorns, or figs from thistles? So every good tree bears good fruit, but a bad tree bears bad fruit. A good tree cannot bear bad fruit, nor can a bad tree bear good fruit. Every tree that does not bear good fruit is cut down and thrown into the fire. Therefore by their fruits you shall know them*[^215].

## A good tree cannot bear bad fruit, nor can a bad tree bear good fruit.

**24. 79.** In this place the error of those is especially to be guarded against who, from these very two trees, suppose that there are two natures, one of which is God's, but the other neither God's nor from God. About this error there has already been a fuller discussion in other books, and, if that is still too little, there will be; but for now we must show that these two trees do not aid them. First, because it is so clear that he is speaking of men, that whoever reads what precedes and what follows would marvel at their blindness. Next, they attend to what is said, *a good tree cannot bear bad fruit, nor can a bad tree bear good fruit*[^216], and therefore they suppose that a bad soul can neither be changed for the better nor a good one for the worse, as though it had been said, "a good tree cannot become bad, nor can a bad tree become good." But it was said, *a good tree cannot bear bad fruit, nor can a bad tree bear good fruit*. The tree, indeed, is the soul itself, that is, the man himself; but the fruit is the works of the man. Therefore an evil man cannot do good works, nor a good man evil ones.

An evil man, therefore, if he wishes to do good works, let him first become good. So in another place the Lord himself says more evidently: *either make the tree good, or make the tree bad*[^217]. But if he were figuring two natures by these two trees, he would not say *make*. For what man can make a nature? Then, there too, when he had made mention of these two trees, he added: *hypocrites, how can you speak good things, when you are evil?*[^218] As long, therefore, as anyone is evil, he cannot bear good fruit; for if he bears good fruit, he will no longer be evil. So it could most truly be said, "snow cannot be warm"; for when it begins to be warm, we no longer call it snow but water. It can come about, then, that what was snow no longer is, but it cannot come about that snow is warm. So it can come about that he who was evil is no longer evil, yet it cannot come about that an evil man does good.

Such a one, even if he is sometimes useful, does not himself do this, but it comes about through him by divine providence ordering it, as was said of the Pharisees: *do what they say, but do not do what they do*[^219]. The very fact that they said good things, and that the things they said were usefully heard and done, was not theirs; for, he says, *they sit on Moses' seat*[^220]. Therefore, preaching the Law of God through divine providence, they can be useful to those who hear, although they are not useful to themselves. Of such men it was said in another place through the prophet: *you sow wheat and reap thorns*[^221], because they enjoin good things and do evil things.

Therefore those who heard them and did the things that were said by them did not gather grapes from thorns, but gathered grapes from the vine through the thorns; just as if someone should put his hand through a hedge, or should gather a grape from a vine that had been entangled with the hedge, that fruit is not of the thorns but of the vine.

## Beware of doing your righteousness before men, to be seen by them.

**24. 80.** It is most rightly asked, indeed, what fruits he wished us to attend to, by which we might know the tree. For many reckon among the fruits certain things that pertain to the sheep's clothing, and in this way are deceived by wolves: such as fasts or prayers or almsgiving; and unless all these could also be done by hypocrites, he would not say above, *beware of doing your righteousness before men, to be seen by them*[^222]. Having set forth this saying, he pursues those very three things: almsgiving, prayer, fasting. For many bestow much on the poor, not out of mercy but out of ambition; and many pray, or rather seem to pray, not looking to God but desiring to please men; and many fast and put forward a remarkable abstinence before those to whom these things seem difficult and are reckoned worthy of honor.

And by tricks of this kind they catch them, while they display one thing to deceive and bring forth another to plunder or to kill those who, under this sheep's clothing, cannot see the wolves. These, then, are not the fruits by which he warns us to know the tree. For these things, when they are done in truth with a good intention, are the proper garments of sheep; but when done with an evil intention in error, they cover nothing other than wolves. But sheep ought not for that reason to hate their own clothing, because wolves often conceal themselves in it.

## What are the fruits by which, when found, we may know a tree to be bad or good.

**24. 81.** What, then, are the fruits by which, when found, we may know a bad tree? The apostle says: *now the works of the flesh are manifest, which are fornications, uncleannesses, debaucheries, idol worship, sorceries, enmities, contentions, jealousies, animosities, dissensions, heresies, envies, drunkenness, carousing, and the like; about which I tell you beforehand, as I have told you before, that those who do such things will not possess the kingdom of God*. And what are the fruits by which we may know a good tree, the same apostle says consequently: *but the fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, long-suffering, kindness, goodness, faith, gentleness, self-control*[^223]. It must indeed be known that "joy" is here used properly; for evil men are properly said not to rejoice but to exult, just as we said above that "will" was used properly, which evil men do not have, where it was said, *all things whatever you wish that men should do for you, these do you also do to them*[^224]. From this proper use of the word, by which joy is not spoken of except in good men, the prophet also speaks: *there is no rejoicing for the wicked, says the Lord*[^225]. So too, where faith is set down, it is certainly not any faith whatever but true *faith*; and the rest of the things set down here have certain images of themselves in evil men and deceivers, so that they deceive altogether, unless one has now a clean and single eye by which to know these things. And so, in the best order, the eye was first dealt with, to be cleansed, and then the things to be guarded against were stated.

## These are the fruits: to do the will of the Father who is in heaven.

**25. 82.** But since, however clean of eye anyone may be—that is, however much he lives with a sincere and single heart—he nevertheless cannot behold another's heart, whatever things could not appear in deeds or words are disclosed by temptations.

Now temptation is of two kinds: either in the hope of obtaining some temporal advantage, or in the fear of losing one. And we must especially be careful that, while striving toward wisdom, which can be found in Christ alone—*in whom are hidden all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge*[^226]—we must, then, be careful that we not be deceived by that very name of Christ, through heretics or through any who understand badly and are lovers of this world. For this is why there follows, and he warns: *not everyone who says to me, "Lord, Lord," will enter the kingdom of heaven; but he who does the will of my Father who is in heaven, he will enter the kingdom of heaven*[^227]—lest we suppose that it already pertains to those fruits if someone says to our Lord *Lord, Lord*, and on that account the tree seem good to us. But these are the fruits: to do the will of the Father who is in heaven, the doing of which he deigned to offer himself as an example.

## No one says, "the Lord Jesus," except in the Holy Spirit.

**25. 83.** But it can rightly raise a difficulty how that saying of the apostle agrees with this, where he says: *no one speaking in the Spirit of God says, "a curse on Jesus"; and no one can say, "the Lord Jesus," except in the Holy Spirit*[^228]; for we can neither say that some who have the Holy Spirit will not enter the kingdom of heaven, if they persevere to the end, nor can we say that those who say *Lord, Lord* and yet do not enter the kingdom of heaven have the Holy Spirit. How, then, is it that *no one says, "the Lord Jesus," except in the Holy Spirit*, except that the apostle properly used the word that is *says*, so as to signify the will and understanding of the one saying it? But the Lord used the word generally where he says, *not everyone who says to me, "Lord, Lord," will enter the kingdom of heaven*[^229]. For even one who neither wills nor understands what he says seems to say it; but he properly says who declares his will and mind by the sound of his voice. So, a little earlier, what was said, "joy," in the fruits of the Spirit was said properly, not in the way in which the same apostle says elsewhere, *it does not rejoice over iniquity*[^230], as though anyone could rejoice over iniquity; for that is the elation of a mind turbulently exulting, not joy; for this only the good have.

Therefore those too seem to say who do not discern by understanding and carry out by will what they utter in sound, but only utter it with the voice; and it is in this sense that the Lord says, *not everyone who says to me, "Lord, Lord," will enter the kingdom of heaven*. But those truly and properly say from whose will and mind the utterance of their speech does not differ; and it is according to this sense that the apostle says, *no one can say, "the Lord Jesus," except in the Holy Spirit*.

## We must not suppose that there is invisible wisdom where we see a visible miracle.

**25. 84.** And this is especially to the point: that, while striving toward the contemplation of truth, we not be deceived—not only by the name of Christ, through those who have the name and do not have the deeds, but also by certain deeds and miracles. Although the Lord did such things for the sake of unbelievers, he nevertheless warned us not to be deceived by such things, supposing that there is invisible Wisdom where we have seen a visible miracle. He adds, then, and says: *many will say to me on that day, "Lord, Lord, did we not prophesy in your name, and in your name cast out demons, and in your name do many mighty works?" And then I will declare to them, "I never knew you; depart from me, you who work iniquity"*[^231]. He therefore knows none but him who works equity. For he forbade even his own disciples to rejoice over such things, that is, that the demons were subject to them, but *rejoice*, he said, *that your names are written in heaven*[^232]—in that city Jerusalem, I believe, which is in heaven, in which none but the just and holy will reign. *Do you not know*, says the apostle, *that the unjust will not possess the kingdom of God?*[^233]

## The unjust too can perform visible miracles.

**25. 85.** But perhaps someone may say that the unjust cannot perform those visible miracles, and may rather believe that those are lying who are going to say *in your name we prophesied and cast out demons and did many mighty works*. Let him read, then, how many things the magicians of the Egyptians did in resisting God's servant Moses[^234]! Or, if he is unwilling to read this, because they did not do it in the name of Christ, let him read what the Lord himself says, speaking thus of false prophets: *then if anyone says to you, "behold, here is the Christ," or "there," do not believe it. For false christs and false prophets will arise and will give great signs and wonders, so as to lead astray, if possible, even the elect. Behold, I have told you beforehand*[^235].

## A clean and single eye is needed to come to the most certain peace and the immovable stability of wisdom.

**25. 86.** How clean and single an eye is needed, then, that the way of wisdom may be found, against which so many deceptions and errors of evil and perverse men clamor! To escape all these is to come to the most certain peace and the immovable stability of wisdom. For it is greatly to be feared that, through zeal for wrangling and contending, anyone may fail to see what can be seen by few—so that the clamor of those who contradict is slight, unless one also clamors against himself. To this belongs also what the apostle says: *the servant of the Lord must not quarrel but be gentle toward all, apt to teach, patient, correcting with modesty those who think differently, that perhaps God may grant them repentance unto the knowledge of the truth*[^236]. *Blessed*, therefore, *are the peacemakers, for they shall be called the children of God*[^237].

## The conclusion of this whole sermon: he builds on Christ who does what he hears from him.

**25. 87.** How terribly, then, the conclusion of this whole sermon is brought in must be carefully attended to. *Everyone, therefore*, he says, *who hears these words of mine and does them will be like a wise man who built his house upon the rock*[^238]. For no one makes firm what he hears or perceives except by doing it. And if the rock is Christ, as many testimonies of the Scriptures proclaim[^239], he builds on Christ who does what he hears from him. *The rain came down, the floods came, the winds blew and beat against that house, and it did not fall; for it was founded upon the rock*[^240]. This man, then, does not fear any dark superstitions—for what else is to be understood by rain, when it is set down in the signification of something evil?—nor the rumors of men, which I think are compared to winds, nor the river of this life with its carnal desires, as though flowing over the earth. For of these three, he who is led on by prosperity is broken by adversity; and none of these does he fear who has his house founded upon the rock, that is, who not only hears the precepts of the Lord but also does them. And to all these dangers he is subject who hears and does not do; for he has no stable foundation, but by hearing and not doing he builds a ruin. For he goes on consequently: *and everyone who hears these words of mine and does not do them will be like a foolish man who built his house upon the sand. The rain came down, the floods came, the winds blew and beat against that house, and it fell; and great was its fall. And it came to pass, when Jesus had finished these words, that the crowds were astonished at his teaching; for he was teaching them as one having authority, and not as their scribes*[^241]. This is what I said before was signified by the prophet in the psalms, when he said: *I will deal confidently in him. The words of the Lord are pure words, silver tried by fire, purged of earth, seven times refined*[^242]. On account of this number I was prompted to refer these precepts too to those seven sayings which he set down at the beginning of this sermon, when he spoke of the blessed, and to those seven workings of the Holy Spirit which the prophet Isaiah recalls[^243]. But whether this order is to be considered in these things, or some other, the things we have heard from the Lord are to be done, if we wish to build upon the rock.
