# How the ten precepts of the Law are to be divided.

**71.** 1. (20, 1-17) It is asked how the ten precepts of the Law are to be divided: whether there are four, down to the precept concerning the Sabbath, which pertain to God himself; and the remaining six, of which the first is, *honor your father and mother,* which pertain to man; or rather there are three of the former and seven of the latter. For those who say there are four of the former separate what was said: *you shall have no other gods besides me;* so that it is another precept, *you shall not make for yourself an idol,* etc., where the worship of images is forbidden. And they want this to be one: *you shall not covet your neighbor's wife; you shall not covet your neighbor's house,* and all the rest down to the end.

But those who say there are three of the former and seven of the latter want whatever is prescribed concerning worshiping the one God to be one—that nothing else besides him be worshiped as God; but they divide these last into two: so that it is one thing, *you shall not covet your neighbor's wife,* and another, *you shall not covet your neighbor's house.* Yet that there are ten precepts neither party doubts, since Scripture testifies this.

**71.** 2. To me, however, it seems more fitting to take three of the former and seven of the latter, because those that pertain to God also seem, to those who look more carefully, to suggest the Trinity. And in truth, what was said, *you shall have no other gods besides me,* is more fully explained when the worship of images is forbidden. Moreover, the coveting of another's wife and the coveting of another's house differ so much in the sinning, that to the words *you shall not covet your neighbor's house* there are joined the other things that Scripture says, *nor his field, nor his manservant, nor his maidservant, nor his ox, nor his beast of burden, nor any of his cattle, nor whatever is your neighbor's.*

But it seems to have distinguished the coveting of another's wife from the coveting of any other thing of another's, since it began both thus: *you shall not covet your neighbor's wife; you shall not covet your neighbor's house,* and to this latter it began to join the rest. For when it had said, *you shall not covet your neighbor's wife,* it did not connect to this the others, saying, nor his house, nor his field, nor his manservant, and the rest: but it altogether appears that these are joined together, which seem to be contained in one precept, and distinct from that where the wife is named. But that where it was said, *you shall have no other gods besides me,* appears to have its more careful working-out in the things that are subjoined. For to what does this pertain: *you shall not make for yourself an idol, nor any likeness of whatever things are in heaven above, and whatever things are in the earth below, and whatever things are in the water under the earth; you shall not adore them, nor serve them,* except to that which was said: *you shall have no other gods besides me*?

**71.** 3. But again it is asked in what way *you shall not steal* differs from what is prescribed a little after concerning not coveting one's neighbor's things. Not, indeed, that everyone who covets his neighbor's thing steals; but if everyone who steals covets his neighbor's thing, then under that generality, where it is prescribed not to covet one's neighbor's thing, what pertains to theft too could be contained. Similarly it is also asked in what way *you shall not commit adultery* differs from what is said a little after: *you shall not covet your neighbor's wife.* For in what was said, *you shall not commit adultery,* that too could be understood.

Unless perhaps in those two precepts, not committing adultery and not stealing, the acts themselves are noted; but in these last, the coveting itself: which differ so much that sometimes a man commits adultery who does not covet his neighbor's wife, when some other cause unites him to her; while sometimes he covets and yet is not united to her, fearing punishment: and this perhaps the Law wished to show, that both are sins.

**71.** 4. Likewise it is commonly asked whether fornication too is included under the name of adultery. For "adultery" (*moechia*) is a Greek word, which Scripture already uses in place of a Latin one. Yet in Greek *moechos* means only "adulterers." But surely this Law was given not only to the men among the people, but also to the women. For just because it was said, *you shall not covet your neighbor's wife,* a woman ought not to think that nothing is here prescribed to her, and that she may, as it were lawfully, covet her neighbor's husband. If, then, here from what was said to a man it is understood—though it was not said—that it pertains also to a woman, how much more by what was said, *you shall not commit adultery,* is each sex bound, since the precept itself can be referred to both, just as: *you shall not kill, you shall not steal,* and the others which likewise, with no one sex expressed, seem to speak to both in common?

Yet where one is expressed, it is surely the more honored that is expressed, that is, the masculine, so that from this the woman too may understand what is prescribed to her. And by this, if a woman who has a husband is an adulteress by lying with one who is not her husband, even if he have no wife; surely a man who has a wife is also an adulterer by lying with one who is not his wife, even if she have no husband. But whether, if one who has no wife does this, or a woman who has no husband, they are held by the transgression of this precept, is rightly asked. For if they are not held, fornication is not forbidden in the Decalogue, but only adultery; although all adultery is understood to be fornication too, as the Scriptures speak. For the Lord says in the Gospel: *whoever puts away his wife, except for the cause of fornication, makes her commit adultery*[^1]. Here he surely called it fornication, if she who has a husband sins with another, which is adultery. All adultery, therefore, is also called fornication in the Scriptures.

But whether all fornication too can be called adultery, no example of such usage in those same Scriptures occurs to me for the moment. But if not all fornication can also be called adultery, where in the Decalogue that fornication is forbidden which men who have no wives commit with women who have no husbands, I do not know whether it can be found. But if under the name of theft is rightly understood every unlawful usurpation of another's property (for he who forbade theft did not permit robbery; but surely wished the whole to be understood from a part—whatever is unlawfully taken from a neighbor's goods); then surely under the name of adultery too every unlawful intercourse, and every unlawful use of those members, ought to be understood as forbidden.

**71.** 5. And as for what was said, *you shall not kill,* it is not to be thought done against this precept when the Law kills, or when God commands someone to be killed. For he does it who commands, when it is not permitted to refuse the service.

**71.** 6. In what was also said, *you shall not bear false witness against your neighbor,* it is commonly asked whether every lie is forbidden: lest perhaps this precept be not against those who say that one may then lie, when the lie profits someone and does no harm to the one you lie to. For such a lie is not *against your neighbor,* so that Scripture seems to have added this for that reason—Scripture, which could have said briefly, *you shall not bear false witness,* as it said, *you shall not kill, you shall not commit adultery, you shall not steal.* But here there is a great question, which cannot be conveniently explained by those in haste, how this is to be taken: *you will destroy all who speak a lie*[^2]; and: *be not willing to tell any lie at all*[^3]; and other things of this kind.

[^1]: Matt 5:32.
[^2]: Ps 5:7 (6).
[^3]: Sir 7:14.
