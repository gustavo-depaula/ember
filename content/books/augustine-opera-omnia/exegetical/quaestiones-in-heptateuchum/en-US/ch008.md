# Book VII: Questions on Judges

## From what time the book of Judges begins.

**1.** At the end of the book of *Joshua* the son of Nun the narrator briefly extended the history to the point where the children of Israel turned aside to worship foreign gods[^1]: but in this book it returns to the order, how the subsequent things were done after the death of Joshua the son of Nun. The book, then, does not begin from that time when the people fell away to worshiping images; but from earlier intervening times, in which were done those things after which it came to that point.

## Concerning the question who is the leader for making war.

**2.** (1:1-3) *And it came to pass, after Joshua died, that the children of Israel inquired of the Lord, saying: Who shall go up with us against the Canaanite as a leader to make war on him? And the Lord said: Judah shall go up; behold, I have given the land into his hand.*[^2]

Here it is asked whether some man was called Judah, or whether it so called the tribe itself, as is customary. But those who had inquired of the Lord were seeking a leader after the death of Joshua the son of Nun; hence it is thought that the name of one man is expressed. But because Scripture is not accustomed to name leaders when they are appointed without also mentioning the origin of their parents, and it is established that after Joshua the people of Israel had leaders, of whom the first is Othniel the son of Kenaz; it is more rightly understood that by the name Judah the tribe of Judah was signified. For from that tribe the Lord willed the crushing of the Canaanites to begin. And when the people had inquired about a leader, the Lord's response had this force, that they should know that God was unwilling for war to be waged against the Canaanites by the whole people. So he said: *Judah shall go up.*

And Scripture continues, narrating: *and Judah said to Simeon his brother:* certainly tribe to tribe. For those sons of Jacob who were called Judah and Simeon, named by their proper names among their other brothers, were no longer living; but the tribe of Judah said to the tribe of Simeon: *go up with me into my lot, and let us make war on the Canaanite, and I too will go with you into your lot.* It is plain that the tribe of Judah asked for itself the help of another tribe, which it would repay when those too began to have need in their own lot.

## At what time what is narrated about Caleb promising his daughter was done.

**3.** (1:9-12) *And Caleb said: Whoever strikes the City of Letters, and takes it, I will give him my daughter Achsah as a wife.* This was already mentioned in the book of Joshua the son of Nun[^3]: but whether it was done while Joshua was living, and is now repeated by recapitulation; or after his death, after it was said: *Judah shall go up,* and Judah began to subdue the Canaanites, in which war all these things are narrated as done, is rightly asked. But it is more credible that it was done after the death of Joshua, but here mentioned by prolepsis, that is, by anticipation, just as other things too.

For now, when the things done against the Canaanites by the tribe of Judah were being set forth, the order of the narration runs thus, among the other warlike deeds of Judah, of which the Lord had said after the death of Joshua: *Judah shall go up. And afterward the children of Judah went down to fight against the Canaanite who dwelt in the mountains and the south and the lowland. And Judah went against the Canaanite dwelling in Hebron, and Hebron came out to meet them. Now the name of Hebron was formerly Kiriath-arba-sepher, and he struck Sheshai and Ahiman and Talmai, the sons of Anak: and they went up from there against those dwelling in Debir. Now the name of Debir was formerly the City of Letters. And Caleb said: Whoever strikes the City of Letters, and takes it, I will give him my daughter as a wife.*

It is established, then, from this so clear order of the things done, that this was done after the death of Joshua. But then, when the cities given to Caleb himself were being mentioned, the narrator, advancing from the occasion, anticipated what was done afterward. Nor yet do I think that Scripture meant to mention twice in number this matter of Caleb's daughter given as the victor's reward without reason.

## The question of Caleb's daughter.

**4.** (1:14-15) Another question arises about Caleb's daughter, in that in the book of *Joshua* the son of Nun it is said of her thus: *and it came to pass, as she was going in, that she took counsel with him, saying: I will ask my father for a field. And she cried out from her ass,* and so on: where she asked her father for a field, and it was granted.[^4] But here it says: *and it came to pass, as he was going in, that Othniel urged her to ask her father for a field.* But in that it was said there, *as she was going in*; and here it was said: *as he was going in,* there is nothing contrary: for they were going in along the way together. But in that it was said there: *she took counsel with him,* that is, with her husband, *saying: I will ask my father for a field; and she cried out from her ass, and asked;* in that she took counsel, there she was urged to ask: of which the one is said there, the other here. But both would be said thus: "and she took counsel with him, saying: I will ask my father for a field: and he urged her; and she cried out from her ass."

Furthermore, that there she is reported to have asked for a field, and the name of that field is not passed over in silence; but here, although she was urged by her husband to ask for a field, she is said to have asked not for a field, *crying out from her beast of burden,* which there was said to be *from her ass,* but for *a redemption of water,* because she had been given over into a land of the south; and Scripture added: *and Caleb gave her, according to her heart, a redemption of the upper and a redemption of the lower*; what this means is obscure: unless perhaps the field itself was asked for this reason, that she might have from its fruits the means to buy water, the lack of which was in those regions into which she was being led as a bride. But, *Caleb gave her a redemption of the upper, and a redemption of the lower,* I do not see what we are to supply, unless of waters—of the upper, namely, in the mountains, of the lower, in the lowland or in the valleys.

## Since all the land is said to have been given to the children of Israel, how many lands are said not to have been given.

**5.** (1:18-19) *And Judah did not inherit Gaza and its territory, and Ashkelon and its territory, and Ekron and its territory, and Ashdod and its surroundings. And the Lord was with Judah, and he inherited the mountain, because he was not able to inherit those dwelling in the valley, because Rechab withstood them, and he had chariots of iron.*[^5]

What I said in the book of *Joshua* the son of Nun, when I treated that place where it is written: *and the Lord gave Israel all the land*[^6], when they did not yet possess many parts of it—that all the land could be understood to have been given thus, because what was not given into possession was given for a certain usefulness of exercise[^7]: this appears much more evidently here, since the cities are mentioned which Judah did not inherit, and it is said: *and the Lord was with Judah, and he inherited the mountain, because they were not able to inherit those dwelling in the valley.* For who does not understand that even this very thing pertained to that, that the Lord was with Judah, lest by obtaining the whole at once he should be puffed up?

For what it adds, *because Rechab withstood them, and he had chariots of iron;* it was said who feared the chariots—not the Lord, who was in Judah, but Judah himself: but if it is asked why he feared, with whom the Lord was, this is what must be prudently understood: that the propitious God breaks even in the hearts of his own the excesses of too great prosperity, so as to turn enemies to their use, not only when enemies are conquered, but even when they are feared; the one to commend his bounty, the other to repress their elation. For surely the enemy of the saints is the angel of Satan; whom nevertheless the Apostle says was given him to buffet him, lest he be puffed up by the greatness of the revelations.[^8]

## How things done in the time of Joshua are narrated.

**6.** (1:20) *And they gave Hebron to Caleb, as Moses had said; and he inherited from there the three cities of the sons of Anak, and took away from there the three sons of Anak.* This was already said in the book of *Joshua* the son of Nun[^9], since it was done while he was living; but here it is mentioned by recapitulation, when Scripture was speaking of the tribe of Judah, from which Caleb came.

## How Jerusalem is said not to have been conquered, nor the Jebusite, when earlier the contrary was said.

**7.** (1:21, 8) It is asked how it was said: *and the children of Benjamin did not inherit the Jebusite dwelling in Jerusalem, and the Jebusite dwelt with the children of Benjamin in Jerusalem to this day,* when above it is read that the same city was taken and burned by Judah, with the Jebusites in it killed? But it is to be recognized that this city was held in common by two tribes, Judah and Benjamin, as the very division of lands shows, which was made by Joshua the son of Nun.[^10] For Jebus is the same as Jerusalem[^11]: therefore these two tribes remained with the Temple of the Lord, when the others—except Levi, which was priestly and did not receive lands in the division—separated themselves from the kingdom of Judah with Jeroboam.[^12]

It is to be understood, then, that the city was indeed taken and burned by Judah, with those killed who were found there: but that not all the Jebusites were destroyed; either because they were outside that city, or because they were able to flee: and that these remaining Jebusites were admitted by the children of Benjamin, with whom that city was held in common with Judah, to dwell in it together. So what was said: *the children of Benjamin did not inherit the Jebusite,* is to be understood as that they were not able, or not willing, even to make them tributary. Or at least *he did not inherit the Jebusite* was said because he did not hold without him the land that was possessed by him.

## How Beth-shan is said to be a city of the Scythians.

**8.** (1:27) *And Manasseh did not inherit Beth-shan, which is a city of the Scythians.* The same is said today to be called Scythopolis. But it can be troubling, how in those parts, very different from Scythia, there could be a city of the Scythians. But similarly it can be troubling how, so far from Macedonia, Alexander the Macedonian founded the city of Alexandria; which he certainly did by waging war far and wide: so too, when the Scythians at some time advanced far in waging war, they could found this one. For it is read in the history of the nations that the Scythians at one time held almost all of Asia, when those who had of their own accord declared war on the king of the Egyptians went to meet him, by whose coming he was terrified, and withdrew into his own kingdom.

## Concerning the daughters of Beth-shan.

**9.** (1:27) *And Manasseh did not inherit Beth-shan, which is a city of the Scythians, nor its daughters.* By *its daughters* it means the cities which it, as a metropolis, had established.

## How what was said in the book of Joshua is repeated.

**10.** (1:28) *And it came to pass, when Israel had prevailed, that he set the Canaanite under tribute, and, taking away, did not take him away.* Something of this kind was already said in the book of *Joshua* the son of Nun, almost in the very words.[^13] Accordingly either it is said here by recapitulation, or it was said there by prolepsis; that is, either here by recapitulating, or there by anticipating.

## How what follows is similarly repeated.

**11.** (1:34) *And the Amorite distressed the children of Dan in the mountain, because he did not permit them to go down into the valley.* And this likewise was either mentioned by anticipation in the book of *Joshua* the son of Nun[^14], or here by recapitulation.

## How it is said concerning the mountain of weeping, and the things that follow—whether they are said by anticipation.

**12.** (2:1) *And the angel of the Lord went up over Clauthmon (the Mount of Weeping).* The writer of the book called the place by this name because he wrote afterward: for when the angel of the Lord went up over it, it was not yet so called. For it received its name from "weeping," because in Greek κλαυθμός means weeping. For there the people wept, when they had heard from this angel the words of the Lord taking vengeance on them, because they had been disobedient, in that they did not exterminate the peoples according to his command, those over whom they prevailed, choosing to make them tributary[^15] rather than to kill and destroy them, as the Lord had commanded.[^16]

Whether they did this from contempt of God's command, or from fear, lest they should drive the enemy to fight more sharply against them for the obtaining of their safety than for the not giving of tribute, without doubt they sinned, either by spurning what was divinely commanded, or by not presuming that he who had commanded could help them. Why he was unwilling to say this to them through Joshua (if indeed it had already begun to be done while he was still living, and had not rather been mentioned by anticipation as what began to be done after his death), is because he willed to reproach all with this through the angel: but not all had yet done it while Joshua was living, although some perhaps had already begun. It is more credible, however, that nothing of the kind began to be done while Joshua the son of Nun was living, and that the children of Israel held under him only so much land as sufficed for them to settle, although in their lots they had the means by which, growing and gaining strength, they might still exterminate their adversaries.

Accordingly, after the death of Joshua, after they had prevailed so that they could do this, they preferred to have them as tributaries according to their own will than to kill and destroy them according to the will of God: on this account the angel was sent to rebuke them. But what was mentioned in the book of *Joshua* the son of Nun[^17], I rather think was mentioned by anticipation, as something which, after his death, either he himself already knew by a prophetic spirit was going to be (if the book which is called Joshua the son of Nun was written by him); or, if it was written by another, he already knew it had been done after the death of Joshua, which in that book he mentioned by anticipation.

## Whether it can be said, even from the anger of God, that certain sins come, from the words of the threatening angel.

**13.** (2:3) What does it mean that the angel of the Lord, among the other things of divine threatening, says: *I will no longer transfer the people whom I said I would cast out; I will not take them away from before you: and they shall be a distress to you, and their gods shall be a stumbling block to you*[^18]; unless that we understand that some sins come even from the anger of God? For that the gods of the nations, among whom, not exterminated by themselves, the Israelites willed to dwell, should be a stumbling block to them—that is, should make them stumble in the Lord their God, and, having offended him, live—God, being indignant, threatened this; which certainly it is plain is a great sin.

## How by recapitulation the people is said to go, each into his own house.

**14.** (2:6, 8) *And Joshua dismissed the people, and the children of Israel went, each into his own house, and each into his own inheritance, to inherit the land.* That this is repeated by recapitulation there is no doubt.[^19] For the death of Joshua the son of Nun himself is also mentioned in this book, so that, as it were from the beginning, all things might be briefly intimated, from the time when the Lord gave them the land, and how they lived under those same judges, and what they suffered; and again it returns to the order of those same judges, beginning from the one who was first appointed.

## How this is to be expounded: they did not know the Lord.

**15.** (2:10) *And another generation rose up after them, who did not know the Lord, and the works which he did for Israel.* It expounded how it said: *they did not know the Lord*; namely, in those illustrious and marvelous works, through which it had earlier come about that Israel knew the Lord.

## How the names of idols ought to be taken.

**16.** (2:13) *And they served Baal and the Astartes.* It is usually said that Baal is the name among the nations of those parts for Jupiter, and Astarte for Juno, which the Punic language too is thought to show. For the Punic peoples seem to say *Baal* for "lord"; whence by *Baalsamen* they are understood to say, as it were, "lord of heaven": for *samen* among them is the word for the heavens. And Juno without doubt is called by them Astarte. And since these languages do not differ much from each other, it is rightly believed that here Scripture says this of the children of Israel, that they served Baal and the Astartes, that is, Jupiter and the Junos.

Nor ought it to be troubling that it did not say "Astarte," that is, "Juno"; but, as though there were many Junos, set down this name in the plural. For it meant the understanding to be referred to the multitude of images, since each single image of Juno was called a Juno; and by this it meant as many Junos to be understood as there were images. But I think that for the sake of variety it meant to mention Jupiter in the singular, the Junos in the plural. For by the same cause of the plurality of images, Jupiters too could be spoken of in the plural. But this, that is, the plural name "Junos," we find in the Greek according to the Septuagint, but in the Latin it was in the singular. Of these, in the one which did not have the Septuagint translation, but was from the Hebrew, we read Ashtaroth; and not Baal, but Baalim. But if perhaps these names signify something else in the Hebrew or Syriac language, yet it is established that they were other gods and false, whom Israel ought not to have served.

## How it is said: And he sold them . . .

**17.** 1. (2:14, 20-23; 3:1-4) *And he sold them into the hand of their enemies round about.* It is usually asked why it said *he sold,* as though some price were understood to have been given. But in the *Psalm* too it is read: *you have sold your people for no price*[^20]; and in the prophet: *you were sold for nothing, and you shall not be redeemed with silver*[^21]. Why, then, *sold,* if *for nothing* and *for no price,* and not rather "given"? Or is it perhaps a manner of speaking of the Scriptures, so that even one who is given can be said to be "sold"? But this sense is best in what was said: *you were sold for nothing*; and: *you have sold your people for no price*: because those to whom you handed over the people were impious, and did not, by worshiping God, deserve that people to be handed over to them, so that the worship itself might seem a price. But what was said: *nor shall you be redeemed with silver;* it did not say: "nor with a price," but: *nor with silver;* so that we understand a price of redemption, such as the Apostle Peter speaks of: *for you were redeemed not with silver and gold, but with the precious blood of the spotless Lamb.*[^22] For by *silver* the prophet signified all money, where he said: *you shall not be redeemed with silver;* since they were to be redeemed with the price of the blood of Christ, yet not with a money price.

**17.** 2. In that the Lord says: *and I will no longer take away a man from before them, of the nations which Joshua the son of Nun left, and dismissed to test Israel by them, whether they will keep the way of the Lord to walk in it, as their fathers kept it, or not: and the Lord left these nations, so as not to take them away then; and did not deliver them into the hand of Joshua,* the reason is shown well enough why Joshua did not destroy all those nations by war; because, if this were done, there would be none among whom these men might be tested.

But they could be for their own usefulness, if, being tested among them, they were not found reprobate; and, being found such as the Lord had commanded them to be, those nations would now be taken away from before them, if they so lived, and they would not need to be exercised by wars. For the words of the Lord are to be taken thus far: *because this nation has so forsaken my covenant, which I commanded their fathers, and they did not obey my voice: I too will no longer take away a man from before them,* that is, an adversary. But the rest are the words of the writer, expounding why the Lord had said that he would not take away a man of the nations which Joshua the son of Nun left.

Then, adding for what cause he had left them: *and he dismissed them,* it says, *to test Israel, whether they keep the way of the Lord to walk in it, as their fathers kept it, or not;* meaning those to be understood to have kept the way of the Lord as fathers who were with Joshua, that is, in the time in which he was living. For above[^23] it reported that another generation rose up after those who lived with Joshua, and that from them began the transgressions which offended the Lord; for the testing, that is, the proving, of whom the nations had been left, and not exterminated through Joshua.

**17.** 3. Then, lest Joshua should be thought to have acted in this by his own, as it were human, counsel, that those nations should be left, Scripture adds: *and the Lord left these nations, so as not to take them away quickly; and did not deliver them into the hand of Joshua.* Then it follows: *and these are the nations which Joshua left, that he might test Israel by them, all who did not know all the wars of Canaan: only on account of the generations of the children of Israel, to teach them war.*

This, then, was the cause in their testing, that they should learn to make war; that is, that they should wage war with as great a piety and obedience to the Law of God as their fathers, who pleased the Lord God even in making war: not because war is something desirable, but because piety is praiseworthy in war.

But what follows: *only those who before them did not know them;* what does it mean, except that it means the nations which those who were before these did not know by making war; for the testing, that is, the proving, of whom they were left?

Then, mentioning what they are: *five,* it says, *satrapies of the foreigners;* which in the books of *Kingdoms* it expresses more plainly.[^24] Now satrapies are, as it were, small kingdoms, over which satraps were set: which name in those parts is, or was, one of a certain honor.

*And every,* it says, *Canaanite, and Sidonian, and Hivite dwelling in Lebanon, before Mount Hermon up to Laboemath: and it came to pass, that Israel was tested by them;* as though it said: "but this was done, that Israel might be tested by them: *to know whether they would hear the commandments of the Lord";* not that God, who knows all things, even future ones,[^25] should know, but that they themselves should know, and by their own conscience should either glory or be convicted, whether they heard the commandments of the Lord, *which he commanded their fathers by the hand of Moses.*

Since, then, they were made plain to themselves as not having obeyed God, among those nations which had been left for their testing, that is, their exercise and proving; therefore God said either those things in which the angel was openly sent and spoke more expressly, or, a little before, where he says: *because this nation has so forsaken my covenant, which I commanded their fathers, and they did not obey my voice: I too will no longer take away a man from before them.*

**17.** 4. Now it was said in *Deuteronomy*, in the person of God speaking about these adversary nations: *I will not cast them out in one year, lest the land become deserted, and wild beasts multiply against you: little by little I will cast them out, until you are multiplied, and grow, and inherit the land.*[^26] The Lord could keep this promise of his toward the obedient, so that the extermination of those nations should take place as the parts held by the Israelites grew, since their multitude would not allow the lands from which the adversaries were exterminated to be deserted. But as for what it says, *lest wild beasts multiply against you,* it is surprising if he did not mean to be understood, in a manner, the beastly desires and lusts, which are accustomed to arise from a sudden success of earthly happiness. For God could exterminate men, and could not exterminate beasts, or destroy them, or rather not permit them to be born?

## Concerning the words: And the Lord raised up a savior of Israel . . .

**18.** (3:9) *And the Lord raised up a savior of Israel, and saved them.* Then, as though it were asked "what savior": *Othniel,* it says, *the son of Kenaz.* For we ought to take here the accusative case, as if it said *Othnielem.* But it is to be noted that it calls even a man a savior, through whom God saves. For *the children of Israel cried out to the Lord: and the Lord raised up a savior of Israel, and saved them, Othniel the son of Kenaz, the younger brother of Caleb: and he heard them.*

Among those things which are called ὑπέρβατα, this kind is rare, since it has also that which the Greeks call ὑστερολογία. For what it says afterward, *and he heard them,* if it is set first, the speech becomes clearer. For the order is: *and the children of Israel cried out to the Lord, and he heard them, and the Lord raised up a savior of Israel.* Then what is here interposed, *and saved them,* and afterward said, *Othniel,* or *Othnielem the son of Kenaz,* if it were said thus, it would become plainer: *and the Lord raised up a savior of Israel, Othniel the son of Kenaz, and saved them.*

## How the land is said to have rested for forty years.

**19.** (3:11) Scripture testifies that the Land of Promise rested from wars for forty years under Othniel the judge: as much time as the beginnings of the Roman empire could have at peace under Numa Pompilius as their only king.

## Whether Ehud lied when he killed Eglon.

**20.** (3:19-20) It can be asked whether Ehud the judge lied when he killed Eglon king of Moab. For when, alone, he was lying in wait for the man alone in order to strike him, he said this to him: *I have a hidden word for you, O king;* so that he might remove from himself all who had been with him. And when this was done, Ehud said again: *I have a word of God for you, O king.* But it can be no lie, since by the name of "word" Scripture is accustomed to call even a deed; and in truth it was so. As for his saying, *a word of God,* it is to be understood that God commanded him to do this—God, who had raised him up as a savior for his people, as in those times such things had to be done by divine appointment.

## How King Eglon is said to be very slender.

**21.** (3:17, 22) It is rightly asked how *King Eglon was very slender, and the fat closed over the wound,* when he was struck. But it is to be understood as said by that manner of expression which is accustomed to be understood from the contrary, as a grove (lucus) is so called because it gives no light (luceat); and one answers "to abound" when there is nothing; and *he blessed the king,* for *he cursed,* as is written in the book of *Kingdoms* about Naboth.[^27] For in that translation which is not according to the Septuagint, but from the Hebrew, we find thus: *but Eglon was exceedingly fat.*

## How the things that follow are said by recapitulation.

**22.** (3:23) *And Ehud went out, and passed by those keeping watch, and shut the doors of the upper room over him, and made it fast.* This was said by recapitulation, as something that had been passed over. For it is to be understood that he did this first, and so came down from the upper room, and passed by those keeping watch.

## How the servants of King Eglon opened with a key what Ehud had not shut with a key.

**23.** (3:25) How the servants of King Eglon opened with a key what Ehud had not shut with a key can be troubling: or, if he had shut it with a key, how he did not take it away with him, so that they could not open it even with a key. Accordingly either another key was brought, or it was such a kind of fastening as could be shut without a key, and not opened without a key. For there are some such, like those that are called bolted.

## Under Ehud Israel had peace for eighty years.

**24.** (3:30) Under Ehud the judge Israel had peace in the Land of Promise for eighty years, namely double the time that was memorable under the Roman king Numa Pompilius.

## Concerning the judge Shamgar.

**25.** (3:31) *And after him rose up Shamgar the son of Anath, and struck the foreigners to the number of six hundred men besides the calves of the oxen; and he saved Israel.* How, after Ehud, this man fought for Israel, and was said to have saved Israel, can be a question. For they had not been again taken captive, or bound by the yoke of servitude. But it is to be understood that *he saved* was said thus, not because the enemy harmed anything, but lest he be permitted to harm; whom it is to be believed he had begun to try by war, and was driven off by this man's victory. But what it means by adding: *besides the calves of the oxen,* is obscure. Did he perhaps make a slaughter of oxen too in fighting, and so it was said that he killed six hundred men, besides what he did with the oxen killed? But why calves? Or is it a custom of the Greek manner of speaking to call even those calves that are grown? For they are said to speak thus commonly in Egypt; just as among us the hens of any age are called chicks. But the translation from the Hebrew does not have *besides the calves of the oxen,* as this one according to the Septuagint does: just as that one from the Hebrew has, *six hundred men killed with a plowshare,* which this one does not have.

## What it is that Barak answered Deborah.

**26.** (4:8) What does it mean that Barak, answering Deborah, said: *if you go, I will go; and if you do not go with me, I will not go: because I do not know the day on which the Lord prospers an angel with me;* as though he could not hear the day from the prophetess? Nor did she answer the day, but set out with him. And what is: *the Lord prospers an angel with me?* Is it here shown that even the acts of the angels are prospered, that is, helped by the Lord, so that they turn out happily? or is it a manner of speaking: *he prospers an angel with me,* that is, "he makes prosperity with me through an angel"?

## How the Lord struck Sisera with fear.

**27.** (4:15) *And the Lord struck Sisera with fear, and all his chariots.* Behold how Scripture commends that God acts in hearts, so that he gives to things the outcome which he has appointed. For he certainly struck Sisera with fear, or stupefied him, in order to deliver him up.

## Concerning Barak, how he went in to Jael.

**28.** (4:22) Where Jael, the woman who killed Sisera, when she had spoken to Barak who was seeking him, it is written of Barak himself, that *he went in to her:* it is to be noted that it does not follow that, when Scripture says of a man that he went in to some woman, he is therefore to be believed already to have lain with her. For Scripture constantly speaks thus: *he went in to her,* so as to mean nothing but that he was joined with her. Here, then, it was said properly: *he went in to her,* that is, "he entered into her house," not so that by these words intercourse is to be understood.

## The order of words in the canticle of Deborah makes obscurity and raises a question.

**29.** (5:7-8) In the canticle of Deborah it is said: *those dwelling in Israel failed, they failed until Deborah rose up, until a mother rose up in Israel, they chose new gods like barley bread: then they attacked the cities of the princes.* Here the intermixed order of words makes obscurity, and raises a question. For how is it to be understood: *they chose new gods like barley bread, then they attacked the cities of the princes;* as though God favored them in attacking the cities of the princes then, when *they chose new gods like barley bread*? But in other places of the Scriptures we have already often learned how hyperbata occur; by the straightening of which, when the words return to order, the sense is made plain. This, then, is the order: *those dwelling in Israel failed; they failed, they chose new gods like barley bread, until Deborah rose up, until a mother rose up in Israel: then they attacked the cities of the princes.*

## Concerning the words: They chose new gods like barley bread.

**30.** (5:8) It can be asked how it was said: *they chose new gods like barley bread;* since barley bread, although it is to be set aside in comparison with wheaten bread, yet itself nourishes too, and is a vital food: but the new gods, whom those who fell away from the living God are said to have chosen, cannot be reckoned among the foods of the soul, but rather among poisons. Or is the likeness to be taken only so far as it holds, so that it is understood to be said for this alone: that, as it often happens through fastidiousness that things to be chosen are rejected, and things to be spurned delight; so, by the fault of a perverse will, as it were by the languor of fastidiousness, when their God was the true one, they chose in false ones nothing but novelty, with the truth despised; and so they chose deadly food as though barley bread, not thinking they would thereby perish, but rather take from it even life, as from a harmless though baser food? The likeness, then, is set down according to their opinion, and the languor of mind, not according to truth. For those new gods are to be compared with no vital foods.

## Why the name of the prophet who is said to have been sent by God to the people is not expressed.

**31.** (6:7-8, 11) *And it came to pass, when the children of Israel cried out to the Lord on account of Midian, that the Lord sent a man, a prophet, to the children of Israel, and he said to them.* Why the name of this prophet is not given—which is very unusual in the Scriptures—the cause lies hidden; yet I think there is one. But because, after the words by which he reproached the people for their disobedience, Scripture continues, saying: *and the angel of the Lord came, and sat under the oak which was in Ophrah*[^28]: it is not absurdly understood that this angel is signified by the name *man,* so that, after he said these words, he came to the aforementioned oak, and sat there. For it is known that angels are accustomed to be called by the name of "men"[^29]: although that one who was an angel should be called a prophet does not easily or evidently occur: but one who was a prophet we read was called an angel[^30]: but if the prophetic sayings of angels are known—that is, those by which they foretold future things—why can an angel not be named by the name of a prophet? Nevertheless, as I said, an express and manifest testimony on this matter does not occur.

## The words of the angel to Gideon.

**32.** (6:12) What the angel says to Gideon: *the Lord is with you, mighty in strength,* is the nominative case, not the vocative. That is: *the Lord, mighty, is with you;* not, "you, mighty one."

## The angel speaks by the authority of God.

**33.** (6:14) It is to be noted that the angel spoke as by the authority of the Lord: *have I not, behold, sent you?* when he spoke to Gideon. For who sent him, but he who sent the angel to him? But Deborah did not say to Barak: "did I not command you?" But she said: *did not the Lord God of Israel command you?*[^31] Here, however, it was not said: "has not the Lord, behold, sent you?" but: *have I not, behold, sent you?*

## Concerning what Gideon answers to the angel.

**34.** (6:15) Where Gideon answers the angel: *in me, O Lord;* that is: "attend to me": *by what shall I save Israel? behold, my thousand are the lowest in Manasseh:* is he understood to have been set over a thousand men, whom Scripture in Greek calls χιλίαρχοι? or what else?

## Concerning what Gideon does not say to the angel.

**35.** (6:18-22) It is to be noted that Gideon did not say to the angel: "I will offer you a sacrifice"; but he said: *I will offer my sacrifice, and set it in your sight.* Hence it is to be understood that he wished to offer the sacrifice not to the angel, but through the angel. Which the angel himself also evidently shows, who did not take the sacrifice from him as though for himself; but said to him: *take the flesh and the unleavened bread, and set them by that rock, and pour out the broth. And when Gideon had done this, the angel of the Lord stretched out the top of the staff that was in his hand, and touched the flesh and the unleavened bread; and a fire was kindled from the rock, and consumed the flesh and the unleavened bread.*

So even the angel himself, in the sacrifice which Gideon offered, fulfilled the office of one ministering: for the fire a man, a minister, as a man, would have applied without a miracle, which this one, as an angel, applied miraculously. Finally, Gideon then knew that it was the angel of the Lord: for Scripture immediately added this: *and Gideon saw that it was the angel of the Lord.*[^32] Earlier, then, he spoke as with a man, whom nevertheless he believed to be a man of God, so that he wished to offer a sacrifice in his presence, as though to be helped by the presence of his holiness.

## Why Gideon dared to offer a sacrifice to God outside the place where God had commanded.

**36.** (6:20) It can be asked why Gideon dared to offer a sacrifice to God outside the place where God had commanded. For God had forbidden sacrifice to be made to him outside his tabernacle[^33]; which tabernacle the Temple afterward succeeded: but in the time in which Gideon lived, the tabernacle of God was at Shiloh; and therefore there only could sacrifice be lawfully made. But it is to be understood that he had first thought that angel to be a prophet, and had consulted God in him, as it were, about offering a sacrifice: which, if he had forbidden it, would certainly not have been done; but since he approved it, and assented that it be done, Gideon followed God's authority in doing it. For God so established those lawful ordinances that he gave laws not to himself, but to men.

Hence whatever he himself commanded beyond those ordinances is to be understood as fulfilled not by transgressors, but rather by the pious and obedient: as Abraham concerning the sacrifice of his son.[^34] For so too Elijah sacrificed outside the tabernacle of the Lord, to convict the priests of the idols[^35]: which he is to be understood to have done by the command of the Lord, who, by revelation and inspiration, as to a prophet, bade him to do it. Although so great a custom of sacrificing outside the tabernacle had grown up that even Solomon is found to have sacrificed on the high places, and his sacrifice not to have been rejected.[^36] And yet those kings are noted who, among their praiseworthy works, did not destroy the high places, where contrary to the Law of God the people was accustomed to sacrifice: and he who destroyed them is praised with greater commendation. So God endured rather than forbade the custom of his people, by which, outside his tabernacle, they yet offered not to foreign gods, but to the Lord their God, even so hearing those who offered. But this which Gideon did, who does not understand was brought about through the angel, so that it might be done prophetically, in which prophecy that rock was commended?

To which rock indeed sacrifice was not made, but from it fire is mentioned to have gone out, by which the sacrifice was consumed. For whether by the water which the struck rock poured forth in the wilderness[^37], or by the fire, the gift of the Holy Spirit is signified, which the Lord Christ most richly poured out upon us.

For in the Gospel too this gift was signified by water, where the Lord himself says: *if anyone thirsts, let him come and drink: he who believes in me, as Scripture says, out of his belly shall flow rivers of living water;* where the Evangelist added: *but this he said of the Spirit which those believing in him were to receive*[^38]. It was signified also by fire, where, at his coming upon those gathered together, it is read: *there appeared to them divided tongues as of fire, which also settled upon each one of them*[^39]: and the Lord himself says: *I came to send fire upon the earth*[^40].

## Whether both are to be thought set down, by saying with hand and with tongue: three hundred men lapped.

**37.** (7:6) *And the number of those who lapped with their hand, with their tongue, was three hundred men.* Many Latin manuscripts do not have *with their hand,* but only *with their tongue,* since they understood thus what was said above: *like dogs*: but the Greek has both: *with their hand, with their tongue,* so that it is understood that they threw into their mouth water snatched up with the hand; and this was like dogs drinking, which take the water they drink not with the mouth applied, like oxen, but snatch it into the mouth with the tongue, as these men too are understood to have done, but throwing the water into the mouth with the hand, which they caught with the tongue.

For the translation from the Hebrew too has it more plainly in these words: *and so the number of those who had lapped, with the hand throwing the water into the mouth, was three hundred men.* For men are not accustomed to drink in such a way that, without the aid of the hand, they draw water with the tongue, like dogs: nor indeed had it been commanded these men that they should do this: but when they had gone down to the water to drink, many drank with bent knee, which would be done more easily and with less labor; but a few, because they did not bend the knee to stoop, drank like dogs, but with water thrown into the mouth by the hand.

Whose number, because they were three hundred, intimates a sign of the Cross on account of the Greek letter T, by which this number is signified; through which it was also prefigured that the nations would rather believe in the Crucified, because it is a Greek letter. Hence by the name of "Greeks" the Apostle signifies all the nations, when he says: *to the Jew first and to the Greek*[^41], and: *to the Jews and to the Greeks*[^42]; often so mentioning circumcision and uncircumcision, because among the languages of the nations the Greek so excels that through it all are fittingly signified.

This number is to be noted also in the home-born servants of Abraham, through whom he freed his brother from the enemy, when Melchizedek blessed him in a great mystery. For that there are eighteen over and above there (for they are reported to have been three hundred and eighteen[^43]); it seems to me to be signified at what time too it would come about, that is, the third, which was to be under grace. For the first is before the Law, the second under the Law, the third under Grace. And each single time is signified by the number six, on account of perfection: for three times six are eighteen. Hence also that woman had eighteen years in her infirmity, whom the Savior, when he found her bent over, raised up, and loosed from the binding of the devil, as the Gospel indicates.[^44]

For that these men, through whom Gideon would conquer, were so proved that they were said to be like dogs in drinking, signifies that the Lord chose the contemptible and ignoble things[^45]: for a dog is held in contempt. Hence he says: *it is not good to take the children's bread, and cast it to the dogs*[^46]: and David, to cast himself away as contemptible, called himself a dog, speaking to Saul.[^47]

## How Gideon is said to have gone down into the part of fifty, he and his servant, and so on.

**38.** (7:11) What does it mean that it is written of Gideon: *he himself went down, and Phara his servant, into the part of fifty, who were in the camp:* which some Latin manuscripts have: *into that part in which there were fifty watchmen each in the camp;* but others: *into the fiftieth part into the camp*? For an obscure saying made several opinions of the translators. But either that was the part of the camp which fifty watchmen guarded; or, if fifty each are to be understood to have guarded round about, these men went down into one part where there were fifty.

## What the table of barley bread is.

**39.** (7:13) That the man who told his neighbor the dream, which Gideon heard, that he might be confirmed about the coming victory, said that he had seen a table of barley bread rolling in the camp, and striking the tent of Midian, and overturning it; I think this is to be understood like that about the dogs[^48], because through the contemptible things of the world (which the table of barley bread signifies) the Savior was going to confound the proud.[^49]

## What it is that Gideon bade to be cried out.

**40.** (7:20) That Gideon bade his three hundred to cry out: *a sword for the Lord and for Gideon,* that is, for this Gideon, signifies this: that the sword was going to work what would please the Lord and Gideon.

## What an ephod is.

**41.** 1. (8:26-27) It is usually asked what an ephud, or ephod, is: which, if indeed it is a priestly garment, as most say, or rather an over-garment, which in Greek is called ἐπένδυμα, or ἐπωμίς, which can rather be translated into Latin as a shoulder-piece; it rightly prompts the question how Gideon made it of so much gold. For it is written thus: *and the weight of the gold earrings which he had asked for was a thousand seven hundred shekels of gold, besides the bracelets and the necklaces and the purple coverings which were upon the kings of Midian, besides the necklaces which were on the necks of their camels: and Gideon made it into an ephud, and set it up in his city in Ophrah; and all Israel committed fornication after it there; and it became a stumbling block to Gideon and his house.* How, then, could this garment be made of so much gold? For the mother of Samuel too made for her son, as we read, an *ephud bar,* which some have translated "a linen ephud," when she gave him to the Lord to be brought up in the Temple[^50]: where it appears more evidently that this is a kind of garment. Or was it said: *he set it up in his city,* so that from this it might be understood to have been made of gold? For it did not say: "he placed," but: *he set up;* since it was so solid and strong that it could be set up, that is, stand when placed.

**41.** 2. When, then, Gideon had done this unlawful thing, *all Israel committed fornication after it,* that is, by following it against the Law of God: where it is not in vain asked, since it was not an idol, that is, an image of any false and foreign god, but an ephod, that is, one of the sacred things of the tabernacle that pertained to the priestly garment, how Scripture calls it the fornication of the people that pursued and venerated it. For this reason, namely, that outside the tabernacle of God, where were those things which God had bidden to be made there, it was not lawful for anything similar to be made elsewhere. Therefore Scripture continues and says: *and it became a stumbling block to Gideon and his house,* that is, that, with God offended, he departed from him: because this too was, in a manner, a kind of idol, that, outside God's tabernacle, anything made by hand should be worshiped for God; whereas those very things which were bidden to be made in the tabernacle were to be referred rather to the worship of God, than that any of them should be held to be worshiped for God, or for an image of God.

**41.** 3. Although by ephud or ephod, by that manner of speaking which signifies the whole from a part, all the things may be understood which Gideon set up in his city, as it were for the worship of God, similar to the tabernacle of God: and therefore through this, because this is the insignia of priestly honor, which Scripture often mentions[^51]; so that this is the sin of Gideon, that he made outside God's tabernacle something similar, where God might be worshiped: not that he set up the ephud of solid gold as though to be adored; but that from the gold itself, which was from the spoil, he made those things which pertained to the ornaments or instruments of the sanctuary, all of which were signified by the ephud, on account of the excellence, as I said, of the priestly garment.

For the ephud itself was not commanded to be made of gold alone, if this is the shoulder-piece of the priestly garment: yet it does have some gold. For it was divinely commanded to be made of gold and hyacinth and purple and scarlet and fine linen[^52]: but because the Septuagint translators set this down in such a way that, having mentioned all the things which Gideon had received from the spoils, they added: *and Gideon made it into an ephud*; it seems to be said as though it is believed to have been made out of all that was mentioned: although there too the manner of speaking can be understood which signifies the part from the whole, so that what was said: *he made it into an ephud,* is understood: "he made an ephud from it," or: "he made an ephud out of it"; not, of course, consuming the whole of it in the ephud, but expending of it as much as sufficed.

For in that translation which is from the Hebrew, it is read thus: *and Gideon made an ephod from it.* For what is written in the Septuagint as *ephud,* this in the Hebrew is reported to be called *ephod.* But not all priests used such a shoulder-piece, which was of gold and hyacinth and purple and scarlet and fine linen, but only the high priest. Hence that which we mentioned of Samuel, made for him by his mother, was certainly not such: since, when he was given to be brought up, he was not the high priest, being of course a boy. Hence, as was said, it is called *ephud bar,* or rather *ephud bat,* as those who know the Hebrew language assert, and it is translated "a linen ephud." But I think that Gideon made that which was the chief garment and ornament of the high priest, by which also the other works of the sanctuary were signified, which he had set up in his city outside God's tabernacle; and on account of this sin it became a stumbling block to him and his house, so that there perished (as Scripture afterward narrates) so great a number of his sons.[^53]

## How the land rested forty years in the days of Gideon.

**42.** (8:27-28) A question arises that is not to be passed over: how the land rested forty years in the days of Gideon, when, after the victory by which he freed the Hebrews, he made an abomination from the gold of the spoils, and after that all Israel committed fornication, and it became a stumbling block to him and his house. How, then, after so great a wickedness, which both Gideon and the people committed, did the land rest for forty years; when Scripture is accustomed to show that, when the people committed fornication against the Lord God, then they rather lost peace, not gained it; and were subjugated to enemies, not fortified against the enemy's assault?

But it is to be understood that Scripture, as it is accustomed, said by prolepsis, that is, by anticipation, that Gideon made the ephud against God's Law from that gold which had been taken from the enemy when they were conquered and laid low, because it wished to say in one place both whence the gold came and what was made of it: but afterward this sin was committed at the end of Gideon's days, when there followed also the evils which Scripture then wove together, after it had mentioned how many years the land rested in the days of Gideon; which years it mentioned by recapitulation, that is, returning to the order which it had overstepped, by first speaking of that stumbling block which was committed last of all.

## What idols the children of Israel served after the death of Gideon.

**43.** (8:33) *And it came to pass, when Gideon was dead, that the children of Israel turned away, and committed fornication after the Baalim: and they set up for themselves Baal-berith as a covenant, that he might be a god to them.* Both the Baalim and Baal-berith are to be understood as idols. So a greater transgression and fornication was committed by the people after the death of Gideon than while he was living, on account of the ephud: since that too, although it had been made unlawfully, yet was of the sacred things of the tabernacle; but this fornication after idols does not have even a false defense of an ancestral religion. Hence, even if that ephud was made not at the end of Gideon's time, but before, God so patiently endured it that peace continued in the land; because, although what he had forbidden had been done, yet there had not been a great departure from him who had bidden such a thing to be made in his tabernacle and in his honor. But now, with graver things committed, and the people's most open fornication after idols, he was unwilling for it to go unpunished.

## What distinction is to be made in the words of the bramble.

**44.** (9:14-15) That the bramble, that is, a certain kind of thorn, is brought in as saying, in a likeness, to all the trees coming to it to make it reign over them: *if in truth you anoint me to reign over you, come, trust in my protection: and if not, let fire go out from the bramble, and consume the cedars of Lebanon:* the sense is obscure, but the finding of the right division makes it plain. For it is not to be read thus: *and if not let fire go out from the bramble:* but a subordinate division is to be made: *and if not,* and then is to be added, *let fire go out from the bramble;* that is: "and if you do not trust in my protection," or: "and if you do not in truth anoint me to reign over you," *let fire go out from the bramble, and consume the cedars of Lebanon.*

For these are the words of one threatening what it can do, if they should be unwilling for it to reign over them. But because it did not say: "fire will go out from the bramble, and will devour the cedars of Lebanon," but said: *let it go out, and let it devour*; it became more obscure than if the division alone were hidden. But it is of a more vehement threatening, and in a manner of more present efficacy, if one says: "if you do not wish to do what I will, let my anger rage against you," that is, let it rage now; why do I hold it back? than if one says: "it will rage," threatening a future punishment in the promissory mood.

## Concerning the words: And God sent an evil spirit.

**45.** (9:23) *And God sent an evil spirit between Abimelech and the men of Shechem.* Whether this word signifies God as commanding or as permitting cannot be easily determined. For what is here set down, *sent,* the Greek has ἐξαπέστειλεν, which is also in the *Psalms* where it is read: *send out your light*[^54]: although in some places our translators, even where there is in the Greek ἐξαπέστειλεν, have translated *sent* (misit), not *sent out* (emisit). It can therefore also be understood thus, that God sent out the evil spirit as though it wished to go between them, that is, gave power to the evil spirit to disturb their peace. But it has not seemed absurd that even an evil spirit is sent by the Lord for the sake of the justice of taking vengeance, so far that some have translated ἐξαπέστειλεν even as *sent in.*

## The words which Zebul sent to the messengers of Abimelech.

**46.** (9:32-33) The words which Zebul, the prince of the city of Shechem, sent to the messengers of Abimelech, have this too: *and now rise by night, you and your people with you, and lie in ambush in the field. And it shall be in the morning, as soon as the sun rises, you shall make haste, and stretch out against the city.*

What some Latin manuscripts have as *you shall make haste* (maturabis), but others as *manicabis,* the Greek has what could be said in more than one word, *you shall rise at dawn.* And perhaps from this comes the saying, *maturabis,* from the morning time (a matutino tempore), although at other times too it is accustomed to be said for hastening a matter. But "manicabis" does not occur to me as a Latin word.

But this is troubling, that when it had said: *as soon as the sun rises,* it added: *you shall rise at dawn;* when dawn itself, which in Greek is called ὄρθρος, signifies the time before the sun, which is now most commonly so called, when it has begun to grow light.

So, then, it is to be understood that what is set down as *morning,* dawn itself is understood. But it was added: *as soon as the sun rises,* so that it might be expressed that it was to be done not when the sun had already risen, but when the brightness of the rising sun had appeared. For dawn does not grow light from any other source, except when the light of the sun returning to it begins to touch that part of the sky which we see from the east. Hence it is that in the Gospel too one Evangelist says that one and the same thing was done at dawn, while it was still dark[^55], another at the rising of the sun[^56]; because that light of dawn itself, however little, was certainly produced by the sun rising, that is, coming to its rising, and casting its brightness from the nearness of its presence. Which light some unlearned men thought was not of the sun, but was that which was first created, before God on the fourth day created the sun.

## Concerning the judge Tola.

**47.** (10:1) *And after Abimelech there rose up to save Israel Tola the son of Puah, the son of the father of his brother, a man of Issachar.* It called the son of his uncle *the son of the father of his brother,* when it would be said more orderly and more commonly and more plainly: *the son of the brother of his father*: for he was the son of his uncle, as is found more evidently in that translation which is from the Hebrew. So what was said, *of the father of the brother,* is not declined from that which is in the nominative *father of the brother* (pater fratris), but from that which is *brother of the father* (patris frater), for this is the uncle. For whether it is set in the nominative as "this is the father of the brother," or set as "this is the brother of the father," it makes the genitive *of this father of the brother* (huius patris fratris).

But another question arises, how the uncle of Abimelech was a *man of Issachar,* that is, a man of the tribe of Issachar, when Abimelech had Gideon as father, and that Gideon was of the tribe of Manasseh. How, then, were Puah and Gideon brothers, so that Puah could be the uncle of Abimelech, whose uncle's son Tola succeeded, according to this narrative, Abimelech himself? Gideon and Puah, then, could have had one mother, from whom they were born of different fathers, and they were brothers as the sons of one mother, not of one father. For women were accustomed to marry from other tribes into other tribes.

Hence Saul too, although he was of the tribe of Benjamin, gave his daughter to David, a man of the tribe of Judah[^57]: and the priest Jehoiada, certainly a man of the tribe of Levi, married the daughter of King Joram, a man of the tribe of Judah[^58].

Hence it came about that we read in the Gospel that Elizabeth and Mary were kinswomen[^59], when Elizabeth was of the daughters of Aaron: from which it is understood that some woman of the tribe of Levi and of the daughters of Aaron married into the tribe of Judah, whence that kinship between the two came about, so that the flesh of the Lord was propagated not only from the royal but also from the priestly stock.

## Concerning what Jephthah sends word of to the king of the children of Ammon.

**48.** (11:24) Among the other things which Jephthah sent word of through messengers to the king of the children of Ammon, he also says this: *do you not inherit whatever Chemosh your god has given you to inherit; and shall we not inherit all whatever the Lord our God has given us to inherit from before your face?* Which some Latin translators thought should be translated so as to say: *do you not possess whatever Chemosh your god has given you in inheritance?* where Jephthah may seem to have affirmed that this god, who is called Chemosh, could give something in inheritance to his worshipers. But others have it thus: *do you not possess whatever Chemosh your god has possessed?* and this so sounds as though he could possess something.

Or was it perhaps said according to this, that the nations were placed under angels, according to the Canticle of Moses the servant of God?[^60] Was, then, that angel under whom the children of Ammon were called Chemosh? Who would dare to affirm this, when it can be understood as said according to his opinion, because he thought his god possessed this, or had given it to him in possession?

But this sense shines out more in those words which the Greek has: *do you not inherit whatever Chemosh your god has given you to inherit?* so that in what is set down, *you,* it is understood to be said as if it were said: "as it will seem to you." "For he has given you to inherit who think this, not that he could inherit anything." Finally, in what follows: *and all whatever the Lord our God has given us to inherit,* he did not say: has given us to inherit, as though he said: "as it seems to us"; but he has truly given us to inherit: *from before your face;* since he took it away from them, and gave it to these: *this,* he says, *we shall inherit.*

## Concerning the sacrifice of Jephthah's daughter.

**49.** 1. (11:29-35) Concerning Jephthah's daughter, that her father offered her as a holocaust to God—since in war he had vowed that, if he conquered, he would offer as a holocaust the one who, going out of his house, should meet him; and when he had vowed this, he conquered; and, with his daughter meeting him, he paid what he had vowed—there is usually a great question, and most difficult to decide; with some desiring to know what this means, and asking piously;

but with some, who oppose these Holy Scriptures with unskilled impiety, charging this above all as a crime: that the God of the Law and the prophets was pleased even with human sacrifices. To whose slanders we reply first thus: that the God of the Law and the prophets, and, to say it more expressly, the God of Abraham and the God of Isaac and the God of Jacob[^61], was not pleased even with those sacrifices where holocausts of cattle were offered; but, because they were significative and certain shadows of things to come, he willed to commend to us the very realities which were signified by these sacrifices: and that this too was a useful reason why those should be changed, and now not be commanded, indeed be forbidden, to be offered, lest we should think that God was truly pleased with such things according to a carnal affection.

**49.** 2. But whether it was fitting that things to come should also be signified by human sacrifices is a question rightly raised—not that we ought to shudder at and dread the deaths, in this matter, of men who are at some point going to die anyway, if those who would gladly accept such things being done to themselves were commended to God for an eternal reward; but if this were true, this kind of sacrifice would not displease God, whereas the same Scripture testifies plainly enough that it does displease him. For although he willed and commanded that all the firstborn be consecrated to him and be his own, he nevertheless willed that the firstborn of men be redeemed from him[^62], lest people should think that they must offer their sons to God in sacrifice, the sons whom they had first received at their birth.

Then he speaks of this more openly, saying that God so disapproves of human burnt offerings that he forbids them, detesting them in other nations and commanding his own people not to dare to imitate them: *but if the Lord your God cuts off the nations whose land you are entering in to inherit before you, and you inherit them and dwell in their land, take heed to yourself that you do not seek to follow them after they have been cut off before your face, and that you do not seek out their gods, saying, 'As the nations serve their gods, so will I do also.' You shall not do so to the Lord your God. For they have done for their gods the abominations that the Lord hates, since they even burn their sons and their daughters in the fire to their gods*[^63].

**49.** 3. What could be shown more plainly by these testimonies of Holy Scripture—to pass over others of the same kind—than that God, by whom this Scripture was bestowed on the human race, not only does not love but even hates such sacrifices, in which human beings are immolated? Those sacrifices he clearly loves and crowns in which some just man, suffering wickedness even to death, contends for the truth, or is killed by the enemies whom he has offended for the sake of justice, repaying them good for evil, that is, love for hatred.

The Lord calls such blood just blood, from the blood of Abel to the blood of Zechariah[^64]. Above all there is the blood that he himself shed for us, when he offered himself as a sacrifice to God; and he so offered it that he was killed by his enemies for the sake of justice. In imitation of him thousands of martyrs contended even to death for the truth and were immolated by their raging enemies; of whom Scripture says: *like gold in the furnace he tried them, and like a sacrificial burnt offering he accepted them*[^65]. Hence the Apostle too says: *for I am already being poured out as a sacrifice*[^66].

**49.** 4. But Jephthah did not make a burnt offering of his daughter to the Lord in this way; rather, the offering of cattle had been commanded, and the immolation of human beings forbidden. What Abraham did seems more like it, which the Lord specially commanded to be done[^67]; he did not command by any general law that such sacrifices should at some point be made to him—indeed, he even forbade them altogether to be made. What Jephthah did therefore differs from what Abraham did, since the latter offered his son when commanded, whereas the former did what was both forbidden by the Law and enjoined by no special command.

Then, not only later in his Law but even at that time, in Abraham's own son, God showed how little he delights in such sacrifices, when he forbade the father—whose faith he had tested by the command—to kill his son, and provided a ram by which the sacrifice might lawfully be completed according to the custom of the ancients suited to their times.

**49.** 5. But if anyone is troubled by this—how Abraham could piously believe that God delights in such sacrifices, if they are offered to God unlawfully, and therefore thinks that Jephthah too rightly believed that such a sacrifice could be acceptable to God—let him first consider that it is one thing to vow something of one's own accord, another to obey one who commands. For if a servant is commanded to do something contrary to the usual practice established in the household by his master, and does it with praiseworthy obedience, he is not therefore beyond punishment if he presumes to do this of his own accord.

Then Abraham had grounds for believing that, because of the divine command, he should not spare his son—not because he believed that God gladly accepts such victims, but because he believed that God had commanded it for this reason, that he might raise up the one slain, and thereby, as a wise God, demonstrate something. For this is read of him in the *Epistle* entitled *to the Hebrews*; and his faith, in that he believed this of God, that he could raise up his son, is praised[^68].

Jephthah, on the other hand, with God neither commanding nor demanding it, and against his lawful precept, of his own accord vowed a human sacrifice. For it is written thus: *and Jephthah vowed a vow to the Lord and said, 'If you indeed deliver the sons of Ammon into my hand, then whoever comes out of the doors of my house to meet me when I return in peace from the sons of Ammon shall belong to the Lord, and I will offer him up as a burnt offering.'*

**49.** 6. By these words he certainly did not vow any animal that he could lawfully offer as a burnt offering according to the Law. For it neither is nor was the custom that cattle should run to meet leaders returning victorious from war. As for dumb animals, dogs are wont to run to meet their masters, playing about them with fawning service; but he could not have thought of these in his vow, lest he be seen to have vowed, to the injury of God, something not only unlawful but even contemptible and, according to the Law, unclean. Nor did he say, "whatever comes out of the doors of my house to meet me, I will offer it as a burnt offering"; rather he said, *whoever comes out, I will offer him.* Here, beyond doubt, he thought of nothing but a human being—though perhaps not of his only daughter; yet who, amid such fatherly glory, could come before her, unless perhaps his wife? For as to his saying not *whatever woman* but *whoever comes out of the doors of my house,* Scripture is accustomed to put the masculine gender for either sex, as was said of Abraham, *rising from the dead one*[^69], when it was his wife who had died.

**49.** 7. Since, then, Scripture seems to have passed no judgment on this vow and deed, as it most clearly judged concerning Abraham when he was commanded and offered his son, but only left it written for readers to judge—just as concerning the deed of Judah the son of Jacob, when he unknowingly went in to his daughter-in-law, yet committed fornication so far as it lay in him, because he thought she was a harlot[^70], and Scripture neither approved nor condemned this, but left it to be weighed and assessed by consulting the justice and the Law of God—

since, then, the Scripture of God has pronounced no sentence either way concerning this deed of Jephthah, so that our understanding might be exercised in judging, we might now say that such a vow displeased God, and that he was brought to that retribution whereby his only daughter, of all people, should meet her father; for if he had hoped and wished for this, he would not at once, on seeing her, have torn his garments and said, *Alas, my daughter, you have brought me low; you have become a stumbling block in my eyes.*

Then, after granting his daughter so long a delay of sixty days, the Lord did not forbid him from the killing of his dearest only child, as he forbade Abraham, but let him, by carrying out what he had vowed, strike himself with the gravest bereavement[^71]—while in no way appeasing God by the immolation of a human being. And so this penalty was meted out to such a father, lest the example of such a vow be left unpunished, with the result that people would either think they were vowing something great to God when they vowed human victims and, what is more horrible, the victims of their own children; or else that such vows would be not genuine but rather feigned, since those who made them would hope, after the example of Abraham, that God would forbid such vows to be carried out.

**49.** 8. We could say all this, I repeat, were we not held back from this opinion by two testimonies of the divine Scriptures in particular; so that we should examine this deed—committed to memory in books of such great authority—more carefully, with the Lord's help, and more cautiously, lest we pronounce a rash judgment in either direction.

The first is that in the *Epistle to the Hebrews* this Jephthah is mentioned among such men that we would fear to blame him, where it is written thus: *and what more shall I say? For time would fail me to tell of Gideon, Barak, Samson, and Jephthah, of David and Samuel and the prophets, who through faith conquered kingdoms, and working justice obtained promises*[^72].

The second is that, where these things are narrated of him—that he vowed and fulfilled such a vow—Scripture prefaces them by saying: *and the Spirit of the Lord came upon Jephthah, and he passed through Gilead and Manasseh, and passed by the watchtower of Gilead, and from the watchtower of Gilead over to the sons of Ammon, and Jephthah vowed a vow to the Lord*[^73]*,* and the rest pertaining to this very vow; so that everything done thereafter seems to be understood as the work of the Spirit of the Lord, who had come upon him. These testimonies compel us rather to ask why it was done than easily to disapprove what was done.

**49.** 9. But first, as for what I mentioned from the *Epistle to the Hebrews*: among those praiseworthy men recalled there, there is not only Jephthah but also Gideon, of whom Scripture likewise says: *the Spirit of the Lord strengthened Gideon*[^74]. And yet his deed—that from that gold of the spoil he made an ephod, and all Israel committed fornication after it, and it became a snare to the house of Gideon[^75]—we not only cannot praise; we even, since Scripture here judged most plainly, do not in the least hesitate to condemn. Nor is any injury thereby done to the Spirit of the Lord, who strengthened him so that he overcame the enemies of his people with such ease. Why, then, is he mentioned among those *who through faith conquered kingdoms, working justice*—unless because Holy Scripture, whose faith and justice it truly praises, is not thereby hindered from truthfully noting their sins as well, if it knows of any and judges it fitting to note them?

For even in this—that Gideon, asking for a sign, as he himself put it, made a test with the fleece[^76]—I do not know whether he did not transgress the precept where it is written, *you shall not put the Lord your God to the test*[^77]; and yet even in his testing the Lord showed what he wished to foretell. By the drenched fleece while the whole threshing floor all around was dry, he prefigured the first people, Israel, where the saints were with heavenly grace as with a spiritual rain; and afterward by the drenched floor with the fleece dry, he prefigured the Church spread throughout the whole world, having heavenly grace not in a fleece as under a veil but in the open, while that earlier people was, as it were, estranged from the dew of that same grace and dried up. Nor was it in vain that, among the faithful and those working justice, on account of his good and faithful life—in which he must be believed to have died—he earned such a testimony in the *Epistle to the Hebrews.*

**49.** 10. But whether, because after it was said, *the Spirit of the Lord came upon Jephthah,* there followed his vowing that vow, conquering the enemies, and rendering what he had vowed, everything is to be attributed to the Spirit of the Lord, so that this sacrifice too is to be regarded as though the Lord had commanded it to be made, as he did with Abraham—this I would not readily say; since in the case of Gideon this difference can be adduced, that after the sin he committed when he made the ephod, after which the whole people committed fornication, no prosperity of his is recorded, whereas after Jephthah vowed his vow there followed that notable victory of his, for the obtaining of which he had vowed, and once it was obtained he paid what he had vowed.

For again it must be considered that Gideon—if not after he made the ephod, yet after he tested the Lord, which is certainly a sin—won safety for the people by a great slaughter, with the enemies cut down and overcome. For it is written thus: *and Gideon said to the Lord, 'Let not your indignation be angry against me, and I will speak yet once more, and I will test once more with the fleece*[^78].' For he feared the wrath of God, because he knew that in testing he was sinning, which God in his Law most plainly forbids[^79]. Yet this sin of his was followed both by the marvelous evidence of the sign and by the great prosperity of victory and the people's deliverance. For God had already determined to come to the aid of his afflicted people, and he used the disposition of this leader, whom he had taken up for this work—a disposition not only faithful and devout but also failing and falling short—both to foretell what he willed and to fulfill what he had said.

**49.** 11. For it was not only through these men—who, even if they sinned, are nonetheless reckoned among the just—but even through Saul himself, who was rejected in every way, that God granted many things to his people; the Spirit of God even rushed upon Saul, and he prophesied, not when he was acting justly but when he was raging against the holy and innocent man David[^80]. For the Spirit of the Lord acts both through the good and through the wicked, both through the knowing and through the unknowing, doing what he knows and determines must be done; he who even through Caiaphas, the Lord's fiercest persecutor, not knowing what he said, brought forth a notable prophecy, that Christ must die for the nation[^81].

For who but the Spirit of the Lord, taking care to foretell things to come, brought it to the mind of the judge Gideon—who wished to test the Lord and did not believe what had already been spoken to him by his own hand concerning the people's safety—precisely this thought of the fleece, first drenched then dry, and of the floor, first dry then watered? So that what fell short of faith is to be charged to his weakness and fault, while the fact that God used even such a disposition of his for what had to be signified to the human race is to be understood as belonging to his mercy and marvelous providence.

**49.** 12. But if anyone says that Gideon did and said everything knowingly, by prophetic revelation, so that through him such signs might be shown; that he did not fall away from faith; that he believed what the Lord had already promised him; but that by a prophetic act he wished to test with the fleece, and that thus his testing was blameless, like the deception of Jacob[^82]; and that when he said to the Lord, *let not your indignation be angry against me*[^83]*,* he did not say it because he feared his wrath but because he was confident that he would not be angry, since he was doing what, as a prophet, he perceived must be done at the dictation of his Spirit—let him say what seems good to him, provided that he does not dare to excuse from sin that deed of the ephod which Scripture itself blamed, whatever it may signify.

For that too—that three hundred men, belonging by their very number to the sign of the cross, took earthen pitchers and enclosed in them burning torches, and when these pitchers were broken the lights suddenly flashing in their numbers terrified so great a multitude of enemies—Gideon seems to have done as it were of his own choice; for Scripture does not say that the Lord admonished him to do this. And yet who but the Lord inspired so great a sign to be done by his mind and counsel? He who prefigured that his saints would have the treasure of the gospel light in earthen vessels, as the Apostle says: *but we have this treasure in earthen vessels*[^84]; vessels in which, broken as it were in the suffering of martyrdom, the greater splendor of their glory shone forth, which by the unlooked-for brightness of Christ overcame the impious enemies of the gospel preaching.

**49.** 13. Whether, then, through the knowing or through the unknowing, the Spirit of the Lord in prophetic times worked the prefiguration and proclamation of things to come[^85], their sins are not on that account to be called no sins; for God, who knows how to make good use even of our evils, used even their very sins to signify what he willed. Accordingly, if it was no sin—whether to vow or to render either any human killing or even a parricidal sacrifice—because it signified something great and spiritual, then in vain did God forbid such things and testify that he hates them; since the very things he commanded to be done are likewise referred to some signification of great spiritual realities. Why, then, would he forbid those things, when they could just as lawfully be done for the sake of that same signification of those same realities, for the sake of which these others were lawfully done—unless because human sacrifices, even when signifying something such as it is profitable to believe, did not please God, that is, when someone is not killed by enemies for the sake of justice, because he wished to live rightly or refused to sin, but a man is immolated by a man after the manner of cattle, like a chosen victim?

**49.** 14. What if, someone says, because animal victims, by the very fact that they had become customary—though they too were referred by those who rightly understood to the signification of spiritual things—nevertheless made people less intent on seeking out the great mystery of Christ and the Church; therefore God, wishing by a remarkable and unexpected event to rouse, as it were, the sleeping minds of men, the more because he had forbidden such sacrifices to be offered to him, arranged for something of this kind to be offered to him, so that the very wonder might beget a great question, and the great question might lift up the zeal of a devout mind to search out a great mystery, and the mind of a man devoutly searching might raise up, like a fish on a hook, the height of prophecy—the Lord Christ—from the deep of the Scriptures?

To this reasoning and consideration we offer no resistance. But it is one question concerning the disposition of the one vowing, and another concerning the providence of God making the best use of his disposition, whatever it was. Therefore, if the Spirit of the Lord who came upon Jephthah commanded him outright to make this vow—which Scripture has not disclosed—yet if he commanded it whose commands it is not lawful to despise, then not only is his folly not to be blamed, but his obedience is even to be praised.

For even if a man kills himself—which it is certainly forbidden to do by human will and counsel—it must surely be understood to be done obediently rather than wickedly, if it is divinely commanded; a question we have discussed sufficiently in the first book of *The City of God*[^86].

But if Jephthah, following human error, thought a human sacrifice must be vowed, then his sin was indeed justly punished in his only daughter, which he himself seems to show clearly enough by his own words, where he says, *Alas, my daughter, you have brought me low; you have become a stumbling block in my eyes;* having also torn his garments. And yet even here his error has some praise of faith, by which he feared God, so as to render what he had vowed, and did not turn aside the sentence of the divine judgment upon himself—whether hoping that God would forbid it, as he did with Abraham, or resolving to do God's will, understood even though he did not forbid it, rather than to despise it.

**49.** 15. And yet here too it may rightly be asked whether it is more truly understood that God did not will this to be done, and that God would rather have been obeyed if it had not been done, since he had shown both in Abraham's son[^87] and in the prohibition of the Law that he did not will this. Nevertheless, if Jephthah had not done it for this reason, he would have seemed rather to have spared his only daughter than to have followed God's will. Rather, then, he understood in the fact that his daughter met him that God was the avenger, and he faithfully submitted himself to a just penalty, fearing a fiercer retribution, as it were, for evasion.

For he believed that the soul of his good and virgin daughter would be well received, since she had not vowed herself to be immolated but had not resisted her father's vow and will, and had followed the judgment of God. For death, just as it is not to be inflicted by anyone of his own accord either on himself or on anyone else, so it is not to be refused when God commands, since by his appointment it must be undergone at one time or another; nor does anyone who shrinks from enduring it labor to avoid it altogether, but only to defer it.

**49.** 16. Now let us inquire, as far as God helps, and briefly weigh, what the Spirit of the Lord prefigured in this deed—whether through Jephthah unknowing or knowing, whether through his imprudence or his obedience, whether through his offense or his faith. For this passage of the Holy Scriptures admonishes us and in a way urges us to think of a certain man mighty in strength. For such is Jephthah said to be, whose name is interpreted "one who opens." But the Lord Christ, as the Gospel indicates[^88], *opened the understanding* of his disciples, *that they might understand the Scriptures*[^89].

This Jephthah his brothers rejected and drove out of their father's house, casting it in his face that he was the son of a harlot[^90], as though they themselves were born of a lawful wife. The chief priests and the scribes and the Pharisees did this same thing against the Lord, who seemed to glory in their observance of the Law, as though he were dissolving the Law[^91], and therefore were as it were not a lawful son. And although he had indeed taken his body from the holy Virgin, which is known to the faithful, yet his mother, as far as the nation is concerned, can also be called that Jewish synagogue.

Let whoever wishes turn over the prophetic books and see how often and with how great severity and indignation of the Lord's word that nation, like an unchaste woman, is reproached for its fornications. Hence comes that most recent instance in this book, whether when, after the ephod that Gideon made, all Israel is said to have committed fornication[^92], or when they went after the gods of the nations by whom they were surrounded[^93]. Hence the divine anger was stirred against them, so that for eighteen years they were crushed by the sons of Ammon[^94].

But were not those priests and scribes and Pharisees, whom we have said were prefigured in them, themselves born of the same nation of Israel—they who, like the others persecuting and driving out the Lord Christ as not a lawful son, drove out Jephthah? But in this the likeness is sketched out, that these men, as I said, being as it were observers of the Law, seemed to themselves to have justly cast out, as lawful sons casting out one not lawful, the man who seemed to act against the precepts of the Law. For in this respect that people is said to commit fornication, in that, not keeping the precepts of the Law, it did not, as it were, show fidelity to its husband.

**49.** 17. Now it is written thus of Jephthah: *and the sons of the wife grew up, and they cast out Jephthah*[^95]*.* The word that is used, *grew up,* signifies figuratively "prevailed"; which was fulfilled in the Jews, who prevailed over the weakness of Christ, because he so willed it, that he might endure from them the suffering that was fitting—just as, signifying this same thing, Jacob prevailed over the angel with whom he wrestled in order to portend this very thing[^96].

They said therefore to Jephthah: *you shall not inherit in our father's house, because you are the son of a harlot woman*[^97]*.* As though they said what the Gospel speaks: *this man is not from God, who thus breaks the Sabbath*[^98]; while they themselves, as it were boasting of being lawful sons, said to the Lord: *we were not born of fornication; we have one Father, God*[^99].

*And Jephthah fled from the face of his brothers, and dwelt in the land of Tob*[^100]*.* He fled, because he hid how great he was; he fled, because he lay hidden from those who raged against him: *for had they known, they would never have crucified the Lord of glory*[^101]. He fled, because they saw the weakness of the one dying, but did not see the power of the one rising again. But he dwelt in a good land—or, to put it more precisely, a best land. For what is in Greek agathon is in Latin called "best"; and this is what Tob is interpreted to mean. Here it seems to me his resurrection from the dead is to be understood. For what land is more rich than an earthly body clothed with the excellence of immortality and incorruption[^102]?

**49.** 18. As for what is said of Jephthah, that after he fled from the face of his brothers and dwelt in the land of Tob, robber men gathered to him and went about with him[^103]—although even before his suffering it was cast in the Lord's face that he ate with tax collectors and sinners, when he answered that the healthy do not need a physician but the sick do[^104], and he *was reckoned among the wicked*[^105], when he was crucified among robbers, and carried one of them from the cross into paradise[^106]—yet after he rose again and began to be, according to what we expounded above, in the land of Tob, wicked men were gathered to him for the remission of sins; and they walked with him, because they lived according to his precepts. Nor does this cease to happen even now, and henceforth, so long as the wicked flee to him, that he may justify the impious who turn to him, and that the unjust may learn his ways[^107].

**49.** 19. Now that—those who had cast out Jephthah (for he was a Gileadite) turned to him and sought him out, that through him they might be delivered from their enemies—with how clear a prefiguration it signifies that those who cast out Christ, turning again to him, find salvation in him! Whether those are understood whom the Apostle Peter, when he had reproached them for that very crime, as is read in the *Acts of the Apostles*[^108], and had exhorted them to turn to him whom they had persecuted, were pricked in their heart, and from him whom they had estranged from themselves they desired salvation (for what is it to be delivered from enemies but from sins? for thus he says to them: *repent, and let each one of you be baptized in the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, and your sins will be forgiven you*[^109]); or whether rather that calling of the nation of Israel is signified which is hoped for at the end[^110].

For this latter seems rather to appear in what is said: *and it came to pass after some days*[^111]; which certainly signifies "after a time," and through this suggests that we are to understand not what is at the recent suffering of the Lord, but what is to be in the future. To this also seems to pertain the fact that the elders of Gilead came to Jephthah[^112], so that by their elderly age the later and last times are to be understood. Now Gilead is interpreted "casting out," or "revelation." Both of these fit this matter aptly enough; because first they cast out the Lord Christ, and afterward he will be revealed to them.

**49.** 20. As for the fact that Jephthah was sought as leader against the sons of Ammon[^113], by whose defeat those would be delivered who under his leadership desired to make war against them—since Ammon is interpreted "son of my people," or "people of grief"—surely either those enemies are signified who, persevering in unbelief out of that very nation, were foretold; or else all those predestined to hell altogether, where *there will be* for them *weeping and gnashing of teeth*[^114], as for a people of grief. Although as a people of grief even the devil and his angels are not unfittingly understood; either because they win eternal misery for those they deceive, or because they themselves are assigned to eternal misery.

**49.** 21. Elegantly indeed, to express the prophecy much more clearly, Jephthah answered the elders of Gilead: *did you not hate me, and cast me out of my father's house, and send me away from you? And what is it that you have come, now that you are in tribulation?*[^115] Something like this was figured in Joseph, whom his brothers cast out by selling him[^116]; and when they were afflicted by famine, they turned to his aid and mercy[^117]. But here the signification of things to come shines forth much more, in that it was not the very brothers who cast out Jephthah that came to him, but the elders of Gilead supplicating him on behalf of that whole people. Just as the same nation is called Israel, whether in those who lived then and rejected Christ, or in those who afterward returned for his aid. For it is said to the enemy people, dragging out and keeping long hatreds whether in its forefathers or in its descendants, and at last converted in those who are then to be converted: *did you not hate me, and cast me out of my father's house?* For this seemed so to those who persecuted, that they cast Christ out of the house of David, in which *of his kingdom there will be no end*[^118].

**49.** 22. *And the elders of Gilead said to Jephthah, 'It is not so; we have now come to you*[^119]*.'* As though the Jews, converted to Christ, said: "then we came to persecute, now we come to follow." They profess also that he will be their head against their enemies. He answers that, if he conquers their enemies, he will be their prince[^120]—which Gideon refused when the Israelites had wished it[^121]. For he answered them: *the Lord shall be your prince*[^122]. For by the name "prince" a king is signified, which that nation did not yet have in the time of the Judges. But they began to have Saul[^123] and thereafter his other successors, who are read of in the books of *Kingdoms.* For in *Deuteronomy,* when it is commanded them what sort of king they ought to have, if it should please them, there he is called not king but prince[^124].

But because this Jephthah figured the one who is the true king—which was even written in the title affixed to his cross, which Pilate did not dare to delete or amend[^125]—therefore it must be believed that it was said: *I will be your prince*[^126]*.* They, however, had said, *you shall be our head*[^127]; since *the head of the man is Christ*[^128], and he himself is the head of the body, the Church[^129].

Finally, after Jephthah had delivered them from all their enemies, he did not become king over them, so that we might understand that what was said pertained rather to the prophecy concerning Christ than properly to Jephthah himself, of whom Scripture thus concludes its narrative: *and Jephthah judged Israel six years, and Jephthah the Gileadite died, and was buried in his city, Gilead*[^130]. He judged Israel, then, like the other Judges; he did not reign there as a prince, like those who are contained in the volumes of *Kingdoms.*

**49.** 23. Now indeed, as for the fact that after the same Jephthah was set up as their leader, he sent messengers to the enemies bearing words of peace first[^131], this shows what the Apostle says, in whom Christ was speaking: *if it is possible, so far as it depends on you, be at peace with all men*[^132]. To treat in full the very words that Jephthah sent would take too long, since we are hastening on; yet they seem to me to be understood thus, so far as concerns the signification of things to come, that in them is noticed the teaching of Christ, admonishing us how we ought to walk—that is, to live—among those who are not called according to the purpose[^133]. For *the Lord knows those who are his*[^134].

**49.** 24. Now indeed, as for the fact that when he was about to make war on his enemies, the Spirit of the Lord came upon him[^135], the Holy Spirit imparted to the members of Christ is signified.

**49.** 25. As for the fact that he *passed through Gilead and Manasseh, and passed by the watchtower of Gilead, and from the watchtower of Gilead over to the sons of Ammon*[^136]*,* the members of Christ are signified advancing to carry off the victory over their enemies. For Gilead is interpreted "casting out," Manasseh "necessity." Those who cast out, that is, those who despise, must therefore be passed by those who advance; and "necessity" too must be passed, lest perchance, when the one who advances has passed those who despise, he should yield to those who terrify.

Also the watchtower of Gilead must be passed, since Gilead is also interpreted "revelation." Now a watchtower is a height for looking out from, or for looking down from, that is, for viewing from above. And so the watchtower of Gilead seems to me fittingly to signify the pride of revelation; whence the Apostle says: *and lest I be exalted by the greatness of the revelations*[^137]. Therefore this too must be passed, that is, one must not remain in it, because of the danger of falling. When these have been passed through, the enemies are easily overcome; which he signifies by saying, *and from the watchtower of Gilead he passed over to the sons of Ammon,* concerning which enemies enough has already been said above.

**49.** 26. *And Jephthah vowed a vow and said, 'If you indeed deliver the sons of Ammon into my hand, then whoever comes out of the doors of my house to meet me when I return in peace from the sons of Ammon shall belong to the Lord, and I will offer him up as a burnt offering*[^138]*.'* Whomever Jephthah thought of in this place according to human reckoning, he does not seem to have thought of his only daughter; otherwise he would not say, when he saw that she had met him, *Alas, my daughter, you have brought me low; you have become a stumbling block in my eyes*[^139]*.* For *you have brought me low* is said as though to indicate that he was hindered in this, that he might not fulfill what he had intended. But whom could he have thought of as the first to meet him, who had no other children? Or did he think of his wife, and God did not will this to be done, and willed not to leave it unpunished, lest anyone thereafter should dare it; and willed, by his great providence, from this very thing that happened to figure the mystery of the Church?

From both, then, the prophecy has been fitted together: both from what the one vowing thought, and from what befell him against his will. For if he thought of his wife, the wife of Christ is the Church. *For this reason a man will leave father and mother and will cleave to his wife, and the two will be one flesh. This is a great mystery,* says the Apostle; *but I speak of Christ and of the Church*[^140].

But because this Jephthah's wife could not be a virgin, in the fact that his daughter rather met him both the forbidden boldness of the one vowing the sacrifice did not remain unavenged, and the virginity of the Church was figured. Nor is it foreign to the truth that the same Church is signified by the name "daughter"; for whose type else did that woman bear, to whom, after she had touched his hem and been healed, the Lord said: *daughter, your faith has made you well; go in peace*[^141]?

And surely—a point about which no one is in doubt—he himself called his disciples the sons of the bridegroom, indicating most plainly that he was the bridegroom: *the sons of the bridegroom,* he says, *cannot fast as long as the bridegroom is with them; but the days will come when the bridegroom is taken away from them, and then they will fast*[^142]. The burnt offering, then, will be the Church, whom the blessed Apostle calls *a chaste virgin*[^143], when in the resurrection of the dead there shall come to pass in her entirety what is written: *death is swallowed up in victory*[^144]*.* Then *he will hand over the kingdom to God and the Father*[^145].

Which kingdom is the Church itself; the king is he himself, whose figure the one vowing bore. But since this will come to pass when the sixth age of the world has been completed, therefore the delay of sixty days was requested from her virginity[^146]. For the Church is gathered out of all the ages. Of these the first is from Adam to the flood; the second from the flood, that is, from Noah, to Abraham; the third from Abraham to David; the fourth from David to the migration into Babylon; the fifth from this migration to the childbearing of the Virgin; the sixth from then to the end of this world. Through these six ages, as through sixty days, the holy virgin Church wept over her virginal things; for although they were virginal, they were nevertheless sins to be lamented, on account of which that whole virgin spread throughout the world says daily: *forgive us our debts*[^147]. But these same sixty days she preferred to call *two months,* as I judge, on account of the two men: the one through whom came death, the other through whom comes the resurrection of the dead[^148]; on whose account also there are said to be two Testaments.

**49.** 27. As for what *became a custom in Israel, that from days to days they came together to lament the daughter of Jephthah the Gileadite, four days in the year*[^149]: I do not think it signifies anything after the burnt offering is completed, which will be unto eternal life, but the past times of the Church, in which there were the blessed who mourn[^150]. By the four days her universality was figured, on account of the four parts of the world, through which she is spread far and wide. But as for the literal sense of the history, I do not think the Israelites decreed this except because they understood that in this matter the judgment of God had been brought forth rather to strike the father, lest anyone thereafter should dare to vow such a sacrifice. For why would mourning and lamentation be decreed, if that vow was a matter of joy?

**49.** 28. But if that too—that the people of Ephraim were afterward defeated by the same Jephthah[^151]—is to be referred to the judgment of God that will be at the end, as the Lord himself says: *those who did not wish me to reign over them, bring here and slay before me*[^152]; then neither there were the forty-two thousand who fell mentioned as an idle number[^153]. For just as those two months, on account of the sixty days, signify the number six of the six ages, so there too seven taken six times figures this same thing, so far as it pertains to the six ages of the world; for six sevens are forty-two. Nor was it in vain that Jephthah himself judged the people for six years[^154].

## How Samson's mother is commanded not to eat anything unclean.

**50.** (13:4) It can be asked how the angel said to Samson's mother, when he announced to her that she would have a son, since she was barren: *and now take heed, and do not drink wine and strong drink, and do not eat anything unclean.* For what is the *unclean* thing? Unless perhaps the dissolution of discipline, which had begun to exist in Israel, had brought them down even to eating those things that God had forbidden among the kinds of animals[^155]. For why should it not be believed that they could do this all the more readily, who were even going over to the worship of idols?

## How Samson's mother says more things were spoken to her by the angel.

**51.** (13:6) As for the fact that Samson's mother, telling her husband how the angel had announced to her that she would have a son, said: *and I asked him where he was from, and he did not tell me his name,* it can be asked whether she spoke truly, since this is not read when the angel was speaking to her. But it must be understood that Scripture was silent about this there, and recalled here what it had passed over in silence there. As for the fact too that she does not say, "I asked him what he was called, and he did not tell me his name," but says, *I asked where he was from,* what she added—*and he did not tell me his name*—seems inconsistent. For she had not asked his name when she asked where he was from, but his place or city, since she thought him a man. For she also called him a man of God, yet like an angel in appearance or bearing—that is, because she saw him so resplendent, as she herself related. But if it is punctuated thus, *and I asked him where he was from, and his name,* so that "I asked him" is understood, and then there follows, *he did not tell me,* there is no problem; for what she says, *he did not tell me,* can be referred to both—that is, neither where he was from nor his name.

## How she recites both more and fewer things than she heard.

**52.** (13:7, 5) Likewise, what the same woman says was told to her by the angel, *that the boy will be a Nazirite of God from the womb until the day of his death,* is not read as having been said by the angel. And what is read as having been said, *he will begin to save Israel from the hand of the Philistines,* was not recalled by the woman. And so she both failed to say something that she heard, and yet she must not be believed to have said anything that she did not hear; rather Scripture did not set down all the angel's words when it inserted his speaking to the woman into the narrative. But it was said, *from the womb until the day of his death,* because in the Law those were called Nazirites who had a vow for a time, according to what Scripture had commanded through Moses[^156]. Hence comes this, that it was commanded him that iron should not go up upon his head, and that he should not drink wine and strong drink. For Samson observed throughout his whole life what those who were called Nazirites observed for certain days, vowing and rendering their vow.

## How Manoah did not know that he was an angel of God.

**53.** (13:15-16) As for what Scripture says, *for Manoah did not know that he was an angel of God,* it is clear that his wife too believed him to be a man. So when he said to him, *let us detain you now, and let us prepare a kid of the goats before you,* he invited him as a man, but in such a way that he might share with him in the meal what he had prepared as a sacrifice. For "to prepare a kid of the goats" is not usually said except when a sacrifice is made. Finally, the angel too answered thus: *if you detain me, I will not eat of your bread;* wherein he shows that he had been invited to a meal.

Then he added: *and if you make a burnt offering, you shall offer it to the Lord.* He said, *if you make a burnt offering,* precisely because Manoah had said, *let us prepare a kid of the goats before you.* But not every sacrifice was a burnt offering; for of a burnt offering none was eaten, because the whole was burned, and for that reason it was called a holocaust. But the angel, although he too would not eat, admonished that a burnt offering should rather be made—yet not to himself, but to the Lord; chiefly for this reason, because the nation of Israel at that time had grown accustomed to sacrifice to any false gods whatever; whence at that time too it had offended God, so as to be handed over to its enemies for forty years[^157].

## How, having seen the angel, they are said to have seen God.

**54.** (13:16-23) What does it mean that, after the angel who was speaking with them was made manifest to Manoah and his wife, the same Manoah said to his wife: *we shall surely die, because we have seen God*—from that statement of the Law, namely, where it is written: *no one can see my face and live*[^158]? They supposed, then, as men do, that they had seen God, since so great a miracle had been done, that he stood in the fire of the sacrifice who had before been speaking with them as a man.

But did they call God the one in the angel, or did they call the angel himself God? For it is written thus: *and Manoah took the kid of the goats, and the offering, and offered it upon the rock to the Lord who works wonders; and Manoah and his wife were watching. And it came to pass, as the flame went up from off the altar toward heaven, the angel of the Lord went up in the flame. And Manoah and his wife were watching, and they fell on their faces to the ground. And the angel of the Lord did not appear again to Manoah and his wife. Then Manoah knew that he was an angel of the Lord; and Manoah said to his wife, 'We shall surely die, because we have seen God.'*

In these words, then, since he did not say, "*we shall surely die,* because we have seen an angel of the Lord," but, *we have seen God,* a question arises whether they understood God in the angel, or called the same angel God. For that third possibility—that they thought him God who was an angel—cannot be maintained, since Scripture says most plainly: *then Manoah knew that he was an angel of the Lord.*

But why did they fear death? For Scripture in *Exodus* had not said, "no one sees the face of an angel and will live," but said, *my face,* when God was speaking. Or was it that, in this very thing that Manoah had recognized God in the presence of the angel, he was so disturbed that he feared death? As for what his wife answered him—*if the Lord wished to put us to death, he would not have accepted from our hand a burnt offering and a sacrifice, nor would he have shown us all these things; nor indeed would he have made us hear these things*—did they believe that the angel himself accepted the sacrifice, because they saw him stand in the flame of the altar? Or did they thereby understand that the Lord had accepted it, because the angel did this in order to show that he was an angel?

But whichever of these it may be, the angel had already said: *but if you make a burnt offering, you shall offer it to the Lord*—that is, not to me, but to the Lord. The fact, then, that the angel stood in the flame of the altar is to be understood as signifying rather that the angel of great counsel[^159], in the form of a servant[^160]—that is, in the man he was to assume—would not accept a sacrifice, but would himself be the sacrifice.

## How Samson is said to have struck the foreigners "leg upon thigh."

**55.** (15:8, 15) What does it mean, what is said—that *Samson struck the foreigners leg upon thigh?* For who has a leg upon a thigh, since the leg is not lower down except from the knee to the ankle? Then, if it signified the place of the body where he wounded them, were all whom he struck wounded in one place of the body? And if this were credible, we might perhaps suspect that he fought with the leg-bone of some animal as with a club, and struck them with it upon the thigh—just as it is written of him that he killed a thousand with the jawbone of an ass.

But neither is that credible, as I said, that in fighting he aimed at only one place where he struck them; and Scripture does not say, "he struck them with a leg-bone upon the thigh," but *a leg upon the thigh.* No doubt, then, it is an unusual manner of speaking that produces the obscurity. For it is said as though one were to say, "he struck them most marvelously"—that is, so that, stupefied with wonder, they would set leg upon thigh; namely, the leg of one foot upon the thigh of the other, as those are wont to sit who are stupefied with wonder. As if one were to say, "he struck them hand to cheek"—that is, with such slaughter that they would set hand to cheek in mournful astonishment.

That this is the sense, the translation made from the Hebrew teaches well enough; for it reads thus: *he struck them with a mighty blow, so that, stupefied, they set calf upon thigh.* For it is the same as if he had said, "they set leg upon thigh"; since the calf is certainly at the back along with the leg.

## As for what Samson said to the men of Judah: Swear to me that you yourselves will not kill me . . .

**56.** (15:12) What does it mean, what Samson said to the men of Judah: *swear to me that you yourselves will not kill me, and hand me over to them, lest perhaps you yourselves fall upon me*? This manner of speaking some have interpreted as, *lest perhaps you yourselves come against me.* But that he said this lest he be killed by them is shown by what is written in the book of *Kingdoms,* when Solomon commanded that a man be killed, saying: *go, fall upon him*[^161].

This is not understood because it is not the custom among us to speak so. For when military authorities say, "go, lift him up," and it means, "kill him," who would understand it, unless someone who knows the custom of that manner of speaking? It is also commonly said among us, "he made short work of him," which means, "he killed him"; and no one understands this except one who is accustomed to hearing it. For this is the general force of all manners of speaking, that, like the languages themselves, they are not understood unless they are learned by hearing or reading.
