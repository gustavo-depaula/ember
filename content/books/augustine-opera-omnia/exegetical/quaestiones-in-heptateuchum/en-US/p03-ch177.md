# Question 177: On the Tabernacle: The whole tabernacle itself is treated separately.

**177.** 1. Since the book that is called *Exodus* takes its end in the setting up of the tabernacle—about which tabernacle many things are also said throughout the earlier parts of the same book, things that make difficulty of understanding, as every *topographia,* that is, description of some place, is accustomed to make in every history—it seemed good to me to speak separately of the whole tabernacle itself, so that it may be understood, if it can be done, of what kind it was, and what it was, the proper sense of the narration being inspected for the present, and the figurative signification deferred to another time: for it is not to be thought that anything was there which was set up at God's command without the mystery of some great thing, the knowledge of which builds up faith and the form of piety.

**177.** 2. (26, 1-6) God, then, orders Moses to make a tabernacle of ten curtains of twisted fine linen, and hyacinth, and purple, and twisted scarlet, with cherubim of the weaver's work: which the Greeks call *aulaiai,* the Latins render *aulaea,* which are commonly called curtains. He did not, then, order ten courts to be made, as some have carelessly rendered: for he did not say *aulas,* but *aulaias.* By woven work, then, he ordered cherubim to be made in the curtains, of which curtains he prescribed the length to be twenty-eight cubits, and the breadth four cubits. And he prescribed that the curtains cohere with one another, and be joined together, five on this side and five on that, so that the space which would be enclosed by them, this would be the space of the tabernacle.

But how these same five curtains were to be connected to one another, he prescribed thus: *you shall make for them,* he says, *loops of hyacinth on the topmost edge of one curtain on one side at the joining; and so you shall make the upper top parts at the second joining*[^1]: that is, where curtain is joined to curtain, namely the third to the second, which second is already joined with the first, that is, connected and linked, standing face to face, one over against the other; since he ordered five to be joined among themselves, so that they were set over against each other.

But whether a square space or a round one was enclosed by these, does not yet appear; but it will appear when he begins to speak of the columns by which the curtains are stretched out. He wished, then, to speak no further than of the connection of three, which is made in two joinings, of the second to the first, and of the third to the second, so that from this as it were a rule the rest might be joined[^2].

He prescribed, then, that fifty loops be made for the first curtain, on the one side by which the second was to be joined to it; and fifty loops for the third curtain, on that side by which it was joined to the second: but the second itself, that is, the middle one between both sets of fifty loops, he wished to have fifty golden rings—surely on one side—by which it would be joined to the fifty loops of the first curtain; and by this it followed that on the other side too it would have as many rings, by which it would be joined to the loops of the third curtain. Which Scripture briefly said thus: *and you shall make fifty golden rings, and you shall join curtain to curtain with the rings, and there shall be one tabernacle*[^3]. The fifty golden rings, then, of the second curtain were inserted into the fifty hyacinth loops of the first curtain; the fifty rings were inserted also into the fifty loops of the third curtain; and so in turn the rest were connected, so that they comprised five, and on the opposite side likewise five.

**177.** 3. (26, 7-11) Then he says: *and you shall make hair-cloth coverings to cover over the tabernacle*: that is, which would come over, not as a roof, but by girding over. For we say that something is so placed over, not as the roof of a house, but as the plastering of a wall, as over a linen band. *Eleven,* he says, *coverings you shall make of them. The length of one covering shall be thirty cubits, and the breadth of one covering four cubits; the same measure shall be of the eleven coverings. And you shall join five coverings together, and six coverings together.* As he wished those curtains to be joined five by five, so these coverings five and six, because they were eleven, not ten. *And you shall double,* he says, *the sixth covering toward the face of the tabernacle*: lest it disturb one that six and five ran into each other unequally. Then he says also how these hair-cloth coverings were to be joined to one another.

And he says the same, but perhaps more plainly: *and you shall make,* he says, *fifty loops on the edge of one covering, which is over against the middle,* that is, over against the second, since it will be the middle between the first and the third: *at the joining,* that is, the juncture. *And fifty loops you shall make upon the edge of the covering which is joined to the second covering,* that is, upon the edge of the third covering, by which it is joined to the second. *And you shall make fifty bronze rings, and you shall join the rings from the loops; and you shall join the coverings, and it shall be one.*

He wished, then, the rings to be set on the middle covering, that is, the second, by which, with fifty loops each, it would be joined to the first and the third. There is nothing else here, except that he now prescribed the rings to be made not of gold, but of bronze. But the loops in the curtains he had said were of hyacinth: but in the hair-cloth coverings, since he was silent of what kind the loops were, what do we more credibly take them to be, but of hair-cloth?

**177.** 4. (26, 12-14) What then follows is so difficult to understand that I fear lest in expounding it become more obscure. For he says: *and you shall put under, in the coverings of the tabernacle, the half of the covering that remains over; you shall undercover what abounds of the coverings of the tabernacle, you shall undercover behind the tabernacle. A cubit on this side, and a cubit on that, of that which remains over in the coverings, of the length of the coverings of the tabernacle, it shall be covering over the sides of the tabernacle, on this side and that, so as to cover it.*

When he had ordered the sixth covering to be doubled toward the face of the tabernacle, what does he mean by saying that a half of a covering remains over in the coverings; and what does he mean by: *a cubit on this side, and a cubit on that,* when a half of a covering is fifteen cubits, since he ordered one covering to be thirty cubits—who could easily understand? Or if it remains over of the length of the coverings for this reason, that he prescribed the first curtains, of fine linen and scarlet and purple and hyacinth, to be twenty-eight cubits long, but these hair-cloth ones he wished to be thirty cubits, the individual curtains are exceeded by two cubits by the individual hair-cloth coverings: which, brought into a sum, except the eleventh which he ordered to be doubled, amount to twenty cubits, by which the circuit of the curtains is exceeded by the circuit of the hair-cloth ones. For the two cubits each of the ten coverings, by which they were longer, make twenty: of which, on either side, ten on this side and ten on that could remain over, not a cubit on this side and a cubit on that, as Scripture says.

Wherefore the exposition of this passage seems to me to be deferred, until the tabernacle stands complete on every side with all its columns, together with the court that is placed around it. For perhaps by anticipation he says something of these hair-cloth coverings which avails for those things of which he has not yet spoken. For what he says: *and you shall make hair-cloth coverings to cover over the tabernacle*[^4], whether he wishes the whole tabernacle to be covered by these coverings, together with the court, namely, of which he afterward speaks as to be placed around; or this inner tabernacle, which he ordered to be made of ten curtains, is uncertain.

It continues, then, and says: *and you shall make a covering for the tabernacle, rams' skins dyed red.* Whether he ordered this covering too to be made for the whole tabernacle round about, or for the inner one only, is likewise held uncertain. But what he adds: *and as coverings, hyacinth-colored skins above,* this is to be taken not round about, but from the roof as it were a vault.

**177.** 5. (26, 15-21) *And you shall make,* he says, *columns for the tabernacle of incorruptible wood; ten cubits one column, and a cubit and a half the breadth of one column; two little brackets for each column, standing over against each other; so you shall make for all the columns of the tabernacle.* Why he ordered these little brackets to be made—of which I said before what they were[^5]—is not sufficiently clear to me. For if they were made for carrying the columns, four at least would be made: but if where the bars rested, even more would be made; for he distributed five bars to each column. Unless perhaps in these little brackets there is not any use, but only signification, as in the eleventh hair-cloth covering. For a column, extending two little brackets as it were arms on this side and that, renders the figure of a cross.

Now let us see the number of the columns, in which the form of the tabernacle too can be observed—whether it is square, or round, or has an oblong rectangle, with longer sides and shorter ends, as most basilicas are built: for this rather is here plainly expressed. For he says thus: *and you shall make columns for the tabernacle, twenty columns on that side which looks toward the north. And forty silver bases you shall make for the twenty columns, two bases for one column on both its parts. And the second side, toward the south, twenty columns, and forty silver bases of them; two bases for one column on both its parts, and two bases for one column on both its parts.* Let the repetition not disturb; for it is a manner of speech, that all those of which he does not speak are so understood. But concerning the bases we have already said why one column has two, because here too he calls the "capitals" *bases*[^6].

**177.** 6. (26, 22-25) We see, therefore, the two sides of the tabernacle, north and south, extended with twenty columns each: there are two remaining, the eastern and the western, which, if they had had as many columns, would without doubt be square. But how many it has, of the western is not kept silent, of the eastern it is kept silent: whether because it had none, and there alone the curtains were stretched without columns from the last column of one side to the other last of the other; or whether for some reason they are kept silent, so that, though silent, they are understood, I do not know.

Afterward, indeed, on that very eastern side ten columns are mentioned, but those of the court of which he afterward speaks, which he orders to be placed around this tabernacle. So, the north and south sides of the tabernacle having been mentioned with twenty columns each, then it continues and adds, saying: *and behind the tabernacle, on the part which is toward the sea, you shall make six columns; and two columns you shall make at the corners of the tabernacle at the rear, and they shall be equal downward; and they shall be meeting together, equal at the capitals into one joining. So you shall make for both the two corners; let them be equal. And there shall be eight columns, and their bases sixteen silver ones: two bases for one column, and two bases for one column on both its parts.*

Concerning the bases the reckoning is similar and the manner of speech is similar. The western side, then—for this is toward the sea—is extended with eight columns; six in the middle, and two at the corners, which he says ought to be equal at one joining; I believe because the corner joins two sides together, and the corner column is common to each side, this one to the western and the northern, that one to the western and the southern. But what he says, *equal also downward,* this surely he says so that they might be balanced to the plumb line, lest they be more robust below than above, as most columns are.

**177.** 7. (26, 26-29) Then he says: *and you shall make bars of incorruptible wood, five for one column on one side of the tabernacle; and five bars for the column of the second side of the tabernacle; and five bars for the column of the rear side of the tabernacle, which is toward the sea.* It is strange if it can be doubted that the eastern side had no columns in this inner tabernacle, around which the court is afterward placed. He orders, then, each single column of all three sides to have five bars.

*And the middle bar,* he says, *shall pass through, among the middle columns, from one side to the other side.* This he seems to say, that the bar would pass from column to column, and be extended through the interval of the columns from the side of one to the side of another: and by this, one column of all these was not going to have its own five bars; to which last one five came, coming from the column nearest to it.

*And the columns,* he says, *you shall gild with gold, and you shall make bronze rings, into which you shall put the bars, and you shall gild the bars with gold.* Lest the columns be hollowed with holes, into which the bars would enter, he ordered rings to be made, by which the ends of the bars would be held on either side. Accordingly these rings are understood to have hung from little loops fixed in the wood, so that they could take and hold the end parts of the bars.

**177.** 8. (26, 30-37) *And you shall erect,* he says, *the tabernacle according to the form which was shown you on the mountain. And you shall make a veil of hyacinth and purple and twisted scarlet and spun fine linen; you shall make it woven work, cherubim. And you shall set it upon four incorruptible columns gilded with gold, and their capitals gold, and their four bases silver. And you shall set the veil upon the columns, and you shall bring in there, within the veil, the ark of the testimony; and the veil shall divide for you between the midst of the holy place and the midst of the holy of holies. And you shall cover with the veil the ark of the testimony in the holy of holies.* All these things are manifest, that within, inside this veil set upon four columns, was the ark of the testimony: which he ordered to be covered by the veil not so that the veil lay over the cover of the Ark, but that it be set over against it.

Then he says: *and you shall put the table outside, beyond the veil; and the lampstand over against the table on the part of the tabernacle which looks toward the south; and the table you shall put on the part of the tabernacle which looks toward the north.* This too is plain.

But what follows: *and you shall make a screen of hyacinth and purple and twisted scarlet and twisted fine linen, the work of an embroiderer. And you shall make for the screen five columns, and you shall gild them with gold, and their capitals gold; and you shall cast for them five bronze bases*: for what use this screen, stretched on five columns, is made, will afterward appear, for here it does not appear. For he wishes it to be the screen of the door of the tabernacle, of this inner one, namely, around which the court is placed.

Then he orders the altar of sacrifices and holocausts to be made; and he says how it is to be made: but he does not yet say where it is placed; but this too will afterward appear.

**177.** 9. (27, 9-13) From here now he speaks of the court until the end, which is to be placed around that tabernacle, of whose construction he first spoke. *And you shall make,* he says, *a court;* which in Greek is called *aule,* not *aulaia*; which some of our translators, not distinguishing, rendered both this and those curtains—which the Greeks call *aulaiai,* not *aulai*—as "courts," saying: *and you shall make a tabernacle of ten courts,* where they ought to have said *of ten curtains*[^7]. But some, much more unskilled, rendered both *aulai* and *aulaiai* as "doors." But just as in the Latin language we find *aulaea,* which the Greeks call *aulaiai*; so what they call *aule,* our people called *aula.* But now by that name not a court, but a royal house is signified in the Latin language; while among the Greeks, a court.

So: *and you shall make,* he says, *a court for the tabernacle on the side which looks toward the south; and the hangings of the court of twisted fine linen; let the length be a hundred cubits for one side; their columns twenty, and their bronze bases twenty; and their rings, and their arches, silver. So also for the side which is toward the north, hangings a hundred cubits in length; and their columns twenty, and their bronze bases twenty, and their rings, and the arches of the columns, and the bases overlaid with silver. But the breadth of the court which is toward the sea, its hangings of fifty cubits; their columns ten, and their bases ten. And the breadth of the court which is toward the east, of fifty cubits; its columns ten, and their bases ten.*

**177.** 10. Here now we see columns mentioned on the east, when he speaks of the court, and calling them ten with bronze bases, as he said also of the western ones: where a most difficult question arises. For it is easy indeed for us to take it that on the east there is one row of columns belonging to the court, by which the inner tabernacle is girded on all four sides; because on that side the inner tabernacle had no columns: but on the west, where there were already the eight columns of the inner tabernacle, how are we to take these ten too, which he mentions as belonging to the outer court, as though on the west there were two rows of columns, eight of the inner and ten of the outer? But if it is so, the sides of the outer court will be longer too than those of the inner tabernacle, so that there may be room for another row of columns to be set from end to end, and not run into the prior row belonging to the inner tabernacle. And if this is so, it will follow that those twenty columns of the two sides of the inner tabernacle, south and north, are separated by shorter intervals than those twenty which the outer court has on those same sides. And because those outer rows of twenty columns, as Scripture says, occupy a hundred cubits each; while these inner ones of as many columns occupy however many cubits fewer—since Scripture did not express this—it will follow that those eight columns of the inner tabernacle on the western side are more spaced out than those twenty of the same tabernacle on the south and north, so that there may be comprised as much space as suffices for stretching around those ten curtains, of which he first said this tabernacle was made.

For they have twenty and eight cubits each, which together make two hundred and eighty: of which, if a hundred each were on the two sides of south and north, where the rows of twenty columns are, surely forty cubits each would be stretched along the other two sides of east and west, and in proportion forty cubits would correspond to eight columns, as a hundred cubits to twenty columns; but the sides of the outer court would not be longer, since they are defined by a hundred cubits each; and therefore there would be no way for that row of ten columns to enclose, from the southern corner to the northern corner, the row of the inner eight columns.

Accordingly, that the inner tabernacle may be surrounded on all sides by the court, it must be also of a shorter length: and thus it must needs be that its twenty columns on the two sides, on which it extends lengthwise, be set more closely than even those same twenty of the outer court; and the eight columns of the inner tabernacle on the western part more sparsely than the ten on the same part of the outer court: since whatever fewer cubits of those curtains is stretched along the twenty columns of the two sides toward the south and the north, is to be compensated by the breadth of the eastern and western sides, so that the two hundred and eighty cubits of the curtains may be completed. For he did not order anything to be doubled of these curtains, as he did of the hair-cloth coverings, where one is more.

Wherefore, if the length of the inner tabernacle, which could be enclosed by the outer court, is shortened only so far that along its twenty columns not a hundred cubits each of curtains are stretched, but, for example, at least ninety-six each, so that they are four cubits shorter: these same four cubits, that is, eight cubits, must surely be stretched along the two other sides, the eastern and western; and so along those eight western columns of the inner tabernacle not forty cubits are stretched, but forty-four, and another forty-four on the east. Since, then, along the ten columns of the outer court fifty cubits are stretched, but along the eight of the inner tabernacle forty-four cubits are stretched, the intervals of the inner eight columns are found to be more spaced than those of the outer ten: for if they were equal, then along the eight columns forty cubits would be stretched, as fifty were stretched along ten; since what eight is in proportion to ten, that forty is to fifty. For the number five, forty has eight times, fifty ten times.

**177.** 11. (27, 14-16) Nor would the unequal intervals of the columns disturb us—that they were set more closely on the sides of the length, where there are twenty, but more sparsely on the side of the breadth, where there are eight—if there did not exist something else that pressed us to change the opinion. For when he had mentioned that the length of the court toward the sea has hangings of fifty cubits, and ten columns, and ten bases of them; and the breadth of the court toward the east likewise of fifty cubits, and ten columns and ten bases[^8]; and these things having been said, the form of the tabernacle with its court surrounding it on every side seemed complete, he threw in something else, where and how it is to be understood is found most difficult:

*And fifteen cubits,* he says, *the height of the hangings for one side; their columns three, and their bases three. And the second side, fifteen cubits the height of the hangings; their columns three, and their bases three. And the covering of the gate of the court, twenty cubits in height, of hyacinth and purple and spun scarlet and twisted fine linen, embroidered with needlework in variety; their columns four and their bases four.*

Where these are set up, that form of the tabernacle being complete, I do not see: but I plainly see that the number of columns is the same, ten, in three and three and four, namely three on the sides and four in the middle. And by this those hangings of fifty cubits will not be woven together, lest there be no entrance in the court: but by the fifteen cubits each the middle twenty cubits will be separated, so that they make the covering of the gate of the tabernacle, that is, the veil, which hangs as an ornament and screen, and holds the space of four columns, which is assigned and set apart for the gate of the court.

Therefore that veil itself, divided and set apart from the sides of fifteen cubits each, he wished to be distinguished also in appearance, so that it might be varied with needlework in those four colors. But those sides of fifteen cubits each, having three columns on this side and that, if they are joined to the gate of the court along the same straight line, there will be no space between the ten columns of the outer court and the eight of the inner tabernacle, where there might be the altar of five cubits[^9], occupying a square space; and before the altar itself, where the altar is served; and between it and the door of the inner tabernacle, where there might be the bronze basin[^10].

For it was ordered to be set where the priests might wash their hands and feet, when they enter the tabernacle, or when they approach to serve the altar: which, unless we understand it outside the tabernacle, in the court, how can they first wash their hands and feet, and so enter the tabernacle? But we cannot set the altar outside the court; because he prescribed both the tabernacle and the altar to be surrounded by the court[^11]. It remains, therefore, that we take those sides of fifteen cubits each, on this side and that, and of three columns each, in such a way from the sides that they make an interval of as many cubits between the gate of the court and the door of the inner tabernacle: the gate indeed open for twenty cubits in four columns, and having a veil varied with embroidered needlework of twenty cubits; but the door of the inner tabernacle, where that screen stretched on five columns is set[^12]: which surely we ought to understand as set not within that row of eight columns, but outside, toward the court.

For then it will be the veil of the door of the tabernacle, which door was opened as it were by folding leaves, where the curtains were not joined to one another by rings and loops. Or if perhaps within that row of eight columns of the inner tabernacle this screen on five columns was set against the door of the tabernacle, so that when it was opened the inner parts would not be laid bare, lest they be seen by those looking in; which screen, however, whether it was set within that row of columns or outside, is not sufficiently clear: without doubt it stood at a moderate interval from that same row of columns, lest the five columns, packed too closely to the four columns, should rather block the entrance than veil it.

**177.** 12. According to this manner, then, and according to this form of the tabernacle, there is now no need to set those twenty columns of the sides of the inner tabernacle toward the south and north more closely, and to make those eight which were on the west more sparse. For those ten of the outer court on that same western part do not make a long row of columns, by which the eight inner ones would be enclosed, but, with three set on each side, and four in the gate, they enclose a space where there may be both the altar of holocausts within the gate of the court before the door of the tabernacle; and the basin between the door of the tabernacle and the altar; and the interval necessary for the ministry between the altar and the gate of the court: and so that whole space of the court is enclosed by ten columns, three on the north, and three on the south, and four on the west, as if you make the Greek letter Pi.

And so this same space was joined to the longer row of columns of the inner tabernacle, as if to the aforesaid letter, on that part where it does not have one, you join the prolongation of the letter which is called iota; so that by its part which is in the middle, it is enclosed on that side, and there remain on this side and that the remaining parts of that iota. There could, then, be counted ten columns in that long row of the western part of the inner tabernacle, but with those eight there were added two, which had been the last in the rows of the sides of the court, north and south. For those ten too which properly belonged to the court on the western part, whence one also entered to the tabernacle, had three on the sides, and four in front, where the gate was, and so embraced the space necessary for the use of the sacrifices within the court before the tabernacle. But on the three columns which were on the sides, there were hangings of fine linen of fifteen cubits each: and on the four where the gate was, there was a veil of twenty cubits varied with embroidered needlework.

**177.** 13. (38, 9-18) Nor let it disturb one that Scripture says: *fifteen cubits the height of the hangings for one side; their columns three, and their bases three. And the second side, fifteen cubits the height of the hangings; their columns three, and their bases three. And the covering of the gate of the court, twenty cubits in height*[^13]. For it calls "height" the same as had been the length of the hangings. For the same is the height when they are woven, which is the length when they are stretched.

Lest we seem to be conjecturing this, Scripture, mentioning this same thing in another place, says: *and they made the court which is toward the south, the hangings of the court of twisted fine linen, a hundred by a hundred*: that is, a hundred cubits of hangings along a hundred cubits of the space which the twenty columns occupied.

Then it continues: *and their columns twenty, and their bases twenty, of bronze. And the side which is toward the north, a hundred by a hundred, and their columns twenty, and their bases twenty, of bronze; and the side which is toward the sea, curtains of fifty cubits; their columns ten, and their bases ten.* It calls "curtains" the same as "hangings." *And the side which is toward the east, hangings of fifty cubits.* After these things it returns to the rear of the tabernacle, to show how those ten columns embraced the space of the court of which it was speaking.

*Fifteen cubits,* it says, *which is at the back.* It calls it "back," because it was the rear of the tabernacle, that is, on the western part. *And their columns,* it says, *three, and their bases three. And at the second back, on this side and that, according to the gate of the court, curtains of fifteen cubits; their columns three, and their bases three.*

It is certainly manifest that the same two are called "backs" in this place, when all the things are mentioned as they were made, which were called "sides" when it was prescribed that they be made: "sides," namely, because, joined on this side and that, they enclosed the space of the western gate of the court; but "backs," because this part of the court was at the rear of the tabernacle, that is, on the western part.

It continues and says: *all the hangings of the court of twisted fine linen, and the bases of the columns of bronze, and their loops of silver, and their capitals overlaid with silver; and the columns overlaid with silver, all the columns of the court.*

Then it adds what it had not yet mentioned in this place: *and the screen of the gate of the court, the work of an embroiderer, of hyacinth and purple and spun scarlet and twisted fine linen; twenty cubits in length and breadth.* Behold where it appears that the same was called above "height," which had been the length of the stretched hangings.

Finally it adds: *and the breadth five cubits.* For with so many cubits of breadth were the hangings of the outer court erected, as those of the inner with four cubits. For so it had said above too: *but the length of the court a hundred by a hundred, and the breadth fifty by fifty, and the height five cubits, of twisted fine linen.* Calling that "height" which it afterward called "breadth"; since what is the breadth of things lying down, the same is the height of things erected. As, what I mentioned a little before, the height when they are woven, the same is the length when they are stretched.

**177.** 14. (26, 8-12) Now let us see what I had deferred: how, from this form of the whole tabernacle, which I have set before the eyes as I could, that difficulty concerning the hair-cloth coverings may be resolved. Which was perhaps the more abstruse for this reason, that something was there said by anticipation which would avail for the work of which he was afterward going to speak, when he would describe the court to be placed around the tabernacle.

Now therefore let us see the words themselves. *And you shall put under,* it says, *what remains over in the coverings of the tabernacle; the half of the covering that remains over you shall undercover; what abounds in the coverings of the tabernacle, you shall undercover behind the tabernacle.* All this that was said has one sense, because the half of a covering that has remained over, that is, what has abounded of the coverings of the tabernacle, is to be undercovered behind the tabernacle.

How, then, a half of a covering remains over, that is, abounds and is left, must be inquired from that series of connected coverings: since he wished five to be connected together, and six together, as, speaking above, he had said that the sixth covering was to be doubled from the face of the tabernacle, that is, from the east. For so many times he signified the rear of the tabernacle to be on the western part, that is, toward the sea.

What, then, is the face of the tabernacle, unless that part is to be understood which is toward the east? That part, then, in which the five coverings are connected, has a hundred and fifty cubits, that is, five times thirty (for each was of thirty cubits, as God had ordered them to be made): but that part in which there were not five, but six coverings likewise connected to one another, had a hundred and eighty cubits, that is, six times thirty; and by this, with one covering of them doubled, as was ordered, toward the face of the tabernacle, in that doubling fifteen cubits were diminished; which being subtracted, a hundred and sixty-five remained.

And therefore, after the hundred and fifty cubits, by which this part of six coverings was also equaled to that part of five coverings, fifteen cubits remained over and abounded. For on that side, on the part of the five coverings, there were a hundred and fifty cubits: but on this side, on the part of the six coverings, with one covering doubled toward the face of the tabernacle, there were a hundred and sixty-five cubits. This part, therefore, had fifteen cubits more.

This he calls the half of a covering, which he orders to be undercovered behind the tabernacle, so that, because that one was doubled from the face, this redundant half from the rear part of the tabernacle would not be doubled, but undercovered—that is, all those fifteen cubits, put under, would be covered: and thus they too would be subtracted from that length, just as those likewise fifteen from the face of the tabernacle were subtracted by the doubling of one covering: and in that way to the hundred and fifty cubits of the five coverings there would correspond from the other part a hundred and fifty cubits of the six coverings; namely with thirty cubits subtracted from the hundred and eighty, by the covering doubled at the face of the tabernacle, and the half covering undercovered at the rear of the tabernacle.

**177.** 15. (26, 13) Now what follows is something else, and brings in another question, on account of which especially I thought the exposition of this passage was to be deferred, so that we might first see both the form of setting up the tabernacle, and what was written about the court to be placed around it. It continues, then: *a cubit on this side, and a cubit on that, of that which remains over in the coverings of the length of the coverings of the tabernacle, it shall be covering over the sides of the tabernacle on this side and that, so as to cover it.*

It is one thing that one part of six coverings will exceed the other part of five coverings, on account of the greater number of coverings, of which we have already spoken: another, what will remain over of the length of the coverings, of which it is now said. For in this way part is not compared to part, and the one is found to exceed the other—that one, namely, which has six coverings, that which has five coverings; which two, that they might be equaled to one another, the doubling of one covering was made at the face of the tabernacle, the undercovering of a half at the rear:

but the hair-cloth coverings themselves, compared to the curtains, of which he ordered ten to be made for the inner tabernacle, woven in four colors, are found to be longer by two cubits each. For those curtains were each of twenty-eight cubits, these of thirty: therefore here he does not say: "of that which remains over of the coverings"; but: *of that which remains over in the coverings, of the length of the coverings.*

What, then, is: *a cubit on this side, and a cubit on that, it shall be covering over the sides of the tabernacle,* unless because that length, in which the hair-cloth coverings are longer than the curtains by two cubits each, one by one, is not all to be driven to one part, that is, so that whatever remains over is gathered to the rear of the tabernacle; but is to be distributed equally, and as much of it given to the front of the tabernacle as to the rear: that is, since two cubits in each covering make that length which remains over, so that this part takes a cubit from it, and that part a cubit; thus from its own single cubits this part will have ten cubits, and that from its own single ones ten; since ten coverings longer by two cubits each make a length of twenty cubits, by which their series seems to exceed the series of the curtains?

**177.** 16. Next it is to be seen for what space to be girded these twenty cubits, which remain over of the length of the coverings, avail. For if the inner tabernacle is covered round about by the hair-cloth coverings, they so remain over that there is nothing at all of them to be used for covering. Whence it remains that they too be undercovered, and by undercovering subtracted, which Scripture does not say. For the ten curtains, which have twenty and eight cubits each, by which the tabernacle is stretched within, embrace as much space as two hundred and eighty cubits can embrace: whence those longer sides, southern and northern, having twenty columns each, take from these cubits a hundred cubits each; there remain eighty, which are distributed forty each to the two remaining shorter sides, the eastern, which has no row of columns, and the western, where there were eight columns.

Accordingly, in the hair-cloth coverings, since, thirty cubits being subtracted, three hundred remained, if with three hundred cubits of hair-cloth coverings the two hundred and eighty of the curtains are covered, twenty will so remain over that there is nothing for them to be used for. Accordingly those two cubits, which the individual hair-cloth ones have more, from which the sum of twenty cubits was gathered, are to be distributed thus: *a cubit on this side, and a cubit on that;* that is, lest they all be driven to one part, so that they avail for covering the sides not of the tabernacle, but of the outer court—that is, that all those three hundred cubits of hair-cloth coverings gird the tabernacle on the outside. For with the sides of the outer court of a hundred cubits each added, that is, of south and north, there remain fifty cubits each of east and west, which all make three hundred, for the covering of which the three hundred of the hair-cloth ones suffice.

This is what he says: *a cubit on this side, and a cubit on that;* that is, the distribution of the two cubits by which each hair-cloth covering is longer, this is *of that which remains over in the coverings of the length of the coverings, it shall be covering over the sides of the tabernacle;* those outer ones, namely, which belong to the court, *on this side and that, so as to cover it*: not those of the court itself, which are stretched with a hundred cubits each and twenty columns; for these were not made longer than those of the inner tabernacle which are stretched with ten curtains, and themselves also have twenty columns. For just as the two sides of the inner tabernacle, on the north and south, extend a hundred cubits each, so also those of the outer court.

Not, therefore, for covering those outer sides of as many columns does the length of the hair-cloth coverings avail, by which they exceed the length of the curtains. For they give to the outer sides as much as they would give to the inner, that is, a hundred cubits each, which make two hundred: but because for the eastern and western sides forty cubits each would suffice, if the inner tabernacle alone were girded by the hair-cloth coverings; but with the sides of the court added, the breadth of the tabernacle grew, so that for covering the eastern and western sides now not forty, but fifty cubits each are necessary: for covering these the greater length of the hair-cloth coverings than of the curtains could avail; so that not both cubits, by which they are longer by two each, would be spent on one part, but *a cubit on this side, and a cubit on that;* and thus from that superabundance the eastern side would have ten cubits, and the western another ten. For twenty are two times ten: because the thirty cubits of the eleventh covering are subtracted from this circuit by doubling and undercovering.

**177.** 17. But since what was rendered in Latin: *a cubit on this side, and a cubit on that, of that which remains over in the coverings of the length of the coverings of the tabernacle, it shall be covering over the sides of the tabernacle,* the Greek has as *plagia,* which some Latins rendered not *sides,* but *oblique parts,* it rightly disturbs us; because, although nothing here seems oblique, where all the angles of the four sides are right; yet those sides cannot be called *plagia,* of which one is in front, the other behind, that is, the eastern and the western, but *plagia* can be called those on the right and on the left, that is, the northern and the southern: since, then, those are not *plagia* which have fifty cubits each, for the covering of which sides we said the exceeding length of the hair-cloth coverings could have availed; how will it be true: *a cubit on this side, and a cubit on that, of that which remains over in the coverings of the length of the coverings of the tabernacle, it shall be covering over the sides of the tabernacle*?

But surely he speaks of covering those sides which he also calls "backs," of fifteen cubits each and three columns each, which, with the gate of the court having twenty cubits and four columns, complete fifty cubits and ten columns. These sides, from their own limits, set the gate of the court in the middle on the one part, but on the other the door of the tabernacle: between the gate of the court and the door of the tabernacle lies a space, as much as those cubits enclose, twenty from the gate, and fifteen each on right and left. In that space is the altar of holocausts within the gate of the court before the door of the tabernacle; and between the altar and the door of the tabernacle the bronze basin, where the priests washed their hands and feet.

But, the measurements being carefully examined, perhaps in those sides of three columns each, which were called *plagia* in Greek, some obliquity too is found; so that not in vain did some of our translators render *oblique* what they found in the Greek as *plagia.* For the hair-cloth coverings cannot, with their fifteen cubits each, cover the fifteen cubits of hangings on those sides, unless, on the rear parts of the tabernacle, before they bend off toward those sides, they have spent no more than ten cubits each. And thus, from the straight line of the rear of the tabernacle, that is, on the western part—which line, when it had eight columns belonging to the inner tabernacle, began to have ten with the sides of the outer court added; and when it had had forty cubits belonging to the eight columns, began to have fifty along the ten columns—from this line, then, when ten cubits coming from each corner have been covered by the hair-cloth coverings, there will remain in the middle thirty not covered by the hair-cloth coverings, but only by those curtains; in the middle of which thirty cubits, by however many cubits they were stretched, was the door of the tabernacle.

Accordingly those sides of three columns and fifteen cubits each, if from those limits of theirs by which they were joined to the gate of the court they were open between themselves by twenty cubits, because so much had the gate which separated those sides; but from the other limits by which they clung to that rear line of the tabernacle, of which we have spoken, they had between themselves thirty cubits; without doubt they were oblique: since they were open between themselves more on this part, where they had thirty cubits in the middle, than on that part where they had twenty cubits in the middle. Thus those ten cubits of the hair-cloth coverings, which was the half of the abounding length, which availed for the rear part of the tabernacle, that is, the western, just as the other ten availed for the front part, that is, the eastern, completed with five cubits each the covering of those sides which were called *plagia* in Greek, five on this side and five on that.

Which, had they been lacking, ten cubits would be covered on those same sides, and five would be bare. And so, as it seems to me, it is better understood that it was hence said: *a cubit on this side, and a cubit on that, of that which remains over in the coverings of the length of the coverings of the tabernacle,* not because they were so, since on this side and that there were five each; but because they were redundant from that length in which the hair-cloth ones were longer than the curtains by two cubits each, of which two cubits of each covering one cubit had availed for the eastern part: there remained surely the other cubit for the western part, so that *a cubit on this side and a cubit on that* was covering over the *plagia* of the tabernacle. Whence it was said, *on this side and that, so as to cover it,* because it would not cover the whole, if those same five cubits had been lacking.

**177.** 18. Now, since it has been sufficiently discussed how all those things are to be understood which seemed obscure in the setting up of the tabernacle, let us strive, briefly if we can, to show what has been accomplished by that same discussion.

From the west, then, one entered, and the first thing for entering was the gate of the court, which was open for twenty cubits, and had four columns, from which hung a veil stretched twenty cubits, but erected five cubits, in those four often-mentioned colors, varied with embroidered needlework. Entering by this gate, one was received into the court, whose sides on the right and left extended inward fifteen cubits each and three columns each; so that they set in the middle the door of the inner tabernacle, on that part to which they came, just as they set in the middle the gate of the court, on that part whence they began.

This court, then, was broader than it was long. For its length was from its gate to the door of the inner tabernacle, about fifteen cubits: but its breadth around the gate was twenty cubits, around the door thirty cubits. Whence those sides are understood to have been oblique, which were on the right and left in three columns each and fifteen cubits. In this court was the square altar of sacrifices, namely five cubits long, and as many broad. Between the gate and the altar there was a space, where those moved about who placed the sacrifices on the altar: but inward, between the altar and the door of the tabernacle, was the place of ashes before the altar, and then the bronze basin where the priests washed their hands and feet, whether about to serve the altar in the court, or about to enter the inner tabernacle. The hangings, moreover, of this court on the sides of three columns each were of fine linen, stretched fifteen cubits, erected five cubits.

**177.** 19. From this court, then, one entered the door of the tabernacle, when you had passed the altar and the bronze basin. But one entered by the curtains being opened, by which—ten of them, five on this side and five on that, set over against one another—the whole inner tabernacle itself was girded. Entering this door, one met the veil which had been set against the door, stretched on five columns, varied with those four colors: which veil, when he prescribed it to be made, he called a *drawing-screen,* I believe because it would run, by drawing and drawing back, when it covered and opened the entrance. This veil passed, the middle part of the tabernacle received one, between this veil and that other inner one, which had been set upon four columns, made of those four colors, and separated between the holy things which were outside, and the Holy of Holies set within. In this middle space, then, between those two veils, there was a golden table, which held the loaves of the presence on the north part; and over against it a golden lampstand of seven lamps on the south part. Thus far the lower priests were permitted to enter.

**177.** 20. But within, that is, in the Holy of Holies beyond the veil of four columns, was the gilded Ark of the Testimony, in which were the stone tablets of the Law, and the rod of Aaron, and the golden urn with manna, and the golden propitiatory above, where the two cherubim stood, overshadowing the propitiatory with their wings and attending to each other and to it. But before the Ark, that is, between the Ark and the veil, was set the altar of incense, which Scripture sometimes calls golden, sometimes gilded—calling it golden, surely, because it was overlaid with gold. To this Holy of Holies it was not permitted to enter except for the high priest, daily, for the sake of offering incense; but once a year with blood for purifying the altar, and if perhaps necessity sometime required, for the sin of the priest, or of the whole congregation, as is written in *Leviticus*[^14]. Thus one entered the tabernacle from the west, that is, from the gate of the court inward to the eastern side where the Ark of the Testimony was.

**177.** 21. But this inner tabernacle, which began not from the gate of the court, but from the door which was called the door of the tabernacle, and ended lengthwise on the eastern side where the Ark of the Testimony was, was enclosed by ten curtains, of which each was twenty-eight cubits, five on this side and five on that, connected to one another by little loops and rings, and set over against one another; and by twenty columns on the longer sides of the north and south, and eight columns on the western side, but on the eastern side by no columns, but by curtains alone. Which ten curtains were erected four cubits, and through the whole circuit were stretched two hundred and eighty cubits: of which a hundred each were on the longer sides of south and north along twenty columns each; but forty cubits on the remaining two shorter sides; one of the west along eight columns; the other of the east, where there were no columns, but the curtains alone were stretched from the two corner columns alone, but none in the middle: and these ten curtains were woven of four colors.

This inner tabernacle, then, was surrounded by the court, on the south by twenty columns, and on the north by twenty. Which two sides of the court had a length equal to the sides of the inner tabernacle; because they too were extended with twenty columns each, by as many cubits, that is, a hundred each. But on the eastern side the court was enclosed by ten columns, fifty cubits: which row of columns was straight, and ran into those two corner ones of the inner tabernacle, which alone the eastern part had; accordingly with these they were completed as ten. But on the western side the court had indeed ten columns, yet not in a straight row, but, as we have already shown, as it were a three-sided portico, four at the gate, and three on each side.

**177.** 22. But the whole court, around the tabernacle, was girded with hangings of fine linen, which were erected five cubits: over these came the eleven hair-cloth coverings, five on one part connected to one another, six on the other. In the connection, then, of the five coverings, there were a hundred and fifty cubits; but on the other part, in the connection of the six coverings, there were a hundred and eighty cubits: since the coverings each were of thirty cubits. But that one part might be equaled to the other, one covering was doubled at the face of the tabernacle, that is, on the east; and a half covering was undercovered at the rear part, that is, on the west: and thus thirty cubits were subtracted, as great as was the length of one covering; and there remained a hundred and fifty, as many as there were on the other part. The circuit, then, of the hair-cloth coverings, by which the court of the tabernacle was girded, was stretched three hundred cubits, just as the circuit of the ten curtains of the inner tabernacle was two hundred and eighty cubits. For the curtains each had twenty and eight cubits in length; but the hair-cloth coverings were thirty cubits long. Wherefore, of the circuit of the curtains of the inner tabernacle, which was two hundred and eighty cubits, a hundred each were on the longer sides of south and north, and forty each on the two shorter ones of east and west: but of the circuit of the hair-cloth coverings, by which the outer court was covered—because the circuit was three hundred cubits—a hundred each were on the longer sides of south and north, since they were equal to the sides of the inner tabernacle, but fifty each on the two remaining parts of east and west.

And by this those two cubits, by which the hair-cloth covering was longer than the curtain, availed not for the sides of south and north, which were equal in the outer court and the inner tabernacle, but for the sides of east and west. For on those sides the breadth of the tabernacle had grown when the court was placed around it on the outside: but fifty cubits of the hair-cloth coverings on the eastern part were stretched along the straight row of ten columns, and for these availed one cubit of those two by which the length of those same coverings had been greater; but the other fifty cubits, which were owed to the western side, for which the other of those two cubits availed, were not stretched along a straight row of columns. For there was that three-sided portico, as it were, which enclosed the space of the court where the altar of sacrifices was, with four columns at the gate, and three on each side: and by this those fifty cubits could not gird the gate too, but were stretched as far as the covering of those oblique sides, which were on three columns each and fifteen cubits. And the hair-cloth coverings were erected four cubits, and by them were covered the hangings of fine linen of the court, whose erection was five cubits.

**177.** 23. But the skins dyed red came over the hair-cloth coverings. And above, that is, on the part of the roof, in place of a vault, the tabernacle was covered with hyacinth-colored skins: whether together with the court and that inner space too, does not appear; but it is more credible that the spaces of the court which were between the outer and inner columns had open sky, especially that of the west, where the altar of sacrifices was.
