# How the Hebrews could reach so great a number in Egypt.

**47.** 1. (12, 37. 40) *And the children of Israel set out from Rameses to Succoth, about six hundred thousand footmen of fighting men, besides their equipment,* or *property;* if the Greek *aposkeue* can rightly be interpreted in this way—a word by which Scripture indicates that not only movable goods but also moving creatures are signified, where Judah, speaking to his father, says: *send the boy with me, and rising up we will go, that we may live and not die, both we and you and our substance*[^1]. For there the Greek has *aposkeuen* where the Latin rendered *substance;* which our translators sometimes render *property,* as now we have chosen to say *equipment,* provided that under that name both people and beasts of burden, or all livestock, are understood. Whether wives too can be understood there, I do not know. Yet since Scripture mentions six hundred thousand footmen, adding the words *besides the equipment,* or *property,* or *substance,* or whatever word better renders *aposkeue,* it is plain that people too were signified—whether in servants, or in women, or in those ages not fit for military service—so that we understand the six hundred thousand footmen to be only those who could be armed in a military column.

**47.** 2. But it is commonly asked whether the Hebrews could reach so great a number during those years in Egypt which a careful reckoning of the number in Scripture can establish; and first, how many those years were is no small question. For God says to Abraham, when that sacrifice had been made of a three-year-old cow, a goat, a ram, a turtledove, and a pigeon[^2], before not only Isaac but not even Ishmael had yet been born: *know assuredly that your seed will be a sojourner in a land not its own; and they will reduce them to slavery and ill-treat them for four hundred years*[^3]. If, then, we take the four hundred years to be understood as in slavery under the Egyptians, it was no small span of time in which the people might so multiply. But that there were not so many years, Scripture testifies by the clearest indication.

**47.** 3. For some think that the four hundred and thirty years should be taken from the time Jacob entered Egypt until the people was freed from there through Moses, since it is written in Exodus: *and the sojourning of the children of Israel, which they sojourned in the land of Egypt and in the land of Canaan, they and their fathers, was four hundred and thirty years*[^4]*.* But they want the years of their slavery to be four hundred, because it is written in *Genesis*: *know assuredly that your seed will be a sojourner in a land not its own; and they will reduce them to slavery and ill-treat them for four hundred years.*

But since the years of their slavery are reckoned after the death of Joseph (for while he lived, not only did they not serve there, but they even held sway), there is no way to reckon four hundred and thirty years in Egypt. For Jacob entered in the thirty-ninth year of his son; since Joseph was thirty years old when he appeared before Pharaoh[^5] and began to rule under him; and when the seven years of plenty had passed, in the second year of the famine Jacob entered Egypt with his other sons[^6]; and so Joseph was then thirty-nine years old, and having completed one hundred and ten years of his life, he died[^7]: he lived therefore in Egypt, after his father came to him, seventy-one years; and if we subtract these from the four hundred and thirty years, there will remain, as the years of slavery—that is, after the death of Joseph—not four hundred, but three hundred and fifty-nine years.

But if we think we ought to reckon from the time Joseph began to rule under Pharaoh, so that Israel is understood in a manner to have entered Egypt when his son was exalted there to such great power[^8], even so there will be three hundred and fifty, which Tychonius wants to be taken as four hundred, so that the part is understood from the whole—that is, from the whole hundred a part of fifty—and he proves that Scripture is accustomed to use this rule of speech. But if, as can be said with somewhat more probability, we hold that Israel entered Egypt when Joseph began to be there, having been sold, we shall have to subtract thirteen years more, so that we take three hundred and thirty-seven years for the four hundred.

But since Scripture records that Kohath the son of Levi, Moses's grandfather, entered Egypt with his grandfather Jacob[^9], and says that he lived one hundred and thirty years[^10]; and that his son Amram, the father of Moses, lived one hundred and thirty-seven; and says that Moses was eighty years old when he freed the people from Egypt[^11]: even if Kohath had begotten the father of Moses in the very year he died, and that Amram likewise had begotten Moses in the last year of his life, the years one hundred and thirty and one hundred and thirty-seven and eighty, reckoned together, make three hundred and forty-seven years, not four hundred and thirty. But if anyone should say that Kohath the son of Levi was born in the last year of Joseph's life, nearly seventy years can be added to that sum, because Joseph lived seventy-one years in Egypt after his father's entry. Therefore even so, the seventy years of Joseph's life from Jacob's entry into Egypt until the birth of Kohath—if he is asserted to have been born then—and the one hundred and thirty years of Kohath himself, and the one hundred and thirty-seven of his son Amram the father of Moses, and the eighty of Moses himself, make four hundred and seventeen years, not four hundred and thirty.

**47.** 4. Accordingly, that reckoning which Eusebius followed in his *Chronicle* rests upon a clear truth. For he reckons the four hundred and thirty years from that promise by which God called Abraham to go out from his own land into the land of Canaan; since the Apostle too, when he praised and commended Abraham's faith—in that promise by which he wants Christ to be understood as prophesied, that is, the promise by which God promised Abraham that in him all the tribes of the earth would be blessed[^12]—says: *this I say: the covenant confirmed by God, the Law made four hundred and thirty years after does not annul, so as to void the promises*[^13]. From that promise, then, by which Abraham was called and believed God, the Apostle says the Law was made four hundred and thirty years after, not from the time Jacob entered Egypt. Then Scripture itself in *Exodus* signified this well enough: for it did not say, "the sojourning of the children of Israel, which they sojourned in the land of Egypt, was four hundred and thirty years"; but it plainly said, *which they sojourned in the land of Egypt and in the land of Canaan, they and their fathers.* And by this it is manifest that the time of the patriarchs Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob is also to be reckoned, from when Abraham began to sojourn in the land of Canaan—that is, from that promise in which the Apostle praises his faith[^14]—until the time Israel entered Egypt. For during all that time the fathers sojourned in the land of Canaan, and then the seed of Israel in Egypt; and so the four hundred and thirty years were completed from the promise until the exodus of Israel from Egypt, when the Law was made on Mount Sinai, which *does not annul the covenant so as to void the promises*.

**47.** 5. In the seventy-fifth year of his life, then, as Scripture says, Abraham went out into the land of Canaan[^15], and begot Isaac when he was a hundred years old[^16]. So there are twenty-five years from the promise until Isaac was born. To these are added all the years of Isaac's life, that is, one hundred and eighty[^17], making two hundred and five: Jacob was then one hundred and twenty years old, for the twins, he and Esau, were born to their father when he was sixty[^18]; and ten years later Jacob entered Egypt, when he was one hundred and thirty years old[^19]. Joseph then was thirty-nine. So the years from the promise until Jacob's entry into Egypt are two hundred and fifteen. But Joseph, from that thirty-ninth year of his age in which his father found him in Egypt, lived seventy-one years; for all the years of his life were one hundred and ten[^20]. So when seventy-one is added to the two hundred and fifteen years, it makes two hundred and eighty-six years. There remain one hundred and forty-four or five, in which the people of Israel is understood to have served in Egypt after the death of Joseph. How much they could multiply in these years, if the fruitfulness of man is considered, with the help of him who willed them to be multiplied greatly, it is found to be no wonder that the people went out of Egypt in six hundred thousand footmen, besides the rest of the company, where there were both servants and the female sex and the age unfit for war.

**47.** 6. So when God said to Abraham: *know assuredly that your seed will be a sojourner in a land not its own; and they will reduce them to slavery and ill-treat them for four hundred years*[^21]*,* it is not to be taken as though the people of God were in that harshest slavery for four hundred years; but because it is written: *in Isaac shall your seed be called*[^22], from the year of Isaac's birth until the year of the exodus from Egypt four hundred and five years are reckoned. When, then, from the four hundred and thirty you subtract the twenty-five that are from the promise until Isaac was born, it is no wonder if Scripture chose to call the four hundred and five years a round four hundred—Scripture, which is accustomed so to name times that what slightly exceeds or falls short of the sum of a fuller number is not counted. So what it says, *they will reduce them to slavery and ill-treat them,* is not to be referred to the four hundred years, as though they held them in slavery for so many years; but the four hundred years are to be referred to what was said: *your seed will be a sojourner in a land not its own*—because whether in the land of Canaan or in Egypt, that seed was a sojourner, before they received as an inheritance the land of God's promise, which came to pass after they were freed from Egypt. So that a hyperbaton is to be understood here, and the order of the words is: *know assuredly that your seed will be a sojourner in a land not its own for four hundred years;* and the clause is to be understood as inserted: *and they will reduce them to slavery and ill-treat them;* in such a way that this insertion does not pertain to the four hundred years. For in the last part of the sum of these years, that is, after the death of Joseph, it came about that the people of God passed a hard slavery in Egypt.
