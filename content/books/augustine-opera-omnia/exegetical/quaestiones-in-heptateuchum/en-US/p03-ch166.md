# Moses wrote the words of the Law.

**166.** 1. (34, 27-28. 1) *And he wrote on the tablets the words of the Testament, the ten words.* It was said of Moses that he himself wrote, to whom God too had a little before said: *write for yourself these words.* But when he first received the Law, whose tablets he cast down and broke, he himself was not said to have cut the tablets of stone, and now it was said: *cut for yourself two tablets of stone;* nor was it said to him that he should write, as it is now said to him; nor is he narrated to have written them himself, as Scripture now narrates, and says: *he wrote on the tablets the words of the Testament, the ten words*: but then it was said: *and he gave to Moses, as soon as he ceased to speak to him on Mount Sinai, two tablets of the testimony, tablets of stone written by the finger of God*[^1];

then a little after: *and turning,* it says, *Moses descended from the mountain, and the two tablets of the testimony were in his hands, tablets of stone written on both their sides, on this side and that they were written, and the tablets were the work of God, and the writing was the writing of God engraved on the tablets*[^2].

Accordingly a great question arises, how those tablets, which Moses was going to break—God surely foreknowing it—are said to be not the work of a man, but of God; nor written by a man, but the writing of God, by the finger of God: but the later tablets, which were to remain so long, and to be in the tabernacle and the Temple of God, although at God's command, were yet cut by a man, written by a man.

Or was it perhaps that in those first tablets the grace of God was signified, not the work of a man—which grace they were made unworthy of by turning back in heart to Egypt, and by making the idol, whence they were deprived of that benefit, and therefore Moses broke the tablets—but by these later tablets were signified those who glory in their own works—whence the Apostle says: *being ignorant of the righteousness of God, and wishing to establish their own, they were not subject to the righteousness of God*[^3]—and therefore the tablets, cut by human work and written by human work, were given, which would remain with these very ones, to signify those who would glory in their own works, not in the finger of God, that is, in the Spirit of God?

**166.** 2. Certainly, then, the repetition of the Law signifies the New Testament; but the former signified the Old, whence it was broken and abolished: especially since, when the Law is given a second time, it is given with no terror, like that in so great a din of fires, clouds, and trumpets; whence the people, trembling, said: *let not God speak to us, lest we die*[^4]: whence it is signified that there is fear in the Old Testament, in the New love.

How, then, is this question resolved, why those were the work of God, these the work of a man; and those written by the finger of God, these written by a man? Was it perhaps that in those first tablets the Old Testament was the rather signified, because God there prescribed, but man did not do it? For the Law was set in the Old Testament, which would convict transgressors, which entered in that the offense might abound[^5]. For it was not fulfilled by fear, which is not fulfilled except by love. And therefore it is called the work of God, because God established the Law, God wrote it: no work of a man, because man did not obey God, and the Law rather made him guilty.

But in the second tablets a man, by the help of God, makes the tablets, and writes them; because the love of the New Testament fulfills the Law. Whence the Lord says: *I have not come to abolish the Law, but to fulfill it*[^6]: and the Apostle says: *the fullness of the Law is love*[^7]; and: *faith which works through love*[^8]. So it became easy for a man in the New Testament, what in the Old was difficult, when he has faith which works through love; and with that finger of God, that is, the Spirit of God, writing it within in the heart, not outside on stone. Whence the Apostle says: *not on tablets of stone, but on the fleshly tablets of the heart*[^9]; *since the love of God,* by which the precept is truly fulfilled, *is poured out in our hearts through the Holy Spirit, who has been given to us*[^10].

This, then, is the Law given first (where the Old Testament is signified, which is only the work of God, and the writing of the finger of God), which the Apostle says: *therefore the Law indeed is holy, and the commandment holy, and just, and good*[^11]. The Law, then, holy and good, is the work of God: where man does nothing, because he does not obey; but is rather pressed by guilt, the Law threatening and condemning. For *sin,* he says, *that it might appear sin, worked death in me through what is good*[^12]. But a man is blessed, when this commandment, holy and just and good, is also his work, but through the grace of God.
