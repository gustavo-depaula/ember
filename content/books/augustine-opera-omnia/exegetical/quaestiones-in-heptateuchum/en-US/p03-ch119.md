# How there the holiness of the Lord is commanded to be formed on a golden plate.

**120.** (28, 36-38) *And you shall make a plate of pure gold, and you shall form on it the shaping of a sign, the holiness of the Lord; and you shall place it upon a twice-twisted hyacinth; and it shall be upon the mitre; according to the front of the mitre it shall be; and it shall be over the forehead of Aaron. And Aaron shall take away the sins of the holy things, whatever the children of Israel shall sanctify, of every gift of their holy things.*

How the holiness of the Lord is to be formed on the plate, I do not see, except by certain letters, which some say are four Hebrew ones—for they believe that the ineffable name of God, which the Greeks call *tetragrammaton,* was, or even still is. But whatever those letters may be, or however they may stand, as I said, I would believe that the holiness of the Lord, or the sanctification, if this is rather to be said, which the Greek has, could be formed only by letters in the gold. But there he says that the priest takes away the sins of the holy things: *whatever,* he says, *the children of Israel shall sanctify, of every gift of their holy things*: which I think is said of those sacrifices which they offer for their sins; so that we understand not holy men, but holy things, from the fact that the things which are offered for sins are holy.

When, then, he had spoken of the plate, he added and said: *and Aaron shall take away the sins of the holy things, whatever the children of Israel shall sanctify, of every gift of their holy things*: that is, the priest shall take away whatever they offer for their sins, which are called both *holy things,* because they are sanctified, and *sins,* because they are offered for sins; as in many places Scripture plainly records this very thing. But what he adds and says: *and it shall be over the forehead of Aaron always, accepted for them in the sight of the Lord,* returns to that plate; in which is understood the ornament of the forehead, the confidence of a good life, which he who truly and perfectly—not by signification, but by truth—is a priest can alone take away sins, nor has he need to offer for his own.
