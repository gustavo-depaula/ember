# How a day is called eternal, since no day is eternal.

**43.** (12, 14) What is written, *and you shall keep this day throughout your generations as an everlasting ordinance* or *perpetual*—which in Greek is said *aionion*—is not to be taken as though any day of these passing things could be eternal; rather, that which this day signifies is eternal. So when we say that God himself is eternal, we surely do not mean that these two syllables are eternal, but what they signify. Yet one must inquire carefully how Scripture is accustomed to use the word "eternal," lest perhaps it called it "eternal" in the sense of a solemn observance that they hold it wrong to omit or to change of their own accord.

For it is one thing when something is commanded as to how long it is to be done, as it was commanded that the ark should go around the walls of Jericho seven times[^1]; it is another when something is commanded to be observed in such a way that no limit of observance is fixed—whether daily, or by months, or solemnly by years, or at set intervals of many or some years. So either he called it "eternal" because they would not dare to cease celebrating it of their own accord; or, as I said, so that not the signs of things themselves, but the things signified by them, might be understood to be eternal.

[^1]: See Josh 6:4.
