# Book IV: Questions on Numbers

How offices of leadership are named from numbers.

**1.** (1, 1-44) What does it mean that he commands individual leaders to be chosen from the individual tribes, and calls them χιλίαρχοι? Whom some Latin translators called "tribunes": but χιλίαρχοι seem to be named from "thousand." But when Jethro, Moses' father-in-law, gave his son-in-law the counsel, which God too approved, about ordering the people under leaders, so that not all the cases of all should burden Moses beyond his strength, he called those to be appointed over thousands of men χιλίαρχοι, those over hundreds ἑκατόνταρχοι, and those over fifties πεντακόνταρχοι, and those over tens δεκάδαρχοι; named from the very numbers over which they presided[^1].

Are the χιλίαρχοι here too to be taken so, that each was over a thousand men? Surely not: for the whole people of all Israel was not then twelve thousand men. For he chose one from each of the tribes, which twelve tribes beyond doubt contained not thousands of men each, but many thousands. The name, then, is common with those who in *Exodus* are called χιλίαρχοι, because each of them was leader of a thousand men: but each of these is a leader of thousands, since whether from "thousand" or from "thousands," the same composition of the name sounds, so that he is called χιλίαρχος.

## What sacred symbolism that number five has.

**2.** (1, 20-46) It is rightly asked what it means that, throughout all the tribes, where the children of Israel are reckoned tribe by tribe at military age, it is said: *according to their kinships, according to their peoples, according to the houses of their families, according to the number of their names, according to their head*; and these five things are repeated in just the same way, until all the tribes are completed: as though it were one thing, *according to their kinships,* another *according to their peoples,* another *according to the houses of their families,* another *according to the number of their names,* another *according to their head*; when rather all these things seem to signify the same thing in other words. But it moves our attention, that they are repeated so carefully throughout all the tribes, that anyone would judge it not to be done in vain, even if he does not understand it.

Surely, then, the number itself intimates something of sacred symbolism, that this same thing is repeated five times in various ways. For this number, as in the very five books of Moses, that is, the number five, is especially commended in the Old Testament. But those four things which are next joined, that is, *males, from twenty years and upward, everyone who goes forth in strength, the review of them*; although these too are repeated throughout all the tribes in exactly the same way, have a necessary difference. For since the number of the whole multitude belonging to one tribe was being treated, the sex had to be distinguished, and therefore it is set down: *all the males.* And lest little ones too should be counted, it is added, *from twenty years and upward.* Again, lest the unwarlike age of old men should be numbered in, it is added: *everyone who goes forth in strength.* And all is concluded with the word of the work that was being done, so that it says, *the review of them.* For a review was being made, so that these thousands of men might be counted. Those five things, then—kinships, peoples, houses of families, number of names, and head; and these four next—sex, age, strength, review—perhaps intimate something in the number itself. For if of these two numbers, that is, five and four, the one be multiplied by the other, that is, five times four, or four times five, they make twenty. By which number that age of young men too is marked. Which number is mentioned also when entry is made into the Land of Promise: and that age of twenty years is named, which has not turned aside to the right or the left. Where it seems to me that the holy faithful from both Testaments, holding the true faith, are signified. For the Old Testament especially excels in the five books of Moses, and the New in the four Gospels.

## The foreigner who is forbidden to approach the tabernacle while it is being raised or taken down.

**3.** (1, 51) As to what he says about the tabernacle being struck, taken down, and raised: *and the foreigner who approaches shall die*; here a foreigner is to be understood even from among those children of Israel who were not of that tribe which he commanded to serve the tabernacle, that is, who was not of the tribe of Levi. But it is strange how *foreigner* is said improperly, since it rather signifies a man of another race, that is ἀλλογενής, and not rather ἀλλόφυλος, which signifies a man of another tribe: by which name Scripture rather uses of men of other nations, so that they are called allophyles, as it were men of other tribes.

## Whether "watches" or "guards" is more rightly said.

**4.** (3, 5-7) *And the Lord spoke to Moses, saying: Take the tribe of Levi; and you shall set them in the sight of Aaron the priest, and they shall minister to him: and they shall keep his guards, and the guards of the children of Israel, before the tabernacle of testimony.*

What the Greek called φυλακαί, some of our translators rendered *guards,* others *watches.* But it is strange if those are not better called "watches" which are wont in camps to have a three-hour observation. Whence it is written: *but in the fourth watch of the night he came to them, walking upon the sea*[^2]: that is, after the ninth hour of the night, after three watches, that is. And in many places of the Scriptures ours have rendered "watches" what the Greeks call φυλακαί.

Where there is no doubt that the spans of nocturnal time are signified: which I think is to be taken here too. For how are the Levites commanded to keep the guards of Aaron, and the guards of the children of Israel, that is φυλακαί, unless perhaps it was said lest they should think themselves to be exempt from keeping the watches, which are properly wont to be kept in camps, on account of the honor by which they served the tabernacle, when they too, on account of the works of the tabernacle, ought no less to keep watches in turn, which were kept in the other camps round about of the children of Israel?

## How from the touching of the tabernacle the Lord threatens death.

**5.** (3, 10. 38) *The foreigner who touches it shall die.* It is to be asked how he said in *Leviticus*[^3]: *whoever touches the tabernacle shall be sanctified*: when here he says: *the foreigner who touches it shall die*; meaning those who were not of the tribe of Levi. Or did he perhaps here, by *touches,* mean the duty of service, which he commanded to be owed to the tabernacle by the Levites alone? For of this he was speaking.

## How firstborn are said to be God's.

**6.** (3, 12-14. 41-47) What does it mean that God reckons the Levites to himself in place of the firstborn of the children of Israel, so that, the firstborn of the people being counted, who were found more in number than the number of the Levites, they were redeemed with silver, five shekels being given for one? Which was not done for the cattle, although he willed that the cattle of the Levites too should be his in place of the firstborn of the cattle of Israel. How did their firstborn or the firstborn of their cattle afterward belong to God, where he commanded that unclean firstborn, whether of beasts or of men, be exchanged for sheep? How were the sons of the Levites not also reckoned for those subsequent firstborn (for the same tribe remained in their descendants, who could be reckoned for the later firstborn); unless it was just that those who were born of those who already belonged to the Lord's portion—given for the firstborn who came out of Egypt—God should now hold as his own, as it were his own from his own, and that those who were thereafter owed to God as firstborn could not justly be reckoned for them? For out of the whole people, and out of all the cattle of the people, a portion was given to God for the firstborn: and this portion was the Levites and their cattle. Now if they had begotten anything, it was God's; this could not be given as it were by the people, which was already not their own: and through this the firstborn afterward owed had to be rendered to God; nor were the descendants of the Levites or of their cattle to be reckoned for these.

## How loaves are said to be always upon the table.

**7.** (4, 7) When he gave command about taking up the table, he ordered the loaves to be taken up with it, saying thus: *and the loaves which shall always be upon it.* Surely these will not always be upon it, but ones like them: because those were taken away, and fresh ones placed daily; provided only that the table was not left without loaves. Therefore he said: *which shall always be upon it*: that is, because there are always loaves, not because those are always there.

## How the following words are to be understood.

**8.** (4, 11) *And over the golden altar they shall cover a blue cloth, and shall cover it with a blue covering of skin.* This expression might seem—which, as it were absurd and not complete, the Latin translators were unwilling to render—that is, *over the golden altar they shall cover a blue cloth*: as though it ought to have been said: "and the golden altar they shall cover with a blue cloth." For *they shall cover a blue cloth* seems to signify this, that the cloth itself should be covered by something else, not that the altar should be covered by the cloth itself. But it seems to me to be not so much a manner of speech as a somewhat obscure sense. For this can be understood: *and over the golden altar they shall cover a blue cloth,* so that he prescribed the blue cloth itself, which would already be over the altar, to be covered with another thing; and so he briefly embraced both, that the altar was to be covered with the blue cloth, and the blue cloth to be covered with another covering. He added, finally, with what he wished the blue cloth to be covered, when he subjoined: *and they shall cover it with a blue covering of skin.*

## What sins are commanded to be redeemed with money.

**9.** (5, 6-8) *A man or a woman, whoever shall do any of the human sins, and despising shall despise, and shall offend, that soul shall declare the sin which it sinned, and shall restore the offense: the principal and its fifth he shall add upon it, and shall render it to him against whom he has offended. But if the man has no neighbor, so as to render to him the offense in respect of himself; the offense that is rendered to the Lord shall be the priest's, besides the ram of propitiation by which he shall make atonement in it for him.*

Here those sins are to be understood whose commission is in those things which can be restored with money. For he would not otherwise say how they are to be restored, unless they were of a monetary loss. For he commands the principal and the fifth to be restored, that is, the whole of whatever it is, and a fifth part of it, besides the ram which was to be offered in sacrifice to expiate the offense.

But he commands that what is restored be the priest's, that is, the principal itself and the fifth, if there is no neighbor to him against whom the offense was committed. Where it is surely understood that what is the priest's is then to be rendered to the Lord, if the man himself who suffered the loss does not survive, nor his neighbor, by whom I think he meant the heir to be understood. But about the man himself Scripture said nothing: yet when it says, *if he has no neighbor,* under this brevity it intimates that his neighbor is then to be sought, if he himself does not survive. But if there is no neighbor either, it shall be restored to the Lord, lest what was committed remain unpunished: which, however, shall not pass into a sacrifice, but be the priest's.

The words of Scripture are surely to be construed thus: *but if the man has no neighbor, so as to render to him the offense in respect of himself*: for what he added, *in respect of himself,* is a matter of the expression; or perhaps he said *in respect of himself* in the sense that it pertains to him, that is, that he himself possesses it. Then it follows: *the offense that is rendered to the Lord shall be the priest's*: but he says "the offense," when the thing that was taken away through the offense is rendered.

## How these things differ, and restitution is commanded in Exodus.

**10.** (5, 6-7) It may be asked how in *Exodus* it is said that, *if anyone has stolen a calf or a sheep,* he ought to restore five calves or four sheep, *if he has killed or sold it*; but if what he took away is found safe with him, *double* is to be restored[^4]: when here he commands the principal and the fifth to be restored, which is far even from double; how much more from fourfold or fivefold?

Unless perhaps, because here he says, *a man or a woman, whoever shall do any of the human sins,* he meant sins of ignorance to be understood as *human sins.* For it can happen that, by paying too little attention, through negligence a man transfers another's property into his own: which is therefore a sin, because, if it were diligently attended to, it would not be committed. And these he meant to be restored with the principal and the fifth, not punished as thefts.

For if we understand here thefts and frauds, which are committed not through the ignorance of negligence but with the intent of stealing and defrauding, and are therefore called *human* because they are done against men; this, unless I am mistaken, will be the outcome of this question, that the one who did it does not render even double, because he is not caught or convicted, but, while those from whom it was done, or whether it was done, are ignorant, he himself declares his offense.

For when Scripture had said: *a man or a woman, whoever shall do any of the human sins, and despising shall despise, and shall offend,* that is, shall commit these things by way of contempt; it added and said: *that soul shall declare the sin which it sinned, and shall restore the offense, the principal and its fifth*: perhaps, then, for this reason only, because he himself declared it; and therefore he was not to be punished with that penalty by which a thief caught or convicted ought to be punished.

## How the words which Scripture says are to be spoken to the woman by the priest are to be taken.

**11.** (5, 21) The words which Scripture says are to be spoken to the woman by the priest, when her husband brings her under suspicion of adultery: *may the Lord make you a curse and an execration,* the Greek has ἐνόρκιον. By which word seems to be signified "an oath by execration"; as if someone should say: "so may such and such not befall me": or surely should swear thus: "may such and such befall me, if I have done it, or have not done it." So this is said: *may the Lord make you a curse and an execration,* as though it were said, "so that men may swear about you what they will swear by execration; 'so may it not befall them,' or, 'may this befall them, unless they have done that.'"

## What it is for a lamb to be offered for sin.

**12.** (6, 14-17) *And he shall offer his gift to the Lord, a year-old lamb without defect for a holocaust, and one year-old ewe-lamb without defect for sin.* This some of our translators were unwilling to render, as if avoiding an unusual expression, and said: *for sin,* not: *unto sin,* although there is a sense in that expression which ought not to have been disturbed. For *unto sin* is said because this very thing which was offered for sin was called *sin.* Whence there is that saying in the Apostle about the Lord Christ: *him who knew no sin he made sin for us*[^5]: God the Father, namely, made God the Son *sin for us,* that is, a sacrifice for sin. As, then, the lamb *for a holocaust,* so that the animal itself was a holocaust; so too the ewe-lamb *unto sin,* so that the animal itself was sin, that is, became a sacrifice for sin—as, of the ram, what follows says, *unto a peace offering,* as though it were itself a peace offering, when it is a sacrifice of the peace offering. Which he afterward makes clear by repeating: for he says *for sin* what he had first said *unto sin*; and this, *a sacrifice of the peace offering,* what he had first said *unto a peace offering.*

## What that means: This is concerning the Levites.

**13.** (8, 23-24) *The Lord spoke to Moses, saying: This is concerning the Levites.* This some rendered: *this is the law concerning the Levites*: but what is written, *this is concerning the Levites,* is understood as said: "this I establish concerning the Levites."

## How the hyperbaton of the words is to be ordered.

**14.** (8, 24-26) Then it follows: *From the twenty-fifth year and upward, they shall go in to minister in the ministry in the works in the tabernacle of testimony; and at fifty he shall withdraw from the administration, and shall work no more (and his brother shall minister): in the tabernacle of testimony to keep the guards; but the works he shall not work.* This sense is made obscure by the ὁπέρβατον, which is so confused that it is as though it were said of the brother himself, *to keep the guards*; whereas it was said of him who shall withdraw from the works, and to whom there shall remain, *in the tabernacle of testimony to keep the guards; but the works he shall not work*; but his brother shall work them, that is, one who has not yet reached the age of fifty, beginning to work from twenty-five years.

It is therefore to be construed thus: *and at fifty he shall withdraw from the administration, and shall work no more, and his brother shall minister.* Then he returns to that fifty-year-old of whom he was speaking, and about him sets out the rest, saying: *in the tabernacle of testimony to keep the guards; but the works he shall not work.* For as for his saying *to keep,* "he shall begin" is to be supplied, as though he said this in one word, "he shall keep the guards." For it is customary, even in Latin expressions, for the infinitive verb to be put for the finite.

## How the unclean kept the Passover.

**15.** (9, 6-12) When, at the time of the Passover, certain men, *who had been made unclean over the soul of a man,* that is, over a dead person, asked how they should keep the Passover; since they had to be purified from their uncleannesses for seven days according to the Law: Moses consulted the Lord, and received the answer that *whoever had had any such thing befall him, or was set on so distant a journey that he could not arrive in time, ought to keep the Passover in another month, on account of the fourteenth day of the month,* on which the count of the moon was observed. But if it be asked what they should do, if perhaps such uncleanness should occur even up to the second month; I think that what was said about the second month was to be held as it were as a rule, so that they should observe it in the third month, or at least that not keeping the Passover under such necessity would not amount to a fault.

## How the rest, which are indeed difficult, are to be explained.

**16.** 1. (9, 15-23) *And on the day on which the tabernacle was set up, the cloud covered the tabernacle, the house of testimony: and in the evening there was over the tabernacle as it were the appearance of fire until morning. So it was always; the cloud covered it by day, and the appearance of fire by night. And when the cloud went up from the tabernacle, then afterward the children of Israel moved on: and in the place wherever the cloud stood, there the children of Israel pitched camp. By the command of the Lord the children of Israel shall pitch camp, and by the command of the Lord they shall move on. All the days in which the cloud overshadows the tabernacle, the children of Israel shall be in camp: and when the cloud prolongs itself over the tabernacle many days; then the children of Israel shall keep the watch of God, and shall not move on.*

*And it shall be, when the cloud covers a number of days over the tabernacle; by the voice of the Lord they shall be in camp, and by the command of the Lord they shall move on. And it shall be, when the cloud is from evening until morning, and the cloud goes up in the morning, then they shall move on by day; or by night, and if the cloud goes up, they shall move on: by day or by month, the cloud abounding and overshadowing over it, the children of Israel shall be in camp, and shall not move on. Since by the command of the Lord they shall move on. They kept the watch of the Lord by the command of the Lord in the hand of Moses.*

**16.** 2. This whole passage is to be carefully explained, since it is made obscure by unusual kinds of expression. *And on the day,* he says, *on which the tabernacle was set up, the cloud covered the tabernacle, the house of testimony*: he called the same tabernacle the house of testimony. *And in the evening there was over the tabernacle as it were the appearance of fire until morning. So it was always.*

Then he carefully expresses what it was that happened always: *the cloud,* he says, *covered it by day, and the appearance of fire by night. And when the cloud went up from the tabernacle, then afterward the children of Israel moved on.* This sentence is not obscure, except on account of that expression where *and* is added. For the order of the words follows soundly, even if that conjunction were lacking, so that it is said thus: *and when the cloud went up from the tabernacle, afterward the children of Israel moved on*: although even what is said, *afterward,* if it were lacking, the sentence could be complete. Then it follows, saying: *and in the place wherever the cloud stood, there the children of Israel pitched camp.*

**16.** 3. But all this that they did, referring it to the command of the Lord, he embraces thus: *By the command,* he says, *of the Lord the children of Israel shall pitch camp, and by the command of the Lord they shall move on.* He calls the command of the Lord the very sign that was made in the cloud, whether when it stood overshadowing the tabernacle, so that the camp too should halt; or when it had gone up, and moved on farther, so that they should follow it, the camp being raised. He indeed changed in this sentence the manner of one narrating, and began, as it were foretelling and announcing beforehand, to have words of future time. For he does not say: *by the command of the Lord the children of Israel* "pitched camp"; but, *shall pitch camp*: nor does he say: *by the command of the Lord* "they moved on"; but: *they shall move on.* And this manner he keeps in what follows too, which manner is most unusual in the Scriptures. For we know that future things have often been foretold by words of past time; as is: *they dug my hands and feet*[^6]; and: *he was led as a sheep to the slaughter*[^7]; and innumerable such things: but that a narrator of deeds done should use words of future time, as in this passage, can most rarely be found in the Scriptures.

**16.** 4. So, after he had spoken about the day and night, by which sign the people moved on or remained; lest they should be thought wont to walk by night and to halt by day, and to do this on all days, he went on and added, saying: *all the days in which the cloud overshadows the tabernacle, the children of Israel shall be in camp; and when the cloud prolongs itself over the tabernacle many days.*

Then, warning that this was done not by their necessity but by God's will: *and the children of Israel shall keep,* he says, *the watch of God,* that is, the watch which God commanded; *and they shall not move on.* And as though it were said: "When, then, shall they move on?" *And it shall be,* he says, *when the cloud covers a number of days over the tabernacle,* that is, days of a fixed number, which number surely pleases God: *by the voice of the Lord they shall be in camp, and by the command of the Lord they shall move on.*

By this voice of the Lord he seems to mean the sign which he gives by the standing and moving of the cloud; for the voice of one speaking too is beyond doubt a sign of will. As for his saying, *and by the command,* I think the same sign is to be understood. Although the voice *and command of the Lord* could also be taken as that which he spoke, as he is wont, "to Moses, and commanded this to be done." For they would not know that they were to move on when the cloud moved, and to stand when the cloud stood, unless this had been commanded them beforehand.

But in the things that have been said it did not yet appear whether they walked only by day, or also by night, according to the sign which the cloud gave by its movement. For perhaps, although they remained many days in camp while the cloud did not move on, it could nevertheless be thought that the cloud was not wont to go up from the camp and give the sign for the journey except by day.

It follows, then, and says: *and it shall be, when the cloud is from evening until morning, and the cloud goes up in the morning, then they shall move on by day.* Here that copulative conjunction is set down by the manner of Scripture. For with it removed the sense is complete in this way: *and it shall be, when the cloud is from evening until morning, and the cloud goes up in the morning, they shall move on by day.*

Then, because they moved on by night too if the cloud went up, and made a night journey if they received that sign, he added and said: *or by night, and if the cloud goes up, they shall move on.* But the expression is rather unusual: for not only is *and* set down, but it is set down in a manner in which it is not customary. Whence it seems to me that the order of the words is inverted, as often happens in Latin expressions too: which kind is called antistrophe. Accordingly, if it be said thus: *or also by night, if the cloud goes up, they shall move on*; or at least thus: *if by night too the cloud goes up, they shall move on,* the sense is most plain.

But further it occurred to one reflecting, so that one wished to know whether, just as it has been learned that they were wont to walk day and night at the cloud's sign, or to be in camp day and night, so also they were wont to remain through some days only, even those by night of which they had walked: which I think Scripture intimated in what follows when it says: *by day or by month of the day, the cloud abounding and overshadowing over it, the children of Israel shall be in camp, and shall not move on.*

For because he had said: *or by night, and if the cloud goes up, they shall move on*; it as it were remained for him to say, "but by day, if it does not go up, they shall not move on," when they seemed as it were to be obliged to move on: but because this too could happen for many days, that they walked by night, the cloud moving on, and did not walk by day while it remained, he therefore set down, *by day or by month of the day.*

He did not say "by month," lest there too the nights of the same month should be taken; but, *by month of the day,* that is, a month on that part on which there was day, not on that on which there was night. *By day, then, or by month of the day, the cloud abounding and overshadowing,* that is, abounding in overshadowing, or overshadowing more abundantly, *over it,* namely that tabernacle, *the children of Israel shall be in camp, and shall not move on.*

Finally he repeated that it was done by divine authority, which surely ought not to be resisted, adding: *since by the command of the Lord they shall move on. They kept the watch of the Lord, by the command of the Lord, in the hand of Moses.* He returned to the word of past time, so as to say, *they kept.* But what he set down at the end, *in the hand of Moses,* is a most usual expression in the Scriptures, because God commanded these things through Moses.

## Why he commands the trumpet to be sounded.

**17.** (10, 7) *But when you gather the congregation, you shall sound the trumpet, and not as a signal.* He does not, then, command this sounding so that the congregation may be gathered; for if this is done, it is a signal: but, the congregation being already gathered, he commands the trumpet to be sounded, as though it now pertained to a musical sounding, not to giving a signal, by which, once given, he gives notice that something is to be done. Accordingly, when this—that, the congregation being already gathered, they sounded the trumpets—some man of the New Testament interprets of something spiritual, it is a sign to him who understands why it is done; not to those who did not understand it, except when it was done for this purpose, that some work might be appointed.

## Concerning the words: And I will take of your spirit...

**18.** (11, 17) *And I will take of the spirit which is in you, and will put it upon them; and they shall bear with you the press of the people, and you shall not carry them alone.* Most Latin translators did not render it as it is in the Greek; but said: *I will take of your spirit which is in you, and will put it upon them,* or *will put it in them*; and they made the sense laborious to understand. For it can be thought said of the spirit of the man himself, by which human nature is completed with the body joined to it, which consists of body and spirit, which they also call soul: of which the Apostle too says: *for what man knows the things of a man, except the spirit of the man which is in him? So too the things of God no one knows except the Spirit of God.* And what he adds and says: *but we have not received the spirit of this world, but the Spirit who is from God*[^8]; he surely shows that the Spirit of God is another, of whom the spirit of man is made a partaker by the grace of God.

Although the Spirit of God could also be understood, as others rendered it, in what is said: *of your spirit which is in you*; so that it is said *your* because what is God's becomes ours too, when we have received him: as it was said of John: *in the spirit and power of Elijah*[^9]. For the soul of Elijah had not been transferred back into him. But if certain people suppose this by a heretical perversity, what will they say in what is written: *the spirit of Elijah rested upon Elisha*[^10]; when that one surely already had his own soul: unless because it was said of the Spirit of God, that through him too he might work such things as he worked through Elijah; not departing from that one so as to be able to fill this one; nor, being divided, being less in that one so as to be able to be in part in this one too? For he is God, who can be so great in all in whom he has willed to be by that grace.

But now, since it is written thus: *and I will take of the spirit which is upon you,* and it is not said: "of your spirit," the solution of the question is easier: because we understand that God meant nothing else than that from the same Spirit of grace they too would have help, from which Moses had it; so that these too should have as much as God willed, not that Moses should therefore have less.

## Concerning the words: Six hundred thousand footmen...

**19.** (11, 21-23) *And Moses said: Six hundred thousand footmen, among whom I am, and you have said: I will give them flesh, and they shall eat for a month of days. Shall sheep and oxen be slaughtered for them, and will they suffice them? Or shall all the fish be gathered for them, and will it suffice them?*

It is usually asked whether Moses said this out of distrust, or out of inquiry. But if we think he said it out of distrust, the question arises why the Lord did not reproach him for this, as he reproached him for what occurred at the rock from which the water flowed, where he seems to have doubted of the Lord's power[^11]. But if we say that he said this in inquiring the manner in which it would be done, the Lord's own answer, where he said to him: *shall the hand of the Lord not suffice,* seems as it were to rebuke him for not having believed these things. But I think it better understood that the Lord answered thus, as though he were unwilling to tell the manner of the future deed, which Moses inquired about, but rather to demonstrate his power by the deed itself.

For it could be objected by calumniators against Mary too, when she said: *how shall this be, since I know not a man?*[^12], that she believed too little, although she inquired the manner and did not doubt of the power of God. But as for what was answered her: *the Holy Spirit shall come upon you, and the power of the Most High shall overshadow you*[^13], it could have been answered thus too, as here: "is it impossible to the Holy Spirit, who shall come upon you?" and so the same sense would be preserved.

But Zechariah, saying some such thing, is charged with unbelief, and punished with the penalty of a stifled voice[^14]. Why, unless because God judges not from words, but from hearts?

Otherwise the words of Moses at that rock from which the water flowed too could be excused, were not the divine sentence clear against him, that he said such things out of distrust. For those words run thus: *hear me, you unbelievers; shall we bring you water out of this rock?* Then it follows: *and Moses, lifting up his hand, struck the rock twice with the rod, and much water came out, and the congregation and their cattle drank*[^15]. Surely for this he gathered the people, for this he took that rod with which he had done such great miracles, and with it struck the rock, and from it the effect of the accustomed power followed.

Those words, then, in which he says: *shall we bring you water out of this rock?* could be taken thus, as though it were said: "surely water cannot be brought out of this rock according to your unbelief": so that finally, by striking, it might be shown that what they in their unbelief did not believe could be done divinely, especially because he had said: *hear me, you unbelievers.* These words could indeed be so understood, were it not that God, who is the searcher of the heart[^16], indicated in what spirit they were said.

For Scripture follows and says: *and the Lord said to Moses and Aaron: Because you did not believe to sanctify me in the sight of the children of Israel, for this you shall not bring this congregation into the land which I have given them*[^17]. And through this it is understood that Moses said those words as though he had struck without certainty, so that, if the effect did not follow, he might be thought to have foretold this, when he said: *shall we bring you water out of this rock?* which would lie hidden altogether in his mind, were not God's sentence to betray it. On the contrary, then, in this passage we ought to understand the words of Moses about the promised flesh to be those of one inquiring rather how it would be done, than of one distrusting; since the Lord's sentence did not follow which would punish, but rather one which would teach.

## Whether that wife of Moses is the daughter of Jethro.

**20.** (12, 1) Concerning the Ethiopian wife of Moses it is usually asked whether she is the daughter of Jethro[^18], or whether he married another, or married an additional one: but it is credible that she was the same: for she was of the Midianites, who are found in Chronicles called Ethiopians[^19], when Jehoshaphat fought against them. For in those places the people of Israel is said to have pursued them, where the Midianites dwell, who are now called Saracens. But now hardly anyone calls them Ethiopians, as the names of places and nations are for the most part wont to be changed by age.

## Concerning the words: He shall see the people, whether it is strong.

**21.** (13, 18-26) *And he said to them: Go up this way through the wilderness, and you shall go up into the mountain, and shall see the land, what it is, and the people who dwell upon it, whether it is strong or weak, whether they are few or many.* He is understood to have explained in what sense he said, *whether it is mighty or weak*; that is: *whether they are few or many.* For how could they, looking out from a mountain, perceive the strength of human forces?

There can be another sense too, much more agreeable to the truth. As for his saying, *you shall go up into the mountain,* he meant "into the land itself," which they wished to explore. For spies could not easily be understood, where, as though traveling, they searched out everything. For if we suppose them to have looked out over the land and explored it from the mountain's summit; how could they search out all that Moses commanded to be searched out? How enter the cities, which Scripture said they entered? How take from that valley the cluster, on account of which a name was given to the place too, so that it was called the Valley of the Cluster? On the mountain itself, then, the land was explored, because that was the very thing to be explored: and there was a certain lower place, from which valley the cluster was taken.

## What the dread of the land is.

**22.** (13, 33) *And they brought forth the dread of the land which they had explored. He said the dread of the land,* not that the land itself was in dread, but the dread which they had conceived from that land.

## How the words of Caleb and Joshua are to be understood.

**23.** (14, 9) Caleb and Joshua son of Nun, speaking to the people of Israel that they should not fear to enter the land of promise, said among other things: *but do not fear the people of the land, for they are food for us. For their time has departed from them, but the Lord is among us; do not fear them.* By what was said, *they are food for us,* they meant to be understood, "we will consume them." But as for what they added: *for their time has departed from them, but the Lord is among us*: carefully enough they did not say: the Lord has departed from them; for impious they were of old: but since to the impious too, by the hidden dispensation of divine providence, a time is given of flourishing and reigning: *their time,* they say, *has departed from them, but the Lord is among us.* They did not say: their time has departed from them, and ours has succeeded: but: *but the Lord is among us,* not a time. For those had a time, these the Lord God, the creator and orderer of times, and the distributor of them to each, as it pleases him.

## What the sins of the unwilling are.

**24.** (15, 24-29) As to what is prescribed about how sins committed not willingly are to be expiated, it is rightly asked what these sins of the unwilling are: whether those which are committed by those who do not know; or whether it can also rightly be said that a sin is of one unwilling, which he is compelled to do: for this too is wont to be said to be done against the will. But surely he wills it on account of something for which he does it, as if he should be unwilling to perjure himself, and does it because he wills to live, if someone threatens him with death unless he does it. He wills, then, to do it, because he wills to live: and therefore not by seeking through himself to swear falsely, but to live by swearing falsely.

But if it is so, I do not know whether these can be called sins of the unwilling, such as are here said to be expiated. For if it be diligently considered, perhaps no one wills the sinning itself, but it is done on account of something else, which the one who sins wills. For all men who knowingly do what is not permitted would wish it were permitted: so far is it that anyone seeks the sinning itself for its own sake, but rather for the sake of that which follows from it. If these things are so, they are not sins of the unwilling, but of those who do not know; which are distinguished from the sins of the willing.

## What sins are with a high hand of pride.

**25.** (15, 30-31) *And whatever soul shall sin with a high hand of pride, whether of the natives or of the proselytes, here provokes God, and that soul shall be cut off from its people; because it despised the word of the Lord, and made void his commandments: with crushing that soul shall be crushed, its sin upon it.* What the sins are that are done *with a high hand of pride,* that is, committed in pride, Scripture itself sufficiently set out in what follows, where it says: *because it despised the word of the Lord.*

It is one thing, then, to despise the precepts, another indeed to esteem them highly, but either to act against them in ignorance, or being overcome. Which two perhaps pertain to those sins which are done by the unwilling, about which he gave notice above how they were to be expiated, God being propitiated; and then he added the sins of pride, when anyone does wrong by being proud, that is, by despising a precept. Which kind of sin he did not say ought to be purged by any kind of sacrifice, judging it as it were incurable, at least by that cure which was carried out through sacrifices, such as are commanded to be done in this Scripture: which, if they be considered in themselves, can heal no sin; but if the things themselves of which these are the sacred symbols be inquired into, in them the purgation of sins can be found.

What is written, then: *the sinner, when he comes into the depth of evils, despises*[^20], signifies the one whom Scripture in this passage says to offend *with a high hand of pride.* This, therefore, cannot be abolished without the punishment of the one who commits it; and so it cannot be unpunished, even when it is healed by repenting: for the very affliction of the penitent is the punishment of the sin, though medicinal and healthful.

For rightly is the sin judged great, when pride despises a precept: but on the contrary, that it may be healed, *a contrite and humbled heart God does not spurn*[^21]. Yet because it is not done without punishment, therefore such things are said here: *here,* it says, *he provokes God*; because God resists the proud[^22]. *And that soul shall be cut off from its people;* since such a one is altogether not in the number of those who belong to God. *Because it despised the word of the Lord, and made void his commandments: with crushing that soul shall be crushed.*

But why *with crushing it shall be crushed,* he consequently adds, saying: *its sin upon it*: and through this, if to such a sin one applies the due crushing to himself by repenting, *a contrite heart,* as has been said, *God will not spurn.* Although in the Greek it is not said in this passage, *with crushing it shall be crushed*; but, *with rubbing-out that soul shall be rubbed out*: which can be taken as though it were utterly extinguished by being rubbed away, and ceased to be. But first the nature of the soul's immortality refuses this interpretation.

Then, if what is rubbed away were utterly made not to be, he would not say of the wise man: *and let your foot rub out the steps of his doors*[^23]. But that distinction is more and more to be considered, whether no one sins except either one who does not know, or one who is overcome, or one who despises; about which it would now be long to dispute.

## Concerning Dathan and Abiram.

**26.** (16, 12-14) What does it mean that Dathan and Abiram, when they had risen up in sedition, being summoned by Moses, and answering proudly and insultingly: *is this a small thing, that you have brought us up into a land flowing with milk and honey, to kill us in the wilderness? Because you set yourself over us, you are a ruler: and you have brought us into a land flowing with milk and honey, and have given us a portion of field and vineyards*; after these things added: *would you have cut out the eyes of those men? We are not going up.* Whose eyes did they call the eyes of those men? Whether of the people of Israel, as though saying: "if you had granted these things, you would have cut out the eyes of those men"; that is, "they would so love you that they would tear out their own eyes and give them to you"? Which great token of love the Apostle too mentions: *that, if it could be done, you would have torn out your eyes and given them to me*[^24].

And then they added full obstinacy: *we are not going up,* that is: "we will not come"; because he had summoned them. Or does he rather call *the eyes of those men* the eyes of the enemies, who had been reported as exceedingly fierce and terrible: as though they said: "even if you had done this, we would not obey you"; except that one form of the verb is put for another, so that they did not say: "we would not go up"; but: *we are not going up,* by a certain manner of speech?

## How Moses and Aaron are commanded to withdraw.

**27.** (16, 20-21) *And the Lord spoke to Moses and Aaron, saying: Separate yourselves from the midst of this congregation.* It is to be noted that the Lord commands a bodily separation to be made then, when vengeance is already imminent upon the wicked: so Noah with his household is separated from the rest who were to perish by the flood[^25]; so Lot with his own is separated from the Sodomites who were to be consumed by fire from heaven[^26]; so the people itself from the Egyptians who were to be overwhelmed by the sea waves[^27]; so these now from the congregation of Korah, Abiram, and Dathan, who first wished to break away by sedition:

with whom, nevertheless, the holy ones, formerly living and conversing—and with the rest whom God reproves, according to the words which he says in rebuking them—could nonetheless in no way be contaminated by them: nor were they commanded to separate themselves, when the Lord either deferred vengeance, or applied such a kind as could not endanger or harm the innocent, as by the bites of serpents, as by the slaughter of deaths, by which God struck whom he willed, as he willed, while another remained untouched; not as by the water of the flood, or the fiery rain, or the water of the sea, or the gaping of the earth, which could consume the mingled alike: not because there too God could not preserve his own; but what need was there of the trial of a miracle where a separation could be made, so that water, or fire, or the gaping of the earth might carry off those whom it found? So too at the end the wheat shall be separated from the tares, so that, the wicked being burned by the flames, the just *may shine as the sun in the kingdom of their Father*[^28].

## Whether the Lord is in the vision, or in the gaping of the earth.

**28.** (16, 30) As to what Moses says of Korah and Abiram and Dathan: *in a vision God will show it, and the earth, opening its mouth, will swallow them up,* some rendered: *in a gaping the Lord will show it*[^29]: I believe, thinking that what was set down in Greek as φάσματι was χάσματι, which is said for what would be said if one said, "in a manifestation," what will openly appear to the eyes. For it is not said *in a vision* in the way visions are wont to be spoken of, whether of dreams or of any figures in ecstasy; but, as I said, in a manifestation. But some, supposing it to be something else, wished to render it *in a phantasm*; which so abhors from the custom of our speech that the word *phantasm* is almost nowhere used, except where our sense is deceived by the falsity of things seen: although this too is said from seeing; but, as I said, the custom of speech has decided otherwise.

## Who are called the underworld.

**29.** (16, 33) *And they themselves, and all things whatever are theirs, went down living into the underworld.* It is to be noted that the underworld is here so called according to an earthly place, that is, in the lower parts of the earth. For variously in the Scriptures, and with a manifold meaning, as the sense of the matters treated requires, the name *underworld* is set down, and is most often taken of the dead.

But since these men were said to have gone down living into the underworld, and from the narrative itself what was done appears well enough; it is clear, as I said, that the lower parts of the earth are called by the name *underworld,* in comparison with this upper earth on whose surface there is life: as, in comparison with the higher heaven, where is the dwelling of the holy angels, Scripture says that the sinning angels, thrust down into the gloom of this air, are reserved to be punished as it were in the prisons of the underworld. *For if God,* it says, *did not spare the sinning angels, but, thrusting them down into the prisons of the gloom of the underworld, delivered them to be reserved for punishment at the judgment*[^30]: since the Apostle Paul calls the devil *the ruler of the power of the air, who works in the children of unbelief*[^31].

## Why the Lord spoke to Moses and Eleazar, and not to Moses and Aaron, as he is wont.

**30.** (16, 36-40) *And the Lord said to Moses and to Eleazar son of Aaron the priest: Take up the bronze censers from the midst of those who were burned, and scatter this strange fire there; because they sanctified the censers of these sinners in their souls: and make them into ductile plates as a surrounding for the altar; since they were offered before the Lord and were sanctified, and have become a sign to the children of Israel.*

In this passage, why the Lord spoke not to Moses and Aaron, as in the foregoing, but to Moses and to Eleazar son of Aaron, this reason for the present occurs to me: that the question was about the lineage of the priests, that is, of what family they ought to be (whence those of another family, because they dared to usurp the priesthood for themselves, perished by so dreadful and astonishing a punishment); and so God willed to speak not to Aaron, who was already high priest, but to Eleazar, who was to succeed him and was already discharging the second priesthood, so that in this way he might commend the order of lineage which ought to be in the successions of priests.

Whence also in what follows it says: *and Eleazar son of Aaron the priest took the bronze censers, as many as those who were burned had offered, and added them as a surrounding for the altar, a memorial to the children of Israel, that no foreigner who is not of the seed of Aaron should approach to place incense before the Lord: and it shall not be as with Korah and as with his conspiracy, as the Lord spoke in the hand of Moses.* In this way, then, God willed through Eleazar to commend not the priesthood, which was already in Aaron, but the lineage of priestly succession.

But as for his saying, *and scatter this strange fire there,* "sprinkle" is to be understood. And as for what he added: *because they sanctified the censers of these sinners in their souls,* the thought is indeed expressed by an unusual expression: but it is to be noted that in a new way the punishment of those by whom this sin had been committed is called *sanctified*; because through them an example was given to the rest, which they would fear.

But why he willed a surrounding for the altar to be made from them, he added, saying: *since they were offered before the Lord, and were sanctified, and have become a sign to the children of Israel*: he did not, then, will to be reproved in them that they were offered by such men; but rather that this be considered and attended to, before whom they were offered, that is, that it was before the Lord, so that the name of the Lord before whom they were offered should prevail more in them than the most wicked desert of those by whom they were offered.

But this Scripture had already mentioned in *Exodus* too, when it says the altar was fashioned[^32]: whence it is understood that the kinds of deeds have been distributed throughout the books, not the order of times woven together.

For about Aaron's rod too, how the deed was done, that, both flowering and budding, it might divinely indicate the choice of his priesthood, Scripture related in this book[^33]; and yet about that very rod it is said in *Exodus* that it should be placed in the Holy of Holies with the manna in the ark, when command is given about fashioning the tabernacle[^34]; which was surely commanded long before the tabernacle itself stood fashioned and complete: but it stood in the first month of the second year from their going out of Egypt[^35], and this book begins from the second month of the same second year, on the first day of the month[^36]: whence it is clear that these things, if we consider the order of the books, are mentioned by recapitulation, that is, by the recalling of things past, which those who attend less diligently think were done in the same order in which they are narrated.

## What are called the sins of the holy ones.

**31.** (18, 1) *And the Lord said to Aaron, saying: You and your sons, and your father's house with you, shall bear the sins of the holy ones; and you and your sons shall bear the sins of your priesthood.* These are the sins which are called "sacrifices for sins." Accordingly *the sins of the holy ones* is said, not those which holy persons commit; but from the fact that they are "holy things," it is said "of the holy ones," because they are offered in holy places: and "sacrifices for sins" are called sins; therefore they are called *the sins of the holy ones.* And *the sins of your priesthood,* that is, those very things which are offered for sins: as he also declares in *Leviticus,* and says they ought to belong to the priest[^37].

## What firstfruits are commanded to be offered.

**32.** (18, 13) *All the firstfruits, whatever shall be in their land, however much they shall bring to the Lord, shall be yours.* Here he says firstfruits, not the firstborn offspring of cattle: for these are named in Greek πρωτότοκα, but the others πρωτογενήματα. But in Latin two names were not found for these two things. And therefore these πρωτογενήματα some rendered *firstfruits*: but firstfruits are called ἀπαρχαί, and are something else. These three, then, are thus distinguished: πρωτότοκα are the firstborn offspring of animals, even of men: but πρωτογενήματα, the first fruits taken from the earth, or from a tree, or from a vine: and firstfruits, of fruits indeed, but already brought in from the field; as from the lump, from the vat, from the jar, from the cask, which were first taken.

## Concerning the red heifer.

**33.** 1. (19, 1-22) Concerning the red heifer, whose ashes the Law commanded to avail for the water of sprinkling and for the cleansing of those who had touched a dead body, we are not permitted to be silent; for in it a most evident sign of the New Testament is prefigured; nor, hastening, are we able to speak worthily enough of so great a sacred symbol.

For first, that Scripture began to speak of this matter in such a way, whom would it not move and make most intent upon the depth of the sacred symbol? *And the Lord spoke,* it says, *to Moses and Aaron, saying: This is the distinction of the Law, whatever the Lord has established.* Beyond doubt there is no distinction except between some two things, or more: for singularity does not require a distinction. Nor did it mention the distinction of any thing whatever, but added: *of the Law*: nor of any law whatever: for it is constantly said in Scripture, of each thing about which there is a lawful precept: "this is the law of this or that thing"; not the universal Law, which contains all the things that are lawfully prescribed: but here, when it had said: *this is the distinction of the Law*; it went on and added: *whatever the Lord has established,* by prescribing, surely, not by creating. For some translators too rendered, *whatever the Lord has commanded.*

If, then, this is the distinction of the Law, *whatever the Lord has commanded*; beyond doubt this distinction is great: and it is rightly understood to distinguish the two Testaments. For the same things are in the Old and the New: there shadowed, here revealed; there prefigured, here made manifest. For not only are the sacred symbols different, but also the promises. There temporal things seem to be set forth, by which a spiritual reward is secretly signified: but here spiritual and eternal things are most manifestly promised.

But of temporal and carnal goods, and of spiritual and eternal ones, what clearer and surer distinction is there than the Passion of our Lord Jesus Christ? in whose death it was sufficiently established that this earthly and transitory felicity is not to be hoped for and desired from the Lord God as a great gift: since in his only-begotten Son, whom he willed to suffer such great things, he declared by a most open distinction that something far other ought to be sought and expected from him. This Passion, then, of our Lord Jesus Christ, as it were the distinction of the two Testaments, this account of the slaying of the red heifer prefigures fittingly enough.

**33.** 2. *The Lord spoke to Moses and Aaron, saying: This is the distinction of the Law, whatever the Lord has established*: and then he begins to give command, adding these words and saying: *speak to the children of Israel.* It can also be construed thus: *and the Lord spoke to Moses and Aaron, saying: This is the distinction of the Law, whatever the Lord has established, saying*: not "whatever the Lord established by creating," as heaven and earth and all things that are in them; but *whatever the Lord established by saying,* namely in the two Testaments; so that then it follows: *speak to the children of Israel, and let them take to you a red heifer without defect.*

The red heifer signifies the flesh of Christ. The sex is feminine, on account of carnal weakness; it is red, on account of the very bloody Passion. But as for his saying, *let them take to you,* in Moses himself he shows the figure of the Law: since according to the Law they thought they were killing Christ, because he broke the Sabbath according to them, and, as they supposed, profaned the lawful observances. As for this heifer being called *without defect,* it is no wonder: for this flesh the other victims too figured, where likewise animals without defect are commanded to be immolated. For that flesh was *in the likeness of sinful flesh*[^38], but not sinful flesh. Yet here, where God more evidently willed to commend the distinction of the Law, it was too little to say *without defect,* unless it were said: *which has no defect in it*: which, if it was said for the sake of repeating, perhaps it is not in vain, in that the very repetition more firmly commended that thing.

Although that too is not far from the truth, that it is understood to be added: *which has no defect in it,* when it had already been said: *a heifer without defect,* because the flesh of Christ had no defect in itself, but had it in others, who are his members. For what flesh is in this life without sin, except that alone which has no defect in it? *And no yoke has been placed upon it.* For it was not subjected to iniquity, finding subjected to which those whom he freed, and breaking their bonds, so that it is said to him: *you have broken my bonds, to you I will sacrifice a victim of praise*[^39]. For upon his flesh no yoke was placed, who had power to lay down his soul and to take it up again[^40].

**33.** 3. *And you shall give it,* he says, *to Eleazar the priest.* Why not to Aaron, unless perhaps it was thus prefigured that the Passion of the Lord would come not to the time that then was, but to the descendants of this priesthood? *And they shall cast it outside the camp*: so too the Lord, about to suffer, was cast outside the city. But as for his saying, *into a clean place*; it was signified thus, because he had no evil cause. *And they shall kill it in his sight*: as the flesh of Christ was killed in the sight of those who were already to be priests of the Lord in the New Testament.

**33.** 4. *And Eleazar shall take of its blood, and shall sprinkle before the face of the tabernacle of testimony of its blood seven times.* This is a testimony that Christ, according to the Scriptures, shed his blood for the remission of sins[^41]. Therefore *before the face of the tabernacle of testimony,* because it was declared in no other way than as it had been foretold by divine testimony: and therefore *seven times,* because that number pertains to spiritual cleansing.

**33.** 5. *And they shall burn it in his sight.* I think that the burning pertains to the sign of the resurrection. For it is the nature of fire to be moved upward, and what is burned is turned into it. For "to burn up" too is a word brought from Greek into Latin from *suspending.* But as for what is added, *in his sight,* that is, in the sight of the priest, this seems to me to be intimated, that the resurrection of Christ appeared to those who were to be a royal priesthood. Now as for what follows: *and its skin and its flesh and its blood with its dung shall be burned,* this very thing is set out, how it will be burned: and it is signified that not only the substance of the mortal body of Christ, which is intimated by the mention of skin and flesh and blood; but also the contumely and rejection of the people, which I think is signified by the name *dung,* would be turned into glory, which the flame of burning signifies.

**33.** 6. *And the priest shall take cedar wood and hyssop and scarlet, and cast it into the midst of the burning of the heifer.* Cedar wood is hope, which ought firmly to dwell in things above. Hyssop is faith, which, though it be a lowly herb, clings by its roots to the rock. Scarlet is charity, which by its fiery color testifies to the fervor of the spirit. These three we ought to cast into the resurrection of Christ, as into the midst of that burning, so that with him our life may be hidden, as the Apostle says: *and your life is hidden with Christ in God*[^42].

**33.** 7. *And the priest shall wash his garments, and shall wash his body with water, and afterward he shall enter the camp, and the priest shall be unclean until evening.* What is the washing of the garments and of the body, but the cleansing of the outer and inner man? this is the priest. Then it follows: *and he who burns it shall wash his garments, and shall wash his body with water, and shall be unclean until evening.* In the one who burns it, I think those are figured who buried the flesh of Christ, committing it to the resurrection as to a conflagration.

**33.** 8. *And a clean man shall gather the ashes of the heifer, and shall place them outside the camp in a clean place.* What do we say the ashes of the heifer are—the remains, namely, of that slaying and burning—but the fame which followed the passion and resurrection of Christ? *For there are remains for the man of peace*[^43]. For it was both ash, because as though dead it was despised by the unbelieving; and yet it cleansed, because it was also believed by the faithful to have risen.

And because this fame shone most of all among those who were among the other nations, and were not of the fellowship of the Jews, therefore I judge it was said: *and a clean man shall gather the ashes of the heifer*: clean, surely, from the slaying of Christ, which had made the Jews guilty. *And he shall lay them up in a clean place,* that is, he shall treat them honorably: yet *outside the camp,* because outside the celebration of the Jewish custom the honor of the Gospel shone forth. *And it shall be for the congregation of the children of Israel for preservation. The water of sprinkling, it is a purification.* Afterward it declares more fully how from these ashes the water of sprinkling was made, by which they were cleansed from the touching of the dead; which surely signifies from the iniquity of this dying or death-bound life.

**33.** 9. But it is strange what follows: *and he who gathers,* it says, *the ashes of the heifer, shall wash his garments; and he shall be unclean until evening.* How shall he be unclean from this, who had approached clean; unless because even those who seem to themselves clean recognize, in the Christian faith, that *all have sinned and lack the glory of God, being justified freely through his blood*[^44]? Yet it said he should wash his garments, not also his body; I believe, because by that gathering of the ashes and their being laid up in a clean place, if these be understood spiritually, it means he was already cleansed within: as Cornelius, hearing and believing what Peter had preached, was so cleansed that before visible Baptism, with his own who were present, he received the gift of the Holy Spirit; yet neither could the visible sacrament be despised[^45], so that, washed even outwardly, he should in a way wash his garments.

*And it shall be,* it says, *for the children of Israel, and for the proselytes who are joined to them, a perpetual statute.* What else does it show, but that the Baptism of Christ, which the water of sprinkling signified, would profit both Jews and gentiles; that is, the children of Israel and the proselytes, as the natural branches and the wild olive grafted into the richness of the root[^46]? But whom would it not make intent, that after the washing it is said of each one: *and he shall be unclean until evening*? And not only here, but in all, or in almost all such cleansings, this is said. Where I do not know whether anything else can be understood, except that every man, after the fullest remission of sins, by remaining in this life contracts something whereby he is unclean until the end of that same life, where this day of his is in a way closed, which the word *evening* signifies.

**33.** 10. Then Scripture begins to say, and pursues, how men made unclean are purified by that water of sprinkling: *he who touches,* it says, *a dead man, every soul of a man shall be unclean seven days: he shall be purified on the third day and on the seventh day, and shall be clean.* And here I see nothing else to be understood, but that the touching of the dead is the iniquity of a man. But the uncleanness of seven days I think is said on account of soul and body; the soul in the number three, the body in the number four. Why this is so, it would be long to dispute. According to this I judge it was said through the prophet: *for three and four impieties I will not be opposed*[^47].

But he adds, and says: *But if he be not purified on the third day and on the seventh day, he shall not be clean. Everyone who touches a dead man, of any soul of a man, and he die, and be not purified*; that is, die after touching a dead man, before he is purified; *he has polluted the tabernacle of the Lord: that soul shall be rubbed out from Israel.* It is to be noted that most rarely is anything found in these books written more evidently about the life of the soul after death. Here, then, when it says that, if he die before purification, the uncleanness remains in him, and that soul is rubbed out from Israel, that is, from the fellowship of the people of God; what else does it mean, but that the punishment of the soul remains, even after death, if, while it lives, it has not been cleansed by this sacred symbol, by which the Baptism of Christ is figured?

*Since the water of sprinkling,* it says, *has not been sprinkled upon him, he is unclean; still his uncleanness is upon him. Still,* namely, even after death. But as for what it said above, *he has polluted the tabernacle of the Lord,* it said this surely as far as in him lies: as the Apostle: *do not quench the Spirit*[^48]: although he cannot be quenched. For if it meant that the tabernacle was made unclean by this, it would surely command it to be cleansed.

**33.** 11. But afterward it commands those made unclean by the dead—that is, by dead works, which are all iniquities—to be cleansed thus: *and they shall take for that unclean man,* it says, *of the ashes of that burned purification, and shall pour over them,* that is, over those same ashes, *living water in a vessel: and a clean man, taking hyssop, dipping it in the water, and sprinkling over the house, and over the vessels, and over the souls, as many as were there, and over him who touched human bone, or one wounded, or one dead, or a tomb; and the clean man shall sprinkle over the unclean on the third day and on the seventh day: and he shall be purified on the seventh day, and shall wash his garments, and shall be washed with water, and shall be unclean until evening.*

The water of sprinkling is one thing, and that with which he shall wash his garments is surely another. *And he shall be washed with water,* which I think is to be understood spiritually, by signification, not by its proper nature. For without doubt it was visible, like all those shadows of things to come. Accordingly, he who is rightly washed by the sacrament of Baptism, which that water of sprinkling figured, is cleansed also spiritually, that is, invisibly, both in flesh and in soul, so that he is clean in both body and spirit.

But as for his saying that the water of sprinkling is sprinkled with hyssop, by which herb we said above faith is signified; what else occurs but what is written: *cleansing their hearts by faith*[^49]? For Baptism does not profit if faith is lacking. And he said this is done by a clean man, where are signified the ministers bearing the person of their Lord, who is truly the clean man.

For of these ministers too it says in what follows: *and he who sprinkles the water of sprinkling shall wash his garments*; that is, he shall observe it in his body too. *And he who touches the water of sprinkling shall be unclean until evening. And everything whatever the unclean man touches shall be unclean: and the soul that touches it shall be unclean until evening.* I have already said above what *until evening* seems to me to signify.

## What "one wounded" and "one dead" are called.

**34.** (19, 16) *Everyone who touches upon the face of the field one wounded, or one dead, or the bone of a man, or a tomb.* It may be asked what is meant by *one wounded, or one dead.* For if he meant one thing by *wounded,* another by *dead*; it must be guarded against that one be thought unclean who also touches a living wounded man, which is surely absurd. But because the dead too can be wounded, he is understood to have distinguished the dead themselves, so that we understand even a wounded dead man, that is, one slain by a wound, or one dead without a wound.

## Concerning the rock from which water was brought forth.

**35.** (20, 11) What it was that water was brought forth from the rock, the Apostle Paul set out, where he says: *and all drank the same spiritual drink. For they drank of the spiritual rock that followed them: and the rock was Christ*[^50]. There was signified, then, the spiritual grace flowing from Christ, by which the inner thirst was watered. But that the rock is struck with a rod figures the cross of Christ. For by the wood coming to the rock, grace flowed forth: and that it is struck twice more evidently signifies the cross. For the cross is two pieces of wood.

## The water of contradiction.

**36.** (20, 13) As to what was said of that water which flowed from the rock: *this is the water of contradiction, because the children of Israel cursed before the Lord, and he was sanctified among them*: first they cursed, when they spoke against the benefit of the Lord, by which they had been brought out of Egypt; and afterward he was sanctified among them, when by that miracle of the flowing water his sanctity was declared. Or did he perhaps show two kinds of men, both those who contradict the grace of Christ, and those who receive the grace of Christ; so that to those it is the water of contradiction, to these of sanctification? For of the Lord himself too it is read in the Gospel: *and for a sign that shall be contradicted*[^51].

## How what Moses says to the king of Edom is to be understood.

**37.** (20, 17. 19) As to what Moses, among the messages to the king of Edom, says among other things: *nor will we drink water from your well,* it is to be understood "for free," that is, that "we will not drink for free": which he afterward makes clear, saying: *but if we drink of your water, I and my cattle, I will give you a price.*

## What the right and the left are.

**38.** (20, 17) *We will not turn aside to the right or to the left*: it is said in the plural, "to the things that are on the right, or that are on the left."

## The water of cursing.

**39.** (20, 24) *You shall not enter into the land which I have given to the children of Israel for a possession; because you provoked me at the water of cursing.* What he called above *the water of contradiction,* he here calls *of cursing*: for he does not say ἀντιλογίας, but: λοιδορίας.

## How "I will devote to destruction" is said of a vow.

**40.** (21, 2) *And Israel vowed a vow to the Lord, and said: If you deliver this people to me as subject,* that is, "if you subject it to me by delivering it," *I will devote it to destruction, and its cities.* Here it is to be considered how he says *I will devote to destruction,* which is vowed, and yet is put for a curse, as is said also of this people: whence there is that saying: *if anyone preaches to you other than what you received, let him be anathema*[^52]. Hence it has been commonly derived that one is said to "devote" (*devotare*): for hardly anyone says that one devotes himself, except by cursing.

## Whence "anathema" is said.

**41.** (21, 3) *And he devoted it to destruction and its city: and the name of that place was called Anathema.* Hence it has been derived that *anathema* seems something detestable and abominable. For that the victor should take away nothing thence for his own uses, but vow the whole to be paid as a penalty, this was to "anathematize," which is commonly called "to devote." But the origin of this word is in the Greek language from those things which, vowed and paid—that is, promised and rendered—were placed up high in the temples, ἀπὸ τοῦ ἄνω τιθέναι: that is, "to place up high," whether by fixing or by hanging.

## In what book the saying is to be understood: The war of the Lord set Moab aflame.

**42.** (21, 13-15) In the journey of the children of Israel, in which they moved on and pitched their camps, among other things it is written: *and from there they pitched camp beyond the Arnon in the wilderness, which stands out from the borders of the Amorites. For the Arnon is the border of Moab, between Moab and the Amorite. Therefore it is said in the book The War of the Lord: Zoob it set aflame, and the torrents of the Arnon, and the torrents it caused to inhabit Er.*

In what book this is written, he did not mention, nor is there any such among those which we call the canonical Divine Scriptures. From such things they find occasions who try to insinuate apocryphal books into the ears of the unwary and the curious, in order to persuade them of fabulous impieties. But here it is said to be written in a book; it is not said in whose holy book, of what prophet or patriarch. Nor is it to be denied that there were already books, whether of the Chaldeans, from whom Abraham came forth; or of the Egyptians, where Moses had learned all their wisdom[^53]; or of any other nation, in some one of whose books this could be written: which nevertheless is not therefore to be taken up into those Scriptures by which divine authority is commended; as neither is that Cretan prophet, of whom the Apostle makes mention[^54]; nor the Greek writers, whether philosophers or poets, whom that same Apostle, speaking to the Athenians, affirms to have said something surely great and truly put: *for in him we live, and move, and are*[^55].

For it is permitted to divine authority to take testimony, from wherever it has found what is true; but it does not therefore affirm that all the things which are written there are to be accepted. But why this was mentioned in this passage does not clearly appear; unless perhaps, in order that the borders there might be established between the two nations, it was done by war, which war the men of that place, on account of its greatness, said was the war of the Lord, so that it was written in some book of theirs: *the war of the Lord set Zoob aflame*: either because that city burned in that same war, or was inflamed—that is, roused—to fight, or whatever else lies hidden in the obscurity of this passage.

## Where it is to be understood that the people drank.

**43.** (21, 16) *This is the well of which the Lord said to Moses: Gather the people, and I will give them water to drink.* This is mentioned as though somewhere above the Lord were read to have said this to Moses: but because it is nowhere found, it is here to be understood that the people, who were complaining of the drought, drank there too.

## How Israel possessed the cities.

**44.** (21, 24-25) *And Israel struck him with the slaughter of the sword: and they took possession of his land from the Arnon to the Jabbok, and as far as the children of Ammon: for Jazer is the border of the children of Ammon. And Israel took all these cities. And Israel dwelt in all the cities of the Amorites, in Heshbon.* Here surely Israel possessed the cities of the Amorites, which it overcame by war; because it did not devote them to destruction: for if it had devoted them to destruction, it would not be permitted them to possess them, nor to take from there anything of the spoil for their own uses. It is indeed to be noted in what way just wars were waged. For harmless passage was denied[^56], which by the most equitable right of human society ought to have lain open. But now, that God might fulfill his promises, he helped the Israelites here, to whom the land of the Amorites had to be given. For when Edom likewise denied them passage, the Israelites did not fight with that nation itself, that is, the children of Jacob with the children of Esau, two brothers and twins, because he had not promised that land to the Israelites; but they turned aside from them[^57].

## Who the riddlers are, or are they poets.

**45.** (21, 27-30) *Therefore the riddlers will say: Come into Heshbon,* and so forth. Who the *riddlers* are does not appear, because they are not in the usage of our literature, nor in the Divine Scriptures themselves is this name found in almost any other place: but because they seem as it were to say a song, by which they sang the war waged between the Amorites and the Moabites, in which Sihon king of the Amorites overcame the Moabites[^58], these riddlers are not improbably supposed to have been then so called whom we call poets; in that it is the custom and license of poets to mingle into their songs the riddles of fables, by which they are understood to signify something. For they would not otherwise be riddles, unless there were there a figurative expression, which, once unraveled, one would arrive at the meaning that lay hidden in the riddle.

## Not all the Moabites came under the condition of Sihon king of the Amorites.

**46.** (22, 4-6) As to what Scripture says, that after Israel conquered the Amorites and possessed all their cities, Balak king of the Moabites sent envoys to hire Balaam, by whom Israel might be cursed, it is shown well enough that not all the Moabites came under the condition of Sihon king of the Amorites, when he overcame them in war: since the nation of the Moabites remained up to that time, when Balak king of Moab reigned. But as for Moab saying to the elders of Midian—that is, the Moabites to the elders of the Midianites: *now this congregation will lick up all who are round about us*; it was not one nation, but neighbors said to neighbors what was alike to be guarded against. For Moab was the son of Lot by one of his daughters[^59], but Midian the son of Abraham by Keturah[^60]. It was therefore not one nation, but two neighboring and adjoining ones.

## Concerning the words: And the divinations in their hands.

**47.** (22, 7. 9. 13) What does it mean that it is written: *and the divinations in their hands,* when it was said of those whom Balak had sent to hire Balaam, that he might curse Israel? Did they themselves divine? Or did they carry something whence Balaam might make that by which he could divine, such as things that were burned in sacrifices, or in some way expended; and were they therefore called *divinations,* because through these he could divine? Or something else? For it is obscurely said.

It is indeed to be noted that *God came to Balaam and said to him: What men are these with you?* and so forth. Nor is it said whether this was done in a dream, although that it was done by night is sufficiently clear, since after these things Scripture says: *and Balaam, rising in the morning.* For it may move one how God spoke with a most wicked man, which, even if it were established to have been done in a dream, would not for that reason leave no question remaining, on account of the very unworthiness.

So too the Lord Jesus Christ says of that rich man, who was planning to destroy his old storehouses and to fill larger new ones: *God said to him: Fool, this night your soul shall be taken from you; and these things which you have prepared, whose shall they be?*[^61] Let no one boast that God speaks to him in the way in which he knows he must speak to such men; since this can be done even to the reprobate: for even when he speaks through an angel, he himself speaks.

## In what Balaam sinned.

**48.** (22, 12-27) As to what Balaam said, when more honored envoys were sent to him again: *if Balak should give me his house full of silver and gold, I cannot transgress the word of the Lord, to make it small or great in my mind,* it has no sin at all: but what follows is not without grave sin. For he ought already to have been steadfast, having once heard what the Lord had said to him: *you shall not go with them, nor curse the people: for it is blessed*; and not to give them any hope that the Lord could—as though Balaam himself, swayed by gifts and honors, against his own people, whom he had said was blessed—change his sentence. But there he showed himself overcome by desire, when he wished the Lord to speak to him again about this matter, of which he had already learned his will. For what need was there to add what follows: *and now stay here, you too, this night, and I will know what the Lord will add to say to me?*

Accordingly the Lord, seeing his desire caught and overcome by gifts, permitted him to go, that through the beast on which he rode he might restrain his avarice: confounding that madness by this very thing, that the ass did not dare to transgress the prohibition of the Lord made through the angel, which he was trying to transgress out of desire, although he suppressed that same desire out of fear. *For God came to Balaam by night, and said to him: If these men have come to call you, rise and follow them, but whatever word I shall speak to you, this you shall do. And Balaam, rising in the morning, saddled his ass, and went with the princes of Moab.* Why, after this permission, did he not again consult God, and after that prohibition thought he should consult him again, unless because his malignant desire appeared, although it was pressed down by fear of the Lord?

Finally Scripture follows: *and God was angry in his spirit, because he was going; and the angel of God rose up, so as not to permit him on the way,* and the rest that follows, until the ass spoke. Nothing here indeed seems more astonishing than that, when the ass spoke, he was not terrified, but moreover answered it, as though accustomed to such marvels, his anger persevering. Afterward the angel too speaks to him, reproving and disapproving his way: yet at this sight, terrified, he adored. Then he was permitted to go, so that now through him a most clear prophecy might be brought forth. For he was by no means permitted to say what he wished, but what he was compelled by the power of the Spirit. And he himself indeed remained reprobate: for the later holy Scripture spoke of him in such a way that certain blameworthy and reprobate persons were said to have followed his way: *following,* it says, *the way of Balaam son of Beor, who loved the wages of iniquity*[^62].

## Concerning the angel who spoke to Balaam on the way.

**49.** (22, 22. 32) Concerning the angel who spoke to Balaam on the way, at the sight of whom his ass did not dare to go forward, Scripture speaks thus: *and God was angry in his spirit, because he was going; and the angel of God rose up to oppose him on the way.* Where first it is to be noted how it said, *God was angry, and the angel of God rose up*: nor did it insert that the angry God sent an angel, but as it were signified the angry God in the angel; since the truth and justice of God made the angel angry. For the word *rose up* is surely to be understood of a stirring.

Then, as for his saying, *to oppose him on the way,* which in Greek is set down as διαβαλεῖν, this the angel himself says in what follows too: *and behold, I have come forth for your hindrance,* where the Greek has διαβολήν. Where perhaps "accusation" is more fittingly understood, so that here what was said, *to oppose him on the way,* is taken as "to accuse." Whence it is believed that the devil too is so called, as being one who should be called in Latin "accuser": not because no one can also accuse well and rightly; but because the devil loves to accuse, surely driven by the goads of envy, such as the testimony borne to him in the *Apocalypse* declares[^63].

But this word is set down in a comedy too, whence it is no doubt Latin, under the same signification, or at least a kindred one, where it is said of a father angry with his son:

> *He hopes he has found a speech  
> by which he may toss you to and fro*[^64].

Now here "toss to and fro" is usually understood as "carry this way and that by a tempest of words," as it were "tear apart and scatter"; which surely he seemed about to do by accusing. But even if we understand *to oppose him on the way* thus, that the angel delayed his haste by holding him back, so as both to show him and to tell him what was needful; the placing of this word even under that signification is not absurd.

## How the things related about the ass are to be understood.

**50.** (22, 23-29) *And when the ass had seen the angel of God resisting on the way, and the drawn sword in his hand, the ass turned aside from the way, and went into the field.* This field was as yet outside the walls of the vineyards. *And he struck the ass with the rod, to set it right on the way. And the angel of God stood in the furrows of the vineyards, a wall on this side, and a wall on that.*

It is rightly asked, if walls on this side and that set the way in the middle, as is wont to be done; how the angel standing there is said to have stood in the furrows of the vineyards: for in a way between walls there could not be furrows of vineyards. But the order of the words is, *to set it right on the way, a wall on this side, and a wall on that.* On this way, then, Balaam wished to set the ass right, so that it should walk between the walls.

But it was inserted: *and the angel of God stood in the furrows of the vineyards,* in one, namely, of the vineyards which set the way in the middle.

*And when the ass had seen the angel of God, it pressed itself against the wall,* that is, against the wall of that vineyard, surely, in which the angel was not, since on the other side he was in the furrows of the vineyards.

*And it pressed Balaam's foot against the wall, and he proceeded still to strike it. And the angel of God moved on, and, going further, stood still in a narrow place*; now not in the furrows of the vineyards, but between the walls themselves, that is, on the way: *in which there was no turning aside to the right or the left.*

*And when the ass had seen the angel of God, it sat down under Balaam.* For, being struck, it did not go back; it did not press itself against the wall, since it was not frightened on the other side, but in the middle of the way, in the narrow place, was the angel: it remained, then, that it should sit down.

*And Balaam was angry, and struck the ass with the rod. And God opened the mouth of the ass, and it said to Balaam: What have I done to you, that you have struck me this third time? And Balaam said to the ass: Because you have mocked me; and if I had a sword in my hand, I would by now have run you through.* Surely this man was carried by such great desire that he was not terrified even by the marvel of so great a portent, and answered as though speaking to a man, although God surely had not turned the soul of the ass into a rational nature, but had made what pleased him sound forth from it, to restrain that man's madness: perhaps prefiguring that *God was about to choose the foolish things of the world, to confound the wise*[^65], on behalf of that spiritual and true Israel, that is, the children of the promise.

## How the Spirit of God is said to have come upon Balaam.

**51.** (23, 6) *And the Spirit of God came upon him*; that is, upon Balaam. The Spirit of God was not made, as though the Spirit of God were a thing made; but *came upon him,* that is, "it came to pass that he was upon him": as: *he who comes after me has been made before me,* that is, it came to pass that he was before me, that he was set before me; *because he was prior to me,* it says[^66]: and as: *the Lord has been made my helper*[^67]; for the Lord is not a thing made, but it came to pass that he helped me: and: *the Lord has been made a refuge for the poor*[^68], that is, it came to pass that the poor took refuge in him; and: *the hand of the Lord has been made upon me*[^69], that is, it came to pass that it was upon me; and many such things are found in the Scriptures.

## What it is to display the leaders to the Lord against the sun, and what the Greek has there.

**52.** (25, 1-4) *And the Lord said to Moses: Take the leaders of the people, and display them to the Lord against the sun; and the wrath of the Lord's indignation shall be taken away from Israel.* God was angry at the fornications of Israel, both carnal and spiritual; for they had both mingled shamelessly with the daughters of Moab, and been consecrated to idols: this he said to Moses, that he should display the leaders of the people to the Lord against the sun. In which word they are understood to have been commanded to be crucified, so that this is: *display them to the Lord against the sun,* that is, openly in the sight of this light. For the Greek says παραδειγμάτισον, which could be rendered: "make examples"; because παράδειγμα means "example." For besides the Septuagint translators, Aquila is reported to have said: *fix,* or rather: *fix up,* which is ἀνάπηξον. But Symmachus, with a more evident word: *hang.*

It is indeed strange that Scripture stopped relating whether this was fulfilled by the Lord's command: which I do not see could have been despised, or, if it was despised, despised with impunity. But if it was done and kept silent, why does Scripture testify that the Lord was appeased and the plague ceased from the fact that Phinehas son of Eleazar ran through the adulterers[^70]; as though, the leaders being crucified as the Lord had commanded, an indignation still persevering seemed to need to be appeased in another way: when beyond doubt what the Lord had foretold and promised could not be false, saying: *take the leaders of the people, and display them to the Lord against the sun; and the wrath of the Lord's indignation shall be turned away from Israel*?

If, then, it had been done, who would doubt that the wrath of the Lord had been turned away from Israel? Why, then, was there need that Phinehas should still, to appease God, take vengeance thus upon the adulterers, and that Scripture should bear him witness that in this way he appeased the Lord?

Unless perhaps we understand that, when Moses was arranging to fulfill that command of the Lord about the leaders of the people, he willed also, according to the Law, to punish such outrages and sacrilegious boldness, so that he commanded each one to kill his neighbor wickedly consecrated to strange gods[^71], and that meanwhile Phinehas too did that deed, and so, the wrath of the Lord being now appeased, there was no need for the leaders of the people to be crucified. This severity, surely, suited to that time, shows evidently enough to the faith of the prudent how great an evil is the evil of fornication and idolatry.

## Whether the cause of the death of Moses and Aaron is the same.

**53.** (27, 13-14) The Lord gives as the cause of the death of Moses the same as also of his brother. For to both of them too he had foretold this beforehand, that they would not for this reason enter with the people of God into the land of promise, because they did not sanctify him before the people at the water of contradiction[^72]; that is, because they doubted of his gift, that water could flow from the rock, as we set out in that very passage of Scripture[^73].

But the sacred symbolism of this matter is given to be understood, that neither the priesthood which was first instituted, whose person Aaron bore, nor the Law itself, whose person Moses bore, brings the people of God into the land of the eternal inheritance; but Jesus, in whom was the type of the Lord Jesus Christ, that is, grace through faith[^74]. And Aaron indeed died before Israel entered into any part of the Land of Promise; but while Moses was still living the land of the Amorites was taken and possessed, yet he was not permitted to cross the Jordan with them. For in some part the Law is observed in the Christian faith. For there too are precepts which we Christians are commanded to observe even today. But that priesthood and those sacrifices hold no part today in the Christian faith, except that they were done and finished in the shadows of things to come.

When it is said of both brothers, that is, of Aaron and of Moses, that they are to be gathered to their people, it is clear that there is no anger of God upon them, the anger that separates a man from the peace of the holy and eternal fellowship. From this it is shown that not only their offices but even their deaths were signs of things to come, not punishments of God's indignation.

## How Joshua, who is said to have the Spirit in himself, is one upon whom Moses is commanded to lay his hands.

**54.** (27:18-19) *And the Lord spoke to Moses, saying: Take to yourself Joshua the son of Nun, a man who has the Spirit in himself; and you shall lay your hands upon him, and shall set him before Eleazar the priest, and shall give him a charge in the sight of all the congregation,*[^75] and so on. It is to be noted that, although Joshua the son of Nun already had the Spirit in himself, as Scripture testifies—and here what else should we understand but the Holy Spirit? for it would not say this of the spirit of a man, which there was no one who did not have—nonetheless Moses was commanded to lay his hands upon him, lest any man, however eminent in grace, should dare to refuse the sacraments of consecration.

## What it means that God gave Moses a command concerning Joshua the son of Nun.

**55.** (27:20) What does it mean that, when God was giving Moses a command concerning Joshua the son of Nun, he said among other things: *and you shall put some of your glory upon him*[^76]? For the Greek phrasing has τῆς δόξης, which means just the same as if it said *of glory,* that is, ἀπὸ τῆς δόξης. Some of the Latin translators, however, rendered it: *you shall give your glory;* not: *of your glory.* But even if it were so, that it said *your glory,* Moses would not on that account cease to have it; nor, because it said *some of your glory,* was what he had thereby diminished. For it is to be understood as if it said: "you shall make him a partner of your glory"; and things of this kind are not diminished as though divided into parts, but they belong wholly to all and wholly to each who share in them.

## Concerning the swearing of which it is said: Any man whatever who vows a vow . . .

**56.** (30:3) *A man, any man whatever who vows a vow to the Lord, or swears an oath, or makes a binding pledge concerning his soul, shall not profane his word: he shall do whatever has gone forth from his mouth.*[^77] This does not pertain to every kind of swearing, but to that in which someone has vowed concerning his soul abstinence from some thing which it was lawful for him to use under the Law, but which by his vow he himself makes unlawful for himself.

## Concerning the vow of a married woman.

**57.** (30:4-6) *But if a woman vows a vow to the Lord, or makes a binding pledge in her father's house in her youth, and her father hears her vows and her pledges, which she has pledged against her soul, and her father holds his peace; then all her vows shall stand, and all her pledges, which she has pledged against her soul, shall remain binding on her. But if her father utterly refuses her on the day that he hears all her vows and her pledges, which she has pledged against her soul, they shall not stand: and the Lord will cleanse her, because her father refused her.*[^78]

Since indeed it is speaking of a woman who is still in her youth in her father's house, there is good reason here to ask also about the vow of virginity; for it is very well known that even virgins are commonly called women in Scripture. And the Apostle too seems to be speaking of a father when he says: *let him keep his virgin;* and: *let him give his virgin in marriage,*[^79] and so on in this manner—where some have understood *his virgin* to mean "his virginity"; yet they show no similar usage of Scripture for this, since it is most unusual. As for what it says, *against her soul,* it is not to be taken as though she harmed her soul by such vows; rather *against the soul* is said with reference to the soul's pleasure, just as, when giving a command about fasting above, it says: *and you shall afflict your souls.*[^80]

## Concerning the words: And the Lord will cleanse her . . .

**58.** (30:6) As for what it says, *and the Lord will cleanse her, because her father refused her;* it said *he will cleanse* in the sense that he will absolve her from the guilt of the unfulfilled vow—just as in many places it is said: *and the priest shall cleanse him,*[^81] that is, he will hold him clean, will judge him clean; as is also that saying: *by no cleansing shall you cleanse the guilty,* that is: "you shall not call clean the one who is unclean."

## How a wife's vow holds when her husband consents.

**59.** 1. (30:7-19) *But if she has been given to a husband, and her vows are upon her according to the utterance of her lips, whatever she has pledged against her soul; and her husband hears, and holds his peace toward her on the day that he hears: then all her vows shall stand, and her pledges, which she has pledged against her soul, shall stand. But if her husband utterly refuses her on the day that he hears all her vows and her pledges, which she has pledged against her soul, they shall not remain: because her husband has refused her, and the Lord will cleanse her.*[^82]

A woman under a father before she marries, and one married under a husband—the Law did not will that she should vow anything to God against her soul (that is, by abstinence from certain lawful and permitted things) in such a way that in those vows a woman's authority should prevail, but rather a man's;

so that, if while she was still unmarried her father had already granted that her vows should be fulfilled, and if she marries before she has fulfilled them, and once it is made known to her husband it does not please him, she should not fulfill them, and is altogether without sin, because *the Lord will cleanse her,* as it says—that is, he will judge her clean. Nor is this to be thought to be done against God, since God himself commanded this, willed this.

**59.** 2. There follows a passage about widows, or women cast off by a husband—that is, women not placed under the authority of a husband or of a father; and it says that their vows are free to be fulfilled, in this way: *and the vow,* it says, *of a widow and of a woman driven out, whatever she has vowed against her soul, shall remain binding on her.*

Then it speaks of a married woman, if, already established in her husband's house, she has vowed something of this kind. For it had earlier spoken of the woman who had vowed in her father's house and had married before she paid the vow.

Concerning her, then, who has vowed in her husband's house, it speaks thus: *but if in her husband's house there is her vow, or a pledge which is against her soul with an oath; and her husband hears, and holds his peace toward her, and does not refuse her; then all her vows shall stand, and all the pledges which she has pledged against her soul shall stand against her. But if her husband should annul them on the day that he hears all whatever has gone forth from her lips, according to her vows and according to the pledges which are against her soul, it shall not remain binding on her: her husband has annulled them, and the Lord will cleanse her. Every vow and every binding oath to afflict the soul, her husband shall establish for her, and her husband shall annul. But if he keeps utterly silent toward her from day to day, then he establishes for her all her vows and the pledges which are upon her; he establishes them for her, because he held his peace toward her on the day he heard. But if her husband should annul them after the day on which he heard; then he shall bear her sin.*[^83]

**59.** 3. It is clear that the Law willed a woman to be under her husband in such a way that none of the vows she has vowed for the sake of abstinence are to be fulfilled by her unless her husband has been the authority for it by permitting it. For although it willed it to pertain to the sin of that same husband if he first permitted and afterward forbade, yet even here it did not say that the woman should do what she had vowed because she had first been permitted by her husband. It said that the sin was the husband's, because he refused what he had first granted; yet it did not on this account give the woman leave that, when her husband had first granted it to her, his later prohibition should be disregarded.

**59.** 4. But whether these things pertain also to vows of continence and of abstinence from intercourse is rightly asked, lest perhaps only those things are to be understood as vowed against the soul which concern food and drink. This seems also to be indicated by that saying where the Lord says: *is not the soul more than food?*[^84] and where a command is given about fasting, it is given thus: *you shall afflict your souls.*[^85]

I do not know, however, whether it can anywhere be read that a vow concerning abstinence from intercourse is said to be against the soul—especially because, although here the Law grants authority to the husband, not to the woman who is subject to the husband, so that the woman's vows are then to be fulfilled if the husband approves, but are not owed if he refuses—yet the Apostle, when he was speaking of the intercourse of married persons, did not there give greater authority to the husband than to the woman; but: *let the husband,* he says, *render to his wife her due, and likewise the wife to the husband: the wife does not have authority over her own body, but the husband; and likewise the husband does not have authority over his own body, but the wife.*[^86]

Since, then, in this matter he willed the authority of both to be equal, I think he suggests to us, for our understanding, that this rule about lying together or not lying together does not pertain to those vows in which the husband and the wife do not have equal authority, but the husband's is greater, and is almost his alone. For the Law does not say that the husband ought not to fulfill his vows if his wife forbids; but that the wife ought not, if her husband forbids. Hence it does not seem to me that, in vows and pledges and obligations of this kind, which are made against the soul, those agreements should also be reckoned which a husband and wife have made between themselves about lying together, or about abstaining from lying together.

**59.** 5. Then, since these too are called ordinances, and we remember that among those ordinances which are mentioned under this same name elsewhere in *Exodus*[^87] many things are commanded which cannot be taken in their proper sense, nor are observed in the New Testament—such as the boring through of a servant's ear,[^88] and the like—it is not absurd that here too something is understood to be said figuratively. So, since there are many abstinences in ceremonial observances that are unreasonable and sometimes even hostile to the truth, perhaps he willed this to be understood here: that those abstinences are valid when they are reasonable; that is, when reason has approved them—reason which, in the place of the husband, ought to govern every movement of the soul, a movement that occurs not only in desiring but also in abstaining—so that, if it is decided by mind and reason, then let it be done; but if it is disapproved by the counsel of reason, let it not be done. But if reason, having first decided that something ought rightly to be done, afterward disapproves it, let the fault be one of counsel: even so, however, let that movement consent to nothing but reason.

## What this means: With their strength . . .

**60.** (31:5-6) What does this mean: *and Moses sent them, a thousand from each tribe and a thousand from each tribe with their strength*[^89]? Does he mean us to understand *their strength* as "their leaders"? or strength given them by the Lord, or obtained through Moses himself? or does he rather call *their strength* the things by which their strength was sustained?

## Concerning the death of Balaam.

**61.** (31:8) A question may seem to arise as to how, when the Israelites had subdued the Midianites, Scripture says that Balaam was killed—Balaam who had been hired to curse the people of Israel—since earlier, when he was compelled to bless, Scripture so concluded that same episode when it said: *and Balaam, rising up, departed and returned to his own place, and Balak went off to himself.*[^90] If, then, Balaam had returned to his own place, how was he killed here, since he had come from so far away, that is, from Mesopotamia? Or did he perhaps return to Balak, and Scripture did not say so? Although it can also be understood that he returned to his own place in the sense that, from the very place where he was offering sacrifices, he returned to that place from which he had gone out to it—that is, where he had his lodging as a stranger. For it is not said: "to his own house," or: "to his own country"; but: *to his own place.* Now every stranger too has his own place, where he dwells for a time. But of Balak himself, who had hired him, it is not said: *to his own place,* but: *to himself*; that is, where he dwelt as ruler. So one could say *to his own place* both of a ruler and of a stranger; but *to himself* I do not see how a stranger could be said, since he had come to his own lodging.

## How they are said to have plundered their strength.

**62.** (31:9) *And they took as spoil the women of Midian, and their goods, and their cattle, and all that they possessed, and they plundered their strength.*[^91] When it had already named the women, the goods, the cattle, and all that they possessed, what then did it add by: *and they plundered their strength*? Surely this is to be understood as the strength of which it was said above, that he sent a thousand from each of the tribes *with their strength.*[^92] Or perhaps the food by which they were sustained is called their strength—the food which, when supplied, gives them their powers, and when withdrawn, makes their powers fail? Hence God, when threatening through the prophet, said: *I will take away the strength of bread, and the strength of water.*[^93] And so Moses too had sent those thousands with provisions, which is what is meant by: *with their strength*; and when the Midianites were conquered, they had plundered this of theirs as well, among the rest.

## Where Balaam gave malicious counsel.

**63.** (31:15-16) *Why have you kept alive every female? For these were a cause to the children of Israel, according to the word of Balaam, to make them depart and despise the word of the Lord because of Peor.*[^94] When Balaam gave this malicious counsel—that women should be set before them as an enticement, women through whom they might commit fornication not only bodily but also spiritually by worshiping the idol—Scripture did not say; and yet it is plain that it was done, since it is mentioned here. So it is possible that Balaam himself, who had already gone to his own place,[^95] also returned, so that we should not understand his place to be the lodging of his sojourn; although Scripture passed over this in silence.

## What the cities of refuge are.

**64.** (35:11-12) What does it mean that it says: *and the cities shall be for you a refuge from the avenger of blood, and he who has killed shall not die, until he stands before the congregation for judgment*[^96]; when it is speaking of those who have killed unwillingly, and elsewhere[^97] says that then each one in such a case among those who have taken refuge is free to leave the city to which he had fled, when the high priest has died? How then does it say here: *and he who has killed shall not die, until he stands before the congregation for judgment*; except because he is judged so that it may then be lawful for him to be in the city of refuge, if it has been made plain in the judgment that he killed unwillingly?

## How the slayer is commanded to be killed.

**65.** (35:19, 12) What does it mean that it says: *he who avenges blood, he himself shall kill the slayer; when he meets him, this one shall kill him*[^98]? For to those who understand it badly it sounds as though license to kill the slayer were given indiscriminately and without judgment to the avenger of his kinsman's death. But it willed this to be understood: that, according to what was said above, he who has killed should flee to one of the cities of refuge, until he stands for judgment, lest he be killed beforehand by the kinsman who finds him; because, even if he killed unwillingly, he is killed if found outside those cities. But when he has stood for judgment in one of those cities, and has been judged a murderer in one of those to which it is granted to flee, he is not allowed to be there; then at last, once he has been judged, the kinsman is permitted to kill him wherever he is found. For there is no need for him to be brought to judgment, since he has already been judged a murderer, and has on that account been driven out of those cities.
