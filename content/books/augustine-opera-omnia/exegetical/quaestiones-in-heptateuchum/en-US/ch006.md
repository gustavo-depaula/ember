# Book V: Questions on Deuteronomy

## God is the helper of men in such a way that the men themselves nonetheless cooperate.

**1.** (1:29-30) In the passage where Moses recalls that he said to the people, who feared the enemies inhabiting the land into which they were to be brought: *do not be afraid, neither fear them: the Lord your God, who goes before your face, he himself will fight against them together with you*[^1]—it is sufficiently shown that God is a helper in such a way that men too do something.

## How God hardened the spirit of Sihon.

**2.** (2:30) *And Sihon king of Heshbon was not willing to let us pass through his territory, because the Lord our God had hardened his spirit, and had made his heart resolute, that he might be delivered into your hands, as at this day.*[^2] In saying this while he addresses the people, Moses recalls something of the same sort as was said in *Exodus*: *I have hardened Pharaoh's heart*[^3]; and what is read in the *Psalms*: *he turned their heart, that they hated his people.*[^4]

Nor is the cause of this hardening passed over in silence here, when it is said, *that he might be delivered into your hands, as at this day*: that is, that he might be conquered by you; which would not happen unless he resisted; and he would not resist unless his heart were hardened. If we should ask after the justice of this matter, *the judgments of God are inscrutable*[^5]: yet there is no injustice with God.[^6] It is certainly to be noted that a heart can be said to be made resolute even in evil.

## How Og is said to be left over from the giants.

**3.** (3:11) *Nevertheless Og king of Bashan was left over from the Rephaim.*[^7] By this name, which is *Rephaim,* those who know the Hebrew language say that giants are signified.[^8] Hence what very many manuscripts have, *was left behind by the Rephaim,* is certainly said more plainly as *was left over,* so that he himself may be understood to have remained from among them: of whom, accordingly, the length and breadth of his iron bed is also recorded, to commend his great size.

## What the difference is between likeness and image.

**4.** (4:16-17) *Do not commit iniquity, and make for yourselves a carved likeness, any image.*[^9] What the difference is between likeness and image is commonly asked.[^10] But here I do not see what difference he wished there to be, unless either he signified one thing by these two words, or he said *likeness* to mean that, if, for example, a statue or figure is made having a human form, the features of some particular man are nevertheless not expressed, as painters or sculptors do when they look at those whom they paint or fashion. For no one would doubt that this is called an image: and according to this distinction every image is also a likeness, but not every likeness is also an image.

Hence if twins are alike one another, the likeness of either of them can be said to be in the other, but not the image. But if a son is like his father, it is also rightly called an image; so that the father is the prototype, from which that image is seen to be drawn: of which images some are of the same substance, like a son; others not of the same, like a painting. Hence what is written in Genesis: *God made man to the image of God*[^11]; it is plain that it was said in such a way that the image which was made is not of the same substance. For if it were of the same, it would be said not to have been made, but begotten. But because it did not add: "and likeness," when above it had been said: *let us make man to our image and likeness*[^12]; it seemed to some that likeness is something more than image,[^13] something to be reserved afterward for the man who is to be reformed through the grace of Christ. But I wonder whether it was not rather for this reason that he wished afterward to mention image alone: because where there is an image, there is at once also likeness.

Hence here too Moses forbids a likeness and an image to be made, perhaps according to that reasoning which we have stated. In the Decalogue, however, it is said generally that no likeness ought to be made,[^14] and no image is mentioned. For when no likeness is made, beyond doubt no image is made either; since if there is an image, there is certainly also a likeness. But it is not the case that, if a likeness is made, an image is at once made too: yet if there is no likeness, it follows that there is no image.

Finally, where he forbade likeness and image, he meant the likeness and image of a man to be understood, where a likeness can be made not of this or that particular man, but of any man whatever; and an image, that is, of this or that man specifically. But when it was speaking of cattle and irrational animals, it said "likeness" only: for who can be found who would set up for himself one particular dog, or anything of the kind, and, looking at it, paint or fashion its image? a thing which is most usual in the case of men.

## What is signified by water.

**5.** (4:18) But what does it mean that it says: *the likeness of any fish, whatever is in the waters under the earth*? Did he mean water too, on account of its tractable bodily mass, to be understood as earth, and according to this should we, in what is written, *God made heaven and earth,*[^15] understand the waters also? For Scripture constantly, by mentioning these two parts, means the whole world to be understood, according to that saying: *my help is from the Lord, who made heaven and earth,*[^16] and countless sayings of this kind. Or was it said *under the earth* for this reason, that unless the earth were higher than the waters, it could not be inhabited by men and have land animals?

## Whether it may seem that the gentiles are permitted to worship the sun and the stars and all the array of heaven.

**6.** (4:19) *And lest, looking up to heaven and seeing the sun and the moon and the stars and all the array of heaven, you go astray and adore them, and serve them, which the Lord your God has distributed to all the nations that are under heaven.*[^17] This was not said as though God commanded these things to be worshiped by the nations, but not to be worshiped by his own people alone; rather it was said either in the sense that God foreknew that the nations would render worship to these heavenly bodies and yet, foreknowing this, created them, while foreknowing that there would be that people of his which would not worship them; or *distributed* was said so that the use is understood which is commended in *Genesis*: *that they may be for signs, and for seasons, and for days, and for years*[^18]—a use which the people of God hold in common with all the nations, but not the worship which the other nations practice.

## How likeness is taken in a general sense.

**7.** (4:23) *Do not forget the covenant of the Lord your God, which he made with you, and make for yourselves a carved likeness of any of the things which the Lord your God has appointed for you.*[^19] Here certainly, speaking generally, it set down *likeness* and was silent about image; since, if no likeness is made, surely no image is made either: because where there is an image, there is at once a likeness; although not where there is a likeness, is there at once an image.

## What this means: from one end of heaven to the other end of heaven.

**8.** (4:32-33) It must be asked how it is said: *inquire of the former days, which were before you, from the day when God created man upon the earth, and from one end of heaven to the other end of heaven.*[^20] For *inquire* is understood. It seems, however, to signify the whole world. But why it says *from one end of heaven to the other end of heaven,* not "from one end of the earth to the other end of the earth," is not easy to discern. For there is a similar manner of speaking also in the Gospel when the Lord says that his elect will be gathered *from one end of the heavens to their other end.*[^21]

Unless perhaps here he means us to understand that what he commends as having been done uniquely among this people has not been heard of either among men or among angels; for this follows: *if anything has been done according to this great word, if any such thing has been heard: whether a nation has heard the voice of the living God speaking out of the midst of the fire, as you have heard, and lived.* But if this is so, that he says this has not been heard of either among men or among angels, what then is that passage in the Gospel: *from one end of the heavens to their other end,* since the Lord said this without doubt when he was speaking of the final gathering of his elect?

## Concerning the words: The Lord made the covenant with you, not with your fathers.

**9.** 1. (5:2-4) What does it mean that it says: *the Lord your God made a covenant with you at Horeb: the Lord did not make this covenant with your fathers, but with you, you who are all here alive today; the Lord spoke to you face to face on the mountain out of the midst of the fire*[^22]? Is it because those who do not enter the Land of Promise (for they all died) do not belong to this covenant—those of whom a reckoning was then made, when from twenty years of age and upward up to fifty years they were numbered as fit for war?[^23] How, then, did the Lord speak to those who are alive today? Is it because from twenty years and under there could then have been many who would remember this well, exempt from that punishment which God appointed for those who were then numbered, that they should not enter the Land of Promise? And surely it is these whom he addresses, who, although they were not twenty years and over when God spoke on the mountain, so as to be numbered then, could nonetheless have been nineteen and under, down to the age of boyhood—an age that could both see and hear and retain in memory the things that were done and said.

**9.** 2. But what does it mean that it says: *the Lord spoke to you face to face*—to those whom shortly before he had been most careful to warn that they had seen no likeness, but had heard only a voice out of the midst of the fire?[^24] Did he use these words on account of the plainness of the events, and, as it were, the presence of the manifested divinity, about which no one could doubt? But if so, what prevents us from understanding this of Moses himself, in what is said of him, that the Lord spoke with him face to face,[^25] so that he too saw nothing with his eyes except the fire? Or is he understood to have seen something more, because it is written that he entered into the cloud or mist where God was?[^26] But even if he saw something more than they, that he did not see the substance of God with mortal eyes can be understood from those words of his in which he says to God: *if I have found grace before you, show me yourself, that I may knowingly see you.*[^27] For it must not be thought that this people, to whom Moses was speaking, then so saw God face to face, when he was speaking on the mountain out of the midst of the fire, as the Apostle says we shall see in the end, where he says: *we see now through a glass in a dark manner, but then face to face.* And what this is, and how great it is, he then disclosed, saying: *now I know in part, but then I shall know even as I am known.*[^28] This too must be taken cautiously, lest a man be thought to be going to have as great a knowledge of God as God now has of man; but a knowledge perfect after its own measure, such that nothing more is awaited to be added to it: so that, as perfectly as God now knows man—yet as God knows man—so then man will perfectly know God, yet as man knows God. For not because it is said: *be perfect, as your heavenly Father is perfect,*[^29] should we hope for equality with the Father, which the only-begotten Word has: although there have not been lacking those who thought this too would come to pass, unless perhaps we little understand what they mean.

## How Moses stands between the Lord and the people.

**10.** 1. (5:5-6, 24) What does it mean that it says: *and I stood between the Lord and you at that time, to announce to you the words of the Lord*—as though the Lord were in a place, that is, on the mountain from which they heard the voices? This is to be taken so that we do not on this account suspect the substance of God to be in any bodily place—God, who is everywhere whole, and does not by intervals of place draw near or withdraw; but his manifestations in that creation which is not what he himself is are presented to human senses in no other way. Hence the Lord, wishing to take away from our mind suspicions of this kind, by which God is thought to be contained in some place, said: *the hour will come, when neither on this mountain nor in Jerusalem will you adore the Father. You adore what you do not know: we adore what we know; for salvation is from the Jews. But the hour comes, and now is, when the true worshipers will adore the Father in spirit and in truth. For the Father too seeks such to adore him. God is a Spirit, and those who adore him must adore him in spirit and in truth.*[^30] So Moses called himself the one in the middle, not between the substance of God and the people by some interval of place; but because it pleased the people to hear the rest of God's words through him, after they had been violently terrified upon hearing, out of the midst of the fire, the voice of God speaking the Decalogue of the Law.[^31]

**10.** 2. But it is rightly asked how these words of Deuteronomy are to be taken, when Moses says: *and I stood between the Lord and you at that time, to announce to you the words of the Lord; because you feared in the face of the fire, and did not go up the mountain, saying: I am the Lord your God,* and so on—which are now words of God, which the Decalogue contains. What, then, does he mean by adding: *saying*? For if we suppose a hyperbaton, so that the order of the words is: *and I stood between the Lord and you at that time, to announce to you the words of the Lord, saying: I am the Lord your God*—it will not be true. For the people did not hear these words through Moses, but out of the midst of the fire: and since they could not endure it once the Decalogue was heard, they asked to hear the rest through Moses.

It remains, then, that we understand what is set down, *saying,* to be set down in place of, as it were, "when he said"; so that this is the sense: *and I stood between the Lord and you at that time, to announce to you the words of the Lord; because you feared in the face of the fire, and did not go up the mountain,* when he said: *I am the Lord your God*: so that we understand, "when the Lord said it." For when the Lord was speaking these words, which it then recounts in full from the Decalogue, the people feared in the face of the fire, and did not go up the mountain, and asked rather to hear the words of the Lord through Moses.[^32]

**10.** 3. The words which Moses recounts in Deuteronomy as said to him by the people, when they were no longer willing to hear the voice of God but asked that what God was saying be said to them through him—that is: *behold, the Lord our God has shown us his glory, and we have heard his voice out of the midst of the fire,* and so on—are not read in exactly the same form in *Exodus,* where they were first narrated, the things that are now repeated.[^33] From this let us understand what I have already mentioned several times: that it is not to be reckoned a falsehood if the same intention is made plain by any other words whatever—and this on account of the Evangelists' words too, which by the unskilled and the slanderous are faulted as though contradictory. For it would not have been a great matter for Moses to attend to what he had written in *Exodus* and to repeat those things in exactly the same words, were it not that it pertains to our holy teachers to impress this very thing upon learners: that they should seek nothing else in the words of those who speak but the intention for the announcing of which the words were instituted.

## What it means that Moses says it was said to him by the Lord concerning the Hebrew people.

**11.** (5:29) What does it mean that Moses says it was said to him by the Lord concerning the Hebrew people: *who will grant that their heart be so within them, that they fear me, and keep my commandments?*[^34] Does he here mean us to understand that this benefit is granted by his grace, namely, that there be in men the righteousness of God which is from faith, not as it were their own, as from the Law?[^35] For he signifies this also through the prophet, saying: *I will take away from them their heart of stone, and will give them a heart of flesh*[^36]—which was said with reference to the feeling that "flesh" has and "stone" does not have, the word of course being used in a transferred sense.

He says this same thing elsewhere also: *behold, the days are coming, says the Lord, and I will accomplish over the house of Israel and over the house of Judah a new covenant; not according to the covenant which I made with their fathers in the day when I took their hand to lead them out of the land of Egypt. For this is the covenant which I will make with them after those days: putting my laws into their heart, and in their mind I will inscribe them, and their iniquities and their sins I will remember no more.*[^37] For here he distinguished the New Covenant from the Old, in that in the Old the Law was given on tablets of stone, but in the New in hearts, which is done through grace. Hence the Apostle too says: *not on tablets of stone, but on the fleshly tablets of the heart*: and in another place: *he made us,* he says, *fit ministers of the New Covenant, not of the letter, but of the spirit.*[^38]

## It is better if you do not even swear.

**12.** (6:13) What it says of the Lord: *and you shall swear by his name,*[^39] is not to be taken as a command, as though it bade swearing; rather it forbade swearing by the name of any other god. But it is better, according to the Gospel, if you do not even swear[^40]; not because true swearing is evil, but lest one fall into perjury through readiness to swear. For he who swears can swear not only what is true but also what is false: whereas he who does not swear at all walks far from perjury.

## How something is said plainly which elsewhere is said obscurely.

**13.** (8:2) *And you shall remember all the way by which the Lord your God has led you in the desert, that he might afflict you, and test you, and make known the things that are in your heart, whether you will keep his commandments, or not.*[^41] Here is said more plainly what elsewhere is obscure by its manner of expression, where it is read: *the Lord your God tests you, that he may know whether you love him.*[^42] For it is understood that *that he may know* is set down for, that he may cause it to be known; which is now said clearly: *that he might test you, and make known the things that are in your heart*: for it does not say, "and might come to know"; which, indeed, if it had said, would have to be understood as *make known.*

## How those who were earlier praised are now said to be stiff-necked, to their reproach.

**14.** (9:6-8) *And you shall know today that it is not for your righteousness that the Lord your God gives you this good land to inherit; for you are a stiff-necked people.*[^43] Surely these are the very ones who for this reason did not deserve to perish in the desert, because they did not know the right hand or the left[^44]: behold, they are now called *stiff-necked.* Hence it must be seen that this was said as a mystery, not because their merits were commended. For lest anyone suppose that those who had deservedly been praised before were suddenly made blameworthy, a little later it is said to them: *remember, do not forget, how greatly you provoked the Lord your God in the desert; from the day you went out of the land of Egypt, until you came to this place, you persevered in being unbelieving toward the Lord.*

But even if some of them were such, while some were faithful and good, even so the Land of Promise is certainly not given to those who do not know the right hand or the left in such a way that we should understand this as though they had not offended God. For their fathers too, who died and were not permitted to enter that same land, are found to have been such that among them were some even good. On account of this the Apostle says that not all, but some of them offended, among whom he mentions their sins.[^45] That these are indeed like their parents, this Scripture of *Deuteronomy* also teaches more evidently, in what it adds next, saying: *and at Horeb you provoked the Lord.* There certainly those provoked him who, for those same evil merits of theirs, were not led into the Land of Promise.

## Since in Exodus Moses is said to have written the words of the law, how it is ascribed to God.

**15.** 1. (10:1-4) *At that time the Lord said to me: Cut for yourself two tablets of stone like the former ones, and come up to me on the mountain; and you shall make for yourself an ark of wood: and I will write on the tablets the words that were on the former tablets, which you broke; and you shall put them into the ark. And I made an ark of incorruptible wood, and cut two tablets of stone like the former ones, and went up the mountain, with the two tablets in my two hands. And he wrote on the tablets, according to the former writing, the ten words, which the Lord spoke to you on the mountain out of the midst of the fire: and the Lord gave them to me.*[^46]

It is not without reason asked how these things are said in Deuteronomy, with Moses recalling and repeating what was done; when in Exodus, where these things are first narrated as said and done, it is written thus: *And the Lord said to Moses: Write for yourself these words; for in these words I have established a covenant with you and with Israel. And Moses was there in the sight of the Lord forty days and forty nights; he ate no bread, and drank no water, and he wrote on the tablets the words of the covenant, the ten words.*[^47] Since, then, in *Exodus* Moses himself is narrated to have written the ten words of the Law on the tablets, how is it here in *Deuteronomy* recalled that God wrote the same words on the tablets?

**15.** 2. Finally, that point which, when we treated of it in passing in *Exodus* and committed to writing what seemed to us in that difference[^48]—namely, why the former tablets, which were broken, are reported to have been written by the finger of God, while the second ones, which were to remain so long in the Ark and the tabernacle, Moses himself is said to have written: we said that by this difference the two covenants were signified, so that in the Old Covenant the Law was commended as the work of God, where man did nothing, because the Law could not be fulfilled by fear—since, when the work of the law is truly done, it is done by love, not by fear; and this love is the grace of the New Covenant.[^49] For this reason, on the second tablets, man is read to have written the words of God, because man can do the work of the Law through the love of righteousness, which he cannot do through fear of punishment.

**15.** 3. Now, then, when it is read in Deuteronomy concerning the second tablets, said thus: *and I cut two tablets of stone like the former ones, and went up the mountain, with the two tablets in my two hands. And he wrote on the tablets, according to the former writing, the ten words*—for it does not say: "and I wrote"; but, *he wrote,* that is, God; just as a little before he had said that the words of God were spoken to him: *cut for yourself two tablets of stone like the former ones, and come up to me on the mountain; and you shall make for yourself an ark of wood: and I will write on the tablets the words that were on the former tablets*—a question arises that must be examined: that here both sets of tablets, that is, both the former and the second, are read to have been written by God, not by man.

But if in *Exodus* too the words of God are read, where he bids those same second tablets to be cut by Moses, nothing else is found than that God himself promised that he would write them. For it is written thus: *and the Lord said to Moses: Cut for yourself two tablets of stone like the former ones too, and come up to me on the mountain: and I will write on the tablets the words that were on the former tablets, which you broke.*[^50]

And so, apart from the book of *Deuteronomy,* the book of *Exodus* alone also contains this question: how God said: *and I will write on the tablets the words that were on the former tablets*; when a little later it is read: *write for yourself these words; for in these words I have established a covenant with you and with Israel. And Moses was there in the sight of the Lord forty days and forty nights; he ate no bread, and drank no water, and he wrote on the tablets the words of the covenant, the ten words.*

For if what is said above, *write for yourself these words; for in these words I have established a covenant with you and with Israel,* pertains to what precedes—when God was commanding these things, that they should be written not on the two tablets of stone, but in that book of the Law where many things are written down—then certainly what follows: *and Moses was there in the sight of the Lord forty days and forty nights; he ate no bread, and drank no water, and he wrote on the tablets the words of the covenant, the ten words,*[^51] makes plain enough that this same Moses wrote these ten words on the tablets, not God.

Unless perhaps, by a forced reading, yet under a sure necessity, we are compelled, where it is said: *And he wrote on the tablets the words of the covenant, the ten words,* to understand not Moses, but the Lord; for above it is set down: *And Moses was there in the sight of the Lord*: so that these ten words are understood to have been written on the tablets by the Lord, in whose sight Moses was for forty days and forty nights eating no bread and drinking no water, as he had promised before.

**15.** 4. But if this is so, that difference of the two covenants which seemed good to us cannot indeed be commended in these words, since both the former and the second tablets were written not by man, but by God: yet this distinction certainly admits of no doubt, that the former tablets both God made and God wrote. For it was not then said to Moses: *cut for yourself two tablets*: but rather it is read thus: *and Moses, turning, came down from the mountain, with the two tablets of testimony in his hands: tablets of stone written on both sides, on this side and on that they were written; and the tablets were the work of God, and the writing is the writing of God carved on the tablets.*[^52] For above it had already said that those same tablets were written by the finger of God, speaking thus: *and he gave to Moses, as soon as he had ceased to speak to him on Mount Sinai, two tablets of testimony, tablets of stone written by the finger of God.*[^53]

There, then, both the tablets were the work of God, and their writing was made by the finger of God. But the second tablets Moses himself is bidden to cut; so that these at least are understood to have been cut by human work, although God himself wrote them, as he promised when he bade them be cut.

Furthermore, if we attend more carefully, both things were said of the second tablets for this reason: because God too works the work of the Law in man through his grace, and man, receiving the grace of God through his faith, belonging to the New Covenant, is a fellow worker with the God who helps (whereas on the first tablets the work of God alone is mentioned, because the Law is spiritual, and *the Law is holy, and the commandment holy and just and good*[^54]: but no work of man is mentioned there, because unbelievers are not blended with the help of grace, but, *being ignorant of the righteousness of God and wishing to establish their own, are not subject to the righteousness of God*[^55]; hence for them the Law avails for condemnation, which the breaking of the tablets signifies); accordingly we are not compelled by a forced understanding to supply that God wrote, where Scripture said: *and Moses was there in the sight of the Lord forty days and forty nights; he ate no bread and drank no water, and he wrote on the tablets the words of the covenant*[^56]: where it strongly sounds that Moses wrote.

But God promised above[^57] that he would write, and in Deuteronomy is narrated not only to have promised thus but even himself to have written, in order to signify what the Apostle says: *for it is God who works in you both to will and to work, for his good pleasure*[^58]: that is, in those who receive grace by faith, and do not wish to establish their own righteousness, but are subject to the righteousness of God,[^59] so that they themselves may be in Christ the righteousness of God.[^60] For there too the Apostle says both: that God works, and that they work. For if they were not working, how could he say to them: *work out your own salvation with fear and trembling*[^61]? He works, then, and we work together with him: for he does not take away, but helps the choice of a good will.

## What is signified by the tribe of Levi.

**16.** (10:8-9) *At that time the Lord set apart the tribe of Levi, to carry the Ark of the covenant of the Lord, to stand before the Lord, to minister, and to pray in his name to this day. For this reason there is for the Levites no portion and lot with their brethren; the Lord himself is his portion, as he said to him.*[^62] Unless by this tribe were signified the whole royal priesthood that belongs to the New Covenant, in no way would a man who was not of that same tribe have dared to say: *the Lord is my portion*[^63]; and in another *Psalm*: *the Lord is the portion of my inheritance.*[^64]

## How the words of the law are commanded to be written, when this was not done.

**17.** (11:20) What does it mean that Moses commanded, in commending the words of the Lord, and said: *you shall write them upon the thresholds of your houses and of your gates*[^65]: when no one of the Israelites is mentioned, or read, to have done this in the proper sense; since no one can, unless perhaps he divides them among many parts of his house? Or is it a hyperbolic commendation, as many things are said?[^66]

## Concerning the tithes of all fruits and the firstlings of the cattle.

**18.** (12:11) It must be asked how he bids that the tithes of all fruits and the firstlings of the cattle not be eaten except in the city where the Temple will be, when he commanded in the Law that these be given to the Levites.

## How God is said to test, and so on.

**19.** (13:1-3) *But if there arises among you a prophet, or a dreamer dreaming dreams, and gives you a sign or wonder, and the sign or wonder comes to pass which he spoke to you, saying: Let us go, and serve other gods, whom you do not know: you shall not hear the words of that prophet, or of that dreamer of a dream; because the Lord your God is testing you, to know whether you love the Lord your God with all your heart and with all your soul.*[^67] Some Latin translators did not put it thus, *to know whether you love*; but, *that he may know whether you love.* Although the sense seems the same, yet what is expressed as *to know* is more easily referred to those people; so that we take *he is testing you to know* as if it were said, "by testing he makes you know."

Here he certainly meant it to be understood that even those things which are spoken by diviners not according to God, if the things spoken come to pass, are not so to be accepted that the things commanded by them should be done, or the things worshiped by them should be worshiped. Nor does God show that these things happen outside his own power: but, as though it were asked why he permits them, he set forth the reason for the testing—namely, to make known their love, whether they have it toward their God; to be made known, however, to themselves rather than to him who knows all things before they come to pass.

## How the things said about tithes ought to be understood, and how the remission of years takes place.

**20.** (14:28-29; 15:1) *After three years you shall bring forth all the tithe of your fruits; in that year you shall lay it up in your cities, and the Levite shall come, because he has no portion or lot with you; and the sojourner and the orphan and the widow who are in your cities; and they shall eat, and be filled; that the Lord your God may bless you in all the works that you do.*[^68] He did not say that one should eat of this tithe with his own household; and by this he commanded that it be expended on the Levites and the sojourners and the orphans and the widows. But it is set down obscurely, because this tithe is not distinguished from that which he willed to be eaten with the Levites in that place which the Lord had chosen for his Temple.[^69]

But in that translation which is from the Hebrew we find this more plainly distinguished. For it says: *in the third year you shall separate another tithe out of all the things that grow for you at that time, and you shall store it within your gates; and the Levite shall come, who has no other portion or possession with you, and the stranger and the orphan and the widow who are within your gates, and they shall eat, and be filled; that the Lord your God may bless you in all the works of your hands that you do.*

First, this very thing is plainer, that it says: *in the third year*; for it is understood "with one year intervening": whereas in the *Septuagint,* since it said *after three years,* it is uncertain whether he meant those years to be in between, so that it would be done every fifth year.

Next, when it says: *and you shall separate another tithe,* it shows well enough that the one which he willed the offerer himself to eat with his own household and with the Levites in the place which the Lord had chosen is a separate thing. For he commanded the offerer to lay up this other tithe within his own gates, not to carry it to that place where the Lord willed himself to be invoked. *And the Levite,* it says, *who has no portion or possession with you, and the stranger and the orphan and the widow who are within your gates, shall come, and shall eat.* Hence it is certainly plainly true that God did not will this tithe to be shared in common by the offerer and by those on whom it is to be expended; but he commanded it to be paid out to those alone who had nothing else, among whom he set the Levite first.

*After seven years you shall make a remission.* Here it is certainly made plain how he also said above: *after three years.* For he did not will these seven years either to be in between: he commanded it to be done in each single year, as a Sabbath of years.

## How a hidden word is finely described.

**21.** (15:9) *Take heed to yourself, lest there be a hidden word in your heart, an iniquity, saying: The seventh year, the year of remission, draws near; and your eye be grudging toward your needy brother, and you give him nothing: and he cry out against you to the Lord, and there be in you a great sin.*[^70] He finely called this a hidden word; for no one who could have thought it dares to say this: that a loan ought not therefore to be given to the needy because the year of remission draws near, when God for the sake of mercy commanded both—that something be lent when anyone is in need, and that it be remitted in the year of remission. How, then, will a man mercifully remit in that year in which there is to be remission, if he cruelly thinks that nothing ought to be given at the time when it ought to be given?

## How the freeing of one's own is to be understood.

**22.** (15:12) *But if your brother, a Hebrew man or a Hebrew woman, is sold to you, he shall serve you six years, and in the seventh you shall send him away free from you.*[^71] He did not will these purchased persons to be released in the year of remission, which every seventh year had to be observed by all; but in the seventh year of his purchase, in whatever year of those this seventh year fell.

## What firstlings he commands to be offered.

**23.** (15:19) *Every firstling that is born among your oxen and among your sheep, the males you shall sanctify to the Lord your God.*[^72] It must be asked whether the things that are called in Greek πρωτότοκα, and could not be called in Latin except *firstborn* (primogenita), are to be understood only of those which are born of mothers: for these, properly speaking, are brought forth (parere) rather than begotten. For "to bring forth" is τίκτειν, which is from the female, whence πρωτότοκον is said: but "to beget" is γεννᾶν, whence properly in Latin *primogenitus* (firstbegotten) is said. From the females, however, were given the firstlings, that is, those first brought forth, not those first begotten by males, in case any happened to be begotten by males upon widows who had already given birth. For otherwise they would not be such as opened the womb, which the Law willed to be the property of those things first born that were owed to the Lord.

If, then, there is in these words a sure distinction, it is not in vain that the Lord is not called by the Father μονότοκος, but μονογενής, that is, only-begotten, which means unique: but he is called *firstborn from the dead*[^73] in Latin indeed, because a Latin word could not be so composed according to the custom of speech; in Greek, however, he is called πρωτότοκος, not πρωτογενής: as though the Father had begotten one equal to himself, but the creation had brought forth. For what is said, *the firstborn of all creation*[^74]—which there too is read in Greek as πρωτότοκος—can be understood thus according to the new creation, of which the Apostle says: *if, then, anyone is in Christ, a new creation*[^75]: of which he is the firstling, because he first rose in such a way that he now dies no more, *nor does death have dominion over him any longer*[^76]; which is promised to come to pass in the end for the new creation that is in him.

But this distinction is not to be rashly affirmed, but to be more carefully searched out in the Scriptures. For it is troubling how it could be said in *Proverbs*: *firstborn, I say to you, my son*[^77]; that is, in whose person it is to be understood as said. For if it is said in the person of God the Father to Christ (and whether what follows is consonant with this view can scarcely be asserted), it calls the same one *firstborn* whom it calls only-begotten: *firstborn,* because we too are sons of God[^78]; but only-begotten, because he alone is of the substance of the Father and equal to the Father and coeternal with him. But it is doubtful whether Sacred Scripture distinguishes between "to bring forth" and "to beget" by the most evident proofs.

## How oxen are commanded to be offered.

**24.** (16:2) *And you shall sacrifice the Passover to the Lord your God, sheep and oxen.*[^79] What does this mean, that he added *oxen*; when he commended the sacrifice of the Passover from a sheep only, which he commanded to be taken from the sheep and the kids or from the goats? This is taken mystically on account of Christ, whose carnal origin is from the just and from sinners. For he did not say: "from the sheep or the goats," although strictly "a sheep from the goats" cannot be understood: but lest perhaps the Jews should say that a goat was to be supplied if it had been said "or from the goats," it was said, *from the sheep and goats.*[^80] What, then, do the oxen mean here? Is it on account of other sacrifices which are to be offered on those same days of unleavened bread?

## How another interval is enjoined.

**25.** (16:9-11) It must be asked how he commanded the observance of what he says: *seven full weeks you shall count for yourself, from when you begin to put the sickle to the harvest: you shall begin to count seven weeks, and you shall keep a feast of weeks to the Lord your God, as much as your hand is able, whatever the Lord has given you, according as the Lord your God blesses you; and you shall feast before the Lord your God.*[^81] For if this Pentecost was commanded to be observed by the whole people, are we to believe that all were commanded to put the sickle to the harvest on one day? But if each one observes this fiftieth day for himself, counting from that day on which he puts the sickle in, it is not one and the same for the whole people: whereas that one is one and the same which is reckoned from the sacrifice of the Passover up to the day on which the Law was given on Sinai.

## If a king is commanded to be appointed from the tribes, why it afterward displeased that Saul was chosen king.

**26.** (17:14-15) *But if you enter into the land which the Lord your God gives you as your lot, and inherit it, and dwell in it, and say: I will set leaders over me, like the other nations also that are around me; in appointing you shall appoint over yourself a leader, whom the Lord your God shall choose: from your brethren you shall appoint a leader over yourself; you cannot appoint over yourself a foreign man, because he is not your brother.*[^82] It can be asked why the people displeased God when they desired a king,[^83] since here it is found to be permitted. But rather from this it is to be understood that it was rightly not according to the will of God, because he did not command this to be done, but permitted it to those who desired it. Nevertheless he commanded that the king should not be a foreigner, but a brother, that is, a native of the same people, not a foreigner. As for what it says, *you cannot,* it is to be understood, "you ought not."

## How the king is forbidden to multiply wives.

**27.** (17:17) Speaking of the king, he said: *he shall not multiply wives for himself, lest his heart turn away: and silver and gold he shall not greatly multiply for himself.*[^84] Hence it is asked whether David did not act against this commandment: for he did not have one wife.[^85] For concerning Solomon it is plain that he transgressed this commandment, both in women, and in gold, and in silver. But from this it is rather understood that it was permitted to kings to have more than one: for they were forbidden to multiply: which prohibition involves no transgression if they were few, as David's were; but not many, as Solomon's were. Although, when he added: *lest his heart turn away,* he seems rather to have commanded this, lest by multiplying he should come to foreign women: through whom it came to pass in Solomon that his heart turned away from God.[^86] Yet the multiplying is forbidden generally, so that even if he had multiplied them from Hebrews alone, he could rightly be charged with having acted against this commandment.

## What the portion is on account of the sale.

**28.** (18:6-8) *But if a Levite comes from one of your cities, from all the children of Israel, where he dwells, according as his soul desires, to the place which the Lord shall choose,* that is, if he desires to go to the place where the Lord is invoked; *and he ministers to the name of the Lord his God, like all his brethren the Levites who stand there before the Lord: he shall eat an allotted portion, besides the sale which is according to the family.*[^87]

What he calls the *sale* is obscure: unless perhaps because he commanded that tithes and firstlings be sold by those who dwelt far off, lest they be compelled to carry many things to the place of the Lord's invocation, or to lead cattle, so that there they might be bought back from them again at the same price; and he had commanded that the Levite who remained in that city, from which those tithes and firstlings were owed, should have a portion there. And therefore he said that this was owed to the Levite *according to the family,* because, by the succession in which he succeeds his forebears, what was rendered to his forebears ought to be observed in his regard.

## How inspectors of portents are forbidden, when the Church inspects portents.

**29.** (18:10-11) Since he forbids there to be inspectors of portents among the people of God, it must be asked how these portents which are forbidden to be inspected are to be distinguished from those which are given by God in such a way that it ought to be said what they signify: such as all the miracles which are found in the Scriptures signifying what pertains to the rule of faith; as when we say what the fleece signified, dry on the rain-soaked threshing floor, or rain-soaked on the dry floor[^88]; or Aaron's rod which blossomed[^89] and brought forth nuts; and other things of this kind. As the divinations which he forbids next are distinguished from the predictions or announcements of the Prophets, so those inspections of prodigies are to be distinguished from the significations of divine miracles.

## How we are commanded to act together with God.

**30.** (20:4) *For the Lord your God, who goes before with you, will fight together with you against your enemies, and will save you.*[^90] Behold how, even in spiritual conflicts, the help of God is to be hoped for and asked—not that we should do nothing, but that, being helped, we should work together. For it says thus: *he will fight with you,* so as to show that they too would do what was to be done.

## How a man for whom a house has not been dedicated is bidden to return home, lest he die in war, and so on.

**31.** (20:5-7) *And the scribes shall speak to the people, saying: Who is the man who has built a new house, and has not dedicated it? let him go, and return to his house, lest he die in war, and another man dedicate it. And who is the man who has planted a vineyard, and has not feasted from it? let him go, and return to his house, lest he die in war, and another man feast from it. And who is the man who has betrothed a wife, and has not taken her? let him go, and return to his house, lest he die in war, and another man take her.*[^91]

These things can be troubling, as though those who have already dedicated their buildings, and have already feasted from the new vineyards, and have already taken their betrothed, die in war in a better condition than those who have not yet done so. But since human feeling is held by these things, and they are highly esteemed by men, it is to be understood that these things are said to those going out to war for this reason: that whoever is held in mind by these things may show it when he returns, lest on this account he act less bravely, while he fears lest he die before he has dedicated his house, or drunk from his new vineyard, or taken his betrothed. For surely, as far as concerns the woman, it is better that she marry another untouched than as a widow: but these things, as I said, were instituted for testing the minds of the men.

## What implements are forbidden upon a woman.

**32.** (22:5) *The implements of a man shall not be upon a woman*[^92]: he means implements of war to be understood, that is, weapons. For some too have interpreted it thus.

## How a greater penalty is indicated for the woman than for the man, if each has failed.

**33.** (22:13-21) *But if someone takes a wife, and dwells with her, and hates her, and lays against her words of accusation, and brings upon her an evil name, and says: I took this woman, and going in to her I did not find her tokens of virginity: then the father of the girl, and the mother, taking them, shall bring forth the girl's tokens of virginity to the elders at the gate, and the father of the girl shall say to the elders: I gave this my daughter to this man as a wife, and, hating her, he now lays against her words of accusation, saying: I did not find your daughter's tokens of virginity: and behold, my daughter's tokens of virginity. And they shall spread out the garment before the elders of that city. And the elders of the city shall take that man, and chastise him, and fine him a hundred shekels, and give them to the father of the young woman; because he brought an evil name upon a virgin of Israel: and she shall be his wife; he cannot put her away for all time. But if this word is true, and the girl's tokens of virginity are not found, then they shall bring forth the young woman to the door of her father's house, and the men of her city shall stone her with stones, and she shall die; because she committed shamelessness among the children of Israel, in prostituting her father's house: and you shall remove the evil from among yourselves.*[^93]

It appears well enough from this how the Law willed wives to be subject to their husbands, and almost their servants: for the man, in giving testimony against his wife on the strength of which she would be stoned if it were shown to be true, is not himself stoned in turn if it is established to be false; but is only chastised, and fined, and bidden to cleave perpetually to her whom he had wished to be rid of. But in other cases, the one who has harmed someone by false testimony, whereby, if it were proved, he had bidden that person to be killed, he bids to be punished with the same penalty with which the other, if it were true, would have had to be punished.

## How a woman who has been violated is bidden to be retained.

**34.** (22:28-29, 19) *But if someone finds a virgin girl who is not betrothed, and, forcing her, lies with her, and is found out; the man who lay with her shall give to the girl's father fifty didrachmas of silver, and she shall be his wife; because he humbled her: he cannot put her away for all time.*[^94]

It is rightly asked whether this is a penalty, that he cannot for all time put away her whom he violated in a disorderly and unlawful way. For if we should wish to understand on this account that he cannot, that is, ought not, put her away for all time, because she has been made his wife, there occurs that which Moses permitted—to give a bill of divorce and to put her away.[^95] But in the case of those who unlawfully corrupt a woman, he did not will it to be permitted, lest he should seem to have acted in mockery, and rather to have pretended that he took her as a wife than to have truly and willingly taken her. This was commanded also concerning the woman against whom her husband brought a false charge about tokens of virginity not found.

## If Moabites are forbidden to enter the Church of the Lord forever, how Ruth entered.

**35.** (23:3-4) *An Ammonite and a Moabite shall not enter the Church of the Lord, and up to the tenth generation he shall not enter the Church of the Lord, and forever.*[^96]

The question is how Ruth entered, who was a Moabite,[^97] from whom even the flesh of the Lord draws its origin.[^98] Unless perhaps it prophesied mystically that she would enter, in what it says, *up to the tenth generation.* For the generations are reckoned from Abraham, when there was also Lot, who begot the Moabites and Ammonites by his daughters[^99]; and they are found, with Abraham himself, to complete ten generations up to Salmon, who begot Boaz, who was the second husband of Ruth. For these are they: Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, Judah, Perez, Hezron, Ram, Amminadab, Nahshon, Salmon: for Salmon begot Boaz[^100], who married the widow Ruth; and by this it seems that after the tenth generation she produced offspring in the Church of the Lord by bearing sons to Boaz himself.

But why it was added: *and forever,* is still rightly asked. Is it because afterward no soul from among the Ammonites and Moabites entered into that Church of the Hebrew people, after the prophecy was fulfilled in that tenth generation?

Or rather was it said thus: *and up to the tenth generation,* so that he meant it to be understood as always, through a kind of totality of the number ten, so that he expounded this in a manner by adding and saying: *and forever*? But if this is so, Ruth seems to have been admitted against the forbidding precept.

Or did he forbid Ammonite men to be admitted, not Ammonite women, that is, men from there, not women? especially because, when the Israelites had overthrown that very nation, they were commanded to kill all the males, but not the females, except those who had known intercourse with a man[^101]; since these had seduced the people into fornication: but they willed the virgins to be kept safe, not imputing to them the fault by which that nation deserved to be overthrown;

which fault he also mentioned here, as though it were asked why he was unwilling for the Moabites and Ammonites to be admitted into the Church of the Lord: for he added: *because they did not meet you with bread and water on the way, when you were going out of Egypt, and because they hired against you Balaam the son of Beor from Mesopotamia, to curse you.* These faults they did not impute, even then when that nation was subdued, to the women, whom they chose rather to preserve safe.

## How he forbids a fugitive slave to be restored.

**36.** (23:15-16) *You shall not hand over a servant to his master, who has been put alongside you by his master*[^102]: not that his master put him there, that is, entrusted him; for then it would rather say "deposited"; but it said *put alongside by his master,* that is, joined to this person when he had departed from that one: so it forbade not that fugitives be received, but rather that they be given back. This indeed could be thought, unless we understand these things to be said to a nation and a people, not to a single man. And so it forbade a man fleeing from another nation to this nation to which he was speaking to be given back to his master, that is, to his king: which even a foreigner observed, Achish king of Gath, when David fled to him from the face of his master, that is, of King Saul.[^103] But he explains this most plainly, when he says of the very fugitive: *he shall dwell among you, in every place where it pleases him.*

## How he prohibits all fornication.

**37.** (23:17) *There shall be no harlot among the daughters of Israel, and there shall be no fornicator among the sons of Israel.*[^104] Behold where he plainly forbade both men and women to fornicate even when not with the spouses of others; showing it to be a sin to be joined to those who are not one's own spouses, when he forbids both that there be harlots and that men go in to harlots, whose shamefulness is publicly for sale. But in the Decalogue he does not seem to have plainly forbidden this under the name of adultery (moechia), since adultery is usually understood to be nothing but adultery proper. Hence what seemed good to us we treated there.[^105]

## How he forbids the hire of a harlot and the price of a dog to be admitted into the house of the Lord, and what a beast of burden is.

**38.** (23:18, 17) *You shall not offer the hire of a harlot, nor the exchange-price of a dog, into the house of the Lord your God for any vow, because both are an abomination to the Lord your God*[^106]:

which is understood thus, that it is an abomination to the Lord your God—and this not one of these two, but both. For he forbids the exchange-price of firstlings to be made from a dog, which he bids to be made from other unclean animals, that is, horses and asses, and any of those things which help man, and which in Latin are called *iumenta* (beasts of burden) from helping (iuvare). But of the dog he was unwilling; whether also of the pig, and why he was unwilling, must be inquired: and if he was unwilling concerning all such animals, why he made only the dog an exception in this place.

But that he should speak of the hire of a harlot, the reason seems to be this: that above he had forbidden there to be a harlot among the daughters of Israel, or any of the sons of Israel to make use of a harlot; and lest it creep into anyone's thought that this sin could be expiated if something from it were offered in the Temple, it had to be said that it is an abomination to the Lord.

## Concerning the words: And you shall remove the evil one from among you.

**39.** (24:7) *That thief shall die,* that is, he who has stolen a man; *and you shall remove the evil one from among yourselves.*[^107] Scripture says this constantly,[^108] when it bids the wicked to be killed: a manner of speaking which the Apostle also used, when he said: *for what is it to me to judge those who are outside? Do you not judge those who are inside? Remove the evil one from among yourselves.*[^109] For the Greek has τὸν πονηρόν, which is also written here: and this is usually rendered "the evil one" rather than "the evil thing." Nor does it say τὸ πονηρόν, that is, this "evil thing"; but τὸν πονηρόν, that is, this "evil one." From which it appears that he meant to be understood the one who has committed some such thing as to be worthy of excommunication. For excommunication now does in the Church what killing did then. Although that apostolic saying can also be understood otherwise, so that each one is bidden to remove the evil or the evil one from himself. Which sense would be more acceptable, if "this evil" or "this evil thing," and not "this evil one," were found in the Greek: but as it is, it is more credible that it was said of a man than of a vice. Although it could elegantly be understood that a man too removes from himself an evil man, just as it is said: *put off the old man*: which, expounding, he says: *let him who stole steal no more.*[^110]

## What is implied when a priest is called a Levite.

**40.** (24:8) *According to all the law which the Levitical priests shall swear to you.*[^111] Here it appears that every priest was a Levite; although not every Levite was a priest.

## How the one taking a pledge is forbidden to enter the house of him who gives the pledge, and how the pledge must be restored.

**41.** (24:10-13) *If there is a debt owed by your neighbor, any debt whatever, you shall not enter his house to take a pledge: you shall stand outside, and the man with whom your debt is shall bring out the pledge to you. But if the man is needy, you shall not sleep on his pledge; in returning you shall return to him his garment about the setting of the sun; and he shall sleep in his garment, and shall bless you, and there shall be to you mercy before the Lord your God.*[^112]

Not without reason does it seem to pertain to a work of mercy that the one taking the pledge should not enter the house, lest disturbance be caused to the debtor: but by this he also admonishes the debtor himself to bring out the pledge to the creditor. As for his bidding that the pledge be restored to the needy man on the same day, so that he may sleep in it who has nowhere to sleep, it rightly prompts us to ask why he did not rather command the creditor not to take away a pledge which he must restore on the same day.

But if he willed it to be done in order to compel the debtor, how does he urge himself to pay, when he knows he will get the pledge back on the same day? Or did he perhaps will this to be done for the sake of memory, lest he forget to pay, and then not pay if he truly had nothing—especially because he is overcome by the mercy of his creditor, to whom he ought not to be ungrateful, if he has received back a pledge in which to sleep; and at the same time the creditor too, when the man has not paid, ought to believe that he has nothing, who needs even this mercy, that the pledge be restored to him for the reason that he has nowhere else to sleep?

## Concerning the words: The fathers shall not die for the sons, and so on.

**42.** (24:16) *The fathers shall not die for the sons, and the sons shall not die for the fathers: each one shall die in his own sin.*[^113] Behold, not only the Prophets said this,[^114] but the Law too says that each is to be put to death by his own fault, not by that of his father or his son. What, then, is it that is said elsewhere: *God repaying the sins of the fathers upon the sons, to the third and fourth generation*? Is that understood of sons not yet born, on account of original sin, which the human race has drawn even from Adam; whereas here a distinction has been made about sons already born, that each one may die in his own sin? For he who was already born when his father sinned draws nothing from his father. But since there too it says: *to those who hate me*[^115]; it is plain that even that condition can be changed, if the sons have not imitated the deeds of their parents. For that punishment from Adam too is repaid temporally, because all die on this account; but not eternally for those who have been spiritually reborn through grace, and have remained in it to the end.

Although it can also rightly be asked, if *the sins of the fathers are repaid upon the sons* to those who hate God, why to the third and fourth generation, either passing over the first and second, or not also to the rest, when impiety and the imitation of evil parents continues. Or did he, by this number, since seven is understood, mean to signify totality: and therefore did not rather set down the number seven itself, so as to say "to the seventh generation," and thus have it understood as all; because in this way he rather intimated the reason for the perfection of this number? For it is held to be perfect because it consists of these two, namely, three, the first whole odd number, and four, the first whole even number. Hence I think that prophetic saying too which is constantly repeated: *for three and four impieties I will not turn away*[^116]; by which he meant all impieties to be understood rather than three or four.

## Concerning the words: You shall not turn aside the judgment of the sojourner . . .

**43.** (24:17) *You shall not turn aside the judgment of the sojourner, and of the orphan, and of the widow. You shall not take in pledge the garment of the widow.*[^117] Why does it not say: "and you shall not take in pledge their garment"? For what is the reason that he forbade the judgment of these three to be turned aside, but that the garment be taken in pledge of the widow alone, and not of those others too?

Unless because he commended the judgments of all to be done for this reason, that they have none by whom they may be defended—whether the sojourner, because he is in a foreign land; or the orphan, that is, the fatherless, because he lacks parents; or the widow, because she has no husband: but when he forbids the garment of a widow to be taken in pledge, I think that he admonishes elegantly enough that those are truly to be called widows who are also poor. For the Apostle too plainly shows this, where he says: *but if any widow has children or grandchildren, let her learn first to treat her own household piously, and to make a return in turn to her parents: for this is acceptable before the Lord. But she who is truly a widow, and desolate, has hoped in the Lord, and persists in prayers night and day.*[^118]

He called her truly a widow who has no one by whom she may be sustained; because she is destitute not only of a husband, but also of descendants and of all means: for he would not call a rich woman desolate. And so the garment is not to be taken in pledge from a poor woman: for by this very thing, that he forbids the garment to be taken away, he shows her to be poor. For surely the creditor would rather take away silver, or anything else, than a garment. Furthermore, if it should occur to the mind: "what if she has many garments, not necessary but superfluous?" how is she understood to be truly a widow, that is, not only desolate, but also one who does not live in luxury? Such a one he set down, saying: *but she who lives in luxury is dead while living.*[^119] For this one he set in opposition, as it were contrary to the true widow, as though such a one is not a true widow. But whatever rich women have been unwilling to try other marriages, in them continence is praised, not desolation commended. For these are widowed of husbands only, not of other things.

## What things he commands to be left behind, and for whom.

**44.** (24:19) As for his admonition that in the harvest no one gather up a forgotten sheaf, and that no one go back to gather more carefully the olive or the grape left behind that was neglected, and his saying that these are to be left for the needy; perhaps it occurs to the mind: "what if these things which are left by the owners of the fields are gathered not by the needy, but by the dishonest?" But it must be considered, first, that he acts with mercy who leaves them with this intention, that the needy may have what is left. Next, when these things are commanded to the people, those who are not in need are at the same time admonished not to seek these things.

But if they should seek them, what else are they to be judged but invaders of another's property, and—what is graver—of the property of the poor? Both, therefore, are addressed by these precepts: both those whose fields they are, that they leave these things mercifully; and those who are not in need, that they abstain from them: since both are spoken of, both by whom things are to be left, and for whom they are to be left.

## What judges are to be understood to judge.

**45.** (25:1-3) *But if there is a dispute between men, and they come to judgment, and they judge, and they justify the just one.* The judges are to be understood to judge, not those who were said to have the dispute. Then it follows: *and they condemn the impious one. And it shall be, if the one who acts impiously is worthy of stripes, you shall set him before the judges, and they shall scourge him in their presence, according to his impiety: they shall scourge him to the number of forty; they shall not add. But if they add to scourge him more stripes beyond these, your brother will be disfigured before you.*[^120]

It must be greatly noted. Since he commanded those sins to be corrected by stripes which are not worthy to be punished by the penalty of death, and that by so few stripes; yet the one who is beaten he called impious, or one acting impiously: so that we may know that the Scriptures did not speak as most people speak; for we read them too carelessly when we think that adultery is not impiety, because the one who has done this seems to have sinned against a man, although the Law bids that sin to be punished with death[^121]; and we say that impieties are graver than these sins, when there are some of them which are punished with forty stripes of the scourge. There is, then, a light impiety, which is worthy of a beating; and there is a graver one which is worthy of death: so too those sins which seem to be committed not against God, but against man, are some worthy of death; others worthy of reproof, or a beating, or an easier pardon. For it is established that the Seventy translators so spoke as to call even the impiety of one who is worthy of stripes.

## How a brother is to marry the wife of his deceased brother.

**46.** 1. (25:5-6) *But if brothers dwell together, and one of them dies, and he has no offspring; the wife of the deceased shall not be outside to a man not akin: the brother of her husband shall go in to her, and shall take her to himself as a wife, and shall dwell with her. And it shall be, whatever child is born shall be reckoned by the name of the deceased, and his name shall not be blotted out from Israel.*[^122]

The Law seems to have commanded this about marrying a brother's wife for no other reason than to raise up offspring for the brother who has died without children. As for what it says, *shall be reckoned by the name of the deceased, and his name shall not be blotted out from Israel,* that is, the name of the deceased; from this it seems to be said that the one who is born should be called by that name by which the deceased was called, to whom he is in a manner propagated.

Hence it seemed better to us to resolve, according to the custom of adoption, the question in the Gospel about the two fathers of Joseph[^123]—one of whom Matthew mentions, namely the one who begot Joseph[^124]; the other Luke, the one whose son Joseph was[^125]: since Joseph did not take the name of either of them.

Unless perhaps this was said: *shall be reckoned by the name of the deceased,* not that he takes his name, but that by his name, that is, as a son—not of him by whose seed he was begotten, but of that deceased man for whom the offspring was raised up—he is appointed heir. For what is added, *and his name shall not be blotted out from Israel,* can be understood thus: not that the boy consequently takes his name; but that the deceased will be seen not to have died without posterity, and therefore his name, that is, his memory, remains.

For even if he had himself begotten a son, he would not have imposed his own name upon him, so that his name should not be blotted out from Israel: but it would not be blotted out for this reason, that he would not depart from this life without children; and his brother is bidden to fulfill by his wife what he could not.

For even if there was no brother, a kinsman too married the wife of him who had died without children, in order to raise up offspring for his kinsman: as Boaz did in marrying Ruth, to raise up offspring for his kinsman, whose wife she had been and by whom she had not given birth; and yet the one who was born of her was reckoned by the name of the deceased, who was called his son, and so it came to pass that the name of the deceased was not blotted out from Israel, nor yet was he called by his name.[^126]

## The question of the two fathers of Joseph.

**46.** 2. Since these things are so, the Gospel question can be resolved more fully in two ways: so that one of those whom Matthew and Luke mention as different[^127] was so kinsman to the other in order to marry his wife, that this one could have had still other parents and ancestors upward, and that one others. For if they had been sons of brothers, they would have had one grandfather; which is not so: for according to Matthew, Matthan is the grandfather of Joseph; according to Luke, however, not Matthan, but Matthat.

But if anyone thinks there is only a similarity of name, so that the writers erred in one letter, making so small and almost no difference; what will he say of their fathers? For according to Luke, Matthat was the son of Levi; but according to Matthew, Matthan is found to have been begotten of Eleazar: and so from there upward the fathers and grandfathers are different; and then the ancestors up to Zerubbabel, who is about the twentieth upward from Joseph in Luke, but in Matthew the eleventh. He is believed to be the same person for this reason, that his father is found in both evangelists to be Shealtiel: although it could have come about that there was another of the same name, having a father of the same name as the one that the other's father had.

For from there upward too they are different: for Zerubbabel has one grandfather in Luke, who is Neri; another in Matthew, who is Jeconiah; and from there upward there is nowhere agreement, until one comes to David, in Matthew through Solomon, in Luke through Nathan. But it seems most difficult that there was no one more closely akin to marry his brother's wife than one who was a blood relation from David at so distant a degree, not joined by any nearer kinship; since in Luke David is almost the fortieth from Joseph, but in Matthew about the twenty-seventh.

Or if those too were sought as kinsmen to marry the wives of the deceased who were akin through the blood of the women, it could have come about that there was someone so akin who begot Joseph by the wife of his kinsman, who died without children: and so he had one father by nature, another by the Law; in whose fathers and grandfathers and then ancestors no kinship would appear, because they were akin not through males, but through females. Nevertheless, if this were so, not even David would at some point occur as one father.

Or if anyone contends that it could be so, where do we put it that it is not the custom of Scripture to set down women in a genealogy in place of males, just as no Evangelist inserted them? For where mothers are mentioned, they are not set down except together with fathers. And by this, either a nearer kinsman was lacking to be joined to the wife of the deceased, so that the origin of kinship had to be traced back to David: or adoption made the other father whom Joseph could have.

## One who, in giving tithes, fulfilled all the commandments.

**47.** (26:14) What does it mean that, among those things which he bids a man say who in giving tithes, and whatever he was bidden to give or expend, fulfilled all the commandments, he is bidden to say this too, with his own praise and commendation: *I have not given any of them to the dead*[^128]? Does he by this forbid the rites for the dead which the nations are accustomed to observe?

## How going to the right can be understood.

**48.** 1. (28:13-14) *You shall not pass beyond any of the words which I command you today, to go to the right or to the left after foreign gods, to serve them.*[^129] It can be asked how going to the right can be understood of one who has gone after foreign gods to serve them; since the right is set down for praise, but that can never be done in a praiseworthy way: for even what is reproved in the way of life, when one turns aside to the right, the things that are on the right are not there reproved, but the one who turns aside to them, that is, who arrogates to himself the things that are God's.

Therefore it is said in *Proverbs*: *do not turn aside to the right or to the left: for the ways which are on the right the Lord knows; but those which are on the left are perverse.*[^130] So the things on the right, which the Lord knows, are good. For *the Lord knows the ways of the just*[^131], as is read in the *Psalm.* Why, then, it was said: *do not turn aside to the right,* it shows next by adding: *for he will make your courses straight.* But far be it that we should deny the things on the right, which the Lord knows, to be straight: but, as I said, to turn aside to them is to wish to attribute to oneself, and not to his grace, what is right. Finally, as I said, it adds and says: *for he will make your courses straight, and will bring forth all your journeys in peace.*[^132]

**48.** 2. Wherefore, what was said in this place of *Deuteronomy*, with which we are dealing—*You shall not pass beyond any of the words which I command you today, to go to the right or to the left after foreign gods, to serve them*—was not said because foreign gods can also be taken to be among the things on the right; but either earthly places were signified, because on the right and on the left there were nations worshiping other gods: or this is to be pronounced separately concerning foreign gods, so that there are two senses; one, namely: *you shall not pass beyond any of the words which I command you today, to the right or to the left,* according to that understanding which I expounded above; but let there be another sense, *to go after foreign gods, to serve them,* so that here too be understood: *you shall not pass beyond any of the words which I command you today.*

And if we wish to state this whole sense, we shall have to repeat the earlier words, which are common to both senses; so that, as there it is said: *you shall not pass beyond any of the things which I command you today, to the right or to the left,* so here too be repeated: *you shall not pass beyond any of the things which I command you today, to go after foreign gods, to serve them.* For by passing beyond the words that were commanded, this too comes about, that one goes after foreign gods. For not this alone was commanded, nor this alone does God not wish to be passed beyond, which he commanded, that one not go after foreign gods, but all the things which he commanded: this, however, in such a way that, after the generality of the precept by which he admonished that none of the words of his commandments was to be passed beyond, he willed to commend this too separately.

**48.** 3. What it says, *to the right or to the left,* can also be understood thus, that he commanded one to go after other gods neither for the sake of the things that are sought for the sake of happiness, nor for the sake of the things that are fled for the sake of unhappiness: that is, that help is to be asked of other gods neither for the things that are loved, nor against the things that are hated: or at least in that way, that they be either conciliated, so as to profit; or appeased, lest they harm. For of certain men too it is written in the *Psalm*: *whose mouth has spoken vanity, and their right hand is a right hand of iniquity*: for this reason, that they suppose a man is made blessed by those things which both good and evil men can have; and therefore it is a right hand of iniquity, because they are unjust who think it a right hand. For it is not a true right hand, but it is *the right hand of those whose mouth has spoken vanity. They called the people blessed for whom these things are,* when rather, as he presently adds and teaches, *blessed is the people whose God is the Lord himself.*[^133] This is the true right hand, of equity, not of iniquity.

One must not, then, go after foreign gods, neither to the right, so that a man should think himself made blessed by them; nor to the left, so that, thinking himself made wretched by them when they are hostile, he should worship them in order to avert this. Or at least, if we understand the right as eternal goods, but the left as temporal goods; let it be believed that Holy Scripture in this place admonished that they are not to be worshiped either for the sake of those or for the sake of these.

## Why the book is called Deuteronomy, as it were a second Law.

**49.** (29:1) *These are the words of the covenant which the Lord commanded Moses to establish for the children of Israel in the land of Moab, besides the covenant which he covenanted with them at Horeb.*[^134] It shows why the book is called *Deuteronomy,* as it were a second Law; where it is rather a repetition of it than anything else: for there are few things there which are not in that which was first given. Yet these two are not called covenants, although these words seem to sound so: for both are one covenant, which in the Church is called "old." For if, on account of these words, two covenants had to be spoken of, there would now be not two, but more, apart from the New. For in many places Scripture speaks of a covenant: such as that which was made with Abraham concerning circumcision,[^135] or that earlier one with Noah.[^136]

## The eyes of the body and the eyes of the heart.

**50.** (29:2-4) *And you have seen all the things that the Lord your God did in the land of Egypt before you to Pharaoh, and to all his servants, and to all that land, the great trials which your eyes saw, those great signs and wonders, and the mighty hand. And the Lord God has not given you a heart to know, and eyes to see, and ears to hear, to this day.*[^137] How, then, does it say above: *you have seen the great trials which your eyes saw,* if *the Lord did not give them eyes to see, and ears to hear*; unless because they saw with the body, and did not see with the heart? for there are eyes of the heart too: for this reason he began from there: *and the Lord God has not given you a heart to know.* To this belong the two that follow, *and eyes to see, and ears to hear,* that is, to understand and to obey. As for his saying: *and the Lord God has not given you*; in no way would he say this in rebuking and reproving, unless he meant it to be understood as pertaining also to their fault, lest anyone think himself excusable on this account. For he shows at once both that without the help of the Lord God they cannot understand and obey with the eyes of the heart and the ears of the heart: and yet that, if the help of God is lacking, a man's fault is not on that account excusable; since the judgments of God, though hidden, are nonetheless just.[^138]

## Whether the Israelites brought wine with them, and how much.

**51.** (29:5-6) *And he led you forty years in the desert: your garments did not grow old, and your sandals were not worn from your feet; you ate no bread, you drank no wine or strong drink, that you might know that this is the Lord your God.*[^139] From this it appears that the Israelites could carry only so much wine in their baggage, when they went out of Egypt, as they could quickly consume. For if they had brought absolutely nothing with them, whence would that have come of which it was said: *the people sat down to eat and drink, and rose up to play*[^140]? For this would not be said of water, since the most plain words of Moses himself show that that voice was not the beginning of war, but the beginning of wine.[^141]

## Concerning the words: Is there any man among you . . .

**52.** (29:18, 21) *Is there any man among you, or woman, or family, or tribe, whose mind has turned away from the Lord your God, to go to serve the gods of those nations? Is there in you any root springing up in gall and bitterness? And it shall be, when he hears the words of this curse, and supposes in his heart, saying: Let holy things befall me, because I walk in the error of my heart: so that the sinner does not destroy together with himself the one who is without sin; God will not be willing to be propitious to him, but then the anger of the Lord and his zeal will be kindled against that man, and all the curses of this covenant will cleave to him, which are written in the book of this Law.*[^142]

It was said thus: *is there in you?* so that we understand him as inquiring, lest perhaps there be. But if there were anyone, he terrified him vehemently, lest perhaps anyone should say in his heart, hearing those curses: *let holy things befall me,* that is, let the curses be holy things to me: *because I walk in the error of my heart,* that is, "far be it that these should come upon me; let these not be evils to me, but holy things, that is, propitious and harmless; *because I walk in the error of my heart,* namely, by going after the gods of the nations, and serving them as though with impunity." It will not be so, he says.

*Let not the sinner destroy together with himself the one who is without sin*: as though he said: "take heed that no one who thinks such things persuade any of you of such things."

*God will not be willing to be propitious to him,* whether to the one thinking such things, or to the one to whom such things have been persuaded, as he himself supposed by saying: *let holy things befall me*; and as though in this way turning the force of that curse away from himself.

*But then the anger of the Lord and his zeal will be kindled against that man,* when he thinks he is turning it away by saying these things in his heart.

*And all the curses of this covenant will cleave to him, which are written in the book of this Law.* All of them, indeed, cannot befall one man: for he cannot die as many times as the kinds of death here named; but he said *all* for "any whatever," so that the one upon whom any of those things befall by which he may perish is not free from all of them.

As for what it says, *so that the sinner does not destroy together with himself the one who is without sin*—where the Greek has ἀναμάρτητον—it is not to be taken as though by ἀναμάρτητον, that is, without sin, he meant one altogether clean and free from every sin; but one who was without that sin of which he was speaking: just as the Lord says in the Gospel: *if I had not come, and spoken to them, they would have no sin*[^143]; not every sin, of course, but this sin, by which they did not believe in him. God also says to Abimelech concerning Sarah, the wife of Abraham: *I know that you did this with a clean heart*[^144]: he did not mean his heart to be understood as so clean that he was like those of whom it is said: *blessed are the clean of heart, for they shall see God*[^145]; but he had a heart clean from that sin which was in question, since, as far as concerned him, he had not desired another's spouse.

## How grace is plainly promised.

**53.** (30:6) *And the Lord will purify your heart all around, and the heart of your offspring, to love the Lord your God with all your heart, and with all your soul, that you may live.*[^146] A plain promise of grace: for God promises that he himself will do what he is accustomed to command to be done.

## How the ease of the law is urged.

**54.** (30:11-14) *For this commandment, which I command you today, is not beyond measure, nor is it far from you: it is not in heaven, saying,* that is, that you should say: *who will go up for us into heaven, and take it for us, and, hearing it, we shall do it? Neither is it across the sea, saying,* that is, that you should say: *who will cross over for us across the sea, and take it for us, and, hearing it, we shall do it? This word is very near, in your mouth, and in your heart, and in your hands, to do it.*[^147]

The Apostle says that this is the word of faith,[^148] which belongs to the New Covenant. But it can be asked why he called those things commandments above which are written in the book of this Law[^149]: unless because by all these things are signified spiritual things pertaining to the New Covenant, if they are well understood.

Likewise it can be asked why what is here set down: *neither is it across the sea, that you should say: who will cross over for us across the sea, and take it for us,* the Apostle stated as: *or who will descend into the abyss*; and, expounding it, added: *this is to bring Christ back from the dead*[^150]: unless because he called the whole of life in this world the sea, which is passed through by death, so that in a manner the sea comes to an end, and *across the sea* death itself is called, as it were "across this life," which is signified by the word *sea.*

Then, what is here added, *and in your hands*; the Apostle does not say, except, *in your mouth and in your heart*[^151]. And this he carried out to the end, saying: *for with the heart it is believed unto righteousness, but with the mouth confession is made unto salvation*[^152]. With good reason, what is translated from the Hebrew, as far as we could inspect it, does not have *in your hands.* Yet I think it was not added in vain by the Seventy translators: but because they meant the hands themselves, by which works are signified, to be understood as taken in the heart, where there is *faith which works through love*[^153]. For if outwardly the things which God commands are done with the hands, and are not done in the heart, no one is so foolish as to think the precepts fulfilled. But if love, which is the fullness of the Law,[^154] dwells in the heart, even if someone cannot work with the hands of the body, he certainly has peace with men of good will.[^155]

## Who the blameworthy or stained sons are, and how they are called in Greek.

**55.** (32:5) *The blameworthy sons have sinned not against him.* What in Greek is τέκνα μωμητά, some have rendered as here, that is, *blameworthy sons*; some, *stained sons*; some, *faulty sons.* Hence there is no great question, indeed none. But this rightly prompts inquiry: if it was said generally, *they have sinned not against him,* since he who sins sins not against him, that is, does not harm God, but himself; how is it to be understood what is read in the *Psalm*: *against you only have I sinned*[^156]; and in *Jeremiah*: *we have sinned against you, O patience of Israel, O Lord*[^157]; and again in the *Psalm*: *heal my soul, for I have sinned against you*[^158]: and whether this is "to sin against God," that is, "to sin in God." Hence Eli the priest says: *if he sins against God, who will pray for him?*[^159] I will say, then, for the present, what seems to me now. Perhaps those who relish these things better will understand something better, or even we ourselves at another time, as far as the Lord helps.

"To sin against God" is to sin in those things which pertain to the worship of God. For what I have mentioned indicates nothing else: for so the sons of Eli sinned, to whom their father said this. So it is to be thought that men are sinned against too who belong to God: for God is read to have said this to Abimelech concerning Sarah: *therefore I spared you, lest you sin against me*[^160]. But to sin against the Lord, or rather to have sinned against the Lord (unless perhaps something written somewhere occurs that resists this sense), these seem to me to be rightly said of those who now do pious penance for their sin, that the forgiving Lord may be glorified.

For David, giving the reason why he said: *against you only have I sinned, and done evil before you*; added and said: *that you may be justified in your words, and overcome when you are judged*[^161]:

whether when God says: *judge between me and my vineyard*[^162]:

or it be understood of the Lord Jesus Christ, who alone could most truly say: *for the prince of the world comes, and in me he has nothing* (that is, nothing of sin that is worthy of death); *but that the world may know that I love the Father, and as the Father has given me commandment, so I do; rise, let us go from here*[^163]: as though he said: "even if the prince of the world pursues the lightest sins with the penalty of death," *in me he has nothing,* but *rise, let us go from here,* that is, "that I may suffer; because in what I suffer, I fulfill the will of my Father, I do not pay the penalty of my sin."

And what Jeremiah says: *we have sinned against you, O patience of Israel,* is certainly said to the Lord in supplication, in penance, with hope of salvation through pardon.

And what is said: *heal my soul, for I have sinned against you,* this same thing is done, that God may be glorified by forgiving: because great is his mercy upon those who confess to him and return to him, who says that he does not will the death of the sinner, so much as that he should turn back and live.[^164]

Hence David himself, not only in the psalm, but also when God reproved him through the prophet, answered not without hope of the Lord's propitiation: *I have sinned against the Lord*[^165]. For one wounded seems in a manner to be the physician's, who submits himself to be healed to the hands of the physician, that in him the work of medicine may be accomplished. But in this canticle the prophet foresaw that there would be some who were going to sin against God by offending him with their great iniquities in such a way that they would not even be willing to do penance and return to God to be healed:

of whom it is also said elsewhere: *for they are flesh, a spirit going forth and not returning*[^166]. It can also be understood thus: "they have sinned not against him," according to this, that they did not harm him by their sin, but themselves.

## What significance the blessing of Moses has, by which he blessed Israel.

**56.** (33:1-5) *And this is the blessing with which Moses the man of God blessed the children of Israel, before he died. And he said: The Lord came from Sinai, and dawned upon us from Seir: he hastened from Mount Paran with the many thousands of Kadesh. At his right hand were angels with him, and he spared his people. And all the sanctified are under your hands, and these are under you. And he received the Law from his words, which Moses commanded us, an inheritance for the assemblies of Jacob. And there shall be a prince in the beloved one, when the princes of the peoples are gathered together with the tribes of Israel.*[^167]

This prophecy is not to be passed over carelessly. For this blessing appears to belong to the new people, whom the Lord Christ sanctified; in whose person these things are said by Moses, not in the person of Moses himself, which appears evidently in what follows. For if it was said: *the Lord came from Sinai,* because the Law was given on Mount Sinai; what does that mean which follows, *and dawned upon us from Seir*: since Seir is a mountain of Idumea, where Esau reigned?

Then, since Moses blesses the children of Israel with these words, as Scripture foretold, how does the same say: *and he received the Law from his words, which Moses commanded us*? Surely, then, it is a prophecy, as we said, announcing beforehand the new people sanctified by the grace of Christ—under the name of the children of Israel for this reason, that they are the seed of Abraham, that is, are the children of the promise,[^168] and its interpretation is "seeing God."[^169] The Lord, then, who came from Sinai is to be understood as Christ, since Sinai is interpreted "temptation."[^170] He came, then, from the temptation of his Passion, of the Cross, of death. *And he dawned from Seir. Seir* is interpreted "hairy,"[^171] which signifies a sinner; for so Esau was born,[^172] and was held in hatred[^173]: but because for those who sat in darkness and in the shadow of death, a light has arisen for them,[^174] therefore he dawned from Seir.

At the same time it is also not absurdly understood to have been foretold that from the nations, which are signified by the name Seir (because it is a mountain belonging to Esau), the grace of Christ would come to the people of Israel. Hence the Apostle says: *so these too have now not believed in your mercy, that they too may obtain mercy*[^175]. They themselves, then, say: *he dawned upon us from Seir, and hastened from Mount Paran,* that is, from the fruitful mountain: for that is what *Paran* is interpreted to mean, by which the Church is signified. *With the many thousands of Kadesh.* And *Kadesh* is interpreted both "changed" and "holiness." Many thousands, then, were changed, and sanctified through grace, with whom Christ came to gather the Israelites afterward.

It continues and says: *at his right hand were angels with him*: this needs no exposition.

*And he spared,* it says, *his people*: granting the remission of sins.

Then he turns the speech to him himself and says: *and all the sanctified are under your hands, and these are under you*: not those, of course, who are proud and wish to establish their own *righteousness*; but those who acknowledge grace, so as to be subject to the righteousness of God.[^176]

*And he received,* it says, *the Law from his words*: the people, of course, of whom it says: *and he spared his people.* He received, then, the Law from his words, *which,* it says, *Moses commanded us*: that is, his people received the Law from his words, because from his teaching they understood the Law to be that very one which Moses commanded us. For he himself says in the Gospel: *if you believed Moses, you would believe me also; for he wrote of me*[^177]. For that people did not receive the Law which it did not understand; but it received it then, when it understood from his words, lacking the old veil, turned to the Lord: this he calls *an inheritance for the assemblies of Jacob,* which is to be understood not as earthly, but heavenly; not temporal, but eternal.

*And there shall be,* it says, *a prince in the beloved one*: he himself, of course, the Lord Jesus, will be prince in the beloved people: *when the princes of the peoples are gathered together,* that is, of the nations: *together with the tribes of Israel,* so that what was said above may be fulfilled: *rejoice, you nations, together with his people; because blindness in part has come upon Israel, until the fullness of the nations should enter in, and so all Israel should be saved*[^178].

## Concerning the blessing of Joseph.

**57.** (33:17) When he blessed Joseph, he said among other things: *the firstborn of the bull is his beauty.*[^179] This is not to be read as though he said, *firstborn of the bull*; but: since he is the firstborn, his beauty is that of a bull: on account of the horns of the Cross, it is understood as a figure of the Lord.
