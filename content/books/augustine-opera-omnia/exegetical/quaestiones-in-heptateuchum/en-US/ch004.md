# Book III: Questions on Leviticus

## How Leviticus 5:1 is to be understood.

**1.** (5, 1) *But if a soul sins, and hears the voice of an adjuration, and is himself a witness or has seen or has been privy to it, if he does not report it, he shall bear his sin*: this is: *if he does not report it, he shall bear his sin.* For the added *and* is a usage common in the Scriptures. But since this sense is obscure, it seems it must be expounded. For it seems to say that a man sins in whose hearing someone swears falsely, and he knows that he swears falsely, and is silent. But he knows it, if he was a witness to the matter about which it is sworn, or saw it, or was privy to it; that is, in some way he came to know it, or beheld it with his own eyes, or the one who swears himself disclosed it to him: for so he could be privy to it. But between the fear of this sin, and the fear of betraying men, there often arises no small temptation. For we can recall one ready for perjury, by admonishing or forbidding, from so grave a sin: but if he does not heed, and before us swears falsely about a matter we know, whether he is to be betrayed, if, betrayed, he incurs even the danger of death, is a most difficult question.

But because here he did not express to whom this is to be disclosed—whether to him to whom it is sworn, or to a priest, or to someone who not only cannot persecute him by inflicting punishment, but can even pray for him—it seems to me that a man frees himself even from the bond of sin, if he discloses it to such as can profit the perjurer more than harm him, whether for correcting him, or for appeasing God for him: provided that he himself also applies the medicine of confession.

## No sacrifice is to be offered for perjury.

**2.** (5, 2-6) But after this kind of sin, which he mentioned concerning someone's perjury not disclosed, he ordered no sacrifice to be offered for it; but then he subjoined: *any soul that touches any unclean thing, whether of a carcass, or of an unclean thing caught by a beast, or of those which are carcasses of unclean abominations, the carcass of unclean cattle, or touches the uncleanness of a man, any uncleanness of his, which if he touches he shall be defiled: and it was hidden from him, but after this he came to know it, and offended.*

Nor here did he mention a sacrifice to be offered for this kind of sin, but added further, and said: *any soul that swears, distinguishing with his lips to do ill, or to do well, according to all that a man distinguishes with an oath, and it was hidden from him, and he came to know it, and sinned in one of these, and confessed the sin for which he sinned against it.*

All these things being connected and set forth without any interposition of a sacrifice, he adds, and says: *and he shall offer for what he has offended to the Lord, for the sin by which he sinned, a female from the sheep, a ewe lamb, or a she-goat from the goats, for the sin; and the priest shall entreat for him for the sin, and the sin shall be forgiven him.*

What, then, is it that for someone's undisclosed perjury, and for touching a carcass or something unclean, he mentioned no sacrifice; but for that sin where someone unknowingly swears falsely, he said a ewe lamb or a she-goat is offered? Or is this sacrifice to be understood for all the things said above? For he wished first to enumerate all things, and so to bring in by what sacrifice they can be expiated. But in all those kinds of sins said above, there are certain things set down somewhat obscurely on account of the modes of expression, such as what he says, *the carcass of cattle.*

For those which are called by the Greeks *ktene,* most of our translators rendered *cattle* (*iumenta*); which name in Latin usage is of those animals by whose labors we are helped, especially in carrying loads, as are horses, asses, mules, camels, and any of this kind: but those which the Greeks call *ktene* are understood so broadly that all, or nearly all, herd animals are included under that name. And therefore, by a new kind of expression, as it were a *pleonasm,* there was added in the Greek, *unclean,* when *cattle* was said: since there are also clean herd animals, which are called *ktene.* But those which Latin usage calls *iumenta,* according to the distinction of the Law are only unclean.

## What it is to "distinguish," which Scripture frequently uses.

**3.** (5, 4-6) Likewise, as for what he says: *any soul that swears, distinguishing with his lips to do ill or to do well,* it is asked what he meant by *distinguishing.* For Scripture constantly uses this word. Whence is that too: *I will render my vows, which my lips distinguished*[^1]: likewise it is said to Ezekiel: *when I say to the wicked man: 'you shall die the death,' and you did not distinguish nor speak*[^2]; likewise it is written: *if a woman established in her father's house vows a vow, distinguishing with her lips against her soul*[^3].

This "distinguishing," then, seems to be as it were a defining, by which something is separated from the rest, which are not bound by the mere saying. So, then, let them be taken as if it were said: *a soul that swears, defining with his lips to do ill or to do well, according to all that a man defines with an oath, and it was hidden from him,* that is, not knowing it was to be done or not to be done, swore to do it: *and he came to know it, and sinned in one of these,* whether because he swore before he came to know, or because he did what he swore, and afterward came to know that it was not to be done nor sworn: *and confessed the sin for which he sinned,* that is, the sin which he sinned: for it is a manner of speech.

But what he added: *against it,* what is understood, unless that he confessed against the sin itself, that is, by confessing accused the sin? *And he shall offer for what he has offended to the Lord, for the sin by which he sinned, a female from the sheep, a ewe lamb.* By a customary manner of speech he says *a female ewe lamb,* as though it could be other than female: *or a she-goat from the goats,* like *a ewe lamb from the sheep,* as though there could be either a ewe lamb except from sheep, or a she-goat except from goats.

But there is some question, indeed no small one, what it is that he constantly says: *but after this he came to know it, and offended*: as though the offense then comes about, when it has been known. Or rather because satisfaction cannot be made except for what is known? But he does not say: "but after this he came to know it and repented": what, then, is: *after this he came to know it and offended*? Or, if he offended after the knowing; so that if he knowingly did what ought not to have been done, then a purgation is owed for the offense? But he did not so speak before. For he seems to be treating those sins which are committed by the ignorant, and thereby by the unwilling. So perhaps by some kind of expression *he offended* was said for "he learned that it was an offense."

Or rather was it said by an inverted order (for Scripture has such kinds of expression too) what in other similar places was said in the right order? For when elsewhere it is so often written: *and he offended and it became known to him*; here only, by an inverted order, as I said, *he came to know it* was said first, then *and he offended.* But in its own order, it could be said thus: *any soul that touches any unclean thing, whether of a carcass, or of an unclean thing caught by a beast, or of those which are carcasses of unclean abominations, the carcass of unclean cattle, or touches any uncleanness of a man, any uncleanness of his which if he touches he is defiled, and if it was hidden from him, and he offended, but after this he came to know it*[^4].

## What it is that two turtledoves are prescribed to be offered.

**4.** (5, 7) *But if his hand cannot avail for what is enough for a sheep, he shall offer for his sin which he sinned two turtledoves or two young pigeons to the Lord; one for sin, and one for a holocaust.* Certainly here he opens the question about which, when he spoke above, we were in doubt. For he seems to say, *one for sin, the other for a holocaust,* because a sacrifice for sin was not offered except with a holocaust. Finally, when he first spoke separately of holocausts, he mentioned a turtledove, and did not say two[^5]: but now he says two for this reason, because a sacrifice for sin was not offered without a holocaust. So what he said above: *and he shall place it upon the holocaust*[^6], there is no doubt that first the holocaust, then that other thing was placed over it: but now of the birds he speaks otherwise, so that first one bird is offered for sin, then the other for a holocaust.

## How he put "soul" for "man."

**5.** (5, 15) But what he says: *a soul, if it was hidden from him by forgetfulness*; that is, if it came about by forgetfulness that it was hidden from him, or from her if you refer it to the soul, since by saying *him* he referred it to the man. For here he calls men *souls.*

## What "from the holy things" (according to another reading) is.

**6.** (5, 15-16) *And if he unwillingly sins concerning the holy things of the Lord.* This kind of sin seems to be set down obscurely, but in what follows it is opened, where he says, after the sacrifice from the ram has been offered: *he shall restore it, and shall add a fifth.* For there it is understood that to sin concerning the holy things by forgetfulness is this, to usurp through forgetfulness something that is owed to the holy things, whether to the priests, or to the offerings, or of the firstfruits, or whatever of this kind.

## What the inculcated words mean by which certain kinds of offenses are read as excepted.

**7.** (5, 17-19) *And any soul that sins, and does one of all the precepts of the Lord which ought not to be done, and does not know it, and offends, and bears his sin, and shall bring a ram without blemish from the sheep at the price of silver for the offense, to the priest: and the priest shall entreat for him on account of his ignorance, which he was ignorant of, and he himself did not know, and it shall be forgiven him. For he offended an offense before the Lord.*

Apart from the unusual density of the expressions, which by now, on account of the constant repetition, ought to be most well known, this whole sense is obscure; since it is asked how this kind of offense is distinguished from those which above he embraced in a certain generality. For reason seems to demand this, that to certain kinds of sins certain kinds of sacrifices, by which they are expiated, are to be applied. But what I have just mentioned does not specially express the sin, but seems to be engaged in that generality concerning which, when he spoke before, he established a sacrifice—for the priest a calf, and for the whole congregation likewise a calf, for the ruler a he-goat, and for any soul, that is, any man, a she-goat, or, if he wished, a sheep, yet a female animal[^7].

Then he began to make exceptions of certain kinds of sins, and to express by name for which what is to be offered; as concerning someone's perjury heard and kept silent, and a carcass and an unclean thing touched, and a false oath through ignorance: from the sheep a ewe lamb, or a she-goat from the goats, or a pair of turtledoves, or two young pigeons, or a tenth part of an ephah of fine flour[^8]; but for the one sinning who through forgetfulness usurped something of the holy things, a ram and restitution of the same thing, with a fifth added.

But now, the kind of sin not being expressed, adding generally: *any soul that offends, and does one of all the precepts of the Lord which ought not to be done* (so in that generality he said: *one of the precepts of the Lord which shall not be done), and does not know it, and offends,* that is, sins unwillingly through ignorance; he orders a ram to be offered, not a she-goat or a female from the sheep, as above in such a generality of sins he had established.

What, then, does this mixing mean? Unless perhaps what he here says: *for he offended by an offense an offense before the Lord,* by what he says, *before the Lord,* he wished the offense to be understood in those things which are done before the Lord, that is, by which the Lord is served in the tabernacle: whence he had said something a little before, when he said: *he sinned concerning the holy things*[^9]; and we understood, he usurped something of the holy things, because he had also prescribed restitution.

And by this, one can sin in these matters not only if something of them is usurped through forgetfulness; but also in many other ways one can through ignorance offend in those things which are rendered to the service of the Lord: this kind of offense he wished afterward to mention generally, and therefore both there and here he ordered a ram to be offered. For the Scriptures are full of it, when it is said, *before the Lord,* and nothing is understood except what is rendered to the Lord, such as a sacrifice, or firstfruits, or some service in sacred things.

## Again on the same matter.

**8.** (5, 7) It is also asked whether it is everywhere to be taken that what was said: *if his hand cannot avail for what is enough for a sheep,* he ought to offer a pair of turtledoves, or two young pigeons; and if this too cannot avail, a certain quantity of fine flour. Since, if it is understood that this is everywhere permitted, it cannot indeed be said that a priest does not have a calf, or the whole congregation, or a ruler does not have a he-goat or a sheep: and if it is so, what need was there afterward to say that someone's undisclosed perjury, or an unclean thing touched, or a perjury done through ignorance, is purged by the sacrifice of a ewe lamb and a she-goat, when the same sacrifices were prescribed also in that generality of sin, to which generality these too could have belonged?

But if these are distinguished by this, that for them it was permitted to offer turtledoves and pigeons, or even, if this were not the case, fine flour, but there, where it was not said, it was not permitted; the poor do not seem to have been provided for; since there could be many offenses not specially expressed, which would be referred to that generality, where the needy would be burdened, if it were permitted to offer only a female she-goat, and a ewe lamb from the sheep, and those little birds, and fine flour.

Unless perhaps someone should say that these excepted and expressly named sins are distinguished from those which were mentioned generally by this, that here he said a ewe lamb, there a sheep, so that the age of the animals makes some difference; provided, however, that it be understood that the poor were equally provided for, so that if they had no quadruped animals, they might offer the aforementioned birds or fine flour for the sins of their ignorance.

But if it disturbs one why, when he had generally included all sins of ignorance, and had distinguished the sacrifices not according to the difference of the sins, which he had not made, but according to the difference of persons; he afterward wished also to distinguish the sins, and to prescribe different sacrifices according to their diversity, as though not all belonged to that generality: the exception made afterward is to be understood in such a way that whatever remained, these being excepted which he mentioned by name and specifically, we understand to be said in that generality.

This manner of speech is not to be found elsewhere: but in the Holy Scriptures it is like that where the Apostle says: *every sin whatever a man does is outside the body.* For here he seems to have passed over no sin, since he says: *every sin whatever a man does*: yet afterward he excepted fornication, when he brought in: *but he who commits fornication sins against his own body*[^10]. Which, according to the custom of our speech, would be said thus: "every sin whatever a man does, except fornication, is outside the body; but he who commits fornication sins against his own body." So here too, when he first said generally that all sins of ignorance are to be expiated by those sacrifices which he mentioned; yet afterward he excepted those to which, expressly and distinctly set down, he would apply a certain purgation of sacrifices, so that, these being excepted, whatever others there were would belong to that generality.

## What it means that it is said in Leviticus 6:10.

**9.** (6, 6. 10) *He shall offer to the Lord a ram from the flock without blemish, at a price, for that in which he has trespassed.* This is not to be construed as though it read *at a price for that in which he has trespassed,* that is, "the price in which he has trespassed"; rather, "if he offers a ram, he shall offer it at a price," that is, one bought. For this too seems to have been meant to bear the sacred symbolism of some signification, since the price itself is not specified. Had it been specified, this might seem to have been commanded so that no cheap animal should be offered, so that even one who did not buy what he offered would nonetheless offer something of equal worth.

And by the addition not only of *at a price,* so that the ram offered should be a bought one, but also of *of the holy shekels*—for it reads thus: *at the price of silver of shekels by the holy shekel*[^11]—he meant the ram to be bought for several shekels, not for one. What *the holy shekel* means we have already discussed where it came up.

But as to the fact that, after saying *and for his offense he shall offer to the Lord a ram from the flock without blemish, at a price,* he then added *for that in which he has trespassed,* it is to be understood as "he shall offer for that in which he has trespassed," that is, on account of that matter, for the sake of that matter. *And he shall take away the whole burnt offering which the fire has consumed, that burnt sacrifice, from the altar.* What is he to take away, if it has been consumed? For he ordered the priest, after the fire of the whole night, to take away the burnt offering, that is, the holocaust which the fire had consumed.

Or what does that addition of his mean, *that burnt sacrifice,* when *burnt offering* is to be understood as the same thing as *burnt sacrifice*? Unless perhaps what we found in a certain Greek copy is the truth—for it does not say "he shall take away the burnt offering," but *he shall take away the remnant of the burnt offering,* that is, the remains of the holocaust which the fire consumed. And these remains, such as ash and coal, it called *that burnt sacrifice,* naming the remains of the consuming by the name of the thing that was consumed.

## What is the order and sense of those words.

**10.** (6, 9) As to what he said above, *this is the law of the holocaust,* and then, expounding what that law is, says, *this is the burnt sacrifice, upon its burning upon the altar the whole night until morning, and the fire of the altar shall burn upon it, it shall not be extinguished*—it would be plainer, according to our customary manner of speaking, if it did not have the word *and*: for once that conjunction is removed, the sense reads thus: *this is the burnt sacrifice, upon its burning upon the altar the whole night until morning the fire of the altar shall burn upon it,* that is, upon the altar. Then, to round off the statement of the thought, he added: *it shall not be extinguished*; for this had already been said by *the whole night.*

## What is the burnt offering or burnt sacrifice.

**11.** (6, 11) *And he shall put on another robe, and shall cast out the burnt offering outside the camp into a clean place*: he here calls *burnt offering* what has already been burned up; and this is read in that Greek copy κατακάρπωσις. But certain Latin translators added *which has been burned up,* and so rendered it: *and he shall cast out the burnt offering, which has been burned up, outside the camp into a clean place.*

## What it means: The fire of the altar shall burn always...

**12.** (6, 12) *And the fire upon the altar shall burn from it, and shall not be extinguished*; that is, from that fire in which the holocaust burned until morning. For he does not want the fire to be wholly extinguished, but rather that, when the holocaust has burned until morning and the remains of the consuming have been taken away from it, even so the fire should not be extinguished, but be renewed again from it, so that the other things set on may burn.

## Whether what it says is to be understood as done daily.

**13.** (6, 12-13) As to what follows and says, *and the priest shall burn wood upon it morning by morning,* *and shall heap the holocaust upon it, and shall lay upon it the fat of the peace offering; and the fire shall burn always upon the altar, it shall not be extinguished*: it must be considered whether by *morning by morning* he meant "daily" to be understood, so that no day should be passed over on which a holocaust and the fat of the peace offering were not found; or whether *morning by morning* means "on whatever day" it should be set on, it should be set on only in the morning.

For if we understand it as "daily," what then, if no one brought one? But if the priests, whether from public funds or from their own, provided the daily holocausts, then upon these were set those things which he ordered to be set upon the holocausts, offered by the people for their sins; and it was not necessary for one offering sacrifices for sin also to offer a holocaust upon which it might be set—except when a pair of turtledoves or two young pigeons was offered: for there it is altogether laid down that one should be offered for sin, the other as a holocaust, and first for sin, then as a holocaust[^12].

Then it may be asked whether the holocaust which he ordered to be set on *morning by morning* itself also burned through the whole night until the next morning; or whether what he said ought to burn the whole night was the evening holocaust, and he then began to speak of the law of the holocaust so as to begin from the evening holocaust—which would be strange if he kept silent about it and did not point out that such things ought to be offered in the evening.

## Concerning what the Lord said to Moses.

**14.** (6, 19-20) *And the Lord spoke to Moses, saying: This is the gift of Aaron and his sons, which they shall offer to the Lord on the day on which you shall anoint him.* There are other sacrifices, which he mentioned in *Exodus,* by which the priests are sanctified over seven days, so that they may begin to discharge their priesthood[^13]; and it is another thing that he now mentions, what the high priest is to offer when he is appointed, that is, when he is anointed. For this follows, and it says: *on the day on which you shall anoint him.* He did not say "you shall anoint them," although he also commands that the priests of second rank be anointed. Then he states the sacrifice itself: *a tenth part of an ephah of fine flour, as a perpetual sacrifice.* It is asked how it can be perpetual, if it is offered on that day on which the high priest is anointed, by him who has been anointed—unless it means that this is always to be offered on the day on which a high priest is anointed, that is, throughout the successions of priests. Although it can also be understood as perpetual in that other way, not this offering itself, but what it signifies.

## What it means: Half of it in the morning and half after midday, and what *bruised grain* is.

**15.** (6, 20-21) *Half of it,* he says, *in the morning, and half of it after midday*; which the Greek has as δειλινόν. *In a pan,* he says, *it shall be made with oil; he shall offer it mixed, in bruised pieces,* that is, the fine flour itself. Then he says *bruised pieces,* if indeed this is rightly rendered from what the Greek has as ἐρικτά, and he put it in the plural, in the neuter gender. For he did not say *bruised* (feminine), as though referring to the same fine flour which he had called *mixed.* But this *bruised grain* is said to be a sacrifice made of fragments. Whether, then, by *bruised grain* he means the fragments themselves, that is, things broken small, or whether he meant the very finely bruised portions of the fine flour, is not clear.

## Concerning the sacrifice as an odor of sweetness to the Lord.

**16.** (6, 21-22) It follows, and he says: *A sacrifice as an odor of sweetness to the Lord. The anointed priest who is in his place from among his sons shall make it.* Perhaps it was on this account that he had said *perpetual,* namely that every high priest should do this when he succeeds one who has died, on the day on which he is anointed; for he added and said: *an eternal law*—though *eternal* too can be understood according to what it signifies.

## What does *it shall all be consummated* mean.

**17.** (6, 23) It follows: *it shall all be consummated*; which the Greek has as ἐπιτελεσθήσεται, and some translators rendered: *it shall all be set on*: where he means a holocaust to be understood, since nothing of it remains thereafter. Finally he adds: *and every sacrifice of the priest shall be a holocaust, and shall not be eaten.* This, then, is what he had said by *it shall all be consummated.*

## Concerning the words: The priest who offers it shall eat it, and so forth.

**18.** (6, 26. 30) Speaking of the sacrifice for sin, he says: *the priest who offers it shall eat it.* He shall not eat what he offers, for that will be consumed by fire; but what remains of it: for it is not a holocaust, that all of it should burn on the altar. But he says afterward: *all that is for sin, of which any of their blood has been brought into the tabernacle of testimony to make atonement in the sanctuary, shall not be eaten, but shall be burned up with fire*[^14]*.*

How, then, do the things that remain of the sacrifices for sins belong to the priests to be eaten? This must be understood to mean that those are excepted of whose blood that altar of incense in the tabernacle of testimony is touched. For he ordered this to be done above with the bullock which the priest would offer for his own sin, and with the bullock which he would offer for the sin of the whole congregation, namely that the flesh which remained should be burned outside, beyond the camp[^15]: this too he has now briefly recalled.

## What that means, Leviticus 7:1.

**19.** (7, 1) *And this is the law of the ram which is for an offense; they are most holy*: that is, they belong to the priests to be eaten.

## What difference there is between sin and offense.

**20.** 1. (7, 7) Why is it that, having spoken of the ram for an offense and expounded the law of the sacrifice, he says: *as that which is for sin, so also that which is for an offense: there is one law for them*? It is asked what difference there is between sin and offense; since, if there were none, it would in no way be said: *as that which is for sin, so also that which is for an offense.* For although the law and its sacrifice differ in nothing, since there is one law for them, nevertheless those two things, of which there is one sacrifice—that is, sin and offense—if they differed in nothing from each other, and if there were two names for one thing, Scripture would not take such care to commend that the sacrifice for both is one.

**20.** 2. Perhaps, then, sin is the committing of evil, while an offense is the abandoning of good: so that, just as in a praiseworthy life it is one thing to turn aside from evil and another to do good—to which we are exhorted when Scripture says, *turn aside from evil, and do good*[^16]—so in a blameworthy life it is one thing to turn aside from good and another to do evil; and the former is an offense, the latter a sin. For if the word itself is examined, what else does "offense" (*delictum*) suggest but "what is left behind" (*derelictum*)? And what does one who offends (*delinquit*) leave behind, but the good? The Greeks too have imposed on this plague two names in common use. For among them an offense is called both παράπτωμα and πλημμέλεια. In this very passage of *Leviticus* it is πλημμέλεια.

But where the Apostle says, *if a man be overtaken in any offense*[^17], the Greek has παραπτώματι: and if the origin of these names is examined, in παραπτώματι one who offends is understood as it were to fall down. Hence a corpse, which the Latins named from "falling" (*cadere*), is in Greek called πτῶμα, ἀπὸ τοῦ πίπτειν, that is, from what is *to fall.*

He, therefore, who does evil by sinning, first falls away from good by offending. And πλημμέλεια is a name similar to "negligence": for in Greek negligence is called ἀμέλεια, because no care is taken for what is neglected. For the Greek says, *I do not care,* οἏ μέλει μοι.

The particle, then, that is added, πλήν, so that it reads πλημμέλεια, signifies *beyond,* so that, as ἀμέλεια, which is called "negligence," seems to mean *without care,* so πλημμέλεια means *beyond care,* which is almost the same thing.

Hence some of our own people[^18] have "preferred" to render πλημμέλεια not as "offense" but as *negligence.* But in the Latin language what else is neglected (*neglegitur*) but what is not read (*non legitur*), that is, not chosen (*non eligitur*)? Hence Latin authors too have said that "law" (*lex*) is named from "reading" (*legere*), that is, from "choosing" (*eligere*). By these traces, as it were, it is gathered that he offends who leaves behind the good, and by leaving it behind falls away from the good, because he neglects, that is, does not choose it. But why "sin" (*peccatum*), which in Greek is called ἁμαρτία, is so named does not for the moment occur to me in either language.

**20.** 3. It can also seem that an offense is what is done imprudently, that is, in ignorance; a sin, what is committed by one who knows. With this distinction these divine testimonies seem to agree: *who understands his offenses?*[^19] And that other: *for you have known my imprudence*: for he at once went on and added: *and my offenses are not hidden from you*[^20]; as if repeating the same thought in another way.

Nor does that saying of the Apostle which I recalled a little before disagree with the same reasoning: *if a man be overtaken in any offense*[^21]: for in that he says "overtaken," he signifies that the man has slipped imprudently. But that sin pertains to one who knows the Apostle James, as it were defining it, says: *to him therefore who knows to do good and does not do it, to him it is sin*[^22]. But whether it be that distinction, or this, or some other, between sin and offense, unless there were some distinction Scripture would not so speak as to say: *as that which is for sin, so also that which is for an offense: there is one law for them*[^23]*.*

**20.** 4. But for the most part they are used indifferently, so that both a sin is called by the name of "offense" and an offense by the name of "sin." For when it is said that in Baptism the remission of sins takes place, the remission of offenses is not thereby not also taking place: nor yet are both named, because in that one name both are understood. So too the Lord says that his blood is poured out for many for the remission of sins[^24]: surely, because he does not say "and of offenses," will anyone dare to say that in his blood there is no remission of offenses? Likewise what is written in the Apostle: *for the judgment indeed was from one to condemnation, but grace from many offenses to justification*[^25]; what else is this but that under the name of offenses sins too are comprehended?

**20.** 5. In this very Scripture of *Leviticus* too, by which we are compelled either to find or to believe some distinction between offense and sin, it is read thus, where God was speaking of those very sacrifices which were commanded to be offered for sins: *but if the whole congregation of the children of Israel be ignorant,* he says, *and a matter be hidden from the eyes of the congregation, and they do one thing from all the commandments of the Lord which should not be done, and they offend, and the sin which they have sinned therein become known to them*[^26]*.*

See how, where he said *and they offend,* there he at once added *the sin which they have sinned*—this very thing, surely, which they "offended."

And a little later: *but if a ruler,* he says, *shall sin and do one thing from all the precepts of the Lord his God which should not be done, unwillingly, and shall offend*[^27]*.*

Likewise in what follows: *but if one soul,* he says, *shall sin unwillingly, from the people of the land, in that it does one thing from all the precepts of the Lord which should not be done, and shall offend, and the sin which it has sinned therein become known to it*[^28].

Likewise elsewhere: *whatever soul shall swear, distinguishing with its lips to do evil or to do good, according to all that a man shall determine with an oath, and it be hidden from him, and he afterward come to know it, and shall sin in one of these; and shall confess the sin for which he has sinned against it, and shall offer to the Lord for those things in which he has offended, for that sin which he has sinned*[^29]*.*

And a little later: *and the Lord spoke to Moses, saying: A soul, if it be hidden from him through forgetfulness, and he sin unwillingly against the holy things of the Lord, he shall offer for his offense to the Lord a ram without blemish from the flock, at the price of silver of shekels by the holy shekel, for that in which he has offended; and for that in which he has sinned against the holy things, he shall make restitution, and shall add a fifth part upon it, and shall give it to the priest: and the priest shall make atonement for him in the ram of the offense, and it shall be forgiven him*[^30]*.*

It goes on still further and says: *whatever soul shall sin, and do one thing from all the precepts of the Lord which ought not to be done, and not know it, and shall offend, and shall bear his sin; and he shall offer a ram from the flock at the price of silver for the offense to the priest: and the priest shall make atonement for him, for his ignorance in which he was ignorant, and himself did not know, and it shall be forgiven him. For he has offended with an offense, an offense before the Lord*[^31].

And further it says thus: *and the Lord spoke to Moses, saying: Whatever soul shall sin and, despising, shall despise the precepts of the Lord, and shall lie concerning the things that are toward his neighbor in a matter of a deposit or of fellowship, or of robbery, or shall do some injury to his neighbor, or shall find what was lost, and shall lie about it, and shall swear unjustly concerning any one of all the things that a man does, so as to sin in these: and it shall be, when he has sinned and offended, that he shall restore the plunder which he plundered, or the injury which he did, or the deposit that was entrusted to him, or the lost thing which he found, of every matter concerning which he has sworn unjustly; and he shall restore it, and shall add a fifth part of it to it, and shall give it to him whose it is; and on the day on which he is convicted of his offense, he shall offer to the Lord a ram from the flock without blemish, at the price for that in which he has offended: and the priest shall make atonement for him before the Lord, and it shall be forgiven him for any one of all the things that he did and offended therein*[^32].

Almost in all the deeds, then, which he says are sins, he also calls the same things offenses. Wherefore it is clear both that they are spoken of indifferently in many places of the Scriptures, and that they have some difference, as Scripture testifies when it says: *as that which is for sin, so also that which is for an offense.*

## Concerning the words: You shall not eat any fat of oxen or sheep or goats, and so forth.

**21.** (7, 23-25) *You shall not eat any fat of oxen or sheep or goats, and the fat of carcasses and of beasts taken by a wild animal shall be put to every use, but it shall not be eaten as food. Everyone who eats the fat from the cattle of which you offer from them a victim to the Lord, that soul shall perish from its people.*

He had said above, concerning fat: *all the fat is the Lord's*[^33]; and we had asked whether it meant all the fat of every clean animal only (for about unclean ones there is no question), and what was to be done with the fat which he forbade to come into food: but now he has said what was to be done with the fat of a carcass and of an animal taken by a wild beast, namely that it should be put to every use—every use, that is, that use to which something of the kind is necessary.

Hence the question remained, what is to be done with the fat of the other animals which are clean for eating. But since he says that every soul shall perish from its people who eats the fat of those cattle from which an offering is made to the Lord, he seems to have laid down that only that fat from clean cattle was forbidden to be eaten from which a sacrifice is made: although we have heard that the Jews take no fat whatever as food.

But what is to be sought is what Scripture meant, not what they have supposed. In short, they do not find what they may rightly do with the fat, from which they abstain, and how they may cast it away, since it has been said: *all the fat is the Lord's*; if they want to understand here not only the fat of the sacrifices, but also that of those animals which are not sacrificed—even unclean ones.

## Concerning the breast and the shoulder, which are given to the priests.

**22.** (7, 29-31) What does it mean that he again gives instruction about the peace offerings, and says that he who offers the gift of his peace offering must give the breast and the shoulder to the priests, yet in such a way that the fat of the breast be offered to the Lord with the lobe of the liver; whereas above, speaking of the peace offerings, he commanded the lobe of the liver to be offered to the Lord along with the fat of the belly and of the kidneys and of the loins[^34], but kept silent about the fat of the breast? Or does he here mention what he there passed over?

Why, then, the lobe of the liver both there and here? Or is there perhaps some difference, in that above he gave the command concerning the peace offering, but here he added *his,* as though the peace offering were one thing and *his* peace offering another?

## Concerning the bullock to be offered for the priest's sin.

**23.** 1. (4, 3-7; 8, 2. 14-15. 28-29) When he earlier mentioned the sacrifices for sins, he said that a bullock was to be offered for the sin of the priest who had made the people sin[^35]; afterward too, when Scripture related how the things which the Lord commanded were carried out with regard to Aaron and his sons, a bullock is said to have been offered for sin[^36]; but above he commanded that the horns of the altar of incense be touched with the blood of the bullock, that some of the blood itself be sprinkled also before the holy veil, and that the rest of the blood be poured out at the base of the altar of holocausts[^37]: but afterward, when Aaron is sanctified[^38], nothing is said about sprinkling the blood before the veil; about the horns of the altar it is said; but the word *of incense* is not added[^39]; and it is added that the blood is to be poured out *at the base of the altar*; he did not say "at its base," as though it were necessary to take that altar whose horns he had touched with the blood:

accordingly, although it is set down ambiguously, it is nevertheless free to understand it as done in the way that had earlier been commanded about the bullock for sin; so that we understand the horns touched were not those of the altar at whose base the blood was poured, but that the horns of the altar of incense were touched, while the blood was poured at the base of the altar of sacrifices.

**23.** 2. Above, because he was commanding generally, in case a priest should sin, he ordered the anointed and consecrated priest himself—by whom he means the high priest to be understood—to offer those sacrifices[^40]: but now, when Aaron is sanctified, Moses offers and himself receives the breast of the wave offering[^41], which he had earlier declared was to be given to the priest[^42]. And I think it is called the breast of the wave offering because the fat was placed upon it, as he mentioned above concerning the peace offering[^43].

Since, then, the high priesthood seems to have begun from Aaron, what are we to think Moses was? If, then, he was not a priest, how were all those things carried out through him? But if he was, how do we determine that the high priesthood began from his brother? Although that *Psalm* too, where it is said, *Moses and Aaron among his priests*[^44], removes any doubt that Moses too was a priest. Nevertheless that priestly vestment, which contains a great sacred symbol, Aaron and his successors the high priests are commanded to receive[^45].

In Exodus, before anything at all is commanded about sanctifying and, as it were, ordaining the priests, when, as Moses goes up the mountain, the priests are ordered not to go up[^46]—and we can understand these to be none other than the sons of Aaron—not because they already existed, but because they were to exist, Scripture even then called them this by anticipation, as many such expressions are: for the son of Nun too was called Jesus[^47], although Scripture relates that this name was given him long afterward[^48]. Both, then, were high priests at that time, Moses and Aaron; or rather Moses, with Aaron under him? Or was he too a high priest on account of the pontifical vestment, while the other was so on account of his more excellent ministry? For from the beginning it is said to him: *he shall be to you in matters pertaining to the people, you to him in matters pertaining to God*[^49].

**23.** 3. It may also be asked, after the death of Moses who anointed the successor of the high priest, who certainly could not succeed except when the high priest had died. Or, because he had already been anointed among the priests of second rank (for the oil was the same with which both the high priest and the second priests were anointed), did that pontiff merely take up the vestment by which his preeminence would appear?

And if it is so, did he take it up himself, or did another clothe him, as Moses clothed his brother's son even after his death? If, then, he was clothed by another, could a high priest be clothed by a priest of second rank—especially because the vestment was such that it had to be put on by another? Or was he clothed first in such a way as also afterward? For once clothed, he did not lay it aside; or, when he had laid it aside, he did not take it up again.

It could therefore perhaps happen that the second priests clothed the first by way of service, not of preeminence. But how did it appear which of the sons ought to succeed the high priest? For Scripture did not specify the firstborn or the elder: unless we understand that it used to be done by some divine indication, whether through a prophet or by some other means by which God is wont to give counsel: although the matter seems to have arisen out of contention, so that afterward there were several high priests for this reason, namely that, when the more eminent contended, for the sake of ending the dispute the honor itself was conferred on several.

## What it means that Moses says to Aaron and his sons, when they are sanctified for entering upon the priesthood.

**24.** (8, 35) What does it mean that Moses says to Aaron and his sons, when they are sanctified for entering upon the priesthood: *you shall sit at the door of the tabernacle of testimony seven days, day and night, lest you die*? Is it credible that they were commanded to sit in one place in one posture of the body for seven days, day and night, so that they should not move from it at all? Nor yet are we to be forced on this account to take it as though something were signified allegorically, which would not be done but only understood; but rather to recognize the idiom of the Scriptures, where it puts *sitting* for *dwelling* and *remaining.* For it is not because it is said of Shimei that he sat in Jerusalem three years[^50] that we are to think he sat in a chair through all that time and did not rise. Hence too those places are called "seats" (*sedes*) where those whose seats they are have their dwelling: for it is from "dwelling" (*sedere*) that this word received its meaning.

## What it means, Leviticus 9:1: He called the senate...

**25.** (9, 1) *And it came to pass, on the eighth day Moses called Aaron, and his sons, and the senate of Israel.* What some of our people rendered as *senate,* the Greek has as γερουσίαν: this, then, is what the translator followed, since "senate" (*senatus*) too seems to be named from "old age" (*senium*). But it would not be fittingly said in Latin, "he called the old age of Israel," for "the old men" or "the elders": although it would be the same idiom if one said, "he called the youth of Israel," for "the young men." But the latter is customary in the Latin language, while the former is not. For it would be said properly if one said: "he called the old age of Israel." Hence some, thinking it unusual for *senatus* to be said in this way, rendered it *order of elders.* But more briefly perhaps it would be better said: "he called the elders of Israel."

## How three kinds of sacrifices are commanded to be offered, and how there are four kinds of them.

**26.** 1. (9, 3-4) Moses says to Aaron: *and speak to the senate of Israel, saying: Take a he-goat from the goats, one, for sin, and a ram and a bullock and a year-old lamb for a holocaust, without blemish, and a bullock from the oxen, and a ram for a peace offering before the Lord, and fine flour mixed with oil; for today the Lord will be seen among you.*

He commended four kinds of animal sacrifices above[^51]: the holocaust, the sin offering, the peace offering, and that of consecration: but the offering of consecration pertains to the sanctification of a priest. The three remaining kinds, then, are here commanded to be offered, and this is said to the elders of Israel so that it may pertain to the whole people. But in this passage the sin offering has three animals: a he-goat, a ram, and a bullock: to the holocaust pertains the lamb; and to the peace offering, the bullock and the ram.

Therefore it is not to be construed in such a way that only the he-goat is understood as for sin, while the three remaining ones—that is, the ram, the bullock, and the lamb—are for the holocaust: but rather that the first three are for sin, that is, that what was said, *take a he-goat from the goats, one, for sin, and a ram and a bullock,* the words "for sin" are to be supplied; and that the remaining one, the lamb, is for the holocaust, that is, for the burnt offering. This we had to point out because it could also be construed in such a way that, once it had been said, *take a he-goat from the goats, one, for sin,* the rest would be said already to pertain to the holocaust.

But what he adds, *without blemish,* can be referred to all of them. Since, then, it is ambiguous which way it is more fittingly construed, this consideration leads us to understand the first three as for sin: that above he commanded a he-goat to be offered for the sin of a ruler[^52]; and for the personal sin of each individual, when one sins before the Lord by doing one of the things that ought not to be done, he prescribed a ram[^53]; but for the sin of the whole congregation, a bullock[^54]. It was fitting, then, that when he spoke to the senate about what the whole people ought to offer, both a he-goat should be commanded on account of the rulers, and a ram on account of the personal sin of each, and a bullock on account of the sin of the whole congregation. For it is one thing that each person in the people has his own personal sin, and all can have their own, and another when there is a common sin, which is done with one mind, and committed with one will by a multitude joined together for some purpose.

**26.** 2. But that he commands a bullock and a ram as peace offerings, these he commands as the principal ones: for it is a matter concerning the whole people. Now, in commanding earlier about peace offerings, he ordered them to be offered from whatever source, whether male or female: provided only that they be offered from oxen and from sheep and goats[^55].

But if it is asked why he prescribed two, a bullock and a ram, it is hard to find out: unless perhaps he meant the bullock as the peace offering of the whole people, and the ram for each one as though for the several individuals: since above too he seems to have prescribed, as it were, two kinds of peace offering; the one which was, as it were, of the whole community he called *the peace offering*[^56]; the other where he said: *if anyone offer the sacrifice of his peace offering*[^57]*.* Here too we found a difference, in that in the one which he called *the peace offering* he did not mention that the fat of the breast was to be offered to the Lord, and that the breast itself and the right shoulder were to be given to the priest[^58]: but in the other he commanded this to be done, which he afterward calls *the sacrifice of his peace offering*[^59]; which perhaps is understood as the private offering of each individual, not the public offering of the whole community.

For Moses too offered peace offerings, and it was not said there *his peace offering*; I believe, because he offered for the whole people. But where there is the whole community, there are also the individuals; yet not where there are individuals are there at once the whole. For individual things can exist without the whole; but the whole cannot consist except of individual things. For the several individuals, gathered together or reckoned into a sum, make up the whole.

**26.** 3. It is indeed to be noted that, when sacrifices are offered for the people, sacrifices were commanded to be offered both for sin, and a holocaust, and peace offerings; but for the priest there were offered a sin offering, and a holocaust, and that of consecration, but not a peace offering: yet that of consecration was offered then, when the priests were sanctified to discharge the priesthood, and these Moses offered for Aaron and his sons[^60]; but afterward Aaron himself, now sanctified and discharging the priesthood, was ordered to offer for himself a bullock for sin, and a ram for a holocaust. But he was not ordered to offer for himself an offering of consecration, because that was offered then for the purpose of his being consummated in sanctification and being able to discharge the priesthood, which, since he was now discharging it, there was no need for him to be consummated again.

## What is the reason for the order to be observed in the oblations set down and prescribed.

**27.** 1. (9, 7-21) *And Moses said to Aaron: Approach the altar, and make your sin offering, and your holocaust: and make atonement for yourself and your house.* It is strange how he says that the sin offering is to be made first, then the holocaust, when he commands above that sin offerings be placed upon the holocausts[^61], except for what he prescribed about birds[^62].

Or did he perhaps mention later here what was done first, that is, the holocaust? For he did not say, as he did about the birds, "do that one first and then that one"; but, "do this and that": yet what is to be done first is indicated by the instruction set out above, where he says that the sin offering is to be placed upon the holocaust. Although it is very troubling that Scripture also relates that Aaron did what he heard in such a way that it first mentions his making the sin offering, then the holocaust. Whether he too did this first, or whether Scripture related first what was done afterward, as it is wont to do in many places, would be left uncertain, were it not that what I mentioned above is read where it treats of the sin offering. For it reads thus: *and the priest shall place it upon the altar upon the holocaust of the Lord, and the priest shall make atonement for him for the sin which he has sinned, and it shall be forgiven him*[^63].

How, then, could this be placed upon the holocaust, unless the holocaust were placed first? He prescribed this also concerning the peace offering, that it be placed upon the holocaust[^64]: but because he does not say this everywhere, not for all peace offerings nor for all sin offerings, it can perhaps be said that this was not prescribed as a rule: but it was said to be done only there, that is, in the peace offering when it is made of oxen, for there he prescribed it; and in the sin offering when it is made of a female from the sheep: but the rest, whether they be peace offerings or sin offerings, need not be placed upon the holocausts.

**27.** 2. It is also troubling that, when Aaron made the gifts of the people, which he mentioned above, not all the things were mentioned as immolated which had been foretold; but only the he-goat for sin and the holocaust, where, however, he did not specify that lamb: but the two others, which we said pertain to the sin offerings rather than to the holocaust[^65], he passed over in silence, that is, the ram and the bullock; unless perhaps he meant the whole to be understood from a part, so that, the he-goat alone being mentioned, we should take the others as having followed.

**27.** 3. When he related concerning the peace offerings of the people how Aaron performed them, he says of the bullock and the ram: *and he killed the bullock and the ram of the peace offering of the people, and the sons of Aaron brought the blood to him, and he poured it round about upon the altar, and the fat that was from the bullock, and from the ram the loin, and the fat covering over the belly, and the two kidneys, and the fat that is upon them, and the lobe that is on the liver; and he placed the fat upon the breasts, and laid the fat pieces upon the altar: and the breast and the right shoulder Aaron took away, as a wave offering before the Lord, as the Lord commanded Moses.*

Now he speaks in the singular, now in the plural, although he is speaking of two animals, a bullock and a ram. So when he says *two kidneys,* it is to be understood as from each animal, and thus they become four; and so with the rest. But as for his saying, *and he placed the fat pieces upon the breasts,* when he did not place the breasts themselves upon the altar—for they were owed to the priest along with the right shoulders[^66]—what does it mean? Is it to be understood: "and he placed the fat pieces that are upon the breasts"? For he placed those very pieces, taken from the breasts, to lay them upon the altar. For so he had also prescribed above. Finally it follows: *and he laid the fat pieces upon the altar, and the breast and the right shoulder Aaron took away as a wave offering before the Lord*: now putting it in the singular and saying *breast,* meaning of course both, one from each animal, which he had called *breasts.*

## From where Aaron is said to have come down.

**28.** (9, 22) What does it mean that it says: *when Aaron had lifted up his hands over the people, he blessed them, and came down, when he had made his sin offering, and the holocausts, and the peace offerings*? But where did he do these things, except upon the altar, that is, standing at the altar and ministering at it? From there, then, he came down, where he stood.

Doubtless the solution of that question seems to be helped by this testimony, where we had asked in *Exodus* how it was possible to minister at the altar, which was three cubits high. For we were forbidden there to understand a step, because God had forbidden this, lest the private parts of the one ministering be uncovered upon the altar, which would surely happen if a part of the altar were a step, that is, if it were joined and attached to it[^67].

In short, he forbade this there, where he was speaking of the built altar, for the altar with its step would be one thing of which the step was a part, and therefore it was forbidden; but here, where the altar's height was such that the priest could not fittingly minister unless he stood upon something, it is to be understood that whatever it was that was set down at the hour of ministering and taken away was not a part of the altar, and therefore was not against the precept by which it was forbidden to have a step: but whatever this thing was, Scripture kept silent about it, and therefore the question arose. But now, when Scripture says that the priest, when he had made the sacrifices, came down—that is, when he had placed the immolated offerings upon the altar—it surely makes clear that he had stood somewhere from which he could come down: and that, because he had stood there, he was able to minister at and serve the three-cubit altar.

## How the people fell into ecstasy.

**29.** (9, 24) *And all the people saw, and were thrown out of their mind.* What other translators rendered *they were terrified,* trying to translate from the Greek what was said, ἐξέστη, whence comes ἔκστασις, which is often read in the Latin Scriptures as "going out of the mind" (ecstasy).

## How fire is said to have gone out from the Lord.

**30.** (9, 24) *And fire went out from the Lord, and devoured what was upon the altar, the holocausts and the fat pieces.* As to what he said, *from the Lord,* it may be asked whether it is because it was done by the Lord's nod and will, or whether the fire went out from that place where the Ark of testimony was. For the Lord is not in any place in such a way as not to be elsewhere.

## Who are said, in Leviticus 10:1-3, to draw near to the Lord.

**31.** (10, 1-3) After, when fire went out from the Lord, the sons of Aaron were burned and died, who dared in their fire-pans, using strange fire, to place incense before the Lord—which was not permitted for this reason, that everything which had to be kindled in the tabernacle was to be kindled from that fire which had come divinely upon the altar and was thenceforth kept—when they were dead, then, Moses said: *this is what the Lord said, saying: In those who draw near to me I will be sanctified, and in all the congregation I will be glorified*: meaning those to be understood as drawing near to the Lord who discharged the priesthood in the tabernacle; and that he is sanctified in them even by taking vengeance, as was done.

Whether it was so that from this it might be known how much less he spares others, if he does not spare these: in which sense it was said: *if the righteous man is scarcely saved, where will the sinner and the ungodly appear?*[^68]

Or rather according to that: *to whom more is given, more is required of him*[^69]?

And that: *the servant who did not know his master's will, and does things worthy of stripes, shall be beaten with few: but the servant who knew his master's will, and does things worthy of stripes, shall be beaten with many*[^70]?

And that: *for to the lowly mercy will be granted; but the mighty shall suffer mightier torments*[^71]?

But where the Lord said this, which Moses mentioned that he had said, is not found in the Scripture that comes before. This, then, is such a case as that in *Exodus,* where he says to the Lord: *you said: I know you above all*[^72]; which the Lord is indeed found to have said to him, but afterward: but since Moses would never have said this falsely, it is understood that he had said it to him even before, although it had not been written; so too with this. Hence it appears that not all the things were written which God spoke to those through whom his Holy Scripture has been ministered to us.

## What it means that is said to Aaron and to his sons.

**32.** (10, 6-7) What does it mean that, forbidding Aaron and his remaining sons to mourn the death of those two, he says: *you shall not uncover your head of its turban,* where he certainly shows that the turbans were coverings of the head; unless it be that mourners did the things that were contrary to the custom of adornment? For just as in our custom, because the head is more usually kept bare, it is covered in mourning; so, because they were adorned by the covering of the head, mourners had to uncover it.

He forbids this for the reason that they should not mourn those by whose punishment the Lord was sanctified, that is, fear of him was commended. And not because they were not to be mourned, for he permits others to mourn them: but because they ought not to mourn at that time, while the days of their sanctification were being observed, the seven not yet completed, during which he commanded them not to depart from the tabernacle. Although it may seem that, because they had been sanctified with that oil, this was said—namely, that they should never mourn anyone.

For it says thus: *but your brethren, all the house of Israel, shall weep for the burning with which they were burned by the Lord. And you shall not go out from the door of the tabernacle of testimony, lest you die: for the oil of anointing, which is from the Lord, is upon you.*

## Aaron and his sons, when about to enter the tabernacle, could not drink wine and strong drink.

**33.** (10, 8-11) *And the Lord spoke to Aaron, saying: You shall not drink wine and strong drink, you and your sons with you, when you shall enter the tabernacle of testimony, or when you shall approach the altar, and you shall not die.* When, then, was it permitted them to drink, since they had to enter the tabernacle daily and approach the altar, on account of their continual service? But if anyone says that sacrifices were not wont to be placed daily, what will he say about the entrance into the tabernacle, which was daily on account of the lampstand and the loaves of presentation to be placed upon the table? But if it be answered that what he now says, *the tabernacle of testimony,* is to be understood as that place where the Ark of testimony was; even there it was necessary for the high priest to enter, on account of the continual incense. For he did not enter once a year; rather, with the blood of purification once a year, but on account of the incense daily. Or is it to be understood that God commanded them not to drink wine at all?

Why, then, did he not rather command it briefly so as to say: *you shall not drink wine*; but added, *when you shall enter the tabernacle, or when you shall approach the altar*? Or is it because the reason for not drinking was not to be kept silent, especially because God foreknew that afterward there would be so many high priests too at one time—that is, not by succession—who would serve the tabernacle and the sacrifices and the incense and all that ministry by turns; when surely those whose turn it was to serve did not drink, while the others did drink?

Or is something else to be understood in this passage? Since, when he forbade the priests to drink wine and strong drink, he went on and said: *a perpetual statute throughout your generations*: it is ambiguous whether it is connected to the preceding sense, that is, pertains to not drinking wine; or to what follows, where it goes on: *to distinguish between the midst of the holy and the defiled, and between the midst of the clean and the unclean; and to instruct the children of Israel in all the statutes which the Lord spoke to them by the hand of Moses*: so that this is to be by the office of the priests a perpetual statute throughout their generations.

In what way he says *perpetual,* we have already often said. This too is set down ambiguously, that he says: *to distinguish between the midst of the holy and the defiled, and between the midst of the clean and the unclean*; whether from the fact that things are holy and clean, or defiled and unclean; or from the fact that persons are holy and clean, or defiled and unclean: that is, whether he meant the priests to distinguish between those very sacred things, which were done rightly or not rightly; or between the persons themselves to be approved or disapproved; or rather it is to be referred to both, whether persons or sacred things.

## When he calls it the breast of separation, whether the shoulder too could be called the shoulder of separation.

**34.** (10, 14) *The breast of separation and the shoulder of the wave offering you shall eat in a holy place.* Although each was given for a distinct purpose, nevertheless both could surely be called *of separation,* because both are set apart for the priest; and both *of the wave offering* or *of removal,* which in Greek is called ἀφαίρεμα, because both are removed and taken away from those for whom the offering is made, so as to be given to the priest. We have indeed read above *the breast of the wave offering, and the shoulder of removal*[^73]; because nothing of the shoulder was placed upon the altar, but the fat of the breast was placed there.

## What difference there is between the sacrifices of peace offerings (plural) and the sacrifice of the peace offering.

**35.** (10, 14) What does it mean that he says: *of the sacrifices of peace offerings,* when he means those things which elsewhere he called *the sacrifices of the peace offering*; and he said this in the singular number, *the sacrifice of the peace offering,* when it was being said of the same thing? Or should it perhaps in this passage, where he says *from the sacrifices of peace offerings,* have been said "of healings"? For in that *Psalm* where it is said, *hear us, O God of our healings*[^74], the Greek has the same word as in this passage too, that is σωτηρίων, which genitive plural is ambiguous in the Greek language, whether the noun is declined from "salvation" or from "the saving thing": since σωτηρία means "salvation" or "health," whence comes the genitive plural τῶν σωτηριῶν; but "the saving thing" is called σωτήριον, and thence the genitive plural is the very same.

If, then, the sacrifice of salvation, which is the sacrifice of the peace offering, can rightly be understood too, since salvation is given by the saving thing, and that is the saving thing from which salvation is received: there is no need that we should here understand sacrifices as it were of many saving things, where it is said *of the sacrifices of peace offerings,* but perhaps of many healings, which nevertheless are received from one saving thing. But what the saving thing of God is, of which it is said: *I will take the cup of salvation*[^75], and of which Simeon says in the Gospel: *for my eyes have seen your saving thing*[^76], the Christian faith knows. They can indeed not absurdly be understood or called also saving sacrifices, which belong to the peace offering.

## Why it is added: and to your daughters, and so forth.

**36.** 1. (10, 15-20) *And it shall be for you and your sons and your daughters a perpetual statute.* Not in vain is *daughters* added; since he forbids certain things pertaining to the food of the priests to be received by females, but commands them to be received by males.

**36.** 2. When Moses sought the he-goat offered for the people's sins, and did not find it, because it had been burned, and was angry that the things which were offered by the people for sin—which the Lord appointed to be eaten by the priests, when the fat and the kidneys had been offered from them—and was angry not with his brother but with his sons, I believe because the ministry of presenting belonged to them: Aaron answered him and said: *if today they have offered the things that are for their sin, and their holocausts before the Lord, and such things have befallen me, and I should eat what is for sin today, would it be pleasing to the Lord? And Moses heard, and it pleased him.*

Aaron seems to have said this: that on the very day on which the children of Israel had first offered for their sin, it ought not to be eaten by the priests, but the whole burned—not making a rule from this for the rest; for thereafter they ate the sacrifices for sins themselves: but because this was the first thing offered first of all on that very first day, it is to be believed that Aaron the priest said this by divine inspiration, so that both what the Lord commanded Moses about the priests should thereafter be observed, and Moses should approve what Aaron said as said divinely.

What, then, of the other sacrifices of the same day, that is, of the ram and the bullock, which we said are to be understood as offered also for sin? Or is there no question about the bullock, since it had to be done in such a way that, of its blood, as had been commanded, some should be brought in to touch the horns of the altar of incense, and it followed that the whole should burn?

What, then, of the ram? Or, since Moses first sought the he-goat, is what was answered to him about the he-goat to be understood also about the ram, which he would have sought in order, had not that answer of the priest pleased him? But what would he ask about the bullock, since it could be done by that law which God had prescribed about the bullock for the sin of the whole congregation, as about the bullock for the priest's sin, that is, that the whole should burn outside the camp[^77]?

For these are the words of Moses when angry with the sons of his brother, when he had not found the he-goat for sin that he sought, because it had been wholly burned: *why did you not eat that which was for sin in the holy place? For since they are most holy, he gave this to you to eat, that you might take away the sin of the congregation, and make atonement for it before the Lord. For none of its blood was brought into the sanctuary before the face within. You shall eat it in a holy place, as the Lord appointed me.*

Surely when he says, *for none of its blood was brought into the sanctuary before the face within,* he beyond doubt distinguishes that which is done thus for the priest's sin, or for the sin of the whole congregation, from the he-goat, of which, because the blood was not commanded to be brought in to touch the horns of the altar of incense within, the whole ought not to have been burned, but eaten by the priests. But why this was done, that is, that it too was wholly burned, Aaron answered, and it pleased Moses.

**36.** 3. Now since six animals were commanded to the elders of the people to be offered for the people, of which he first mentioned four—a he-goat and a ram, and a bullock, and a year-old lamb; of which it is clear that the he-goat is for sin, and clear too that the year-old lamb is for a holocaust; but the two middle ones, the ram and the bullock, we had seen set down ambiguously, whether they pertained to the sin offering and were joined to the he-goat, or rather to the lamb so as to be offered for a holocaust—whence we set out in its place what seemed to us: but afterward, so that the six animals might be completed, he mentioned the bullock and the ram as a peace offering; and yet in what follows, when they are immolated and repeatedly mentioned, no mention is made of those very animals the ram and the bullock, which he had placed between the he-goat and the lamb, but only that bullock and that ram are mentioned which he had commanded to be offered as a peace offering, so that it may be believed there were now not six animals, but rather four[^78]:

it can perhaps be understood that those two, which he had first placed between the he-goat and the lamb, he named again, and that there is not a different bullock and a different ram for the peace offering: so that, when he had said the he-goat for sin, and the ram and the bullock without saying for what, that is, for what purpose, but the year-old lamb for a holocaust; afterward he meant to say what was to be done with the bullock and the ram, that is, that they were commanded to be offered neither for sin like the he-goat, nor for a holocaust like the lamb, but for a peace offering.

But if we understand this, the question will remain why a he-goat was offered for the sin of the congregation, when God, speaking from the beginning about the sacrifices that were to be offered for sins, commanded a bullock to be offered for the sin of the congregation, as for the sin of the priest—not a he-goat, but a bullock; and of which bullock he commanded the blood too, similarly as for the priest's sin, to be brought in to touch the horns of the altar of incense[^79]: and what the reason was that, for Aaron's sin, Moses too offered a bullock[^80], and Aaron himself another bullock[^81], as it ought to be offered for the priest's sin according to God's precept; but for the people's sin not a bullock, as had been prescribed, but rather a he-goat was offered[^82].

Since this troubled us, it seemed to us, as we said above, that not the he-goat alone was commanded to be offered for the people's sin, but also the ram and the bullock, so that "for sin" is to be supplied for these three: because there were both rulers in the people, for whom the he-goat was to be offered; and each individual could have his own sins, for which the ram; and all could have some one sin, for which the bullock, as had been prescribed from the beginning, that a bullock ought to be offered for the sin of the whole congregation[^83]. But for this reason, when they were immolated, the he-goat alone is named, so that the others too, though unmentioned, might be understood, by the idiom of the whole from a part, because all were for sins.

## How water makes a thing unclean, and whether it too is to be called unclean.

**37.** (11, 33-34) When he treated of the carcasses of unclean animals: *and every earthen vessel,* he says, *into the inside of which any of them shall fall, whatever is inside shall be unclean; and it shall be broken,* that is, the vessel itself. *And all food that is eaten, upon which water from it shall come, shall be unclean to you.* It is not any water at all that is to be understood, which, if it come upon food, makes it unclean; but water from that vessel which was made unclean by the unclean carcasses, if the vessel itself happened to have water in it.

## What animals are live-bearing.

**38.** (11, 47) *To instruct the children of Israel between the live-bearing animals that are eaten, and the live-bearing animals that are not eaten*: which the Greek has as ζωογονοῦντα, some of our people preferred to render *live-bearing,* because this word our usage has somehow received, rather than to make an unfamiliar new one, if one could say "living-begetting." For those things are called ζωογονοῦντα which do not vivify, that is, make to live, but which beget living offspring, that is, not eggs but young.

## What it means that he says about the woman who has given birth.

**39.** (12, 4) What does it mean that he says about the woman who has given birth: *she shall touch nothing holy, and shall not enter into the sanctuary*? What sanctuary does he mean to be understood; when it is read that only the priests were wont to enter the tabernacle, and as far as the inner second veil, but beyond the veil itself where the Ark was, none but the one high priest? Or could the area before the tabernacle too, where the altar of sacrifices was, be called the sanctuary? For often even the courtyard itself is called a holy place, when it is said: *in a holy place they shall eat them*[^84]. There, perhaps, women were wont to enter, when they offered their gifts, which were to be placed upon the altar.

## What it means that he says: If she bears a male, she shall be unclean seven days, and so forth.

**40.** 1. (12, 2-8) What does it mean that he says: *if a woman bears a male, she shall be unclean seven days; according to the days of the separation of her purification she shall be unclean: and on the eighth day he shall circumcise the flesh of his foreskin; and for thirty-three days she shall sit in her clean blood. She shall touch nothing that is holy, and shall not enter into the sanctuary*? What difference is there between those seven days during which he says she is unclean, and the thirty-three during which she shall sit in her clean blood? For if she is no longer unclean for the thirty-three days, why does she not touch what is holy? Or is there still this difference, that she is in blood, though clean: so that this is the distinction, that when she is unclean, wherever she sits she makes it unclean; but when she now sits in clean blood, only this is not permitted her—to touch what is holy, and to enter into the sanctuary? For this is what he means by *according to the days of the separation of her purification,* since he says in another place that the uncleanness of a woman who is purified by her monthly courses is of seven days, when everything upon which she sits will be unclean[^85]. He says *of separation,* because she withdrew somewhat, lest she defile everything, while she was passing those days. These days of her uncleanness, if she bore a female, the Law doubled, and made fourteen: but the rest, during which she sat in her clean blood, it had likewise commanded to be observed double, that is, sixty-six, so that all should be in the birth of a male forty, in that of a female eighty. But some Greek codices do not have *in her clean blood,* but *in her unclean blood.*

**40.** 2. *And when the days of her purification shall be fulfilled, for a son or for a daughter she shall offer a year-old lamb without blemish for a holocaust, and a young pigeon or a turtledove, for sin, at the door of the tabernacle of testimony to the priest: and he shall offer it before the Lord, and the priest shall make atonement for her, and shall purify her from the fountain of her blood. This is the law of her who bears a male or a female. But if her hand does not find what is enough for a lamb, she shall take two turtledoves, or two young pigeons; one for a holocaust, and one for sin: and the priest shall make atonement for her, and she shall be purified.* Rightly, then, above it is not to be read: *she shall offer a year-old lamb without blemish for a holocaust,* or *a young pigeon or a turtledove for sin,* as some codices have; but as it is said, *and a young pigeon or a turtledove for sin*: since afterward he says: *if her hand does not find what is enough for a lamb, she shall take two turtledoves*: where the word *and* seems superfluous; for when that conjunction is removed, it follows soundly, *she shall take two turtledoves or two young pigeons,* one bird, namely, for a holocaust, the other for sin.

**40.** 3. But for what sin? Is it a sin to have given birth? Or is that propagation from Adam here shown, of which the Apostle says: *from one to condemnation*[^86]; and because *through one man sin entered into the world, and through sin death, and so it passed upon all men*[^87]? And here it appears well enough how it was said: *for I was conceived in iniquities, and in sins my mother nourished me in the womb*[^88]. Why, then, does Scripture not say that what was born is purified by this sacrifice, but rather the woman herself who gave birth? Or was the purification indeed referred to her, from whom that origin was drawn, on account of the fountain of blood; yet could it not be done without the purification of the offspring itself, which arose from that very blood? For to what does it pertain that he said above: *for a son or for a daughter she shall offer a year-old lamb without blemish for a holocaust, and a young pigeon or a turtledove for sin,* if nothing was done by this sacrifice for those who were born?

**40.** 4. But if anyone tries to construe it so as to say that it is not to be joined: *for a son or for a daughter she shall offer a year-old lamb without blemish for a holocaust, and a young pigeon for sin*; but rather is to be read thus: *and when the days of her purification shall be fulfilled for a son or for a daughter,* that is, when the days of purification shall be fulfilled over him or over her, namely the son or daughter; so that then, from another sense, it follows: *she shall offer a year-old lamb without blemish for a holocaust, and a young pigeon for sin,* that is, for her own sin, when the days of her purification over the son or the daughter shall be completed—whoever, then, shall think it is to be construed thus, will be refuted from the Gospel, where, when they did some such thing for the Lord who was born of a virgin, more on account of the custom of the Law than on account of any necessity of expiating and purifying sin in him, it is read thus: *and when his parents brought in the boy Jesus, to do for him according to the custom of the Law*[^89]: it is not said "for his mother," but *for him*; although those things were done which are here prescribed about two turtledoves or two young pigeons.

For so even he himself deigned to be baptized with the baptism of John, which was a baptism of repentance for the remission of sins[^90], although he had no sin. Rightly, then, some of our people rendered this passage in *Leviticus* too so as not to say *for a son or for a daughter* (over), but *on behalf of a son or on behalf of a daughter.* For they understood this to be the force of this preposition in this passage, where the Greek says: ἐφ’ υἱῷ ἢ ἐπὶ θυγατρί. It is indeed to be noted how poor the Lord willed to be born, so that for him there was not offered a lamb and a young pigeon or a turtledove, but a pair of turtledoves or two young pigeons, as is read in the Gospel[^91]; which the Scripture of *Leviticus* commanded to be offered then, if the hand of the one offering did not have *what is enough for a lamb.*

## How the one is explanatory of the other, Leviticus 13:20.

**41.** (13, 2) *If in any man there be made in the skin of his body a shining scar of a mark, and there be in the skin of its color a touch of leprosy.* As though by way of explanation he said the latter, so that what he had said first might be understood; for, since he had said, *if in any man there be made in the skin of his body a shining scar of a mark,* lest we should understand "scar" as the trace usually left by a healed wound is wont to be, he made clear that he was speaking of color, when he added: *and there be in the skin of its color a touch of leprosy.*

Whatever, then, it is, it is a defect of color which he calls by the name of "scar." But as for his saying *a touch of leprosy,* it is not that the color is perceived by touch; rather he says *a touch of leprosy* as though the man himself, or his body, were touched by leprosy, that is, stained and marred: as it is wont to be said, "the fever touched him," or "did not touch him." In short, the spot itself he calls a "touch," and always calls it by this name. Hence some of our people rendered not *touch* but *spot*: by which name what is read would indeed seem to sound plainer; but the Greek too could have said not ἀφήν, that is *touch,* but μῶμον, that is *blemish,* whence it says ἄμωμον, "unblemished": although Scripture is wont to call ἄμωμον that which is pure not from that alone which occurs in color, but from every defect; whence by μῶμον it means not a spot of color, but the mark of every defect to be understood. It could, then, for what pertains to color alone, say σπίλον; which word the Apostle used, where he says of the Church: *not having spot or wrinkle*[^92].

But he said neither μῶμον nor σπίλον, but ἀφήν, that is touch: which even in the Greek language is said unusually of colors, and yet the Septuagint translators were not loath to say this in Greek; why, then, should the Latins have been loath? But as to its being called *a scar of a mark,* it is so called either because it signifies something, or rather because it marks off the man himself from the rest by a certain sign, that is, renders him notable.

## Concerning the words: and the priest shall see, and shall defile him, and so forth.

**42.** (13, 3) What does it mean that he says: *and the priest shall see, and shall defile him*? to whom he will come to be cleansed. But *shall defile* is said for "shall pronounce defiled," if he sees in him those things which Scripture says about the spot of leprosy.

## What the bright white color is, and so forth.

**43.** (13, 4. 7. 2) *But if there be a bright white in the skin of its color, and its appearance be not lower than the skin. Bright white*—"touch" is to be supplied, that is, that spot of color, not a hair. As to what he afterward says, *but if the signification be turned and changed in the skin*; he calls "signification" what above is read in Latin as *mark.* For the Greek both above and here used one and the same word, saying σημασίαν.

## Concerning the words: and the priest shall separate him seven days a second time, and shall see, and so forth.

**44.** (13, 5-6) *And the priest shall separate him seven days a second time; and the priest shall see him on the seventh day a second time: and behold, the touch is dim, the touch has not turned in the skin; and the priest shall purify him*: *for it is a mark*: that is, he shall pronounce him cleansed; for it is not leprosy, but a mark.

## How it is said again: he shall defile him, and so forth.

**45.** (13, 7-8) *But if the signification be turned and changed in the skin, after the priest has seen him so as to cleanse him: and he be seen again by the priest, and the priest see him, and behold, the signification is changed in the skin; the priest shall defile him: it is leprosy.* Here too he said *shall defile* for "shall pronounce defiled": and *and* is superfluous, by the manner of Scripture's speech. By this, then, he seems to have given notice that, when only the white and bright color is seen, of a different hue from the healthy color, it should still be tested by the priest, so that if he sees the hair too changed to a white color, and the place of the skin in which the white color is made lower, then he should pronounce leprosy, that is, defile the man by pronouncing him leprous.

*But if,* he says, *there be a bright white in the skin of its color,* that is, if that touch be bright white; by which name he calls the same spot: *and its appearance be not lower than the skin; and the hair be not turned to white, but it is dim,* that is, the hair itself, because it is not white: *and the priest shall separate the touch seven days; and the priest shall see the touch on the seventh day,* that is, that spot: *and behold, the touch remains before him; the touch has not been changed in the skin,* that is, it is not found of a different hue and unlike the skin.

Therefore what was defective has been healed: but still he commands the same soundness to be tested through another seven days, and therefore it follows and says: *and the priest shall separate him seven days a second time,* that is, another seven days; *and the priest shall see him on the seventh day a second time: and behold, the touch is dim,* that is, because it is not white and bright, and through this of the same hue as the healthy color; *the touch has not been changed* in the skin, as he also said a little before, that is, it is not unlike the rest of the skin: *and the priest shall purify him,* that is, he shall pronounce him cleansed from the suspicion of leprosy; not that he had leprosy, which he no longer has, but because it was not leprosy, in that, in that bright and white color of the touch, that is, of that spot which had appeared, when it was awaited whether the place would become lower and the hair there be turned to white, this did not happen, but rather that touch which had before been bright and white was found dim, that is, like the rest of the color, not bright:

there was, then, no leprosy, *for it is a mark,* not leprosy, that has appeared in this way: yet on this account too, though cleansed from the suspicion of leprosy, *he shall wash his garments*; because in that mark too there was something on account of which the garments had to be washed; *and he shall be clean.*

## How the signification of leprosy is said to be changed.

**46.** (13, 7-8) Then it follows: *but if the signification be turned and changed in the skin, after the priest has seen him so as to cleanse him*; that is, after the priest saw him on the seventh day previously healthy, so as to cleanse him, that signification was changed, that is, that mark in the skin: *and he be seen a second time by the priest,* that is, after another seven days: *and the priest see him, and behold, the signification is changed in the skin,* that is, it did not remain in that soundness in which he had seen him after the first seven days. *And the priest shall defile him: it is leprosy.*

Here, now, because what had appeared healthy after the first seven days did not remain in its state but was changed back into its former defect, leprosy is pronounced; so that there one does not wait either for a lower place or for a hair turned to white. For since leprosy is not notable and defective except by variegation, this very thing—to return from a defective color to a healthy one, and from a healthy one to return to a defective one—is so notable that one was not to wait there for what he had commanded to be waited for in the first instance, namely a lower place and the whiteness of the hair, but now, even by this variegation, it is without doubt leprosy.

## Likewise on the same matter.

**47.** (13, 9-17) Then it follows: *and if there be a touch of leprosy in a man, he shall come to the priest: and the priest shall see; and behold, a white scar in the skin; and this has changed the hair white, and from the soundness of the living flesh into a scar.* If from this we remove *and*—for it is set down by the manner of Scripture's speech—this will be the sense: *and the priest shall see; and behold, a white scar in the skin; and this has changed the hair white from the soundness of the living flesh into a scar.* The order is: *it has changed the hair white in the scar from the soundness of the living flesh*; that is, whereas the living and sound flesh has dark or black hair, this scar has white hair.

*It is an old leprosy in the skin of his color: and the priest shall defile him,* that is, shall pronounce him defiled. *He shall not separate him, because he is unclean*: this he seems to say, because where the hair has been found changed to white, of the same hue as the white defect of the skin, he is no longer separated to be tested; nor is it awaited whether the place will also become lower; but from this alone, that the skin is white, of a different hue from the rest, and has white hair of a different hue from the rest which are in living and sound flesh, an old leprosy is pronounced: "old" for this reason, that it is no longer to be tested through those twice-seven days.

*But if the healthy color be restored, and it be turned white*: since he had said that, the whole having become white throughout the whole skin, by that very fact he is now clean; because there would no longer be any variegation there.

Then he says: *and on whatever day the living color be seen in him, he shall be defiled*: where he shows well enough that variegation is disapproved.

And through this what he has just said: *but if the healthy color be restored, and it be turned white, and he shall come to the priest; and the priest shall see: and behold, the touch has turned to white; and the priest shall purify the touch; he is clean.* We ought not to take "the healthy color restored" to mean that the healthy color was there; for it was already that very color through which, nevertheless, he was being made unclean on account of the variegation.

He therefore said "the healthy color restored" so that it should be what it had been, that is, white, the healthy color perishing. For then again he will be clean, when the whole has become white, because there will be no variegation there. But to take "restored" for "perishing" is too unusual an expression. For he seems rather to have had to say: "but if the white color be restored": yet he in fact says, *the healthy color restored, and it be turned white*; as though he said: "if the healthy color be restored into white."

## When he spoke of leprosy of the head, he also called it a battering.

**48.** (13, 30) What does it mean that, when he spoke of leprosy of the head, he also called it a battering, although it occurs only in color, whether of the skin or of the hair, and although its appearance seems lower than the rest of the skin, yet without pain and without any vexation? Or did he mean to call what is unclean a "battering" for a "blow," as though the man had been struck by this uncleanness?

## What he says about the leprosy of garments and other things, and so forth.

**49.** (13, 47-48) What does it mean that, when he spoke of the leprosy of garments and of other things pertaining to the uses of men, he says: *either in a woolen garment, or in a tow garment, or in the warp, or in the wool, or in linens, or in woolens*; when he had already said above, *in a woolen garment, or in a tow garment*? For tow is surely linen. Or did he mean garments to be understood there, but here any woolen thing and any linen thing whatever? For the saddlecloths of beasts are not garments, though they be woolen; nor are nets garments, though they be linen. First, then, he meant to speak specifically of garments, then generally of all woolen and linen things.

## What he meant by: In any workable skin.

**50.** (13, 48) It is asked what he meant by: *in any workable skin*; which some rendered: *in any prepared skin.* But the Greek does not say ἐργασμένῳ δέρματι, but says ἐργασίμῳ, which word is also set down in the *book of Kings,* where Jonathan says to David: *be in the field on the workable day*[^93], that is, on the day on which work is done. And through this here too we ought to take *a workable skin* as one on which work is done, that is, one fitted for some work. For there are skins which are had only for ornament, not for work.

## How it is said in Greek, in any workable skin.

**51.** (13, 49) What does it mean that he says: *in any workable vessel of skin*; if not what is made of skin, that is, in any leathern vessel? But in this passage he says *vessel* for what the Greeks call σκεῦος; for this name is general for all utensils. But it is another thing that is called ἀγγεῖον; for this too is called a vessel in the Latin language: but ἀγγεῖον is rather understood as the vessel that holds liquid.

## Concerning the words: And whomever he touches who suffers an issue of seed, and so forth.

**52.** (15, 11) What does it mean that he says: *and whomever he touches who suffers an issue of seed, and has not washed his hands with water, shall wash his garments, and shall wash his body with water, and shall be unclean until evening*? For it is set down ambiguously, his saying *has not washed his hands with water,* as though after he has touched. But it is to be understood, "whomever he touches with unwashed hands, that very one whom he touches shall wash his garments," and so forth.

## What it means that is said about the high priest.

**53.** 1. (16, 16. 19) What does it mean that, when he prescribed how the high priest ought to enter the holy place, which is within the veil, he said among other things: *and he shall make atonement for the holy ones from the uncleannesses of the children of Israel, and from their iniquities, and concerning all their sins*? How, then, for the holy ones, if from the uncleannesses of the children of Israel and from their iniquities concerning all their sins?

Or is it because he does not say "for the uncleannesses" of the children of Israel, but *from the uncleannesses,* that this is to be understood: *he shall make atonement for the holy ones from the uncleannesses of the children of Israel,* that is, for those who are holy from the uncleannesses of the children of Israel, not consenting to their uncleannesses: not because atonement was to be made for them alone, but because it was to be made for them too, lest anyone should think they had been so holy that there was nothing for which atonement should be made for them, although they were free from the uncleannesses of the children of Israel and from their iniquities? *Concerning all their sins,* that is, the iniquities which came from all their sins.

This sense too is possible: *he shall make atonement for the holy ones from the uncleannesses of the children of Israel*; that is, that this is to be understood as being entreated for them, namely that they should be safe from the uncleannesses of the children of Israel. But *he shall make atonement* can only be taken as what is said by another word, "he shall propitiate."

Hence too it is called a "propitiatory" (*propitiatorium*), which others rendered "place of atonement"; but in Greek it is called Ἱλαστήριον. But what in this passage the Latin says, *he shall make atonement for the holy ones,* the Greek has ἐξιλάσεται, which is understood only for sins. Whence it is written in the *Psalm*: *who is propitious to all your iniquities*[^94].

Here, then, the more fitting sense is that the priest is understood to propitiate God also for those who are holy from the uncleannesses of the children of Israel; and because, although they are holy in such a way that they do not consent to the uncleannesses and iniquities of the children of Israel, they nevertheless have something on account of which God's propitiation is necessary for them.

In a certain Greek copy, indeed, we found: *and he shall make atonement for the holy thing*; not *for the holy ones*: and that *holy thing* in the neuter gender, that is ἄγιον. For it could be understood, he shall entreat the holy God, and there would be no question: but how it can be understood, *he shall entreat this holy thing,* is hard to say, unless perhaps that holy thing is whatever God is; because the Holy Spirit too, who is surely God, is said in Greek in the neuter gender, τὸ πνεῦμα τὸ ἄγιον. And perhaps this is so, if indeed that codex is the truer, which seemed the more correct, ἐξιλάσεται τὸ ἄγιον, that is, τὸ πνεῦμα τὸ ἄγιον, which cannot be said in Latin in the neuter gender. Although in three other codices, one Greek and two Latin, we found nothing but what we said above: *he shall make atonement for the holy ones.*

This can also be taken so that it is understood not of holy men, but of those things which are holy, that is, the tabernacle itself, and whatever in them had been sanctified to the Lord: so that this is the meaning: *he shall make atonement for the holy things from the uncleannesses of the children of Israel,* he shall propitiate God for the things that have been sanctified to the Lord, from the uncleannesses of the children of Israel; because the tabernacle was in their midst.

For so it follows: for when he had said, *he shall make atonement for the holy ones from the uncleannesses of the children of Israel, and from their iniquities, concerning all their sins*; he at once added: *and so he shall do to the tabernacle of testimony which is created among them, in the midst of their uncleanness*: so that for this purpose that propitiation for the holy ones seems necessary, that is, for the tabernacle and all the things in it that are called holy. For a little later too he says this of the altar, that by the sprinkling of blood the priest should cleanse it, and sanctify it from the uncleannesses of the children of Israel.

## Concerning the words: And making an end, making atonement for the holy thing.

**54.** (16, 20) *And he shall make an end, making atonement for the holy thing.* Will he make an end of the holy thing? Or, making atonement for the holy thing, according to what we said above? For here too it is said in Greek in the neuter gender, τὸ ἄγιον. Will he, then, making atonement to the Lord, make an end of the holy thing, that is, perfectly sanctify what he sanctifies? Or does *he shall make an end*—which he says—mean making atonement for the holy thing, that is, that holy thing which is τὸ πνεῦμα τὸ ἄγιον?

## Concerning the two he-goats to be immolated.

**55.** (16, 8-10. 26-33) Concerning the two he-goats, one to be immolated and the other to be sent away into the desert—which they call ἀποπομπαῖον—there is wont to be dispute: and by some the one to be immolated is taken in a good sense, but the one to be sent away in a bad sense.

Yet this sense is not to be confirmed for the reason that the man in whose hand the he-goat is sent away into the wilderness is, when he returns, commanded to wash his garments and his body with water, and so to enter the camp; as though this too were an indication why that he-goat is to be taken in a bad sense, from whose contagion the man must be washed. For he likewise says that the man who has taken the flesh of the other he-goat and of the bullock, and burned it outside the camp, must be washed; because he commands it to be done thus with that one and with the bullock, of whose immolated blood the sprinkling is made, and which are for sins: and through this the distinction of these two he-goats is not to be treated negligently in its allegorical signification.

Likewise, when he had appointed the tenth day of the seventh month as a Sabbath of Sabbaths, on which the aforesaid purification should be made by that one priest who succeeds the father, speaking of the same priest he says: *he shall make atonement for the Holy of the Holy*: which I do not now know whether it is to be taken otherwise than: "he shall make atonement in the Holy of the Holy," by a certain manner of speech; namely in that holy place which the high priest alone entered, which is within the veil, where was the Ark of testimony and the altar of incense.

For he will not entreat that same place as though it were God, but because he will there entreat God, it is so set down: *he shall make atonement for the holy of the holy.* For this too is set down in Greek in the neuter gender, τὸ ἄγιον τοῦ ἁγίου. Or is it perhaps the Holy Spirit of the holy God, that is, τὸ ἄγιον πνεῦμα τοῦ ἁγίου θεοῦ? Or is it rather that *he shall make atonement* is said for "by entreating he shall cleanse"?

For he weaves the words thus: *and he shall make atonement for the Holy of the Holy, and the tabernacle of testimony, and the altar he shall make atonement, and for the priests and for the whole congregation he shall make atonement.* How, then, will he make atonement for the tabernacle and the altar, unless, as has been said, we understand "by entreating he shall cleanse"?

## Concerning what animals slain the law makes threats.

**56.** (17, 3-4) As to what he says: *whoever shall kill a bullock, or a sheep, or a goat in the camp, and whoever shall kill it outside the camp, and shall not have brought it to the door of the tabernacle of testimony,*—and in this he establishes a sin, and threatens the doer; he is not speaking of those that are killed for the use of eating, or for anything else, but of sacrifices. For he forbade private sacrifices, lest each person dare in a way to be a priest for himself; but rather should bring it there, where through the priest it may be offered to God. For so they will not sacrifice to vain things: for he gave warning that this too was to be guarded against in that custom.

Since, then, it was not permitted to offer sacrifices except in the tabernacle, to which the Temple afterward succeeded—whence too Jeroboam, king of Israel, dared to make calves, to which the people might sacrifice, lest by the necessity of this Law those who were under his kingdom should be drawn away from him while they went to Jerusalem to offer their sacrifices in the Temple of God; in which deed he was condemned by the Lord[^95]—it is rightly asked how Elijah lawfully sacrificed outside the Temple of God, when he both obtained fire from heaven and confounded the prophets of demons[^96].

This does not seem to me to be defended by any other reasoning than that by which Abraham's deed too is defended, namely that, commanded, he was willing to immolate his son to God[^97]. For when he who established the Law commands something to be done which he forbade in the Law, the command itself is held as Law, since he is the author of the Law. For there could not be lacking other miracles besides sacrifice, by which the prophets of the groves might be overcome and confounded: but the Spirit of God, who had been in Elijah, whatever he did in this matter, cannot be against the Law, because he is the giver of the Law.

## Concerning the words: The soul of all flesh is its blood?

**57.** 1. (17, 10-12) What does it mean that, forbidding blood to be eaten, he says: *the soul of all flesh is its blood*? Which whole passage he sets out thus: *and a man, a man of the children of Israel, or of the proselytes who are joined among you, whoever shall eat any blood; I will set my face against the soul that eats blood, and will destroy it from its people. For the soul of all flesh is its blood. And I have given it to you to make atonement for your souls: for its blood shall make atonement for the soul. Therefore I said to the children of Israel: No soul of you shall eat blood; and the proselyte who is joined among you shall not eat blood.*

Surely, if we say that the soul of a beast is its blood, is the soul of a man too to be thought blood? God forbid. How, then, does he not say: "the soul of all flesh of a beast is its blood"; but, *the soul,* he says, *of all flesh is its blood*? In all flesh, surely, the flesh of a man too is reckoned. Or is it because there is something vital in the blood, since by it most of all there is life in this flesh, which is diffused through all the veins throughout the whole body, that he called the very life of the body the soul—not the life that departs from the body, but that which is ended by death? By which manner of speech we say that this life is temporal, not eternal; mortal, not immortal: although the nature of the soul is immortal, which was carried away by the angels into Abraham's bosom[^98]; and to which it is said: *today you shall be with me in paradise*[^99]; and which was burning in the torments of hell[^100].

According to this signification, then, by which this temporal life too is called soul, the Apostle Paul said: *for I do not hold my soul more precious than myself*[^101]; where he meant to show himself prepared even to die for the Gospel. For according to the signification by which that soul is called which departs from the body, he was rather making it precious, for which he was acquiring so great a merit. There are also other expressions of this kind. This temporal life, then, is held in the body most of all by the blood. But what does it mean that he says: *I have given it to you at the altar of God, to make atonement for your soul*; as though soul made atonement for soul? Is it that blood makes atonement for blood, as though we were anxious about our blood, when we want atonement to be made for our soul? This is absurd.

**57.** 2. But it is much more absurd that the blood of a beast should make atonement for the soul of a man, which cannot die: since Scripture manifestly testifies in the *Epistle to the Hebrews* that that blood of the victims availed nothing toward entreating God for the sins of men, but signified something that would avail. *For it is impossible,* it says, *that the blood of goats and bulls should take away sins*[^102]. It remains, then, that, since that Mediator makes atonement for our soul, who was prefigured by all those sacrifices which were offered for sins, that should be called soul which signifies the *soul.*

**57.** 3. Now the thing that signifies is wont to be named by the name of the thing it signifies; as it is written: *the seven ears are seven years*; for he did not say, they signify seven years: and: *the seven oxen are seven years*[^103]; and many things of this kind. Hence it is that it was said: *the rock was Christ*[^104]: for he did not say: "the rock signifies Christ," but as though it were this, which surely in substance it was not, but by signification. So too blood, because on account of a certain vital corporeity it signifies the soul, is in the sacraments called soul.

But if anyone thinks the soul of a beast is the blood, there is no need to labor over this question. Only it must be very much guarded against that the soul of a man, which gives life to human flesh, and is rational, be thought blood, and this error must be refuted in every way. Expressions are also to be sought by which, through that which contains, that which is contained is signified, so that, since the soul is held in the body by the blood (for if it has been poured out the soul departs), the soul is more fittingly signified through it, and its name has been received as blood. As a church is called the place where the Church is gathered. For the Church is human beings, of whom it is said: *that he might present to himself a glorious Church*[^105]. Yet that the house of prayers itself too is called by this name, the same Apostle is witness, where he says: *have you not houses to eat and drink in? Or do you despise the church of God?*[^106] And the daily usage of speech has secured this, that "to go forth into the church," or "to take refuge at the church," is not said except of one who has gone forth to or taken refuge at the very place and walls in which the gathering of the Church is contained. It is also written: *and he who sheds blood is he who defrauds the hireling of his wages*[^107]. He called the wages *blood,* because by them is sustained the life which is called by the name of blood.

**57.** 4. But since the Lord says: *unless you eat my flesh, and drink my blood, you shall not have life in you*[^108]; what does it mean that the people is so greatly forbidden the blood of the sacrifices which were offered for sins, if by those sacrifices was signified this one sacrifice, in which true remission of sins takes place: from whose blood, however, taken as nourishment, not only is no one forbidden, but rather all are exhorted to drink it who wish to have life? It is therefore to be asked what it signifies, that a man is forbidden in the Law to eat blood, and is commanded to pour it out to God. For concerning the nature of the soul, why it was signified through the blood, we have said as much as for the present seemed enough.

## Where the nakedness of father and mother is.

**58.** (18, 7-8) *You shall not uncover the nakedness of your father and the nakedness of your mother, their nakedness.* He forbids lying with one's mother: for there is the nakedness of father and mother. For afterward he forbids that of the stepmother too, when he says: *you shall not uncover the nakedness of your father's wife; for it is your father's nakedness*: where he explained how in the mother there is the nakedness of both, that is, of father and mother; for in the stepmother there is only the father's.

## What the nakedness of a sister born at home or abroad is.

**59.** (18, 9. 11) *You shall not uncover the nakedness of your sister by your father, or by your mother, who was born at home, or who was born abroad—their nakedness.* *She who was born at home* is understood as by the father; *she who was born abroad* is understood as by the mother, if perhaps the mother had borne her by a former husband, and had come into the house with her when she married this man's father, whom Scripture warns not to uncover the nakedness of his sister.

Here he seems not to have forbidden, and as it were to have passed over, lying with a sister born of both parents: for he does not say, "you shall not uncover the nakedness of your sister by your father and mother," but, *by your father or by your mother.* But who does not see that this too is forbidden, all the more? For if it is not permitted to uncover the nakedness of a sister born of either parent, how much more of one born of both? But what does it mean that, after inserting the prohibition of lying even with one's granddaughters, whether by a son or by a daughter, it follows and says: *you shall not uncover the nakedness of the daughter of your father's wife*?

For if he had said only this far, we would understand that lying was forbidden even with a stepmother's daughter, whom the stepmother had borne by a former husband, and who was not this man's sister, either by father or by mother: but when he added, *she is your sister by the same father, you shall not uncover her nakedness,* he makes clear that this prohibition was made about a sister, being a daughter of the father and of the stepmother, of whom he had already spoken above. Or did he mean to forbid this again more openly, because above it was somewhat obscure? For Scripture often does this.

## What the nakedness of a brother is, and so forth.

**60.** (18, 14) *You shall not uncover the nakedness of your father's brother, and you shall not go in to his wife.* He explained what he meant by *you shall not uncover the nakedness of your father's brother,* that is, of your paternal uncle: for this is what *you shall not go in to his wife* means. For in the wife of the paternal uncle he meant the nakedness of the uncle to be understood; as in the father's wife, the father's nakedness.

## What the nakedness of a brother's wife is, and so forth.

**61.** (18, 16) *You shall not uncover the nakedness of your brother's wife; it is your brother's nakedness.* It is asked whether this is forbidden while the brother is alive, or when he is dead: and it is no small question. For if we say that Scripture spoke of the wife of a living brother, this too is surely contained in the one general precept by which a man is forbidden to approach another man's wife[^109]. What, then, does it mean that he so carefully distinguishes these persons, whom he calls members of the household, from the rest by particular prohibitions? For what he forbids about the father's wife—that is, the stepmother—is not to be taken of a living father, but rather of a dead one. For while the father is alive, who does not see that it is forbidden all the more, if any man's wife is forbidden to be defiled by adultery? Of these persons, then, he seems to speak, who, not having husbands, could come together in marriage, were they not forbidden by the Law, as the custom of the Persians is said to be.

But again, if we understand it to be forbidden, when the brother is dead, to marry the brother's wife, there occurs that which Scripture commands to be done, for the sake of raising up seed, if he had died without children[^110]: and through this, when this prohibition is compared with that command, lest they be contrary to each other, an exception is to be understood, that is, that it is not permitted to anyone to marry a dead brother's wife if the dead man left descendants; or even that this was forbidden, that it should not be permitted to marry a brother's wife, even one who had departed from a living brother by divorce. For then, as the Lord says, on account of the hardness of the Jews Moses had permitted them to give a bill of divorce[^111], and through this dismissal it could be thought that anyone might lawfully join to himself a brother's wife, where he need not fear adultery, since she had departed by divorce.

## What the nakedness of a woman and her daughter is, and so forth.

**62.** (18, 17) *You shall not uncover the nakedness of a woman and her daughter*: that is, lest anyone think it permitted him to marry the daughter of his wife. For it is not permitted to uncover the nakedness of a woman and her daughter at the same time, that is, to be joined with both, the mother and the daughter.

## It is not permitted to approach the daughter of a wife's son or daughter.

**63.** (18, 17-18) *You shall not take the daughter of her son and the daughter of her daughter.* He forbade also marrying the granddaughter of a wife, by her son or by her daughter. *You shall not take a wife in addition to her sister, to be a rival.*

Here he did not forbid taking an additional wife, which was permitted to the ancients on account of the abundance of propagation: but he did not want a sister to be taken in addition to a sister; which Jacob seems to have done[^112], either because it had not yet been forbidden by the Law, or because he was deceived by the fraud of another substituted for her, and she rather came by agreement whom he received later; but it was unjust for the first to be dismissed, lest he cause her to commit adultery.

But as for his saying *to be a rival,* is it set down so that there should not be rivalry between sisters, which between those who were not sisters would have been something to make light of? Or is it rather so that it should not be done for this reason, that is, lest it be done with this intent, that a sister be taken in addition to be a rival to her sister?

## How a menstruous woman too is not to be touched.

**64.** (18, 19) *And you shall not approach a woman in the separation of her uncleanness to uncover her nakedness*: that is, you shall not approach a menstruous woman. For she was separated according to the Law, on account of the uncleanness. Since he had forbidden this sufficiently enough above[^113], what does it mean that here too he meant to join this to the same precepts? Or is it perhaps that, because it was said among the things above, lest it be thought to be taken figuratively, it is set down here too, where such things are forbidden as are without doubt to be observed even in the time of the New Testament, when the observance of the old shadows has been removed? Which he seems to have signified also through the prophet Ezekiel, who, among those sins which are of manifest, not figurative, iniquity, mentions this too, if anyone approach a menstruous woman; and among the merits of righteousness, if he does not approach her[^114]. In which matter nature is not condemned, but it is declared harmful to the offspring to be conceived.

## How a neighbor's wife too is not to be approached.

**65.** (18, 20) *And you shall not give the lying of your seed to your neighbor's wife, to be defiled by her.* See where he again forbids adultery, which is committed with another man's wife, which is forbidden in the Decalogue too[^115]. Whence it appears that those women, whose nakedness he forbids to be uncovered, are forbidden in such a way that they are not to be married even when their husbands are dead.

## How idolatry is forbidden.

**66.** (18, 21) *And of your seed you shall not give to serve a ruler.* Here I do not see what is to be understood, except a ruler who is worshiped in place of God. For he does not say δουλεύειν, but λατρεύειν, in the Greek, which the Latin is not wont to render except by saying *to serve*; but there is a great difference. For to serve men as servants serve, which is not λατρεύειν but δουλεύειν, Scripture does not forbid: but to serve in the sense of λατρεύειν is not commanded toward men, but only toward the one true God, as it is written: *you shall adore the Lord your God, and him only shall you serve*[^116].

But not only by this word which he uses, λατρεύειν, does he sufficiently signify what ruler he means, that is, one to whom worship is given as to God; but also by what he adds: *and you shall not profane the holy name,* whether of God, of whose people it is given in that way to serve a ruler; or the holy name of the people of Israel itself, on account of which it is said: *be holy, for I am holy*[^117]. Most opportunely too he adds here: *I am the Lord*; warning, surely, that to him alone is owed λατρεία, that is, that service by which God is served.

## How the land is said to shudder.

**67.** (18, 25) As to what he says, *and the land shuddered at those who dwell upon it,* on account of their evil deeds, which he mentioned above, it is not to be thought said because the land has sense by which it perceives and shudders at these things; but by the name *land* he signifies the men who are upon the land. Accordingly, when men do these evils, they defile the land, because the men who imitate these things are defiled; and the land shudders, because the men who neither do nor imitate them shudder.

## Concerning the words: You shall not lie, nor make a false accusation.

**68.** (19, 11) *You shall not steal, nor lie, nor shall anyone make a false accusation against his neighbor.* That about stealing is set down in the *Decalogue*[^118]. But as for his saying, *nor lie, nor shall anyone make a false accusation against his neighbor,* it would be strange if it were not contained in that precept which is set down there: *nor shall you bear false witness against your neighbor*[^119]; since a false accusation cannot be made without a lie, which is comprehended under the general head of false witness. But whether these things are to be admitted by some compensation is a great question: as concerning lying it seems to almost everyone that, where no one is harmed, one ought to lie for someone's welfare.

Is it, then, so also with theft? Or can theft not be committed where no one is harmed? On the contrary, it can be committed even when the one to whom it is done is being looked after, as if someone should steal a sword from a man who wants to kill himself. For a false accusation I do not know whether it can be made for anyone's benefit: unless perhaps it was for a greater joy, which they would afterward enjoy, that Joseph made a false accusation about the cup against his brothers, on whom he had also pressed the false charge of spying[^120]. Although, if we try to determine these things by definitions, perhaps theft is not theft except when, by something belonging to another being secretly taken away, a neighbor is harmed; and a false accusation is not a false accusation except when, by the charging of a false crime, a neighbor is harmed: but we cannot say that lying exists only then, when a neighbor is harmed; for when what is false is said by one who knows it, beyond doubt it is a lie, whether anyone or no one be harmed by it.

Accordingly the great question about lying—whether a lie can ever be just—would perhaps easily be solved, if we looked only at the precepts, not also at the examples. For what is more absolute than this precept: *you shall not lie*? For it is said in the same way as: *you shall not make for yourself an idol*; which, once done, can never be just; and as it is said: *you shall not commit adultery*: but who would say that adultery can ever be just? And: *you shall not steal*: for according to that definition of theft, theft can never be just. And: *you shall not kill*[^121]; for when a man is justly killed, the Law kills him, not you: can it likewise be said: "when a man justly lies, the Law lies"?

But the examples make the question most difficult. The Egyptian midwives lied, and God rewarded them with good things[^122]; Rahab lied on behalf of the spies of the land, and was therefore freed[^123]. Or, from the time it was said in the Law, *you shall not lie,* is it to be understood that not even in such a cause is a lie permitted, as that in which Rahab is read to have lied? But it is more credible that, because the lie was unjust, it was therefore forbidden; not that, because it was forbidden, it was therefore made unjust. Perhaps, then, as we said of the midwives, what was rewarded in them was not that they lied, but that they freed the Hebrew infants, so that on account of this mercy that sin was made venial, yet not so that it should be thought not to have been a sin: so too of Rahab it is to be understood that the freeing of the spies was rewarded in her, so that on account of that same freeing pardon was given to the lie. But where pardon is given, it is clear there is a sin. But this must be guarded against, lest anyone thus think that, even for other sins, if they be done for the freeing of men, pardon can thus be granted. For many intolerable and too detestable evils follow from this error.

## Concerning the words: You shall not harm your neighbor.

**69.** (19, 13) *You shall not harm your neighbor.* If it were clear to men what it is to harm and not to harm, this general precept would perhaps suffice for keeping innocence. For all the things that are forbidden to be committed against a neighbor are to be referred to this one thing that was said: *you shall not harm your neighbor.* For as to what follows, *you shall not seize by force,* unless it be referred to this, that one should not be harmed by seizing, it sometimes happens that someone harms by not seizing. For a sword must be seized from a madman, and if one does not act as he ought, he will rather have harmed.

## How it is added: Your hand shall not be avenged.

**70.** (19, 17-18) What does it mean that, when he had said above: *you shall not hold your brother in hatred in your mind: reproving you shall reprove your neighbor, and you shall not incur sin on his account,* he consequently added: *and your hand shall not be avenged*? Is it for what is meant by "shall not be punished"? For you act with a good mind when you impose discipline on a sinning neighbor, lest you incur his sin by neglecting him. For to this pertains what he set down before: *you shall not hold your neighbor in hatred in your mind.* For it can seem to the one who is reproved that you hate him, though it is not in your mind. Or does *your hand shall not be avenged* rather warn this, that you should not seek to have your hand avenged, nor be carried away by the lust for revenge? For what else is it to wish to be avenged, but to rejoice or take comfort in another's evil? And therefore it was said: *you shall not be angry at the children of your people.* For thus has anger rightly been defined, as being the lust for revenge. But some codices have: *and your hand shall not avenge*: that is, that by reproving you should not wish to avenge yourself, but rather to look after him whom you reprove.

## God forbids the custom of cutting the body.

**71.** (19, 28) *And you shall not make cuttings over a soul in your body. He said over a soul,* "over the funeral of a dead person": for grief is for the soul that has departed. To this grief pertains mourning, in which mourning some nations have the custom of cutting their bodies. God forbids this to be done.

## Concerning the words: So that the rulers commit fornication from their people.

**72.** (20, 5) *So that they commit fornication after the rulers from their people.* Not that the rulers are from their people, but that they commit fornication from their people. For he means here those rulers to be understood who were worshiped in place of gods: as the Apostle says: *according to the ruler of the power of the air*[^124]; and in the Gospel the Lord: *now the ruler of this world has been cast out*[^125]; and: *behold, the ruler of the world will come, and in me he will find nothing*[^126].

## Concerning the words: Whatever man shall commit adultery with the wife of a man, and so forth.

**73.** (20, 10) *A man, any man whatever who shall commit adultery with the wife of a man, or whoever shall commit adultery with the wife of his neighbor, let them die the death*: he said in the plural, *let them die the death,* that is, he who committed adultery, and she who was adulterated. Here he meant some difference between any man whatever and a neighbor, although in many places he puts *neighbor* for "every man."

But what kind of expression is this, that, having already spoken of a man, he repeated the same about a neighbor, when it is a consequence that one ought all the more to abstain from a neighbor's wife, if one ought to abstain from the wife of any man whatever? For if he had spoken first of a neighbor, then, lest it be thought permitted to commit adultery with the wife of a non-neighbor, it would have had to be added about any man whatever: but as it is, if what is less is not permitted, how much less is permitted what is the greater evil? For if it is not permitted to commit adultery with the wife of any man whatever, how much more with a neighbor's?

Or does this repetition perhaps as it were explain what was said first, so that a man may understand how great an evil it is to commit adultery with the wife of a man, because, if he does this, he commits adultery with a neighbor's wife? For every man is a neighbor to a man.

## Concerning the words: And the woman who shall approach any animal to be mounted by it, and so forth.

**74.** (20, 16) *And the woman who shall approach any animal to be mounted by it, you shall kill the woman and the animal: let them die the death; they are guilty.* It is asked how an animal can be guilty, since it is irrational and in no way capable of the Law. Or is it that, just as words are transferred by the manner of speech which in Greek is called μεταφορά, from the animate to the inanimate, as the wind is called "wicked," or the sea "angry"; so here too there is a transfer from the rational to the irrational? For the animals are, it must be believed, ordered to be killed because, contaminated by such an outrage, they bring back to mind the unworthy memory of the deed.

## What "to see" signifies, when it is said: He who sees the nakedness of his sister.

**75.** (20, 17) *Whoever takes his sister, by his father or by his mother, and sees her nakedness &lt;and she sees his nakedness&gt;; it is a disgrace: they shall be cut off in the sight of their kindred. He has uncovered the nakedness of his sister, they shall bear their sin.* What does he mean in this passage by *sees,* if not "has known by lying with"? as in the Law it is said: *he knew his wife*[^127], for "he was joined with her." And what does he mean by *they shall bear their sin,* when he was speaking of their punishment, if not that he meant to call the punishment of sin itself *sin*?

## In what degree of kinship a kinswoman is forbidden.

**76.** 1. (20, 20. 25) *Whoever lies with his kinswoman has uncovered the nakedness of his kinship: they shall die without children.* It is asked how far this kinship is to be understood, since from a distant degree it is surely permitted to take a wife, and always has been permitted. But it is to be understood that, from those degrees which he forbade, it is not permitted, and that it was said with reference to them: *whoever lies with his kinswoman*: where he also left some unmentioned ones to be understood, such as a sister by both parents, such as the wife of the mother's brother, that is, of the maternal uncle. For about the wife of the paternal uncle he forbade it first, although this is declared to be not kinship but affinity.

But what does *they shall die without children* mean; since children both were born before from unions of this kind, and are born even today? Or is this to be understood as established by the Law of God, that whoever are born of them should not be reckoned as children, that is, should succeed to their parents by no right?

**76.** 2. *And you shall not make your souls abominable in cattle, and in birds, and in all the creeping things of the earth, which I have separated for you as unclean.* Here he seems to signify that these things are not unclean by nature, but by some sign of sacred symbolism; since he says, *which I have separated for you as unclean,* as though they would not be unclean to them, if they had not been separated for them.

## Whether the stoning is commanded, or only of the one having a familiar spirit, or of the woman who has one.

**77.** (20, 27) *And a man or a woman, if perhaps there be in him one having a familiar spirit, or an enchanter, let them both die the death: you shall stone them with stones; they are guilty.* Whether the man and the woman, or the man and the one having a familiar spirit, or the woman and the one having a familiar spirit or the enchanter. But rather this: both the one who has it, and the one whom he has.

## Concerning the words: They shall not take a harlot, because he is holy.

**78.** (21, 7-8) *They shall not take a harlot and a profane woman, and a woman cast out by her husband; because he is holy to the Lord his God.* Above he had said, *they shall not take*; but now, *because he is holy,* not "because they are holy": he was speaking of the several priests who are at one time, and of each one of them he said, *because he is holy*; by the manner of speech Scripture is wont to use. For afterward he mentions that one high priest who entered into the Holy of Holies. For he also concluded in the plural, saying: *and you shall sanctify him: he shall offer the gifts of the Lord your God; he is holy, because I the Lord am holy, who sanctify them.* But as far as the gifts are concerned, since he said, *he shall offer the gifts of the Lord your God,* not only that high priest offered, but also the priests of second rank. And through this, as for his saying, *they shall not take a harlot and a profane woman and one cast out by her husband,* the priests of second rank too were forbidden this: for of the high priest he speaks afterward, who was also commanded to take none but a virgin[^128].

## Who is the great priest among his brethren.

**79.** (21, 10) *And the great priest from among his brethren*: that is, he who is great among his brethren, namely that one great priest. *Upon whose head was poured of the anointing oil*: Scripture calls the oil itself *the anointing.*

## What garments the consecrated one is said to put on.

**80.** (21, 10) *And whose hands have been consecrated to put on the garments*: those, surely, which are most elaborately described in the priestly vestment[^129].

## What it means: He shall not uncover his head of the turban.

**81.** (21, 10-11) *He shall not uncover his head of the turban, and shall not rend his garments, and shall not go in over any dead soul.* It is understood that he is forbidden to do in mourning the things he mentioned above, that is, to bare his head of the turban, and to rend his garments. For rending the garments belonged to the mourning of the ancients: as is written of Job, when the ruin of his sons, crushed, was announced to him[^130]. But baring the head of the turban could be a mourner's act for this reason, that it is the removal of an adornment.

But as for his saying, *he shall not go in over any dead soul*; how he calls a dead body a dead soul is difficult to understand: yet this is the usual idiom of the Scriptures, which is most unusual to us. The name of its ruler, then, the body too receives when bereft of soul, since it is to be restored to it in the resurrection; just as the building which is called a church is no less called a church even when the Church, which is human beings, has gone out from it. But since the body does not receive the name of soul in a living man, it is strange how it should then be called soul, when it has lost its soul. Further, if we understand a dead soul as one separated from the body, so that he seems to have called the separation itself death—that is, that the soul is dead in being severed from the body, not lost in its own nature (for not even when we are said to be dead to sin[^131] is the nature said to have perished, but that we no longer make use of sin: so that the soul is thus understood as dead, that is, dead to the body, because it has ceased to use it, while it lives in its own nature)—how can anyone go in over a dead soul, which this priest is forbidden, when whoever goes in goes in over a dead body, not over a soul which has departed from the body?

Or did he call by the name of *soul* the temporal life itself, which surely is dead in the deceased body, when that soul which cannot die has departed? Not that the life itself was a soul; but that, through the presence of the soul, by which it subsisted, it received the soul's name: as we distinguished when we spoke of blood[^132], why it was said: *the soul of all flesh is its blood*[^133]. For the blood itself too is dead in the body of a dead man: for it did not depart with the departing soul. Scripture, then, forbade the high priest to go in even over the funeral of father or mother; which it did not forbid to the second priest. For it follows: *he shall not be defiled over his father and over his mother.* But the order of the words is: "he shall not be defiled over his father, nor over his mother."

## How it is said: And he shall not go out from the holy places.

**82.** (21, 12) *And he shall not go out from the holy places*: at that time, beyond doubt, when the funerals of his own were being held, just as during the seven days in which he was being sanctified, he is forbidden to go out from the holy places[^134]; but not always. Indeed, if the high priests of that time were not forbidden to take wives or to beget children, a great question arises.

Since the Law calls a man unclean until evening even from conjugal intercourse, even when he has washed his body with water[^135]; and the high priest is commanded, on account of the continual incense, to go in within the veil twice daily, every day[^136], where the altar of incense was, and it was not lawful for anyone unclean to approach the holy things: how did the high priest fulfill this daily, if he was begetting children? For if illness befell him, who would discharge his office in his place, if one should ask; it can be answered that by the grace of God he did not fall ill: can it likewise be answered so concerning the begetting of children? Whence it follows as a consequence that either he was continent, or on some days the incense was interrupted; or, if it could not be interrupted, since it had to be placed only by the high priest, he did not become unclean by conjugal intercourse, by the merit of his special sanctification. Or, if there pertains to him too what he says in what follows about all the sons of Aaron, that none of them should approach the holy things if any uncleanness has befallen him[^137]; then it surely remains to take it that on some days the incense was not placed.

## How he is forbidden to go in over his father's funeral.

**83.** (21, 11) But as for the high priest being forbidden to go in over his father's funeral, it may be asked how he could already be high priest, his father not yet being dead, since he commands them to succeed their fathers. For this reason it was necessary, the high priest not yet being buried, that a priest should at once be substituted on account of the continual incense, which had to be placed daily by the high priest.

Although that question about the illness of the high priest remains, if even one about to die had necessarily to be ill for some days: unless perhaps this too be solved by saying that high priests were not wont to die except suddenly, no illness preceding; as Scripture testifies of Aaron himself[^138].

## Concerning the words: I am the Lord who sanctify him the priest.

**84.** (21, 15) It is to be noted how often he says, *I am the Lord, who sanctify him,* speaking of the priest; when he said this to Moses too: *and you shall sanctify him*[^139]. How, then, do both Moses sanctify and the Lord? For Moses is not in place of the Lord: but Moses sanctifies by visible sacraments through his ministry; the Lord, however, by invisible grace through the Holy Spirit, where is the whole fruit even of the visible sacraments. For without this sanctification of invisible grace, what do the visible sacraments avail? But it is rightly asked whether this invisible sanctification too, without the visible sacraments by which a man is visibly sanctified, likewise avails nothing: which is surely absurd. For one might more tolerably say that, without the former, the latter does not exist, than that, if it exists, it does not avail; since in the latter is all the usefulness of the former.

But even this, that the latter cannot exist without the former, how it may rightly be said is to be considered. For visible Baptism availed nothing to Simon Magus, who lacked invisible sanctification[^140]: but those in whom this invisible sanctification was present, and availed, had likewise received the visible sacraments, being baptized in like manner. And yet it is not shown where Moses, who visibly sanctified the priests, was himself sanctified by those very sacrifices or by the oil: but who would dare to deny that he was invisibly sanctified, in whom such great grace was preeminent? This can be said of John the Baptist too: for he appeared as a baptizer before he was baptized[^141]. Whence we can by no means deny that he was sanctified: yet we do not find this done in him visibly before he came to the ministry of baptizing. This too of that thief, to whom, crucified with him, the Lord said: *today you shall be with me in paradise*[^142]. For neither was he, without invisible sanctification, endowed with such great felicity.

Accordingly it is gathered that invisible sanctification was present to some and availed them without the visible sacraments, which have been changed according to the diversity of times, so that there were some then and others now: but that visible sanctification, which is done through the visible sacraments, without that invisible one can be present, but cannot avail. Yet the visible sacrament is not therefore to be despised: for one who despises it can in no way be invisibly sanctified. Hence it is that Cornelius and those who were with him, when they now appeared sanctified by the Holy Spirit invisibly poured out, were nevertheless baptized[^143]: nor was the visible sanctification, which the invisible had already preceded, judged superfluous.

## It is commanded that a priest must be clean, whoever wishes to approach the holy things.

**85.** (22, 1-3) *And the Lord spoke to Moses, saying: Say to Aaron and his sons, and let them take heed concerning the holy things of the children of Israel, and they shall not profane my holy name, in the things which they sanctify to me. I am the Lord. And you shall say to them: Throughout your generations, every man whoever of all your seed shall approach the holy things, whatever the children of Israel shall have sanctified to the Lord, and his uncleanness be upon him, that soul shall be cut off from me. I am the Lord your God.*

All doubt is removed, that none of the priests, whether high or second, ought to approach the holy things, if his uncleanness were upon him. The continence of the priest was therefore a consequence, lest on account of the begetting of children on some days the continual incense should not be placed, which was wont to be placed only by the high priests twice a day, morning and evening[^144]: since after conjugal intercourse, even with the body washed, he was unclean until evening[^145], by whom it had to be placed. But as for his saying, *which the children of Israel sanctify,* it is to be understood as offering to the priests to be offered by them to the Lord.

And the kind of sanctification is to be noted which is done by the vow and devotion of the one offering. But whether, just as in this way the things offered by men are sanctified, so too the men themselves are said to sanctify themselves in the same way, when they vow themselves in some matter, is to be observed in the Scriptures.

## What the uncleanness of a soul is.

**86.** (22, 4) *And he who touches any uncleanness of a soul*: that is, anything dead, the touching of which, according to the Law, defiles.

## To curse God or to name the name of the Lord is forbidden with a difference.

**87.** (24, 15-16) *A man, a man, if he curse his God, shall incur sin: but he who names the name of the Lord, let him die the death*: as though it were one thing to curse one's God, another to name the name of the Lord; and the former were sin, but the latter so great a wickedness that it is even worthy of death. Although in this passage what is said, *the name of the Lord,* is to be so understood that it is done with a curse, that is, that he names it by cursing. What, then, is the difference between that sin and this crime of so great wickedness? Or did he perhaps, by repeating this very thing, show that the former is not a light sin, but so great a wickedness that it is to be punished with death? But because he brought this in by way of distinction, not saying "for he who names," but *but he who names*; it has become somewhat obscure. And therefore, if this is rightly understood, the kind of expression too is to be noted.

## How the soul of a man is said to be struck.

**88.** (24, 17) *And a man who strikes any soul of a man, and he die, let him die the death.* He does not say: "whoever strikes a man, and he die," but, *the soul of a man*; although rather the body of a man is struck by the striker, as the Lord too says: *do not fear those who kill the body*[^146]. By the manner, then, that Scripture is wont to use, he calls "soul" the very life of the body, which is by the soul, and from this he meant to show the murderer, in that he strikes the soul of a man, that is, by striking deprives a man of life. Why, then, did he add: *and he die,* if he already shows the murder by this very thing, that he struck the soul of a man, that is, that the man was deprived of life by the striker? Or did he mean to explain how it is to be taken, what he had said, the soul of a man struck, and so says: *and he die,* as though he said, "that is, he died"? for this is what it is for the soul of a man to have been struck.

## How it is to be understood: When you enter into the land which I give you, and it rests, and so forth.

**89.** (25, 2-7) *When you enter into the land which I give you, and the land which I give you rests, the Sabbaths of the Lord. Six years you shall sow your field, and six years you shall prune your vine, and shall gather its fruit: but in the seventh year a Sabbath, a rest shall be for the land, a Sabbath to the Lord.*

How is it to be understood: *when you enter into the land which I give you, and the land rests; six years you shall sow your field,* and so forth? As though the command were to be carried out then, when the land has rested; whereas the land rests for the very reason that this is done. For he means the rest of the land to be understood in the seventh year, in which he commanded no one to do any work on it by agriculture. But surely the long hyperbaton creates obscurity for this sense.

This, then, seems to be the order of the words: *when you enter into the land which I give you, and the land which I give you rests, the Sabbaths of the Lord: the things that spring up of themselves you shall not reap your field, and the grape of your sanctification you shall not gather: it shall be a year of resting for the land. And the Sabbaths of the land shall be food for you, and for your manservant, and your maidservant, and your hired servant, and the sojourner who has attached himself to you, and your cattle, and the beasts that are in your land—all that is born of it for food.*

But he inserted, by way of explanation, how the land rests, and said: *six years you shall sow your field, and six years you shall prune your vine, and shall gather its fruit: but in the seventh year a Sabbath, a rest shall be for the land, the Sabbaths of the Lord. Your field you shall not sow, and your vine you shall not prune.*

And by his saying, *you shall not prune,* we ought to take all cultivation as forbidden in that year. For if it is not to be pruned, neither is it to be plowed, or propped with supports, nor is anything else to be applied that pertains to its cultivation: but, as the whole is wont to be understood from a part, so by pruning all cultivation is signified. And by field and vine, since he forbade both the one to be sown and the other to be pruned, every kind of field is to be understood. For neither in an olive grove, nor in a field of any other kind, about which he kept silent, is anything to be worked.

But as for his saying, *and the Sabbaths of the land shall be food for you, and for your manservant, and your maidservant,* and so forth, he made clear enough that the owner of the field too is not forbidden to eat the things which, no cultivation being applied, grow of their own accord in that year, but is forbidden to gather them as produce. So, then, he was permitted to take something from it for food as one passing by, so as to take only what he would at once consume by eating, not what he would store for use.

## How that: And the land shall not be sold into profanation.

**90.** (25, 23) *And the land shall not be sold into profanation*: other codices have, *into confirmation*; which corruption I think occurred first in the Greek in one or the other, on account of the similar sound of the word: for βεβήλωσις means "profanation"; but βεβαίωσις, "confirmation." But the former sense is clear: *and the land shall not be sold into profanation*: that is, lest anyone dare to sell the land, which he received from God, to profane persons, who would use it for impiety and the worship of strange and false gods. But the latter is somewhat obscure: *the land shall not be sold into confirmation*: which I think is to be understood only as, lest the sale be so confirmed that the seller does not receive it back at the time of release, as has been commanded. But what follows can suit either sense, whether it be read: *and the land shall not be sold into profanation,* or, *into confirmation.* For he went on and added: *for the land is mine, because you are proselytes and sojourners before me.*

## How that: You shall give a price for the land.

**91.** (25, 24) *And throughout all the land of your possession you shall give a price for the land*: but other codices have, *you shall give a redemption for the land.* The sense, then, is this: *the land shall not be sold into profanation,* that is, to those who would use it to the injury of the Creator; or *into confirmation,* that is, so that the buyer should possess it perpetually, and not restore it to the seller at the fixed interval of years according to God's precept. *For the land,* he says, *is mine*: whence according to my precept you ought to use it.

And to show that it was his, not theirs, what they themselves were in it he consequently added, saying: *because you are proselytes and sojourners before me*: that is: Although there be proselytes to you, that is, newcomers, who from foreigners are joined to your nation; and sojourners, that is, not remaining in their own land: yet you all too are before me newcomers and sojourners. God says this either to the Israelites, because he gave them the land of other nations, which he had expelled: or to every man, since before God, who always remains and, as it is written, fills heaven and earth[^147], surely by his presence, every man is a newcomer by being born, and a sojourner by living; since he is compelled to migrate by dying.

## How that which he adds: as sojourners, a redemption.

**92.** (25, 24) Then he adds and says: *throughout all the land of your possession you shall give a price for the land,* as sojourners, or *a redemption.* By this, unless I am mistaken, he means to be understood what they rendered in a way from it, through the cessations of each seventh year, and of the fiftieth year[^148], which he calls the year of release; so that the very lying fallow of the land was, as it were, the price of dwelling or the redemption from him whose it is, that is, from its Creator God.

## What the soul of God is.

**93.** (26, 11) *And I will set my tabernacle among you, and my soul will not abominate you.* By his soul God means his will. For he is not an animal having a body and a soul; nor is his substance of the kind that his creation is, which is called a soul, which he made, as he himself testifies through Isaiah, saying: *and every breath I have made*[^149]: which that he says of the soul of man, the context makes clear. As, then, when he speaks of his eyes, and his lips, and the other names of bodily members, we surely do not take him to be defined by the form of a body; but we understand all those names of members only as the effects of his operations and powers: so too when he says, *my soul,* we ought to take his will. For that perfect and simple nature which is called God does not consist of body and spirit; nor is it changeable even in its spirit, as the soul is: but *God is spirit,* and always the same, *with whom there is no change*[^150]. From this the Apollinarists took occasion, who say that the Mediator of God and men, the man Christ Jesus[^151], did not have a soul, but that he was only the Word and flesh, since he said: *my soul is sorrowful even unto death*[^152]: but by his very action, which is declared to us through the Gospel history, the offices of a human soul so appear that to doubt of this would be the part of a madman.

## It is said that the sword will consume the transgressors of the law, if they are in the land of their enemies.

**94.** (26, 33. 36) What does it mean that, when God was threatening the penalties of disobedience, he said among other things: *and the sword shall consume you as it ranges about*; then he says: *and your land shall be deserted, and your cities shall be deserted: then the land shall well perceive its Sabbaths, all the days of its desolation; and you shall be in the land of your enemies*? How, then, shall the sword consume them, if they shall be in the land of their enemies? Or shall it consume them in their own land, since, the slaughter of deaths having been made, they will not be there? Or does he say *the sword shall consume you* as if he said, "it shall kill you," so that to this consuming belong those who shall fall by the sword, not all: since a little later he says: *and upon those of you who are left I will bring fear into their heart*? Or is it said by hyperbole, *the sword shall consume you*: by which manner of speech their abundance too was said to be like the sand of the sea[^153]? In this manner is said too what follows: *and the sound of a flying leaf shall pursue them*: that is, because there shall be in them such great fear that they dread even the lightest things.
