# On the deed of Moses, when he killed the Egyptian.

**2.** (2:12) On the deed of Moses, when he killed the Egyptian to defend his brothers, we have argued sufficiently in that work which we wrote on the life of the Patriarchs against Faustus: whether the disposition in him was praiseworthy, by which he committed this sin—as the richness of the soil is wont to be praised, even before useful seeds, by a certain fertility of plants, however useless—or whether the deed itself is to be justified altogether[^1]. Which seems not to be, because he as yet bore no lawful power, neither received divinely nor ordained by human society. Yet, as Stephen says in the *Acts of the Apostles*, he thought his brothers understood that through him God was giving them deliverance[^2]: so that by this testimony Moses seems to have been able to dare this, already admonished divinely (which Scripture is silent about in that place).

[^1]: See Contra Faust. 22, 70.
[^2]: See Acts 7:25.
