# To Orosius: A Refutation of the Priscillianists and Origenists

To dig out and uproot the root of a most loquacious error is the shorter way.

1.1. To answer you when you ask, my most beloved son Orosius, I neither ought to reply to everything you have set down in your memorandum, nor ought I to fail to reply at all, lest I seem to have despised your zeal—which is most pleasing to me—and thereby give offense intemperately. For in certain of our little works, which you either have read or will be able to read, many things have been said that may avail against the heresy of the Priscillianists, even though I had not set out to refute them; but while I was doing something else, I now understand that I was doing this too, now that I hear from you what they hold. For what I have argued in many places against the Manicheans concerning the soul—that although it is in its own way immortal, yet it is shown to be changeable, by falling away to the worse and advancing to the better, and through this it is most clearly shown not to be the substance of God—at the same time also overturns the teaching of Priscillian. For once this one thing is most firmly established, which is both most true and which each person, once reminded, most easily recognizes in himself, neither those nor these find anything from which to weave their fables. But what need is there to go pruning the branches of a most loquacious error, the root of which it is the shorter way to dig out and uproot—especially since you yourself rejoice that those most fabulous ravings have now been refuted among you?

## Because the soul was made and is not the nature of God, the heresy of Priscillian, which contends that the soul was originally the nature of God, is thereby refuted.

2.2. But the question concerning the soul is still being debated—whether, since it is now established that it is not a portion or an emanation of him, God should be said to have created it from nothing, seeing that it appears harsh and impious to say that the will of God, by whose willing it was created, is nothing—this indeed no longer pertains to refuting the sacrilegious folly of Priscillian. For whether the soul was made from nothing, or whether it should not be said to have been made from nothing because it was made by the will of God, which will is certainly not nothing—nevertheless, because it was made and is not the nature of God, that heresy is surely refuted which contends that the soul was originally the nature of God, so that it might have a basis from which to connect whatever else of falsehood it adds on. But because we ought not to scorn this question either, nor to leave it undiscussed, we must inquire of those who for this reason refuse to believe that the soul was made from nothing, lest they assert that the will of God by which it was made is nothing—we must inquire, then, whether they grant that no creation at all was made from nothing. For if they think this, it is to be feared that they may try to introduce some other nature, which is neither God nor yet nothing, which God must have had as a kind of matter lying ready beneath him; otherwise he could not have found anything from which to make whatever he made. For when it is asked from what God made his creation, some sort of matter is sought, as wood or any other body is for a carpenter, which unless he has it, he will be wholly unable to make the things that are demanded of his craft. When, therefore, the answer is given, "from nothing," what else is answered but that there was no matter lying ready beneath him which he himself had not made, so that he might have something from which to make whatever he willed, and which unless he had it he could not make? For the matter of the world, which is in some fashion discerned in changeable things, was established by the very one by whom the world was established. Wherefore, even if God made or makes anything from some other thing, yet he did not make it or make it from a thing which he himself had not made. For which reason, setting the nature of the soul aside for a little, if they grant that God made something from nothing, let them attend and see that, whatever that something is, he made it by his will; for he would not have made anything unwillingly; nor yet, for that reason, is that same will nothing, because by his will something was made by him from nothing. Why, then, do they fear to say of the soul what they do not hesitate to say of any other things whatever? Or, if they hold that the soul alone was made by the will of God, do they contend that he did not make the rest by his will? What more absurd, what more insane could be said? But if he certainly made by his will all the things he made—and yet, when we say this, we do not say that that same will is nothing—let them accept this concerning the soul as well.

## When it is said, God made from nothing, nothing else is said than: There was no source from which to make it, and yet, because he willed it, he made it.

3.3. For when it is said, *God made from nothing*[^1], nothing else is said than: There was no source from which to make it, and yet, because he willed it, he made it. So far, then, is the will not said to be nothing in that which he made from nothing, that it is especially commended. For to him it is said: *For it is in your power to act whenever you will*[^2]: whether there be a source from which it may come to be, or there be none, the will suffices where the power is supreme. How, then, in that which is created from nothing, is the will of the Creator said to be nothing, when something can be created from nothing precisely because the will of the Creator suffices for itself even without matter? But if perhaps they wish to acknowledge that not only the soul but no creation at all was made from nothing—on the ground that whatever God made, he made by his will, which will is certainly not nothing—let them consider whence man was made according to the body. For certainly, as Scripture testifies, God made him from the mud or from the dust of the earth, and beyond doubt made him by his will[^3], and yet the will of God is not dust or mud. Just as, then, in that which was made from mud, although it was made by his will, the will is not mud, so in that which was made from nothing, although it was made by his will, the will is not nothing.

## That men fell from the heresy of Priscillian into the error of Origen.

4.4. But now, as for what you have added that is to be lamented—that among you men fell from the heresy of Priscillian into the error of Origen, and could not be healed of that plague unless the very medicine had introduced something diseased—this grief is indeed not to be blamed: for truth, not falsehood, ought to have driven out falsehood, since to do this is to change the evil, not to escape it. Nevertheless, because you recall that concerning God himself, the founder of all created things—that is, concerning the coeternal and unchangeable Trinity itself—those who brought the books of Origen to you handed down what the truth holds (against which truth Priscillian restored the ancient Sabellian dogma, in which the Father himself is held to be the Son and also the Holy Spirit—this man being worse in that he also holds such a view of the soul that he refuses to let it have its own proper nature, but dares to affirm with the Manicheans that it flows down from God himself as a kind of portion, and is so foully defiled and changed for the worse)—your province has gained no little good from those books, especially in that matter in which the error is most grave; for it is concerning the Creator, not concerning the creation, that such false and such wicked things are held. Whether, then, those who had strayed have been restored to this faith, or those who did not yet know it have learned it by reading those disputations, let them rejoice to have learned this, which is sound. But as for what is also erroneous in them—although I see that you have already come to know it—nevertheless how such things are to be argued against you will be able to learn better there, where the error itself both long ago arose and not long ago was exposed.

## On the final sentence of the supreme and most truthful judge.

5.5. As far as I can, however, I too admonish you to dare to think nothing about the correction of the devil and his angels and their restoration to their former state—not because we envy the devil and the demons and in that way repay, as it were, malevolence in kind, since they, driven by nothing but the goads of envy, try to disturb our journeys by which we make our way to God, but because, out of our own presumption, we ought to add nothing to the final sentence of the supreme and most truthful judge. For he himself foretold that he would say to those like them: *Go into the eternal fire, which is prepared for the devil and his angels*[^4]*.* Nor ought it to move us, so as to take "eternal" here for "long-lasting," that elsewhere it is written: *Forever and unto the age of the age*[^5]. ## Those are called eternal times which have before them no time.

5.6. And perhaps, indeed, according to the usage of the Scriptures, these people may find something said to be  
which is not without end, as God in the books of the Old Testament constantly says: *This shall be a perpetual ordinance for you*[^6]—which the Greek has as  
—when he often gave such commands in this way concerning sacred rites that were going to have an end; although, if the words are understood more carefully, the things perhaps which were signified by those rites were not going to have an end. For so—not to wander further—we call God eternal, certainly not these two very brief syllables, but that which is signified by them. The Apostle spoke of eternal times[^7] that are prior and ancient, which in Greek reads: . For writing to Titus he says: *In hope of eternal life, which God, who does not lie, promised before eternal times*[^8]*.* But since the times reckoned backward seem to have their beginning from the foundation of the world, how are they eternal, unless because he called those eternal which have before them no time?

## On the punishment of the wicked that is without end, and on the eternal life that the righteous are to receive.

6.7. But whoever prudently notes that what was said, *Go into the eternal fire*, was said of that which has no end, proves it from the contrary in that very gospel passage concerning the eternal life that the righteous are to receive: for that too does not have an end. For he concluded thus: *So these will go into eternal burning; but the righteous into eternal life*[^9]. In both the Greek has  
. If mercy prompts us to believe that the punishment of the wicked will not be without end, what are we going to believe concerning the reward of the righteous, since in both parts, in the same place, in the same sentence, in the same word, eternity is pronounced? Or shall we say again that the righteous too will relapse, from that sanctification and eternal life, into the uncleanness of sinners and into death[^10]? Far be this from the soundness of the Christian faith. Both, therefore, are called eternal as being without end—that is,  
—lest, while we grieve over the punishments of the devil, we should doubt concerning the kingdom of Christ. Finally, if "eternal" and "eternale"—that is,  
and —are accustomed to be placed in the Scriptures, under either sense, sometimes without end and sometimes with an end, what shall we reply concerning those words of the prophet, where it is written: *Their worm shall not die, and their fire shall not be quenched*[^11]? Whatever kind of punishment was signified by the name of "worm" and "fire," surely, if it shall not die nor be quenched, it has been foretold as without end; nor was the prophet doing anything else, when he said this, but foretelling it as without end.

## On the kingdom of Christ, which will not have an end.

7.8. Concerning the kingdom of Christ also—not according to that by which in the beginning he was the Word, God with God[^12] (for from that no one ever doubted that he is the king of all ages[^13]), but according to the assumption of manhood and the sacrament of the mediator[^14] and the incarnation from the Virgin—it has been said most plainly that it will not have an end, where the angel, speaking to Mary, his future mother who would yet remain a virgin, says among other things: *He will be great and will be called the Son of the Most High, and the Lord God will give him the throne of David his father, and he will reign over the house of Jacob forever, and of his kingdom there will be no end*[^15]. In what respect will *there be no end of his kingdom*, unless according to that by which *he will reign over the house of Jacob*? For what he says, *forever*, he expounded, on account of that very ambiguity, by adding *of his kingdom there will be no end*, lest anyone should here take "forever" in the way that an "age" will at some point have an end. But can the kingdom over the house of Jacob and on the throne of David be understood except in the Church and in that people which is his kingdom? Of which the Apostle also says: *When he hands over the kingdom to God and the Father*[^16]—that is, when he has led his saints to the contemplation of the Father, and certainly to his own as well, according to that by which he is God equal to the Father. For he does not so hand it over that he himself loses it, since the Father too gave to the Son *to have life in himself*[^17], nor did he himself lose it. And through this, if *of his kingdom there will be no end*, then surely his saints, who are his kingdom, will reign with him without end[^18]. But where the Apostle says in that place, *Then the end, when he hands over the kingdom to God and the Father*, there he signifies an end that does not consume but perfects. As it was said: *For Christ is the end of the law, unto righteousness for everyone who believes*[^19]—by which the law is perfected, not by which it is abolished. This he signifies there too, where he says: *I have not come to abolish the law, but to fulfill it*[^20].

## That the world was made great and good by the goodness of God, by the supreme and uncreated Good.

8.9. But as for what they say concerning the rational creatures—that is, the holy angels and the unclean demons and the very minds of men—that a lesser fault earned a greater place, I do not see with what effrontery they try to persuade the Church of Christ of it. We therefore better believe that God was not led to the construction of the world by the sins of rational spirits, lest those absurdities follow: that it would be necessary for us to have two suns, or three, or however many, if a great number of spirits, through free choice, had committed a fault so great that they had to be enclosed in similar globes of the heavenly bodies. Rather, the world was made great and good by the goodness of God, by the supreme and uncreated Good, so that in it all things came to be very good according to their nature, some better than others, arranged in distinct degrees from the highest creatures down to the lowest, in such a way that all things might exist while the more excellent did not exist alone, and that they might have the limit of that number which the Creator God of all the natures of creatures saw was to be set within himself—he who did not learn them once they were made, but knew that they were to be made. Hence what they say, that all things had already been made in his wisdom before they were brought forth into these proper forms and modes and appeared in their own orders, is not soberly said. For when would they have been made, before they were made? Rather, in the wisdom of God the accounts of all the things to be made could exist, yet not the things themselves as made. For all things were made through it; it itself was not also made, since it itself is the Word of whom it is said: *All things were made through him*[^21]. God, therefore, knew all the things he made before he made them. For we cannot say that he made things unknown to him and learned them only once they were made, that he did not know what he was making, but knew what he had made. For if we said this of any human craftsman, we would judge most foolishly. He knew them, therefore, as to be made, not as made; he knew so that he might make them, not because he had made them. Accordingly, although they were already known—since they would not come to be except from one who knew them—nevertheless the things did not begin to be made, which were known in order that they might come to be, except later; the things which were known, in order that they might rightly come to be, before they came to be.

## With what audacity, in the end, it is asserted that the world—that is, heaven and earth—would not have existed except for the necessity of purifying the rational spirits.

8.10. But since the truth—which neither forms opinions about things unknown nor deceives anyone—promises that our purification[^22], indeed our perfection too, even after the resurrection of spiritual bodies, will come to be all the way up to equality with the angels, how are those same holy angels, to whom we, fully purified, will be equal[^23], said still to be in need of being purified from their sins? And since God promises a new heaven and a new earth[^24], a dwelling place for the saints and for those purified from every stain of this age, with what audacity, in the end, is it asserted that the world—that is, heaven and earth—would not have existed except for the necessity of purifying the rational spirits, who would not be either in heaven or on earth except on account of the merits of their sins? What need, then, have those who are purified of a new heaven and a new earth, if the purified are restored to this, that they may be as they were before heaven and earth, without heaven and earth? But if this were altogether true, we ought to stretch our hope all the way to that which Scripture promises. And if from there we were to be transferred to something better, we would much more reasonably learn this then, once we were already placed there, than now not only rashly believe it but even shamelessly try to teach it. And what is more absurd than to say: Heaven and earth would not exist unless the structure of the world were necessary for those to be purified—when Scripture promises to those who are purified another heaven and another earth[^25]?

## That in a certain generic way every creature is in each individual man.

8.11. Further, concerning the sun and the moon and the rest of the stars, that they are heavenly bodies we see; that they are animate, we do not see. Let this be read in the divine books, and we believe it. For the testimony from the Apostle's epistle[^26], which you have set down as something they are accustomed to cite, can be understood even of men alone, since in each individual man every creature exists—not universally, as heaven and earth and all that is in them exist, but in a certain generic way; for there is in him both the rational, which the angels are either proved or believed to have, and, so to speak, the sensible, which the beasts also do not lack (for they use the senses and sense-movements to seek what is useful and avoid what is harmful), and the vital that is deprived of sense, such as can be observed in trees; for in us too the growths of the body come about without our sensing it, and the hairs do not feel it when they are cut, and yet they grow. Moreover, the bodily creation itself appears more evidently in us, which, although it was made and formed from earth, nevertheless has in it certain particles from all the elements of this corporeal world, contributing to the balance of health. For the members are quickened by heat, which comes from fire, whose light also shines forth through the eyes; and by air the coursing of the veins which they call arteries, and the air-passages of the lung, are filled; and were there no moisture, no spittle would flow together, and dryness would consume life. For the blood itself too, in a moist gliding, filling now these veins, now those, is diffused through all parts as if by streams and rivers. Thus there is no kind of creature that cannot be recognized in man; and so every creature groans and grieves in him, awaiting the revelation of the sons of God[^27]. And every creature, even through the resurrection of the body—though not in all men—will nevertheless be freed from the slavery of corruption[^28], because in each individual the whole is present. And if that same passage of the apostolic writings can be better understood in some other way, still it does not follow from those same words that we should believe that the sun and the moon and the stars groan together until at the end of the age they are freed from the slavery of corruption.

## We neither detract from the faith due to the evangelist who writes, nor do we shrink from the voice of the Jew who blasphemes.

9.12. Now since I said, "Let it be read in the divine books, and we believe it," lest perhaps you be misled by what those who assert these things are accustomed to bring forward from the book of holy Job, where it is written: *How shall a man be righteous before the Lord, or how shall one born of woman cleanse himself? If he commands the moon and it does not shine, and the stars are not clean before him; how much more is man rottenness, and the son of man a worm?*[^29] For from this they wish it to be understood both that the stars have a rational spirit and that they are not clean from sin, but are in the heavens for this reason, that a lesser fault earned a greater or better place. I do not think this sentence is to be received as having divine authority: for Job himself did not utter it—he to whom in a singular way testimony is divinely borne that he *did not sin with his lips*[^30] before the Lord—but one of those friends, who were called *all comforters of evils*[^31] and were reproved by the divine sentence. But just as in the Gospel, although it is altogether true that the things were said, yet not all the things that were said are believed to be true—since the truthful writing of the Gospel testifies that many false and impious things were said by the Jews—so in this book, where many persons are narrated to have spoken, not only what is said but also by whom it is said must be considered, lest, indiscriminately receiving the things written in any holy book whatever, we be forced to confess—which God forbid—that the things were true and righteous which the foolish woman suggested to her holy husband, that by speaking something against God he might be freed from that intolerable suffering by dying[^32]. I would not say this as though those friends, reproved by the Lord and rightly blamed by that very holy servant of God, could speak nothing true[^33], but because not all the things they spoke are to be thought true. Although, therefore, they said nothing true against Job, nevertheless even from their words one who knows how to discern things wisely said can take up some sound sentence as testimony to the truth; but when, inquiring, we wish something to be proved to us by the testimony of the holy oracles, let it not be said to us that we must believe even what is written in the Gospel, if perhaps the evangelist records that this was said by one to whom faith is not to be given. For there it was said to the Lord Christ by the Jews: *Do we not say truly that you are a Samaritan and have a demon?*[^34] This voice we detest all the more, the dearer Christ is to us; yet that it was uttered by the Jews we cannot doubt, who believe the gospel narrative to be most true. Thus we neither detract from the faith due to the evangelist who writes, nor do we shrink from the voice of the Jew who blasphemes. And not only to impious and wicked men, but not even to those little ones in the faith and still untrained and unlearned who are perhaps recorded as speaking there, do we accord faith as to canonical authority. For not because that man whose eyes, blind from birth, the Lord had opened, said, *We know that God does not hear sinners*[^35], ought we therefore so to receive that sentence as of gospel authority that we resist the very words of the Lord in the Gospel, who confirmed by his divine voice that the one who said, *Lord, be merciful to me a sinner*, went down from the temple more justified than the Pharisee who recalled and boasted of his own righteous deeds[^36]. Let this man, recently enlightened in the flesh, not be angry that we have said he, in the novitiate of his faith—when he did not yet know who it was who had healed him—put forward a less circumspect sentence, *that God does not hear sinners*: since the Apostles themselves, chosen before the rest and clinging to the Lord's side and hanging upon his mouth, are found to have said many blameworthy things, which it would be tedious to recount, so that blessed Peter, for certain of his words, deserved not only to be rebuked but even to be called Satan[^37].

## By participation in whom they are righteous, in comparison with whom they are not even righteous.

10.13. Although, in comparison with the righteousness of God, if not even the holy angels in heaven are said to be righteous, this does not seem to me an unwarranted statement—not because, in order to be righteous, they fell away from righteousness, but because they were made and are not God, and cannot have as much of the spiritual light as he has from whom they were made. For there is the supreme righteousness where there is the supreme wisdom: and this is God, of whom it is said: *To God alone wise*[^38]. But it is another question how much of that very righteousness the angels grasp and how much they do not grasp. For by participation in whom they are righteous, in comparison with him they are not even righteous.

## However far anyone advances in knowledge, he will find himself below those letters which God set, as it were as a firmament, over all human hearts.

11.14. But it is another question, as I said, this one, and another the question concerning the stars and the sun and the moon, whether they have rational spirits within these conspicuous and shining bodies—bodies which whoever doubts to be bodies is wholly ignorant of what a body is. Nor do these things much pertain to us, that we should take care to investigate with the utmost zeal things that are remote from our senses and from the understanding of human weakness, and that are not so set down in the Scriptures themselves that the knowledge of them is enjoined upon us. Rather, lest by headlong suspicion we rush into sacrilegious fables, the divine Scripture cries out: *Do not seek things too high for you, and do not search into things too strong for you, but think always upon those things which the Lord has commanded you*[^39], so that in these matters a reckless presumption rather than a cautious ignorance should seem to be blamed. Certainly the Apostle says: *Whether thrones or dominions or principalities or powers*[^40]. That there are, therefore, thrones, dominions, principalities, powers in the heavenly array, I most firmly believe, and that they differ among themselves in some way I hold with undoubting faith; but—so that you may despise me, whom you think to be a great teacher—what these things may be and how they differ among themselves I do not know. Nor do I think I am imperiled by that ignorance, as I would be by disobedience, if I were to neglect the Lord's commands; and therefore I think that by the Spirit of God, through our authors, the writers of the holy oracles, these things were not fully set forth, but touched in passing and lightly grazed, so that if to anyone such as we are something of this kind has perhaps been shown through a higher revelation, he should not believe that those were inferior through whom the holy proclamations of the canonical Scriptures were ministered to us. For however far anyone advances in knowledge, he will find himself below those letters which God set, as it were as a firmament, over all human hearts. There is no need, therefore, *to be wiser, but to be wise unto sobriety, as God has apportioned to each the measure of faith*[^41]. These things will perhaps be taught you by more learned men, if you bring to them as great a knowledge for learning as you have care for knowing, lest you suppose things unknown to be known, lest you believe what is not to be believed or fail to believe what is to be believed. Rather, that one Master[^42] and true one will teach you, whether through them or by whatever means he wills—he who, as you labor for his Church, looks within you, where he has also bestowed this upon you: he himself will unlock the truth more fully, who beholds the charity that knocks—the charity which he has deigned to give.
