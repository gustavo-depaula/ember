# Book II

## Which the Apostle himself did not do, but judged them to be old wives' tales.

**1. 1.** Now those things must be sifted which this man thinks support him from the books of the New Testament against the prophetic writings, as though the Apostles of Christ had condemned them by their own opinion. For that "he thinks the Apostle called" the divine "words of the Law and the Prophets profane and old wives' tales and endless genealogies, because he said, *Avoid profane and old wives' tales;* and in another place, *Do not give heed to Jewish fables and endless genealogies, which furnish questions rather than edification*"—who but a heretic, very blind, would so err? For why did the Apostle himself not do this, if he judged these to be old wives' tales? Why does he say to the Galatians: *Tell me, you who wish to be under the Law: have you not heard the Law? For it is written that Abraham had two sons, one by a slave woman and another by a free woman; which things are an allegory: for these are the two Testaments.* And to the Corinthians: *For I do not want you to be ignorant, brothers, that our fathers were all under the cloud and all passed through the sea, and all were baptized into Moses in the cloud and in the sea, and all ate the same spiritual food, and all drank the same spiritual drink: for they drank from the spiritual rock following them; and the rock was Christ?*

## The Jews have, besides the legitimate and prophetic Scriptures, certain traditions of their own.

**1. 2.** But he does not know that the Jews have, besides the legitimate and prophetic Scriptures, certain traditions of their own, which they do not have written but hold by memory, and which one transfers to another by speaking—which they call the Deuterosis—where they even dare to say and believe that God created two women for the first man; from which they weave genealogies that are truly, as the Apostle says, endless, bringing forth most unfruitful questions. But if it never happened to this man to hear such things, ought he also to have become so deaf to the Gospel that he did not notice the words of the Lord Christ, by which he reproved the Jews because they taught their sons impiety about not honoring their parents? For there he also recalled, by way of attestation, the precept of God which is written in the Law. Nor did he reprove them for anything else than that they rejected the commandment of God in order to establish their own traditions. For when the Pharisees and Scribes had asked, *Why do your disciples not walk according to the tradition of the elders, but eat bread with common hands?* he, answering, said to them: *Well did Isaiah prophesy of you hypocrites, as it is written: This people honors me with their lips, but their heart is far from me. And in vain they worship me, teaching the doctrines and precepts of men. For, forsaking the commandment of God, you hold the tradition of men, the washings of pitchers and cups, and you do many other things like these. And he said to them: Well do you make void the precept of God, that you may keep your tradition. For Moses said: Honor your father and your mother; and: Whoever curses father or mother, let him die the death. But you say: If a man says to father or mother, Corban (which is a gift), whatever benefit you might have had from me: and you no longer permit him to do anything for his father or mother, annulling the word of God by your tradition, which you have handed down, and you do many such things like these.* Where Christ evidently shows both that the Law which this profane man blasphemes is God's, and that the Jews have their own traditions alien to the prophetic and legitimate books, which traditions the Apostle called profane and old wives' tales and endless genealogies—as not a heretic but a catholic reader understands. Moreover, if I should wish to gather all the testimonies by which I might show how both the Lord himself and his Apostles used the Law and the Prophets, which this man thinks old wives' tales, when would I have enough time—or to whom would what we have said not be enough?

## Some Fabricius or other thinks the Apostle lies, and praises him for it.

**2. 3.** Nor is this man so blind and deaf to the light and voice of the Lord or the Apostle that he does not know how, in the books belonging to the New Testament, the authority of the Law and the Prophets is confirmed and commended by Christ and the Apostles. Accordingly, he thinks he has devised a way to escape the mass of testimonies, drawn from the old books, set down in the writings of the Gospel and the apostles, by which his stiff-necked tongue, will it or not, is worn down. For he says "that the Apostle spoke in five persons, according to the qualities of dispositions: For, introducing the things of God," he says, "to a people still untrained, he ought not to have begun from the more perfect, and, excluding from them the custom of antiquity, to have begun from the more difficult, lest a perfect teaching disturb those still new to the faith." Then, wishing as it were to prove what he said, he recalls the same Apostle saying: *Since I am free from all, I have made myself subject to all, that I might gain the more; and I became to the Jews as a Jew, that I might gain the Jews, and to those who are under the Law as one under the Law, though I myself am not under the Law, that I might gain those who are under the Law, and to those who are without the Law as one without the Law, though I am not without the Law, but am in the Law of Christ, that I might gain those who are without the Law. I became weak to the weak, that I might gain the weak; I became all things to all, that I might gain all.* He wants four persons to be understood, in which he supposes the Apostle spoke: one of the Jews, another of those who are under the Law, a third of those who are without the Law, a fourth of the weak. A fifth is owed, since he had promised five: "this," he says, "so as to add it, he already stated above in another place: We speak wisdom among the perfect, so that the fifth person, of the perfect, may appear." All this he has contrived for this reason: that if at any time something should be brought forward from an apostolic epistle, where the Law and the Prophets attest to the Gospel, he may say that the Apostle spoke this not to the wise and perfect but to the Jews as a Jew, or to those who were under the Law as though he himself were under the Law—and thereby that, slyly and lyingly pretending, he built up among the imperfect what among the perfect he would tear down, not by lying but by speaking the truth. This contrivance of error was, evidently, fabricated for him by some Fabricius or other, whom he boasts of having found at Rome as a master of truth, as it were. Who would not shudder at this monstrosity—I do not say a Christian sense, but any human sense whatever?

## Paul in many places testifies that he is, by a certain proper office, the Teacher of the gentiles.

**2. 4.** For first it must be considered how most fraudulent and most lying he professes himself to be, who thinks the Apostle lies and praises him for it; and what the Apostle said not by the craft of a deceiver but by the affection of one who feels with others—because he came to the aid of the various diseases of souls with as merciful a heart as he would have wished aid given to himself, were he laboring under a like error or weakness—this man turns into a detestable falsehood. Then I ask: To the Romans, Corinthians, Galatians, Ephesians, Colossians, Philippians, Thessalonians, how was the Apostle speaking? In which of those five persons did he reckon them to be? Without doubt, since they were in the foreskin, they were gentiles, not Israelites in the circumcision. For he testifies that to these a ministry was assigned to him, where he says that Peter and James and John gave to him and Barnabas the right hands of fellowship, that he indeed with Barnabas might go to the gentiles, but they to the circumcision. And in another place he says most openly: *As long as I am the Apostle of the gentiles;* and in many other places he testifies that he is, by a certain proper office, the teacher of the gentiles. Since, then, there were so many gentiles without the Law to whom he preached the Gospel, what need was there for him to introduce to them the Law and the Prophets as witnesses about Christ, and to bring upon them, in their ignorance, the bond—as this plague supposes—of error, from which he ought rather to have rejoiced that they were free? What need was there for him to have begun thus to the Romans: *Paul, a servant of Christ Jesus, called an Apostle, set apart for the Gospel of God, which he had promised before through his Prophets in the holy Scriptures concerning his Son, who was made for him from the seed of David according to the flesh?* Why does he not rather put on himself their person? Why does he make himself as though under the Law to those who had been without the Law? Why does he say to them: *For I say to you gentiles: as long indeed as I am the Apostle of the gentiles, I will glorify my ministry, if by any means I may provoke to jealousy my own flesh, that I may save some of them. For if the rejection of them is the reconciliation of the world, what will their acceptance be, but life from the dead? But if the firstfruit is holy, so also is the lump, and if the root is holy, so also are the branches.* For he says these things of the Israelites, of whom he had already said before: *What advantage, then, has the Jew, or what is the profit of circumcision? Much in every way. First, indeed, because the words of God were entrusted to them. For what if some of them did not believe? Will their unbelief make void the faith of God?* Then here, where I had begun to recall, it follows and he says: *But if some of the branches were broken off, and you, though you were a wild olive, were grafted in among them and became a partner of the root and of the richness of the olive, do not boast against the branches. But if you do boast, it is not you who carry the root, but the root you. You will say, then: The branches were broken off, that I might be grafted in. Well; by unbelief they were broken off. But you stand by faith; do not be high-minded, but fear. For if God did not spare the natural branches, neither will he spare you. See, therefore, the goodness and the severity of God: toward those who fell, indeed, severity, but toward you goodness, if you remain in goodness; otherwise you too will be cut off. And they, if they do not remain in unbelief, will be grafted in. For God is able to graft them in again. For if you were cut from the naturally wild olive and grafted contrary to nature into a good olive, how much more will these, who are according to nature, be grafted into their own olive? For I do not want you to be ignorant, brothers, of this mystery, that you may not be wise in your own eyes: that blindness in part has come upon Israel, until the fullness of the gentiles should come in, and so all Israel should be saved, as it is written: There will come out of Zion one who will rescue and turn away impiety from Jacob; and this is my covenant to them, when I take away their sins.* It is far too long to run through it all, or to gather into one view the things which are scattered everywhere through the apostolic Scriptures. What need was there to say these things to the gentiles? Why did he not rather become without the Law to them, as those were without the Law? Why did he not rather praise their gods and proclaim their sacrifices, if both this Scripture which the people of Israel received, and those rites of the gentiles, as this man says, pertained to demons? What of the fact that the unhappy man dares to call the God of Israel not only a demon but even worse than the other demons? Why, then, did the Apostle, who had become all things to all—not by a compassionating mercy, as the truth shows, but by a deceiving cunning, as this man foolishly thinks—why did he not rather pretend himself to be a servant of those demons whom the Romans worshiped, whom this man thinks milder demons, so as to become like them and thus gain them?

## Paul spoke first about Christ Jesus the cornerstone.

**2. 5.** Does he not likewise say to the Ephesians: *On account of which, remember that you, when you were gentiles in the flesh, who are called the uncircumcision by that which is called the circumcision made by hand in the flesh; you who were at that time without Christ, alienated from the society of Israel and strangers to the covenants and to the promise, having no hope and without God in this world; but now, in Christ Jesus, you who once were far off have been made near in the blood of Christ. For he is our peace, who made both one and broke down the dividing wall of the barrier, the enmity, in his flesh, abolishing the Law of commandments in decrees, that he might found the two in himself into one new man, making peace, that he might reconcile both in one body to God through the cross, slaying the enmities in himself: and coming, he preached peace to you who were far off, and peace to those who were near; because through him we both have access in one Spirit to the Father. Therefore you are now no longer strangers and sojourners, but you are fellow citizens of the saints and members of the household of God, built upon the foundation of the Apostles and Prophets, Christ Jesus himself being the chief cornerstone?* Let this blasphemer say how he calls the Israelites, who served a worse demon, as he blasphemes, found near, and the gentiles, who had been subject to milder demons, far off. How does he say they had been alienated from the society of Israel and strangers to the covenants and to the promise, when they were having no hope and without God in this world, if Israel was not the people of God and of Christ? Against this most clear and most excellent trumpet of truth the madman blares and insults, saying that the Apostle spoke in five persons, so as to deceive some by means of others, pretending himself to be what he was not—when he sees that the Apostle preaches to the gentiles, who had been far from the God of the Israelites, the same God, the same Law, the same Prophets, the same covenants. Who spoke first about the cornerstone? Was it not the prophet, full of the Spirit of God, whom this plague blasphemes? For he says: *Behold, I lay in Zion a cornerstone, chosen, precious; and whoever believes in him will not be put to shame.* Which testimony the apostle Peter also recalled. Was it not first said in a psalm belonging to the sacred writings of the former people: *The stone which the builders rejected, this has become the head of the corner?* For instructed by these writings, the apostle Paul set down what I recalled a little before, *Christ Jesus himself being the chief cornerstone.* Hence the Lord himself convicted the Jews, in whom blindness in part had come, and rendered them confounded, saying: *Have you never read in the Scriptures: The stone which the builders rejected, this has become the head of the corner; this was done by the Lord, and it is marvelous in our eyes?* "All these things, then, Christ brought forward lyingly from the Law and the Prophets, and lyingly the Apostles brought them forward," as this man blasphemes, "because, namely, the weak could not yet grasp the solid truth?" Is he so blind that he does not know that it is one thing to nourish little ones, another to deceive the trusting; one thing to feed, that they may grow, another to act, that they may not live? For this man says and thinks such things about the God of the Law and the Prophets and about those writings altogether that, according to his opinion which he holds about them, when the Apostles preached to believers the God of the Law and the Prophets and confirmed the authority of such Scriptures, they would seem to have given the little ones not milk to drink but poison. And whoever believes this about them is foolish, grows empty, is insane.

## That Christ is truly proclaimed to be from the seed of David is the root into which the wild olive is grafted, nor are these old wives' tales.

**2. 6.** Then, someone says, did the Apostle say in vain: *I could not speak to you as to spiritual people, but as to carnal, as to little ones in Christ I gave you milk to drink, not solid food; for you were not yet able.* And: *We speak wisdom among the perfect.* And: *The animal man does not perceive the things which are of the Spirit of God?* Far be it that we should think he said these things emptily and untruthfully. Yet is he therefore to be believed to have wished to deceive any of those who believed him? He gave, then, to little ones small things, not false; milky, not harmful; nourishing, not destructive. Moreover, if it is not true that the Son of God was made according to the flesh from the seed of David; if it is not true that the natural branches were broken off on account of unbelief, so that the believing wild olive, coming from the gentiles, might be grafted into the holy root of the Israelites and become a sharer in the richness of the olive; if "they are not the words of God, but of some most wicked demon or other," as this man blasphemes, of which he says, *First, indeed, because the words of God were entrusted to them:* then surely he who gave these to the little ones, who proclaimed them as true, who wished them to be believed, was destroying the wretched, not nourishing them. And since we know this to be alien to the faith and teaching of the truthful Apostles of Christ, it remains for us most vehemently to detest this man as a plague and an enemy of the Christian faith, who is so blinded and hurled headlong by madness that he does not see that even Timothy himself—to whom he thinks it was commanded by apostolic authority that he should avoid the old Law and the Prophets as old wives' tales—was not to be deceived by the same Apostle, and was to be reckoned in that fifth person of the perfect, so that he might not lyingly speak to him, saying: *Remember that Jesus Christ rose from the dead, from the seed of David, according to my Gospel.* For if Christ is proclaimed from the seed of David by old wives' tales, how is this man ordered to avoid the very things which he is ordered faithfully to remember? But if Christ is truly proclaimed from the seed of David, there is the root into which the wild olive is grafted, nor are these old wives' tales—unless, besides those Scriptures, the ravings of the Jews pertaining to what they call the Deuterosis: not those words of God which were entrusted to the circumcision, where even the carnal Jews had learned that Christ would come from the seed of David, as they answered the Lord's question, although they were unable to understand him as the Lord of David himself, not according to the flesh but according to the divinity; yet both are in those words—both what they believed and what they did not understand—both, *Of the fruit of your womb I will set upon your throne,* on account of Christ from the seed of David; and, *The Lord said to my Lord: Sit at my right hand,* on account of Christ, the Lord even of David himself.

## The Apostle cries out that he is speaking the truth, and this in Christ.

**2. 7.** Was the Apostle, then, speaking deceitfully when he said: *I speak the truth in Christ, I do not lie, my conscience bearing witness to me in the Holy Spirit, that there is great sadness for me and continual grief to my heart. For I myself was wishing to be anathema from Christ for my brothers, my kinsmen according to the flesh, who are Israelites, whose are the adoption and the glory and the covenants and the giving of the Law and the worship and the promises; whose are the fathers, and from whom is Christ according to the flesh, who is over all, God blessed forever, Amen?* The Apostle cries out at the head of this sentence that he is speaking the truth, and this in Christ, who is truth itself, his conscience bearing witness to him in the Holy Spirit, and he concludes that sentence with an "Amen" at the end; and this man says: "The things which the Apostle here speaks are not true; he deceives the weak, because they cannot grasp the truth; he suckles the little ones with vanities and pours the poison of diabolical lies into the hungry sons, so as to nourish them." O monster, to be kept away not only from Christian ears but even from the bounds of the Christian world! Are this adoption, this glory, these covenants, this giving of the Law, this worship, these promises, these fathers, from whom is Christ according to the flesh, who is over all, God blessed forever—are these old wives' tales? Is that which one is especially ordered to hold, who is ordered to avoid old wives' tales, an old wives' tale?

## The Apostle's sentence, which refutes both those who use the Law badly and those who think it bad.

**2. 8.** But what wonder is it if he raves with such great impiety, who wishes to be a destroyer of the Law, when the same Apostle says that certain people, wishing to be not destroyers but teachers of the Law, understand neither the things they say nor the things about which they make assertions? But let us hold against both that which he subsequently added. For at once, lest anyone should suppose that those people do not understand the things they say, nor the things about which they make assertions, for the reason that they wish to be teachers of a bad Law: *But we know,* he says, *that the Law is good, if anyone uses it lawfully.* This sentence refutes both those who use the Law badly and those who think it bad. Moreover, if it is good, by what madness is the God denied to be good who gave the good Law? What condemnation can seem to suffice for these men, who accuse, under the name of old wives' tales, the Law which the Apostle so praises? And they think they do this by the testimony of the same epistle, where the Apostle so praises it, wishing to be not teachers but blasphemers of the Law by not understanding the things they say, nor the things about which they make assertions?

## Christ was promised by the Prophets of God to be going to come from the seed of David.

**3. 9.** "But it could not have come about," he says, "that the advent of our Savior should be announced by the Prophets of the Jews." Why could it not have come about, since the Apostle says, *Because the words of God were entrusted to them?* "But before the Savior's advent," he says, "the Holy and divine Spirit was not upon the earth." This vanity, not truth, says. For with what but the Holy Spirit did the Lord fill his Prophets, of whom it was said at the beginning of the epistle to the Romans, which I have also recalled above: *Paul, a servant of Christ Jesus, called an Apostle, set apart for the Gospel of God, which he had promised before, through his Prophets in the holy Scriptures, concerning his Son, who was made for him from the seed of David according to the flesh.* And this testimony indeed this man too set down, forbidding the one to whom he writes to believe other Prophets about Christ, besides those whom the Apostle calls his Prophets in the letter to the Romans—whom, I believe, he supposes were not Prophets of the Jews. But of whatever nation he may suppose them to have been, why does he not there notice: *which he had promised before through his Prophets?* For if through any of his Prophets he promised beforehand the Gospel about his Son, what is it that this man says, that the Holy and divine Spirit was not upon the earth before the Savior's advent? And yet whence could these have been prophets announcing Christ from the seed of David according to the flesh, except of that nation of which David himself was—from whose seed Christ was promised by the Prophets of God to be going to come?

## Through Moses there is Law, because it is commanded; but through Christ grace comes about, when what is commanded is fulfilled.

**3. 10.** "But the Law," he says, "was given through Moses; but the truth is from Jesus Christ." It is not written thus, but thus: *The Law was given through Moses, but grace and truth came about through Jesus Christ.* The Law, therefore, was given through Moses, but grace came about through Jesus Christ, when, through his Spirit, with love poured out in our hearts, it comes about that what the Law commands is fulfilled. For what is commanded through the letter is fulfilled not by the letter but by the Spirit. Hence that which is written: *You shall not covet.* Through Moses there is Law, because it is commanded; but through Christ grace comes about, when what is commanded is fulfilled. But the truth came about through Christ on account of those things which are promised in the prophecy of the Law, because in Christ they stand out, rendered.

## The Apostle wished to show that the Gospel pertains not to the Jews only but to all the gentiles.

**3. 11.** But as for what the Apostle says to the Romans, *How will they call upon him in whom they have not believed? Or how will they believe him whom they have not heard?*—he said this of the gentiles, not of the Jews, as this man dreams here. For the Teacher of the gentiles wished to refute those who thought the Gospel was to be preached only to the nation of the Jews, not also to the uncircumcised gentiles. Wishing to show that it pertains not to the Jews only but to all the gentiles, he first set down a testimony from the prophet: *Everyone who calls upon the name of the Lord will be saved.* And then, when he had said, *But how will they call upon him in whom they have not believed? Or how will they believe him whom they have not heard?* he immediately added: *But how will they hear without a preacher? Or how will they preach unless they are sent?* Thus refuting those who denied that preachers of Christ were to be sent to the uncircumcised gentiles.

## Many and manifest testimonies about Christ and the Church, whence they were foretold, which we thus see fulfilled, and which—with the gospel and apostolic writings agreeing—we also hope to be fulfilled.

**3. 12.** This too, like a most unskilled man, he does not understand—what is written: *First Apostles, then Prophets.* For he thinks that there were no Prophets before the Apostles, not knowing that those Prophets are recalled there by the Apostle who lived after Christ's advent. Whoever wishes to know them, let him read the Acts of the Apostles, and that which he says to the Corinthians: *But let two or three Prophets speak.* But if there were no Prophets before the Apostles, who were those through whom God promised the Gospel beforehand about his Son, who was made for him from the seed of David according to the flesh? Who was it who said: *The stone which the builders rejected, this has become the head of the corner?* Who was it who said: *Your throne, O God, is forever and ever, a rod of uprightness is the rod of your kingdom. You have loved righteousness and hated iniquity, therefore God, your God, has anointed you with the oil of gladness above your fellows?* How is the God whose throne is forever and ever anointed by God, except Christ Jesus, who received the name Christ from the very anointing? For chrism is anointing, Christ is the anointed one. Who was it who said—what Christ himself testifies was foretold about him: *The Lord said to my Lord: Sit at my right hand, until I place your enemies under your feet?* And he confirms that David said this in the Spirit—the Spirit which this man denies was upon the earth before the Lord's advent. What is it that the Apostle says: *But Isaiah says: There will be a root of Jesse, and one who will rise up to rule the gentiles; in him will the gentiles hope?* What Spirit was it who, through the same Isaiah, foretold so long before: *He bears our sins and grieves for us; and we thought him to be in grief and in stroke and in affliction. He was wounded on account of our sins and weakened on account of our iniquities; the discipline of our peace was upon him, and by his bruise we are healed; we all like sheep have gone astray, each man has gone astray in his own way; and the Lord handed him over on account of our iniquities. And he, on account of the affliction, did not open his mouth: he was led as a sheep to the victim, and as a lamb before its shearer is mute, so he did not open his mouth. In humility his judgment was taken away; his generation, who will recount it, since his life is taken from the earth? From the iniquities of my people he was led to death;* and the rest, which it is long to weave together. Where also about the Church it was foretold so long before, which we read recalled by the Apostle and now already see fulfilled: *Rejoice, barren one who does not bear; break forth and cry out, you who do not travail, because many are the children of the desolate one, more than of her who has a husband. For the Lord said: Enlarge the place of your tent and of your hangings; fix them, do not spare; stretch out your cords and strengthen your stakes. Spread out still to the right and the left, and your seed will possess the gentiles, and you will make the deserted cities to be inhabited. Do not fear, because you have been confounded, nor be abashed, because you have been reproached. For you will forget your eternal confusion, and you will not remember the reproach of your widowhood. For the Lord who made you, the Lord of hosts is his name; and he who rescued you, the God of Israel, will be called the God of all the earth.* Who is it who said: *I was watching in a vision of the night, and behold, with the clouds of heaven there was one coming as the Son of man, and he came up to the Ancient of days and was brought forward in his sight. And to him was given dominion and honor and a kingdom, and all peoples, tribes, and tongues will serve him; his power is a perpetual power, which will not pass away, and his kingdom will not be corrupted?* These and many other manifest things about Christ and the Church—whence were they foretold, which we thus see fulfilled, and which, with the gospel and apostolic writings agreeing, we also hope to be fulfilled, if the Spirit of God was not upon the earth before the advent of Christ, or if the advent of Christ was not foretold by the Prophets of Israel, as this man foolishly thinks—who, by denying Christ promised beforehand, has no source from which to assert him sent? And rightly so, because the one he himself proclaims was neither sent nor promised; for he is not true but invented.

## By many attestations the apostolic authority commends that those Scriptures are of the true and good God.

**4. 13.** Now I do not wonder at this—that an untaught man supposes it was said of the Prophets of the Jews, what the Apostle says: *One of themselves, their own prophet, said: The Cretans are always liars, evil beasts, lazy bellies; this testimony is true.* He is ignorant that it was said about a certain Epimenides, who was a Cretan, in whose books this is found; which man is not found among the Prophets of God, nor does he pertain to those words of God which the Apostle says were entrusted to the Jews—who does not lie. For this reason the Apostle did not recall his name, as he is wont to recall the Prophets of God, sometimes saying, *As David too says, But Isaiah dares and says, As Hosea too says,* or, with their names left unspoken, when he says, *As it is written;* and that Scripture is understood in which there is the authority of God; or he asserts that God evidently speaks, when he brings forward some testimony from the Law or the Prophets of God, such as this: *You shall not muzzle the mouth of the ox that treads the grain. Is God concerned about oxen?* he says. *Or does the Scripture say it on account of us?* Showing, surely, that in the same Scripture God speaks. Or that: *But the Scripture, foreseeing that God justifies the gentiles by faith, foretold to Abraham, saying: In your seed all the gentiles will be blessed.* He put the Scripture itself in place of God, because it is God's. Or that about Abraham: *In the promise of God,* he says, *he did not hesitate through unbelief, but was strengthened by faith, giving glory to God and believing most fully that what he promised he is able also to do.* Against which divine and apostolic voice this rabid dog, barking, dared to say that "Abraham did not believe his God promising him offspring,"—ignorant of that which he says: *If a son will be born to me who am a hundred years old,* which is the joy of one marveling, not the distrust of one doubting. Or that: *The word of God cannot fail. For not all who are of Israel are Israel, nor, because they are the seed of Abraham, are all sons; but in Isaac will your seed be called: that is, it is not those who are the sons of the flesh who are the sons of God, but the sons of the promise are reckoned as the seed.* Or that about Elijah: *But what does the divine answer say to him? I have left myself seven thousand men, who have not bent their knees before Baal.* By these and similar attestations the apostolic authority commends that those Scriptures, which this man blasphemes, are of the true and good God. But where the Apostle says something also about the authors of the gentiles, he does not call them Prophets of God, nor does he say that God is the author of those Scriptures, although he finds some true things there, as he says about this Cretan: *One of themselves, their own prophet, said: The Cretans are always liars;* therefore not the prophet proper to the Jews, but to the Cretans—which was surely said for this purpose, that he should not be thought a prophet of God. And in the Acts of the Apostles, when he was speaking to the Athenians, he says of God: *For in him we live and move and are: as some too among you have said.*

## In the Gospel the Lord showed that these things about himself, which had been done, were foretold.

**4. 14.** "But to the Apostles," he says, "when they were asking about the Prophets of the Jews what ought to be thought—who were supposed to have sung something in the past about his advent—our Lord, disturbed that they still even now thought such things, answered: You have dismissed the living one who is before you, and you tell tales about the dead." What wonder is it—since he brought forward this testimony from some apocryphal Scriptures or other—if heretics, who do not accept those same writings, invented such things about the Prophets of God? For in the Gospel the Lord—which is not apocryphal but known to all in the light of truth—even after the resurrection, accompanying his disciples on the road, beginning from Moses through all the Prophets, showed that these things about himself, which had been done, were foretold.

## What the Lord said about those who have worked iniquity.

**4. 15.** "About this," he says, "he reproves again, where he says: Many will say to me in that day: Lord, in your name we cast out demons and in your name we prophesied and in your name we did many mighty works. And then I will say to them: Depart from me, since I never knew you, because you have worked iniquity." Far be it that the Lord said these things about the holy Prophets, among whom were Moses and the rest; but it was said about those who, after his Gospel was preached, seem to themselves to speak in his name, not knowing what they say; among whom this man too, doomed to perish, finds a place for himself.

## That there were Prophets before the advent of the Savior himself, the Lord's parable testifies.

**4. 16.** He also says that the Lord said: "I am the door of the sheep; all the others, as many as came before me, are thieves and robbers." But it is not written thus, but thus: *All, as many as came, are thieves and robbers.* For in this place he wished those to be understood to have "come" who had not been sent—whom Jeremiah too reproves, saying: *Thus says the Lord about the prophets who prophesy in my name, and I did not send them.* But those whom this madman blasphemes were sent by the Lord, they did not come of their own accord. Whom the Lord also declares—by a parable, indeed, but with a most open signification—where he says: *Hear another parable: There was a man, a householder, who planted a vineyard and put a hedge around it and dug a winepress in it and built a tower and let it out to farmers and went abroad. But when the time of the fruits drew near, he sent his servants to the farmers, that they might receive its fruits. And the farmers, having seized his servants, beat one, killed another, and stoned another. Again he sent other servants, more than the first, and they did to them likewise. But last of all he sent to them his son, saying: They will respect my son. But the farmers, seeing the son, said among themselves: This is the heir; come, let us kill him, and we shall have his inheritance. And having seized him, they cast him out of the vineyard and killed him. When, therefore, the lord of the vineyard comes, what will he do to those farmers? They say to him: He will badly destroy the bad men and will let out his vineyard to other farmers, who will render to him the fruits in their seasons. Jesus says to them: Have you never read in the Scriptures: The stone which the builders rejected, this has become the head of the corner. This was done by the Lord, and it is marvelous in our eyes? Therefore I say to you that the kingdom of God will be taken from you and will be given to a nation producing its fruits.* What is more open than this? What clearer? What more evident? But this man is of the number of those who stoned the servants of this householder. For he does this not with blows of stones but with hard curses. For this parable testifies both that the vineyard of God was first planted in the nation of the Jews and that the Prophets were sent before the advent of the Savior himself. And when he says, *The kingdom of God will be taken from you and will be given to a nation producing its fruits,* what kingdom does he mean but that which they hoped for, not that which they had received—that is, of eternal life? Hence elsewhere he says: *Search the Scriptures, in which you think you have eternal life; these bear witness about me.* And elsewhere: *Woe to you lawyers, who have taken away the key of knowledge. You yourselves did not enter, and those who were entering you prevented.* Is impudent vanity not worn down by these masses of testimonies? Who listens to this man, except one who does not listen to the holy Scriptures, or listens to them as badly as this blind man rushes against them?

## That among the same people of the Jews there were righteous men and Prophets of God.

**5. 17.** "But the Lord said to them," he says, "Your parents ate manna and died—teaching that none of those over whom death held sway pertained to the Lord." None, indeed, but of those parents to whom he was speaking such things; for whom did he wish the parents of the unbelieving to be understood as, except unbelievers? Hence in another place he says: *Woe to you, scribes and Pharisees, hypocrites, who build the tombs of the Prophets and adorn the monuments of the righteous and say: If we had been in the days of our fathers, we would not have been their partners in the blood of the Prophets. Therefore you are witnesses to yourselves that you are the sons of those who killed the Prophets.* He called them sons, surely, by imitation of the crime, not by propagation of lineage. For it could not be a charge to them that they were born of those according to the flesh, but that they showed themselves like them in unbelieving cruelty. And therefore he connects and says: *And you, fill up the measure of your fathers. Serpents, brood of vipers, how will you flee from the judgment of hell? Therefore, behold, I send to you Prophets and wise men and scribes, and some of them you will kill and crucify, and some of them you will scourge in your synagogues and persecute from city to city, so that upon you may come all the righteous blood which has been shed upon the earth, from the blood of righteous Abel to the blood of Zechariah son of Barachiah, whom you killed between the temple and the altar. Amen I say to you, all these things will come upon this generation.* Surely it is established, surely it is manifest, that these men are sons, by imitating, of the evil ones at whose hands the holy and righteous Prophets—from Abel himself, whom his brother killed, to Zechariah, whom these killed—endured most impious and most wicked persecutions. For how does the blood of those who lived long before these were even born come upon these, except because there is one kind, one lump, one mass of the impious, connected to one another by imitation? At the same time it is also declared that among the same people there were righteous men and Prophets of God, whose tombs these men—to whom the Lord speaks these things—were building, and whose monuments they were adorning. Worse, then, and more monstrous is this man, who lacerates with a cursing tongue those dead men to whom even the impious paid honor. And, cruelly raging against his own soul, he blasphemes—while he wishes to seem a Christian—those whom Christ asserts to be Prophets and righteous men.

## The true life, surely, by which the righteous live, even when they die in body.

**5. 18.** But now, as for the fact that he wished to join the holy patriarchs Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob to those of whom the Lord says, *Your fathers ate manna in the desert and died,* on the ground that these fathers too died—whether he is to be judged fraudulent or rather blind is uncertain. But whichever of these he may choose, he is to be detested. For by this he wished to show that the Lord, having recalled these same fathers, says: *He is not the God of the dead but of the living*—when he rather said that they live. For, having brought forward a testimony from the Law, where it is written, *I am the God of Abraham and the God of Isaac and the God of Jacob,* he added: *He is not the God of the dead but of the living,* and added: *For all those live.* The true life, surely, by which the righteous live, even when they die in body. But when would this man say these things, if he were alive?

## This, then, is to enter: not to be content with the surface of the letter, but to arrive at the interior things of understanding.

**5. 19.** In this sense he wishes to take also what the Lord says to the Jews: *You know neither me nor my Father, nor do you have the word of God abiding in you.* Which is surely not contrary to what he said: *You have taken away the key of knowledge; you yourselves did not enter, and those who were entering you prevented.* For they did not have the word of God in themselves, but they had it in the writings which they read. For if they had it in themselves, they would both enter and permit others to enter. But not to enter is not to understand. Behold why they knew neither him nor his Father: because they did not understand what they read, not because they had not preached the God and the Christ whom they read of. This, then, is to enter: not to be content with the surface of the letter, but to arrive at the interior things of understanding.

## For there could have been some equal to John, some less than he; but none greater, on account of the Lord's pronouncement.

**5. 20.** He also argues "about John the Baptist, that the Lord says: Among those born of women there has not arisen one greater than John the Baptist. But he who is least in the kingdom of heaven is greater than he." Thus, reasoning as it were, "as though John did not pertain to the kingdom of heaven, and on this account much less the other Prophets of that people, than whom John is greater." But these words of the Lord can be most rightly understood in two ways. For either the Lord in that place called "the kingdom of heaven" that which we have not yet received and in which we are not yet—whence at the end he is going to say, *Come, blessed of my Father, receive the kingdom;* and because the holy angels are there, anyone less among them is surely greater than any holy and righteous person bearing a body which is corrupted and weighs down the soul. Or, if in that sentence he wished "the kingdom of heaven" to be understood as that by which the Church is also signified in this present time—whose children are all, from the foundation of the human race until now, who could be righteous and holy—then surely the Lord signified himself, because in the time of his birth he was less than John, but greater in the eternity of his divinity and the lordly power. Accordingly, by the former exposition it is punctuated thus: Among those born of women none has arisen greater than John the Baptist; but he who is least in the kingdom of heaven, and then it is subjoined: is greater than he. But by this latter exposition thus: Among those born of women there has not arisen one greater than John the Baptist; but he who is least, and then it is subjoined: in the kingdom of heaven is greater than he. But however much each understanding accords with the truth, so much does it suffice to refute this man's vanity. For whether any angel less than the rest is greater than John, or whether here the Lord is understood, less in age than John his forerunner but greater in majesty, nothing is thereby disparaged in the other Prophets. For there could have been some equal to John, some less than he; but none greater, on account of the Lord's pronouncement: yet all holy and righteous and good.

## And so the soul, because it invisibly prevails over all the things of which we are composed, is better signified by blood.

**6. 21.** "But Moses," he says, "extinguished in human beings all hope of a future resurrection, who pronounced that the soul is mortal, who said that it is the blood." Then, by reasoning, he tries with great force to show "that the soul is not the blood," and labors over a manifest matter, not understanding the Law. For it was said, *The soul of all flesh is the blood,* in the way it was said, *The rock was Christ*—not because it was this, but because by it this was signified. But the Law did not wish to signify the soul by the blood—namely, an invisible thing by a visible thing—without reason, but because the blood, diffused through all the veins from the heart itself, holds the chief place in our body more than the other humors, so that wherever a wound has been inflicted, no other humor but the blood itself comes forth. And so the soul, because it invisibly prevails over all the things of which we are composed, is better signified by that which visibly prevails over all the things of which we are composed.

## A question about the resurrection of the body.

**6. 22.** But that which the Apostle says (for this testimony too this man set down), *Flesh and blood will not possess the kingdom of God,* raises a question not about the soul but about the resurrection of the body. But this too is resolved in two ways. For either by the name of flesh and blood he named the corruption of flesh and blood, which will not exist in the resurrection; or by "flesh and blood" he named human beings given over to flesh and blood, to all the enticements of worldly pleasures, who will not possess the kingdom of God. But this whole passage of the apostolic discourse, where it is written, *Flesh and blood will not possess the kingdom of God,* if it be considered more diligently, will rather persuade us that he wished to call by these names the corruption of the flesh as it now is, and to add, in order to explain what he meant: *Nor will corruption possess incorruption.* Because, when that change which is hoped for in the resurrection has come about, no corruption at all will remain. Although, then, even after the resurrection the Lord said to his disciples, *Handle me and see, for a spirit does not have flesh and bones, as you see me to have,* nevertheless according to substance it will be flesh, not according to corruption, which now imposes this name on the flesh. Hence the prophet says, *All flesh is grass.* For is even the Lord's flesh, which he raised up to heaven, grass? In the way, then, that the prophet called it flesh when he said, *All flesh is grass*—being about to say, surely, *The grass has withered*—in that way the Apostle too says, *Flesh and blood will not possess the kingdom of God,* because there will be no corruption there, by which now, like grass, the nature of the flesh withers. For it is not another sentence, *Nor will corruption possess incorruption;* rather, this repetition is an exposition of the previous sentence, so that what was there said, *Flesh and blood,* we may understand as the corruption, not the substance, of the flesh; and what was there said, *The kingdom of God,* we may understand as incorruption. And thus let us think that nothing else was said by *Flesh and blood will not possess the kingdom of God* than if it were said, Corruption will not possess incorruption—that is, the corruption of flesh and blood will not exist in the incorruption of that kingdom, on account, namely, of the change of which, speaking soon after, he added: *This corruptible must put on incorruption.* Thus the corruption which was signified by the name of flesh and blood will not exist in the incorruption of that kingdom, because the flesh, which is now corruptible, will then, changed, be incorruptible.

## The *bath of regeneration* is understood to be what those now receive who are baptized in Christ.

**6. 23.** But Moses, the man of God, if he had believed the soul to be mortal—which he would surely have believed, if he had said it was the blood not for the sake of signification but of proper nature—would not in another place say: *Everyone who touches a dead man, anyone who has died from any human soul, and has not been purified, pollutes the tabernacle of the Lord: that soul will be cut off from Israel; since the water of sprinkling has not been sprinkled around upon him, he is unclean, his uncleanness is still in him.* "Still," he surely said—even after death, since he is not purified—where he understands the prefigured bath of regeneration, which those now receive who are baptized in Christ, whoever faithfully hears: *If you believed Moses, you would believe me also; for he wrote about me.*

## The law belongs to the Old Testament, grace to the New.

**7. 24.** But what wonder is it if this unhappy man, turned away from the light of truth and on that account opposed to the light of truth, casts up against the Old Testament the things which he does not understand in the New Testament? As also that passage from the apostle Paul, where he says to the Corinthians: *But if the ministry of death, formed in letters on stones, was made in glory, so that the children of Israel could not gaze upon the face of Moses on account of the glory of his countenance, which is being abolished, why will not the ministry of the spirit rather be in glory? For if the ministry of condemnation is glory, much more will the ministry of righteousness abound in glory. For even what was glorified was, in this respect, not glorified, on account of the surpassing brightness. For if that which is being abolished is in glory, much more is that which remains in glory.* In this way this man set down the apostolic words, and does not differ much from the better interpretation. Because, then, it was said, *The ministry of death, formed in letters on stones,* he thinks from this that Moses ministered to death—that is, to the author of death, namely the malign spirit, whom this man supposes to be the author of this world—not knowing that the Law was called the ministry of death, according to that which he says elsewhere: *The letter kills, but the spirit gives life.* For the Law, although just and holy and good, brought death to the transgressors, whom the grace of God did not help to fulfill the righteousness of the Law. For it was fitting that in the Old Testament the Law be imposed upon the proud, and upon those trusting in the strength of their own will, a Law which would not give righteousness but command it, and that thus, entangled in the death of transgression, they might flee to grace—a grace not merely commanding but helping—which was revealed in the New Testament. Hence these blasphemers of the divine words think that the Law given through Moses was bad, because it was called the ministry of death, figured in letters on stones, not considering that it was said on account of those who supposed that the Law was sufficient by their own free choice and, not helped by the spirit of grace, were held guilty of transgression under the letter of the same Law. Hence he says elsewhere: *The Law works wrath. For where there is no Law, neither is there transgression*—showing thereby why he said, *The Law works wrath.* For the transgression of it would not be bad, unless the Law itself were good.

## How under the law the lifted-up are dashed down, and under grace the dashed-down are raised up.

**7. 25.** It is much, and too long, to gather all the things which the blessed Apostle says in this sense, distinguishing the Law from grace—in that under the former the lifted-up are dashed down, under the latter the dashed-down are raised up; and that the former is good to such a degree that it commands good things, the latter to such a degree that it confers good things. The former makes a hearer of righteousness, the latter a doer. And therefore under the former the sinner—indeed, moreover, the transgressor—lies convicted, having lost the excuse of ignorance. But under the latter, both sparing and helping, he is not destroyed because he has worked what is bad, and is kindled to work what is good. What wonder is it, then, if the former was called the ministry of death, where the letter kills, by forbidding the evil that is done and commanding the good that is not done; but the latter was called the ministry of the spirit—the spirit, namely, that gives life—so that we may rise from the death of transgression and not read righteousness as guilty persons on tablets, but, as free persons, have it in our hearts and in our morals? This is the New Testament differing from the Old; because there the old man is confined in the straits of dread, here the new man walks abroad in the breadth of love.

## *For Christ is the end of the Law unto righteousness for everyone who believes.*

**7. 26.** But that which was said about Moses, the minister of the Old Testament—that the children of Israel could not gaze upon his face on account of the glory of his countenance—was a sign that they were not going to understand Christ in the Law. And therefore a veil was placed between the face of Moses and them, so that, as it is written, the children of Israel should not gaze up to the end. But who is the end of the Law? To this let not I but the Apostle himself answer: *For Christ,* he says, *is the end of the Law unto righteousness for everyone who believes.* An end perfecting, not destroying. For "end" is so called as that for the sake of which all things are done, whatever are done by some function. For between a function and an end there is this difference: that a function is in the things which we ought to do, the end is that for the sake of which we do them. Since, then, all those things were done for the sake of Christ, whom the children of Israel did not understand in the things that were being done, this the veil signified, which did not allow them to gaze to the end—that is, up to the face of Moses, which signified Christ. But it was said that this glory is done away with, because all the signifying shadows are done away with when the thing which is signified has come. For just as the knowledge which now is will be done away with, as the same Apostle says, when that comes which he calls "face to face," so too those things which had been handed down to the Jews in shadows in the Old Testament had necessarily to be done away with by the revelation of the New Testament.

## In the psalms is written what Paul says in his epistle.

**7. 27.** Nor indeed did all in that people fail to understand Christ figured through those shadows of the Old Testament: for these things were not unknown to Moses himself and the other Prophets, who foretold him to those who came after. For in that very Epistle to the Corinthians, where these things are said which this man, not understanding, set down as though adverse and hostile to the Old Testament, why does he say: *But having the same spirit of faith, according to what is written, I believed, on account of which I also spoke, we too believe, on account of which we also speak?* For where is it written, *I believed, on account of which I also spoke?* Surely in the psalms, which belong to those words of God that were entrusted to the Jews. *Having the same spirit of faith,* he says: what is "the same," unless that which those also had through whom these things were ministered? Why also in the same epistle did he set down a testimony from the Law, when he had premised, *That your abundance may be for their want, that their abundance too may come to be for your want, that there may be equality,* subjoining and saying, *As it is written: He who had much did not have too much, and he who had little did not lack?* Why does he bring upon them the authority of the Law, which he says was a ministry of death, if he understood it in the way this plague understands?

## In what way the Law was rightly called the *ministry of death* and yet is holy and just and good.

**7. 28.** And, that all ambiguities may be removed as to in what way the Law was both rightly called the ministry of death and yet is holy and just and good, let us recall that which is set down in the Epistle to the Romans. For when he had said, *So that we may serve in newness of the spirit and not in the oldness of the letter*—which sentence is most similar to the one which this man, not understanding, set down—he at once foresaw that these verbose and blasphemous men would be, who would suppose from this that the Law had been reprehended, and soon subjoined: *What then shall we say? Is the Law sin? Far be it: but I did not know sin except through the Law. For I would not have known concupiscence, unless the Law said: You shall not covet. But sin, having taken occasion, worked in me through the commandment all concupiscence. For without the Law sin is dead. But I was once alive without the Law; but when the commandment came, sin revived. But I died, and the commandment which was unto life was found by me to be unto death. For sin, having taken occasion, deceived me through the commandment and through it killed me. And so the Law indeed is holy, and the commandment holy and just and good. Did then that which is good become death to me? Far be it: but sin, that it might appear sin, worked death in me through the good.* Behold what the ministry of death is, behold what *the letter kills* is; behold how the Law is not sin, and the commandment was unto life, and the Law holy, and the commandment holy and just and good. And yet because by that very good the disobedient soul is killed, where the grace of God does not help, the Law was made the ministry of death in the Old Testament on account of the killing letter; and grace was made the ministry of life in the New Testament on account of the life-giving spirit. But what the ministry of death and the ministry of condemnation is, this is: *sin, having taken occasion, worked in me through the commandment all concupiscence;* this is, *when the commandment came, sin revived;* this is, *the commandment which was unto life was found by me to be unto death;* this is, *sin, having taken occasion, deceived me through the commandment and through it killed me;* this is, *the Law entered in, that the offense might abound;* this is, *the Law works wrath;* this is, *the strength of sin is the Law.* For the prohibition of sin, which is the Law, surely increases the desire of sinning, which is not extinguished except by the contrary desire of doing rightly, where faith works through love. But this the commanding letter does not supply, but the helping spirit: not the Law, then, but grace; not the Old Testament, generating unto slavery, which is Hagar, but the New, in which the children are not of the slave woman but of the free woman, by which freedom Christ has set us free. And yet the Law is holy and the commandment holy and just and good. Through this holy and just and good commandment, in those who do not have the Spirit of Christ, sin works all concupiscence. Showing what he himself had been in the Old Testament, the Apostle too says, *sin, having taken occasion, worked in me through the commandment all concupiscence.* Through what commandment, but that which he says: *I would not have known concupiscence, unless the Law said: You shall not covet?* Is it bad not to covet? On the contrary, it is supremely good. The Law, then, is good which says this; but where the life-giving spirit is not present, this very Law, which says what is good, kills, because it is the strength of sin, when through it sin works all concupiscence by inflaming through prohibition; which is not extinguished through the commanding letter by fear of punishment, but through the helping spirit by love of righteousness. Therefore he says, *Sin, that it might appear sin, worked death in me through the good.* For he does not say, "through the bad," but "through the good." Here let them awake, if they can, who with a blind and raging heart reprehend the Law of God and his minister Moses. For it is the ministry of death precisely because sin worked death through the good. It is the ministry of condemnation precisely because sin worked condemnation through the good.

## That the true, just, and good God blinded the minds of the unbelievers of this age.

**7. 29.** But not all who are called Christians pass over to Christ, but those from whom the veil is removed which remains in the reading of the Old Testament. For those who are in the Old Testament, with the veil hindering, understand neither the Old nor the New; but those who pass over to Christ, the veil removed, understand through the New both the Old and the New. Would that these blind assailants of the Law and the Prophets too might so pass over to Christ that they not be among those in whom the Gospel itself is veiled. For *among those who perish it is veiled,* says the Apostle, *in whom the God of this age blinded the minds of the unbelievers, so that the illumination of the Gospel of the glory of Christ, who is the image of God, may not shine.* Here this wretch wishes the God of this age to be understood as evil—as though Moses had served him in the Old Testament, as though the Apostle had said this. Here, if it were necessary to understand the God of this age as the god of the impious—that is, the devil, since all the gods of the gentiles are demons, and surely much most of all the prince of demons—it would be no wonder, since the belly too is called the god of certain people; for the Apostle says, *whose god is the belly;* nor is the belly therefore a god. So, if the devil can be called the god of this age, the devil is not therefore a god; because neither are demons gods, although the gods of the gentiles are demons. For "age" (saeculum) can be understood in an evil sense; whence the apostle [Peter] says: *He will rescue you from the present evil age.* But since another understanding lies open, what need is there here to think the devil signified, and not rather the true, just, and good God, who blinded the minds of the unbelievers of this age—so that it should not be punctuated here, "in whom the God of this age," and then subjoined, "blinded the minds of the unbelievers," but rather thus, "in whom God," and then subjoined, "blinded the minds of the unbelievers of this age"—that is, blinded the minds of the unbelievers of this age?

## God enlightens by mercy, blinds by judgment—a judgment most just, even if most hidden.

**8. 30.** But it does not please these people that the good God should blind the minds of some. For they do not attend to the words of the Savior saying: *For judgment I came into this world, so that those who do not see may see, and those who see may become blind.* He, therefore, who—according to the apostolic sentence—has mercy on whom he wills, and hardens whom he wills, without doubt enlightens whom he wills and blinds whom he wills. But there is no injustice with God, to whom the Church says: *Mercy and judgment I will sing to you, O Lord.* He enlightens, then, by mercy, blinds by judgment—a judgment most just, even if most hidden. For his judgments are inscrutable. To whom nevertheless it is said: *You have sat upon the throne, you who judge with equity.*

## How the Gospel is not hostile to the Law, just as Abraham and Moses are not hostile to each other.

**8. 31.** This God Moses served, and the other Prophets, whom the Lord himself testifies without doubt to be righteous. For the Jews were building their tombs and adorning their monuments, to whom he says: *You build the tombs of the Prophets and adorn the monuments of the righteous.* But all those, although for the dispensation of the time they ministered the figures of the Old Testament, nevertheless pertained, by the grace of God, to the New Testament, though not yet revealed—to which Abraham pertained. For from this, if they read with the veil removed, these men would understand that the Gospel is no more hostile to the Law given through Moses than Abraham and Moses himself are hostile to each other—whom even these men confess to have worshiped the same God, although they so blaspheme him as to deny that he is God. And yet the Apostle so sets the promises which were made to Abraham, because they signified the New Testament, against the Law given through Moses, that these two seem hostile to each other. For what else does he say to the Romans? *For not through the Law was the promise made to Abraham or to his seed, that he should be the heir of the world, but through the righteousness of faith. For if those who are by the Law are heirs, faith is emptied and the promise is made void. For the Law works wrath. For where there is no Law, neither is there transgression.* Let them attend to how he thus argues, as though against the Law, so as to convict it by that earlier promise which was made to Abraham, that whoever are heirs of God are heirs not from the Law but from the promise. And likewise to the Galatians: *Brothers,* he says, *I speak after the manner of man; yet a man's confirmed testament no one annuls or adds to. To Abraham the promises were spoken, and to his seed. He does not say, And to seeds, as in many, but as in one: And to your seed, which is Christ. But this I say: a testament confirmed by God, the Law, which came four hundred and thirty years after, does not invalidate so as to void the promise. For if the inheritance is from the Law, it is now no longer from the promise. But to Abraham God granted it through a promise. Why then the Law? It was set forth for the sake of transgression, until the seed should come to whom the promise was made.* I do not know whether these men, who accuse the Law without understanding it, find anything from the Gospel or from the apostolic writings which seems so adverse and contrary to the same Law as does this, which the Apostle sets against it from the promises which were made to Abraham. If, then, they hate the Law, let them love Abraham.

## For Abraham is not found to have defiled himself by any adultery, since he did not love the slave woman by the slipperiness of lust but received her from his wife.

**9. 32.** But they do not want this either. For they even cast the charge of fornication against the very father of the gentiles, to whom the promises were made which we now see fulfilled in all the gentiles. For this man, surely, to whom we are answering, shows himself to be of those of whom it was foretold, the Apostle saying: *But the Spirit manifestly says that in the last times some will depart from the faith, giving heed to seducing spirits and doctrines of demons, in the hypocrisy of liars, having their conscience cauterized, forbidding to marry.* For Abraham is not found to have defiled himself by any adultery, since he did not love the slave woman by the slipperiness of lust but received her from his wife, when his wife did what she wished with her own right, wishing to have children by her husband, though from another womb: where there was thought of no wantonness at all, but only of begetting. But this man "casts against Abraham, even up to decrepit old age, the charge of fornication—surely because even after Sarah's death he took another wife." Where, even if no sacrament of a hidden matter were understood, for this reason alone Abraham ought to have done it: lest the heretics—against the Apostle, whom Tertullian too stands by as an abettor—should think it a crime to take a wife after a wife's death. But just as this man thinks he finds in the apostolic writings things he may say against the Law given through Moses or against the Old Testament, let him find something of the kind against Abraham that may seem to sound from the gospel writings: he will not find it at all. Wherever Abraham is named in the books of the New Testament, he is named with due honor, so that the Lord himself said to the Jews: *If you are the sons of Abraham, do the works of Abraham.* And thereby this man, who blasphemes Abraham, surely blasphemes Christ, who bears such testimony to Abraham.

## Abraham the Apostle so praises among the perfect, especially in those two sons of his, one by the slave woman, the other by the free woman.

**9. 33.** But let him say, if he can, which of those five persons the Apostle was bearing when he so proclaimed Abraham. For if he had become to those who had been without the Law as though he himself were without the Law, those people did not know Abraham. He ought, then, to have found some chief—whether of the Romans or of the Greeks—or some philosopher, whom he might proclaim to such people, so as to suit himself to them, as this man pleases, by pretending himself such as they were: not Abraham, some foreigner or other, father of the Hebrew nation, far from their morals, far from their rites, far from their kinships, an alien. But if he had become as a Jew to the Jews, or, to those who were under the Law, as though he himself were under the Law, why did he say they were not heirs from the Law? Why did he say, *The Law works wrath?* Why did he say, *The Law was set forth for the sake of transgression?* Which those who gloried in the Law could not bear with an even mind. But if, as a weak man, he was speaking to the weak and thus, as it pleases the deceivers, was suckling them by deceiving them, why did he so drive them from the old shadows in which the weak rested, as to say: *Behold, I Paul say to you that if you are circumcised, Christ will profit you nothing?* But if, in that fifth person, he was speaking wisdom to the perfect—whom alone this man thinks worthy not to be deceived by the Apostle—why, on the contrary, does he himself wish to be perfect, so as to blaspheme Abraham, whom the Apostle so praises among the perfect, especially in those two sons of his, one by the slave woman, the other by the free woman? If the Old Testament with Ishmael displeases him, let the New Testament with Isaac please him.

## But let us hear and understand the two Testaments in the two sons of Abraham and his two women made fruitful by his union.

**9. 34.** Or is he going to set up against the Apostle a chair of pestilence and dispute about the quality of the figures and say that he ought not to have drawn figures of honorable things from shameful things? For even conjugal intercourse with Sarah herself seems to this man a shameful thing—Sarah, whom the Teacher of the gentiles testifies signifies the free woman, our mother, the eternal Jerusalem—although the teacher of the pestilent wrinkles his nose and his brow and, with a crinkled countenance, shudders at this parable; and much more, and more contemptuously, when he hears the same Teacher of the gentiles, regarding what is written about male and female, *The two will be in one flesh,* add and say, *This is a great sacrament, but I speak in Christ and in the Church.* Did this man, then, know—and the blessed Apostle not know—that where the sacraments of so great a matter were to be made known, that is, the sacred signs, even in a shameful matter and in those words to which modesty is owed, they ought not to have been made known or said? Let this man surely go back, with his like companions who said, *This is a hard saying; who can hear it?* But let us hear and understand the two Testaments in the two sons of Abraham and his two women made fruitful by his union: just as we know—without any obscenity, though these men are unwilling—the two in one flesh, Christ and the Church; just as we receive, with a faithful heart and mouth, the mediator of God and men, the man Christ Jesus, giving us his flesh to eat and his blood to drink, although it may seem more horrible to eat human flesh than to kill it, and to drink human blood than to shed it; and in all the holy Scriptures, if anything is expounded as having been figuratively done or said, according to the rule of sound faith, concerning whatever things and words are contained in the sacred pages, let us hear that exposition drawn out not contemptuously but wisely; and let us leave this man babbling empty things and treating, by a kind of—if it may be said—unskilled skill, of the quality of figures, not knowing what he says. For when he says that something is to be signified by congruent, not contrary things, the empty man can say that God ought always to be written in bright gold, never in black ink, since *God is light, and there is no darkness at all in him.* For he is a man who thinks that the Apostle, in order to suit the weak and imperfect, said many false and reprehensible things; for how venerably he accepts those Scriptures appears through his epistles by the testimonies which he sets down from the Law and the Prophets. Nor does he consider that by so perverse a sense even those things can be defended at which, unclean and impious, he shudders as though he were devout and clean, in the old books of God. For if someone like him should say to him: those things which offend you in the Law and the Prophets are false, but on account of the weak and imperfect the Holy Spirit wished them so set down—he will have nothing to answer his fellow empty man. For he is convicted by a false rule, but his own, and is throttled—not by the hand of one wise and learned, but of one foolish, as he himself is, and untaught—yet by his own sword.

## A series of responses to the adversary's assertions.

**10. 35.** After these sacrilegious empty words, indeed, of a sacrilegious and empty man, to which I think I have sufficiently replied—that is, about the beginning of Genesis, about the fashioning of light, about the day or the sun, about the reason for establishing man, about the sin of Adam, about the fashioning of man, about the serpent's persuasion, about the curse on man and about the tree of life, about the repentance of God, about the flood, about the bow in the clouds, about the hardening of Pharaoh's heart, about the lying spirit according to the prophet Micaiah, about the testimony of the prophet Isaiah, on account of which he says, *I have begotten and exalted sons,* to whom again he says, *Wicked sons, a worst seed;* and what he likewise said is written in him, *I am the God who makes good things and creates evils,* about the destruction of the people commanded to Moses, about the curse which he thinks shameful, about the cruelty which he thinks God confessed, about the zeal which he thinks malice in the case of King David, about that which is written, *It repents me that I appointed Saul as king;* about the spirit of Moses, whose Scriptures he thinks the Apostle called old wives' tales, about the quality of the figures, about Abraham, about the sons of Eli the priest, about the sacrifices which he thinks are offered to none but demons, about the Prophets of God, whom he thinks did not exist before the Lord's advent, about that which is set down in the Law, that the blood is the soul, about the God whom Moses served, whom he thinks was not the true God, about the variety of persons in which he thinks the Apostle spoke deceitfully. All of which I have pursued not in the order which is in his book, but as the interconnected series of our discussion required.

## The adversary began, by short, frequent sentences set against one another, to praise Christ and to accuse the God of the Law.

After all these things, then, he set down a title reading thus: "The discernment of the spirits of malignity and of goodness." And he began, by short, frequent sentences set against one another, to praise Christ and to accuse the God of the Law, in this manner, as it were exhorting the one to whom he was writing: "Why then, brother," he says, "withdrawing from the iniquity of past error, let us attend to Christ, the true and highest God, not the prince of this age and maker of the world, in which it has been most often declared that we are pilgrims. Let us attend, I say, to that devout and gentle one, who, showing us to be of his kindred, called us the light of the world; not to him who, according to the Jewish Scriptures, assigning us an earthly beginning, decreed for us an end in the earth. Who, calling us brothers, persuaded us to be watchful and to be wise about divine things; not to him who did not even permit us to have the sense of discernment." And in this manner he wove together many other things.

## Final exhortation: let us attend to Christ, the true and highest God, the only Son of the true and highest God.

**11. 37.** To this passage of his book I have thought to respond in such a way that I too should exhort you: let us attend to Christ, the true and highest God, the only Son of the true and highest God—not the malign prince of this age, but nevertheless the maker of the world, that is, of heaven and earth, who commanded us, as pilgrims, to lead a temporal life in this mortality. Let us attend, I say, to that merciful and gentle one, who made us his brothers by grace, not by nature. For he it is, not another, as this man thinks, who, according to the Scriptures, gave us an earthly body but a soul by his breath, making both, begetting neither of them. Who commanded and made us to be watchful and wise about divine things. For he it is, not another, as this man thinks, who admonished us about the discernment of good and evil, lest we should learn it by sinning. Who, calling us to immortality, promised us the heavenly kingdom. For he it is, not another, as this man thinks, who, after sin, separated us as guilty from the felicity of eternal life and punished us with earthly labor. Who commanded us not, as this man says, to be ignorant of nothing, but to know useful things: nor, as this man thinks—and dissents from the truth—did he condemn in us the knowledge which comes from being wise about righteousness, but the knowledge which comes from experiencing sin. Who had mercy on us because we were dying by error. For he it is, not another, as this man thinks, who destined us to death not from the time we began to be wise—which this man says—but from the time we transgressed. Who exhorts us to spurn our own goods—indeed, rather to store them up in a safer place—since he himself it is, not another, as this man thinks, who, showing himself to be the Lord not only of heavenly but also of earthly things, either commanded or permitted his own people, to whom this was fitting for the time, to take away and have the things which the impious possessed, who were to be punished by despoiling. Who forgives offenses to the converted: since he himself it is, not another, as this man thinks, who repays to the perverse what is fitting, unto the third and fourth generation. Who forgives the offenses not of all, as this man says, but of those whom he foreknew and predestined beforehand.

But he it is, not another, as this man thinks, who avenged the offenses of certain people, for the penal grief of mind and the greater terror even of those who had not committed them, not by spiritual but by bodily deaths, so that the condition of mortals—by which they were, after a little while, going to die—might in this way too serve the providence of God and turn to the use of discipline. Who did not forbid us to be angry altogether, since he himself too was angry where it was fitting, but commanded us to be angry and not to sin. But he it is, not another—not one who seeks out causes for avenging, as this man asserts, but who attends to causes to be avenged when he himself knows them. Who admonished us never to swear, so that, since we can be deceived, we might be made far from perjury by not swearing. But he it is, not another, as this man thinks, who confirmed even by swearing the truth of his pronouncement, for rebuking the unbelieving, where he judged it to be needful. But as a man who swears brings God as a witness, so God brings himself. Who commanded us to stand in the faith of the true word. For he it is, not another, as this man thinks—not one who, as this man blasphemes, changed his will, but who changed the things which he willed, without any change of his will. Who taught us the way of truth. For he it is also the God of the Prophets, who never, as this man cavils, deceives his own people with false promises. Who commanded us to be irreproachable. For he it is also the God of the Prophets, who never, as this man charges, reproached himself, nor did anything repent him as it does a man, but he foretold, as by a human manner of speaking to human beings, the change of future things which he foreknew from eternity that he would change without any change of himself. Who in the Gospel too commended to us the wrath of God as to be feared. For he it is also the God of the Prophets, who named not a disturbance of his own mind but a just and severe vengeance by the name of wrath or indignation. Who willed that one should be harmed by another not in any way whatever, but harmed by injury. For he it is also the God of the Prophets, who—whether through human beings or through holy angels—usefully either punished or terrified, even by the temporal deaths of bodies, those whom he willed.

Who taught that a woman is not to be looked at so as to covet her. For he it is who also in the Law said, *You shall not covet;* nor, as this man charges, did he decree seven marriages for each man, but permitted chaste wedlock for the sake of propagation. But the fathers of daughters he himself not only did not make their husbands, but even forbade that it should come about, as other incestuous unions. Who taught us, according to the inner renewal in the spirit of the mind, to be neither male nor female, and promised that we will be with him forever like the angels. For he it is also the God of the Prophets, who joined male and female, for the sake of propagating the race, by nuptial chastity, and showed that second marriages—which in the New Testament too are permitted—are lawful. But he commanded that the wives of brothers who had died without children be joined to oneself in matrimony, for raising up the dead man's posterity, not by the affection of lust but of devotion; while he altogether forbade the fathers of daughters to be mingled in marriages with them. Who commanded us spiritually to tread upon every kind of serpent. For he it is also the God of the Prophets, who, for an unbelieving people, in order to signify the sins by whose venom they were invisibly dying, sent visible serpents by which they might be admonished, and by a corrective stroke figured the deaths of souls by the deaths of bodies. Who said, *Give alms, and behold, all things are clean to you.* For he it is also the God of the Prophets, who commanded such things also by the voice of the Prophets. Nor did he wish the firstborn sons of human beings to be immolated to himself, but consecrated, signifying the firstborn from the dead, in whom it was fitting that all be freed from perpetual death. Who set incorruptible foods before corruptible ones. For he it is also the God of the Prophets, who willed that sacrifices, which he did not need, should go before, in shadows of things to come, to signify the true sacrifice, and who, by the severity of discipline, avenged sacrileges committed by deaths of bodies far milder than are the eternal punishments of the hells. Who commanded that earthly riches be not, as this man says, condemned, but set spiritual and heavenly things before them. For he it is also the God of the Prophets, who makes both the rich, by most kindly bestowing, and the poor, by most justly either not giving or taking away.

Who commanded us to pray for our enemies. For he it is also the God of the Prophets, who not only never willed that sons be immolated to him by the hands of their parents, as this man charges, but even established in the Law that it should not be done. Who intimated that good is to be done to all without respect of persons. For he it is also the God of the Prophets, who—when he commanded human beings to be killed without distinction of age or sex—how does this man, or anyone, know what good recompense he allotted to them after death, by whose deaths the living were either amended or terrified? Who commanded us to bear injuries with an even mind and to forgive. For he it is also the God of the Prophets, who set, as the measure of punishment, eye for eye, tooth for tooth, lest anyone should think more owed to him for vengeance than he had suffered of injury. And this for the reason that it was said of the wisdom of God that it carries law and mercy on its tongue. Nor would we knowingly forgive their debts to our debtors by mercy, unless we discerned those very debts by the Law. Who, in such great power, humbling himself to strengthen us, having become man, spoke with human beings. For he it is also the God of the Prophets, who spoke with the first fathers: therefore saying that besides himself there is no other God, because even the highest Trinity, with the property of the persons remaining, is one God. Who commanded his Apostles to give freely what they had freely received, yet, for their own sustenance, decreed that they should not carry even another tunic—for this reason, that, following, he added, *For the laborer is worthy of his wages*—so that those who announced the Gospel should live from the Gospel. Yet he showed that gifts are to be offered to God, who needs nothing and, by taking gifts, confers more. For he it is also the God of the Prophets, who both forbids his own people to take gifts, by which the eyes of judges are blinded, and himself takes gifts, although he has no need, so that by devotion he may make wealthy the souls of those who offer. Who healed a man on the Sabbath, showing that it was now the time when, according to the prophecy of the Song of Songs, the day should breathe and the shadows be removed.

For he it is also the God of the Prophets, who commanded that a man gathering wood on the Sabbath—because he did not yet distinguish the times of the two Testaments, but had despised the Law of God with a contumacious and impious mind—was to be stoned; and by his bodily death (which who doubts will come, after a little while, to every man) he sanctioned, by fear, an obedience that would profit the rest. Who said that he had come for the sake of saving human beings. For he it is also the God of the Prophets, who by a just judgment hardens whom he wills, as in the Gospel: he came for judgment, not only so that those who do not see may see, but also so that those who see may become blind. Who gave us the commands of perpetual life. For he it is also the God of the Prophets, who gave commands holy and just and good to the proud—who nevertheless trusted not in his grace but in their own strength—not for them to live by, but for them to die by, so that they might be convicted. Just as the Apostles, as one of them says, were the good odor of Christ both among those who were being saved and among those who were perishing; to some, indeed, an odor of life unto life, to others an odor of death unto death. Who appeared as the salvation of the maimed, giving gait to the lame, tongue to the mute, hearing to the deaf, light to the blind. For he it is also the God of the Prophets, who not only most mercifully heals these same defects but also most justly brings them on. For no one ought, with an impiety no less than the others, to think him contrary to Christ and to call this one good but Christ evil, on the ground that this one made the dry wood of Aaron's rod, supported by no root, bear flower and fruit, but Christ withered a tree by a curse, because he found on it none of the fruits whose time had not yet come.

## He is one God in both Testaments.

**12. 38.** But when he says "that one is the father of peace and love, another the author of war and fury," wishing the former to be understood as "Christ, but the latter as the God of the Law and the Prophets," the empty man can say that Christ himself is contrary to himself, or that there were two, not one, namely repugnant to each other: one who said, *Peace I give to you;* and another who said, *I came not to send peace upon the earth, but a sword;* especially because it displeases him that some good things should be figured by the names of bad things. But as for his saying that "one is privy to incest and adulteries, but another is the lord of chaste purity and holiness," let him understand the former as the devil; but let him know that the God of the Prophets is no less the lord of chastity and holiness than Christ, because the God of the Apostles is the same as the God of the Prophets—those earlier in time than these, but their associates in faith; he is in both Testaments the God of just action and of devout prayers; he is in both the author of religious sacrifices.

## Satan, who always has the desire of harming, can harm only as much as he has received power from the Almighty.

**12. 39.** See indeed from what he wished to prove that the defects of human bodies pertain not to God as their author but rather to the devil: because the Lord says in the Gospel, about the woman whom he made whole, that Satan had bound her for eighteen years, on account of which she was bent over and could not raise herself. As though Satan, since he always has the desire of harming, could harm anyone unless he had received power from the Almighty. For what else was declared not only in the book of Job—which this man surely does not accept—but even more in the Gospel itself, where the unclean spirits would not go even into the swine unless the kindly Savior himself granted it to them at their request—those whom he could certainly have banished into the abyss—wishing to teach us a necessary thing, namely that we may know they can far less harm human beings by their own power, who could not harm even cattle of any kind whatever? But this power the good God can give by a justice hidden from us; he cannot give it unjustly.

## John called those heretics antichrists who had already begun to exist in the times of the Apostles.

**12. 40.** Then, as for what the Apostle said "about the advent and impious exaltation of the antichrist," this man wishes to drag it to this, that "we should understand him to be the God of the Prophets"—whence rather it is proved to be the temple of God where the Apostle foretold that the man of sin, the son of perdition, exalting himself above every God and what is worshiped, would sit. For it is the true God in whose temple that false one will sit: to whom this man too pertains, who, under the name of Christ—which is the name of God, that is, wishing to seem himself a Christian—exalts himself against Christ and shows himself an antichrist, not that one greater than the rest, but one of those of whom the evangelist John says: *Now many antichrists have come to be.* For he called those heretics who had already begun to exist in the times of the Apostles. But these began to exist after the ascension into heaven of the Lord Jesus Christ, from that Simon Magus, whom we read was baptized in the Acts of the Apostles. But after him there were certain disciples of his in the same impiety, the later succeeding the earlier, in whose succession the fourth was Basilides, who first more openly dared to say that the God whom the Jewish nation worshiped was not the true God. After these there was also a certain Carpocrates, who denied that this visible world was created by the highest God, but by certain powers of demons, denying also that God gave the Law which was given through Moses. Cerdo arose afterward, who is found to have been the first to say there are two gods, one good and the other evil, long before the heresy of the Manicheans had emerged, whose error in this raging delirium is the more notorious. Of this Cerdo, Marcion was a disciple. Apelles too taught such things. There were also, from a certain Patricius, some Patriciani—or there are—similarly opposing the divine old books. All these most openly are minded against the God of the Law and the Prophets, that is, the true God, by whom the world was made. This man is of some heresy of these; for I do not think he is a Manichean.

## "As far as concerns this book which you sent, I think it has been sufficiently answered."

**12. 41.** But of whatever heretical error he may be—whether he himself or that Fabricius or other, whose disciple he boasts of being—as far as concerns this book which you sent, I think it has been sufficiently answered. For those props of his fury have been cast down, from which all the blasphemies that he wrote in that volume sprouted, leafing out with a varied loquacity in most impious curses and insults against God. To strike each of their branches one by one was too long; but the very roots had to be cut off. But if you review what we wrote against Faustus the Manichean and against Adimantus—who is boasted to have been a follower of Mani while he lived—you will find many things which avail equally against this man. And perhaps, if those works were read, it would have been either not at all or not much necessary to write these.

## Conclusion: God created all things good, great in great things, but not small in small things.

**12. 42.** Indeed, after the end of the book—in which ending he commends the fewness of those in his error, because, namely, wisdom belongs to the few (which is common, to each in his own proper vanity, to all the heretical adversaries of the Catholic Church, which is spread through all lands in copious fertility—for all of them glory in fewness, and seek a multitude that they may be able to seduce it)—after this end of the book, then, there appears again the beginning of another, by the same man as author perhaps, but at least of the same error, which is not doubted. He had begun to argue that the flesh had another maker, not God. Whence, when he had said very few things, the things begun were finished in the very beginning. But whether the author himself, or the copyist of the manuscript, could not complete what he had begun, I do not know. About this dementia, however, of human beings not considering what they say, we have already written many things against the Manicheans. And in the very beginning of this work, suitable foundations, as I reckon, have been laid, by which the prudent and devout reader may understand that the flesh is not to be withdrawn from the works of God on the ground that the nature of spirit is superior, nor that these temporal things are evil because eternal things are deservedly preferred to them, nor that earthly goods are to be detested because heavenly ones are better, since God created all things good, great in great things but not small in small things. Now that other thing, which had begun to be written in the same manuscript, is the work of Adimantus, that disciple of Mani who by his own name was called Addas, where, concerning each Testament, testimonies as though contrary to one another are brought forward with crafty deceitfulness, as though from this it were shown that both cannot be from one God, but one from one and the other from another. But against this malign contrivance we have already long since written, as I recalled a little before, and I believe you have that little work of ours. Although there are some very few things at the end of that work of Adimantus to which I did not respond—for, as is wont to happen, certain other matters running up which seemed more pressing, those were thus left interrupted. But, as I said, at the end they are very few, which, if the Lord wills, I will take care to unfold as soon as possible.
