# Book I

## On the plan and subject undertaken in the work.

**1. 1.** In the book you sent, most beloved brothers, the invention of some heretic or other—which, as you wrote, was being read in the seaside square when the manuscript itself was being carried about for sale and was being read with dangerous curiosity and delight by the crowds running together—in order that I might answer it as briefly as I could, I first asked of what error it was. For it is not only the Manicheans who condemn the Law and the Prophets, but also the Marcionists and certain others, whose sects have not become so well known among Christian peoples. This man, however, whose name I did not find in that same book, detests God the maker of the world; whereas the Manicheans, although they do not accept the book of *Genesis* and even blaspheme it, nevertheless confess that a good God made the world, even if out of an alien nature and matter. But although it has not become apparent to me of what sect this blasphemer is, the divine Scripture, which he assails with abusive arguments, must be defended against his tongue. And since he wishes in some way to appear a Christian—whence he also sets down some testimonies from the Gospel and from the Apostle—he must be refuted from the Scriptures belonging to the New Testament as well, so that it may be shown that in his censure of the old writings he raves more rashly than cleverly.

## What a devout man might have asked with a reverent mouth, this impious man asks with a sacrilegious one.

**2. 2.** And first, what a devout man might have asked with a reverent mouth, this impious man asks with a sacrilegious one: how we are to understand what is written, *In the beginning God made heaven and earth*[^1]—though he did not add *the earth,* of which he speaks afterward. He asks, then: "In what beginning? In the one in which this same God began to exist, or from the time when it wearied him to exist idle?" To him the reply is that God neither began to exist nor was wearied by his own idleness, since he neither at any time was not, nor grew sluggish by ceasing, nor labored by working, nor lacked a dwelling place without a heaven, nor, once heaven was made, found a dwelling like a wanderer whose roamings were now over. For he is able most blessedly to abide in himself; yet to his temple, which is all the saints, both angels and human beings, he so imparts his own indwelling that they have from him such a good that they can be blessed, not he from them such a home without which he could not be blessed[^2]. Either, then, what is written—*In the beginning God made heaven and earth*—is to be understood so that "the beginning" is that from which they began to exist (for they were not always coeternal with God, but, being made, received a beginning from which they should begin to exist); or surely it is that in the beginning coeternal with himself God made heaven and earth, that is, in the only-begotten Son[^3]. For he is the wisdom of which the Apostle says, *Christ the power of God and the wisdom of God*[^4]. And to God who made heaven and earth it is said in the psalm, *You have made all things in wisdom*[^5]. Or if this man is unwilling to accept the testimony of the psalm, let him hear the Apostle speaking about Christ: *For in him all things were created, in heaven and on earth, visible and invisible*[^6].

## God neither began to exist nor will cease to exist.

**2. 3.** God, therefore, neither began to exist nor will cease, but certain of his works, having a beginning, cease to exist at a fixed end, as do times and temporal things; certain others, having a beginning, will remain, such as the eternal life that holy human beings are to receive. This man did not see this, for he supposed that it must be said and laid down as a rule that no beginning is without an end. He could not even attend to number itself, whose beginning is from one and whose end is in no number at all. For no number, however great—whether it is stated, or, if it can no longer be stated, is conceived—is such that something cannot be added to it to make it greater. And I suppose that, whatever heresy this man holds under the name of Christ against Christ, he promises himself a life that is at any rate blessed in Christ, a life that can have its beginning only when the end of this wretched life has come. Let him answer, then, whether that blessed life, which he does not deny will have or has a beginning, is going to have an end or not. If he says it will have one, how will he dare to call himself a Christian? If he says it will not, where is what he dared to say, that no beginning is without an end?

## It was made long ago from its beginning—from its own beginning, that is, not from God's, who has no beginning.

**3. 4.** He also asks: "If this world is something good, why was it not made by him long ago from the beginning, which would have been better?"—as though God had made the world something better than himself, or as though it ought not to have been made good because it is not equal to its maker. But as for what he asks—why this was not made long ago from the beginning—let him have his answer: on the contrary, it was indeed made long ago from its beginning—from its own beginning, that is, not from God's, who has no beginning. And if this beginning could be signified by the words *In the beginning God made,* why does he cavil as though asking in what beginning he made, when he himself in turn asks why it was not made long ago from the beginning? Accordingly, his own words must be turned back upon him. For just as he, on account of what is written—*In the beginning God made heaven and earth*[^7]—argues and says, "In what beginning, then? In the one in which this same God began to exist, or from the time when it wearied him to be idle?"—so too let us, in response to what he says, "Why was this not made by him long ago from the beginning?" answer and say to him: From what beginning, then? From the one in which this same God began to exist, or from the time when it wearied him to be idle? For this is what displeases him, that God did not make heaven long ago from the beginning, when he says, "Why not long ago from the beginning?"—as though God ought to have made it from that beginning from which is the very one who made it. Why was he not afraid here, lest, by saying that from the beginning from which God himself exists he ought to have made heaven, it be said to him: Then God has a beginning, and thereby, according to your own opinion, he will also have an end—for you yourself said that no beginning is without an end? Moreover, if God has no beginning, how could he make anything from the beginning from which he himself exists? And so he made it from that beginning from which he made it—that is, from which began to exist what he made. Either, then, the God of these men never made anything good; or, according to this man, whatever good he made, he made from that beginning from which he himself exists, and one who has a beginning must fear an end. Or else he ought to acquiesce in the words of holy Scripture and understand that God, who began from no beginning, made heaven in the beginning—either from which heaven began to exist, or in the Son, who, when the Jews asked who he was, answered that he was the beginning[^8].

## That "from the beginning" is the same as "in the beginning."

**3. 5.** Unless perhaps he is going to say that one thing is "a beginning" (*initium*) and another "the beginning" (*principium*). If, then, it had been written "In the beginning [*in initio*] God made heaven and earth," this man would have raised no objection against it—he who said, "Why was it not made long ago from the beginning?"—since he would read "In the beginning [*in initio*] God made," not thinking it impious if God is believed to have a beginning (*initium*), but only if he has a beginning (*principium*). But if this is so, then on his authority the Gospel must be corrected, where it is written, *In the beginning was the Word*[^9]*.* For why should he not say here too: In what beginning, then? In the one in which the Word itself began to exist? And since the Word was God, he can say here too, just as he said in this book of his, "In the one in which this same God began to exist?" Let him state here too, if he pleases, that definitive rule of his, where he hears *In the beginning was the Word;* let him say here too, if he dares: no beginning is without an end—so that even by the Manicheans themselves, who perhaps read him most gladly because they find him an enemy to the Law and the Prophets, he may be judged insane. But since the God who made the world displeases him, why does the one through whom the world was made not displease him? For of Christ it is written, *He was in this world, and the world was made through him*[^10]*.*

## A good thing, even one that is not the highest—indeed even the lowest—could not exist except from the highest good.

**4. 6.** Let him therefore understand that God can make good things, but cannot need the good things made by him, and so did not need to make them, since he does not need the things made. But, being supremely good, he made all things—unequal to himself, indeed, yet good. For a good thing, even one that is not the highest, indeed even the lowest, could not exist except from the highest good. But he thinks far too ill of God who denies that something is good on the ground that he recognizes it is not equal to God. For God will be not the highest but the lowest good, if whatever is less than he is no longer good. Moreover, if among the things he founded those that are the first goods are exceedingly unequal to their Founder—because he made them, while these were made—then surely one who did not need such things to increase his blessedness is much less to be believed to have needed the lower things, and the lowest things altogether. Yet he made them, as the founder of all good things. For the Lord Jesus, through whom the world was made[^11], shows that God created and creates not only the heavenly things but also the earthly, and even those slight earthly things that are seen, when he says: *If God so clothes the grass of the field, which today is and tomorrow is cast into the oven, how much more you, O you of little faith?*[^12] One and the same God, therefore, is the founder of heaven and earth, of stars and herbs—whatever stands by its own measure, form, and order in heaven and on earth, whatever lives in heaven and on earth, whatever perceives in heaven and on earth, whatever understands in heaven and on earth. These things ought to have been equal neither to him by whom they were founded nor even to one another, and for that reason they are all things. For if they were equal, there would be one kind of good, not all things. But as it is, they are all good precisely because some are better than others, and the goodness of the lower adds to the praises of the better; and in this very inequality of good things there is a pleasing gradation, where the comparison of the lesser is the commendation of the greater.

## Evil, then, is nothing but the privation of good.

**5. 7.** But the things that are called evils are either defects in good things—which cannot by themselves exist anywhere at all outside of good things—or the punishments of sins, which come from the beauty of justice. But even the defects themselves bear witness to the goodness of natures. For what is evil through a defect is surely good through nature. A defect, indeed, is contrary to nature, because it harms a nature, and it would not harm unless it diminished the nature's good. Evil, then, is nothing but the privation of good. And thus it is nowhere except in some good thing—even if not in a supremely good thing, since the supremely good endures incorruptible and unchangeable, as God does, yet only in a good thing, since it does no harm except by diminishing what is good. And thus good things can exist without evils, as God himself does and all the higher heavenly things; but evils cannot exist without goods. For if they do no harm, they are not evils; but if they do harm, they diminish a good. And if they harm still more, they still have a good to diminish; even if they consume the whole, no nature will remain to be harmed, and thus there will be no evil by which harm could be done, once the nature has failed whose good might be diminished by harm.

## Changeable goods are good for this reason, that they were made by the highest good; they are changeable for this reason, that they were made not from him but from nothing.

**6. 8.** But whether any nature—that is, substance—is reduced utterly to nothing is a most subtle question to discuss. Yet faith most truly sings to God: *You will change them, and they will be changed; but you are the same*[^13]*.* Nothing, therefore, either made or governs changeable goods except the unchangeable good, which is God. Moreover, changeable goods are good for this reason, that they were made by the highest good; they are changeable for this reason, that they were made not from him but from nothing. Hence even mortal things, although in some part they are penal for those whose perfect blessedness only immortality can fill out to the full measure, nevertheless have their own place of comeliness in the beauty of the ages. But the sense by which such beauty is perceived is greater than the human. Yet that faith which says to its God, *You have arranged all things in measure and number and weight*[^14]*,* although out of love of living it shrinks from the condition of death, nonetheless praises the Creator of all good things even for the good mortal things. For this very man, who reproaches God and does not believe him to be God because he sees that his earthly works are mortal, could not fill out even that discourse of his own—which pleased him so well that he committed it to letters and to memory—except with sounds that belong to each of his own words and that nevertheless both begin and pass away; thus the beauty of the discussion by which he wishes to persuade us that whatever arises and dies cannot be good, he could not unfold except with syllables that arise and die. What wonder is it, then, if in so vast a totality of nature there is some lowest good in the orders of the ages which is beautiful not by the permanence of things, like certain sublime spiritual things, but, befitting its kind, by a lowly and earthly rising and setting?

## God not only saw created things afterward, but also foresaw beforehand those to be created as good in their kind and order.

**6. 9.** Since these things are so, let him not cavil at Scripture when it says, *God saw the light, that it was good*[^15]. For God, supremely good, both created and saw to be good in their kind and order not only the light, which he called day, and the firmament, which he called heaven, and the sun and moon and the other stars, but also the trees and the herbs and whatever mortal things there are in the waters and on the lands. Nor was he afraid—he who by the revealing Spirit of God wrote this book—of future impious censurers, talkers of vanity and seducers of minds, first of their own minds and then of others', whom blasphemous loquacity delights; for these too, insofar as they are human beings, insofar as they consist of body and rational soul, insofar as the members of their body are distinguished by their own functions and, in most harmonious difference, by a marvelous ordering agree into the unity of their own peace[^16], insofar as the soul surpasses them by a natural excellence and rules them, insofar as it fills and quickens the fivefold senses of the flesh[^17] with unlike power yet a fellowship of agreement, insofar as also by mind and reason—which the bestial soul cannot do—they can be wise and understand: God saw that they were good, and therefore created them. For he did not only see them after they were created, but also foresaw them before they were to be created; and that by a perverse will and blind error they are malicious, he did not for that reason see that they were not to be created, for he foresaw where they were to be ordered. And so from that same mass of the first transgression, deservedly condemned, he made them vessels of wrath—if they persevere to the end in this malice—that they might profit the vessels of mercy, in that their keen edge of vanity makes them more diligently inquire into the hidden things of truth[^18]. For *great are the works of the Lord, sought out in all his wills*[^19]*.* But what wonder is it that what pleases the divine wisdom displeases human folly? For what else is *He saw the light, that it was good*[^20], but that it pleased him?

## One thing is the light that is God, another the light that God made.

**7. 10.** But this empty babbler and blasphemer, who wrote a book full of sacrilegious insults—in which, would that his own work displeased him and he did not think it good but saw that it was evil—see what he has said: "So little," he says, "did he previously know what light was, that, seeing it then for the first time, he judged it best." Then the Lord Jesus too, when, having heard the centurion's words, he marveled and said to his disciples, *Amen, I say to you, I have not found such great faith in Israel*[^21], did not previously know what that faith was, so that, seeing it then for the first time, he judged it best. Or was it indeed another who was working that faith in the centurion's heart than he who marveled? And surely there is greater occasion for the foolish and unbelieving to blaspheme in the fact that Jesus marveled at whatever measure of a man's faith than in the fact that God saw the light, that it was good. For anyone can see even familiar things, that they are good—that is, can approve as pleasing to him things pleasing to him; but whoever marvels surely signifies, according to the customary way human beings speak, that what he marvels at is unexpected and unforeseen by him. But Jesus, who foreknew all things, by marveling commended to others what was to be marveled at, and by marveling he praised. And what did God see made by himself that he did not first see, in the light which he himself is, was to be made? And what would holy Scripture so necessarily repeat so often as that God saw that the things he made were good—so that hereby the piety of the faithful might be formed not to judge of visible and invisible creation according to human sense, which is often offended even by good things whose causes and order it does not know, but to believe and learn from God as he praises? For the more religiously anyone has believed God before he came to know, the more easily, by making progress, he comes to know something[^22]. God saw, then, that the light he made was good, since what it pleased him to be made, when made it pleased him to remain—to whatever measure of existing or of remaining had been appointed for each thing by so great a maker. But one thing is the light that is God, another the light that God made. And the light incomparably better, the very one who made it, would in no way need that light which he made. And from the very point on which this man cavils—why God did not make these good things as long ago as he himself has been—we ought rather to understand this: that he did not make them as one needing them, since without them he could be in his own perfect blessedness, eternal without beginning. For the sole cause of these things being made was God's goodness, precisely because there was no necessity of them for him. Whatever this man, therefore, reproaches God with—that he supposedly then for the first time saw light, having been ignorant of light—how silly and empty this is he would see, if he had within himself anything of light.

## God made matter itself too. It is not to be thought evil because it is formless, but is to be understood good—formable, that is, capable of receiving form.

**8. 11.** Furthermore, he assigns "to the foolishness of the writer" the fact "that he said darkness had always existed without a beginning, but that light took its beginning from darkness"—as though he had read in that book at which he cavils that darkness was eternal, when it is written, *In the beginning God made heaven and earth. But the earth was invisible and unformed, and darkness was over the abyss*[^23]. From that point, then, darkness began to exist, from which the confused mass of heaven and earth began to exist, before the light was made by which what had been dark without light might be illumined. But what is unfitting if the beginnings of the world's matter were dark, so that with the coming of light what was made might be rendered better, and so that in this way might be signified the disposition of one making progress—what was afterward to come to be? Although whoever, by God's gift, has been able to examine these things more keenly will perhaps find in the creation, which is recounted as made thus without intervals of temporal delays, a wonderfully distinct order of things. For matter is not altogether nothing—the matter of which we read in the book of Wisdom, *You who made the world from formless matter*[^24]. Not, then, because it is called formless is it altogether nothing; nor was it itself coeternal with God, as if made by no one, nor did another make it so that God should have something from which to make the world. For far be it that the Almighty be said to have been unable to make unless he found something from which to make. Therefore God made this too. Nor is it to be thought evil because it is formless, but is to be understood good—formable, that is, capable of receiving form. For if form is something good, it is no small good to be capable of good. Just as a confused voice is a cry without words, but the voice becomes articulate when it is formed into words. That one, then, is formable, this one formed: the one that takes form, and the one that has it. For from what each of these comes to be is plain. For no one would say that the sound of the voice comes to be from words; rather, who does not understand that the words that sound come to be from the voice?

## God made the world from formless matter, but created that matter together with the world.

**9. 12.** Nor is God to be thought to have first made formless matter and, with some interval of time interposed, then to have given form to what he had first made formless; but just as from one speaking sounding words come to be—where the voice is not first formless and afterward receives form, but is uttered already formed—so God is to be understood to have made the world indeed from formless matter, but to have created that matter together with the world. Yet it is not without use that there is first recounted that from which something comes to be, and afterward what comes to be from it; for although both can come to be at the same time, both cannot be recounted at the same time.

## It is not lawful to doubt that the true, highest, and good God made all the things we discern, and the better things we do not discern.

**10. 13.** Whether, then, by the name of heaven and earth, or of the invisible and unformed earth and the dark abyss, the formless matter itself was first signified by the appellations of known things (since it is utterly unknown to the human senses and is scarcely in any way understood—when things are changed for the worse, as though something more deformed were drawing near to it, yet does not reach that formlessness, remaining still some slight visible or intelligible form); or whether by "heaven and earth" spiritual and corporeal nature was first generally intimated; or whether something else that can here be understood without violating the rule of faith—nevertheless it is not lawful to doubt that the true, highest, and good God made all the things we discern, and the better things we do not discern, although the manner in which he made them cannot be grasped by the human mind. But with these unlearned blasphemers of the sacred writings we must not deal with that subtlety of reasonings with which these matters are to be inquired into and discussed among the peaceful children of God[^25].

## The Father is light, and his splendor is the only-begotten Son.

**11. 14.** But if this man thinks that against the books of the Law and the Prophets he knows what he says he knows—that "the highest God is the incomparable splendor of incomprehensible light"—I would first wish to hear from him: of what light he supposes the highest God to be the splendor; whether the light itself is also God; and whether he understands the Father to be the light and his splendor to be the only-begotten Son, whom nevertheless he has confessed to be the highest God. If he thinks this, I approve and praise it. But that he does not believe the one whom he believes to be light from light, or the incomparable splendor of incomprehensible light, to be the maker of the world—this I disapprove and blame, since there he reads *The world was made through him*[^26], where he reads *He was the true light, which enlightens every human being coming into this world*[^27]. I disapprove also if he does not know, and disapprove and detest still more if he does know and lies in wait to deceive those who do not know that it was written first in the old divine writings: *Draw near to him and be enlightened*[^28], and *The commandment of the Lord is clear, enlightening the eyes*[^29], and *Enlighten my eyes, that I may never sleep in death*[^30]. For this mortal man was surely not praying that he might never die in body, nor wishing that sleep might not come to his bodily eyes; rather, he was asking that those eyes be enlightened for him of which the Apostle says, *The eyes of your heart enlightened*[^31]*.*

## The Son is day from day, light from light, the salvation of God.

**11. 15.** Moreover, if it displeases this man that light took its beginning from darkness—for this too he stirs up with the words of garrulous vanity—let him say this to the same Apostle, who, writing to the faithful, says, *You were once darkness, but now you are light in the Lord*[^32]. For who did this but he who, when darkness was over the abyss, said, *Let there be light. And light was made*[^33]? This the same Apostle expressed more openly in another place, saying, *God, who said for light to shine out of darkness, has shone in our hearts*[^34]. But if he thinks the prophetic Scriptures lack this—that the Son is light from light or the splendor of light—let him read what is read in those same writings concerning Wisdom: *For she is the brightness of eternal light*[^35], or that passage in the prophetic psalm: *Sing to the Lord a new song, sing to the Lord, all the earth; sing to the Lord and bless his name, proclaim well his salvation day from day*[^36]. For who is day from day but the Son, light from light? And that the salvation of God is Christ, let him read in the Gospel, where the aged Simeon, when he recognized him in his mother's hands—least in the flesh, greatest in spirit—and, taking him into his hands, said: *Now you dismiss your servant, Lord, according to your word in peace, for my eyes have seen your salvation*[^37].

## First, he will confess that from the highest light, which is God himself, some lowest light could be made, yet a good one.

**12. 16.** Here, if he answers that one light is that of which it was said, *You were once darkness, but now you are light in the Lord*[^38]—because this is the spiritual light of the mind, not of the flesh—and another that of which it is written, *God said: Let there be light. And light was made*[^39], which pertains to the bodily eyes: first, he will confess that from the highest light, which is God himself, some lowest light could be made, yet a good one. Then, how does he know even of what sort and how great it is? Finally, whether it is spiritual or corporeal? Unless perhaps faithful human beings, who, as long as they are in the body, are pilgrims away from the Lord[^40], can already deservedly, by reason of their faith itself, be called light, while the angels cannot, who always see the face of the Father[^41]? Whence, then, does he know whether such a light was first made? Whence does he know how in that light evening and morning[^42] can be understood? How, finally, it was presented by the number six in the works of God, and how on the seventh God rested—from which a certain form of that sevenfold number has been transferred to these days most familiar to us, which revolve by the circuit of the sun? But even if the light made was corporeal, whence does he know how it could exist before the sun and before the firmament, which was afterward called heaven[^43], removed from the sight of earthly things in the higher parts of the world, so that God alone divided between it and the darkness? For between the darkness that makes for us the most familiar night, and the light that makes for us the most familiar day, he commanded the luminaries visible to us to divide[^44]. But who could bear—though this is to be laughed at rather than indignant over—this man's informing us that "the hours designate the day, and that the sun marks off and bounds the hours," and his wishing us to believe "that Moses did not know these things, and therefore named the day before the sun was made"[^45]? But let people gather to hear this man's book, and let it be put to them which is more credible: that this man is ignorant of a certain light and a certain day which Moses knew, or that Moses was ignorant of the light and the day which not only this man but even those who do not understand his words know.

## The form of the two heavy elements, water and earth.

**13. 17.** What of the fact that some most unskilled person also raises a question "about the gathering of the waters"? Indeed not a question, but a censure, as though "it was not rightly said, *Let the waters be gathered into one gathering, and let the dry land appear*[^46], on the ground that everything was held by the waters"—not knowing how water, thinned into airy vapors, grows rarefied and with a misty haze occupies much space, but very little if, gathered, it is condensed, and then no longer flits about light, but flows heavy. What wonder, then, if, being rare, it had covered the earth, and, being thickened, laid it bare? What if also, by divine command, the earth sank down into great hollows, and so whatever of moist nature lay throughout it, flowing together into the concave parts, withdrew from the rest and uncovered what it had covered—gathered into the lower places lying beneath, where the sea might flow between and around, and abandoning the higher places, where the world might stand out? I pass over the fact that it can here be understood as signified that the formless matter was intimated by the name of water or of the abyss, and received the form of those two heavy elements, water and earth; and that for this reason it was said, *Let the water be gathered,* since a slipping and moving form was given to it, but *Let the dry land appear,* because it is fixed unmoving. For that which the prophet, the author of this book, rather intended—that his narrative of things done should be also a prefiguration of things to come—is not to be thrust upon contentious and unbelieving minds. Since, then, so many exits lie open to those who inquire piously, lest so great an authority be rashly censured, whence does this man, except by diabolical instinct, choose to cavil at those matters which he is not fit to scrutinize?

## The punishment rendered to the first disobedient man in his own self, that in turn he should not be obeyed even by himself.

**14. 18.** But that, blind and ungrateful, he inveighs against God the creator of man and dares to say to him who formed him, "Why did you make me thus?"—though he is wholly ignorant of how he was made—is the audacity of a deeply headlong mind. But the vessels of wrath are permitted to chatter such things, so that the vessels of mercy may, as it were, be roused from the sleep of negligence[^47] and, in their zeal to answer pestilent curses, bring care to wholesome words. For behold, in the very thing wherein this man reproaches "the creator of man, because he forbade him to take the food of the discernment of good and evil[^48], as though he had wished him to be equal to the cattle, who do not know how to distinguish these things, and had denied this to one to whom he had given power over the beasts[^49]"—in which alone man surpasses the beasts—how necessary it is for living a good life that we learn that certain things are unhappily learned and that some things are more happily not known! For how much more happily would we not know diseases and pains? If, then, a physician were to forbid us some food which he foreknew that, once taken, would make us fall ill, and on this account were to call that same food the food of the discernment of health and weakness—because through it a man, once he had begun to be ill, would by experience discern what difference there is between the bad health he had contracted and the soundness he had lost, which he would surely have done better not to know, and would have remained in that soundness which he lost, believing the physician through obedience rather than the disease through experience—would we say that such a physician had begrudged us a knowledge of this kind? Who doubts that sin is an evil? And yet it was said in praise of the Lord Jesus Christ that *he had not known sin*[^50]. He had not known this evil, then, and therefore did not have that discernment of good and evil from which Adam was forbidden. Here, if it be asked, How did he reprove what he had not known?—for indeed he did not fail to reprove sins: *but all things that are reproved,* as the Apostle says, *are made manifest by the light*[^51]*—*how, then, were the things ignorant by the one by whom they were reproved? Will it not be most rightly answered: both he knew and he did not know? Most rightly indeed: for he knew through wisdom, he did not know through experience. To this divine wisdom, therefore, Adam ought to have believed, so that, obedient to God's command, he might have refrained from that knowledge of evil which comes through experience. For thus he would not have known evil, unless he had done it. But he did it to himself, not to God. For he could do nothing by the will of disobedience except what he himself would suffer by the law of justice. For this is the punishment rendered to the disobedient man in his own self: that in turn he should not be obeyed even by himself. On this matter we have discussed more fully in other works, and especially in the fourteenth book of *The City of God.*

## Obedience is commended, which is the greatest virtue.

**14. 19.** But now we answer briefly to what this man says, that "the founder of man forbade to one whom he had made a great good, since he had wished him to be like the cattle, without the discernment of good and evil[^52]." For this discernment is not the wisdom of a blessed man, but the experience of a wretched one. Hence the tree took its name, from whose food man was forbidden, so that obedience might be commended—obedience, which is the greatest virtue and, so to speak, the origin and mother of all the virtues in that nature to which free choice of the will was so given that it is nevertheless necessary for it to live under the power of a better. Although there have not been lacking those to whom it seemed that that discernment of good and evil was some great good, of which they were not yet capable, who, usurping it against the prohibition, sinned through disobedience.

## God testifies that he will translate into the eternal kingdom a holy and numerous family, made so by his grace, out of the human race justly and deservedly condemned.

**14. 20.** But to those who think that man ought to have been made such that he would not will to sin, let it not be displeasing that he was made such that he could not sin if he did not will to. For surely, if one who could not sin were better, was one not therefore well made who could also not sin? Or is one to be so foolish as to think that a man sees something better that ought to have been made and yet supposes that God did not see this; or supposes that he saw it and believes he was unwilling to make it; or that he was indeed willing, but in no way able? God avert this from the hearts of the devout! If, then, right reason demonstrates that the rational creature which deserts God by no disobedience is better than the one which has so deserted him, let whoever is wise in this know both that the former does not fail among the heavenly things—the one which never deserts God—and that the latter was not so made that it should be compelled by any necessity to desert God. And because it deserted by will, nothing has been thereby diminished from the most wise dispositions of God, who uses both the evil well and the perverse rightly, and who testifies that out of the human race justly and deservedly condemned he will translate into the eternal kingdom a holy and numerous family, made so not by its own merit but by his grace[^53].

## God was not ignorant that man would sin, but at the same time also foreknew what was just and good and what he himself would do with the sinner.

**14. 21.** Since these things are so, God neither ought to have hidden the tree which—because of the misery that would follow for man, if, from it usurped against the prohibition, he should discern from what good he had fallen and into what evil he had fallen—he called *the tree of the discernment of good and evil*[^54]. For why should he hide what he gave a command about and through which he commended obedience? Nor was he ignorant that man would sin, but at the same time also foreknew, by his supreme divinity, what was just and good and what he himself would do with the sinner. Nor did he institute something that would harm, if man had been unwilling to harm himself, but rather instituted what would profit, since man would not without a good reward have kept obedience, and would not without a useful example—that his holy descendants might keep it—have paid the penalties of disobedience. Nor did he will what he could not, for this is what he willed: that man should be either obedient, or not unpunished if disobedient. Nor did he fruitlessly will to command what man was not going to keep, since the punishment of the despiser taught others to obey. Nor in man did a part of God resist God; for if man's soul were a part of God, it could in no way have been deceived either by itself or by another, nor compelled by any necessity to do or to suffer anything evil, nor changed at all for the better or the worse.

## The soul is not a part of God, nor created or brought forth from his substance and nature, but was made from nothing.

**14. 22.** But that breath of God which gave life to man was made by him, not from him. For neither is a man's breath a part of the man, nor does a man make it from himself, but from airy breath taken in and breathed out. But God was able to make it even from nothing, and living and rational—which a man cannot do. Although some suppose that the first man was not animated when God breathed into his face and he was made into a living soul[^55], but that he then received the Holy Spirit. But whichever of these is shown to be more credible—on which it would now take long to discuss—it is not lawful to doubt that the soul is not a part of God, nor created or brought forth from his substance and nature, but made from nothing.

## Man would in no way have been deceived if, with his heart lifted up in himself, he had not withdrawn from God.

**15. 23.** It is not, then, as this blasphemer says, that "the serpent is found in a better place than God, because it prevailed to deceive the man whom God had made[^56]." For man would in no way have been deceived if, with his heart lifted up in himself, he had not withdrawn from God. For that sentence is true because it is divine: *Before ruin the heart is lifted up*[^57]. And where it is lifted up against God, there it is deserted by him and darkened in itself[^58]. But what wonder is it if, while it is darkened, it does not know what follows, since it is not light from itself but is enlightened by him[^59]? That God, then, is always unconquered[^60], even conquered man shows, since he would not have been conquered if he had not withdrawn from the unconquered. But how is the deceiver of man a conqueror, when he too was deceived by himself? Hence both the one who deceived and the one whom he deceived were both deceived in withdrawing from him who cannot be deceived; and both were conquered in withdrawing from him who cannot be conquered. And whoever withdraws further from him is the more conquered, because he is so much the lower as he is the worse. And therefore it is necessary that he who seems to conquer by first inflicting evil on another is himself the more conquered by losing a good, nor can it come about that he should have a better place when his cause is worse. And though for a time the devil seemed to prevail when man was overcome, even so he was conquered for eternity when man was restored[^61]. Nor are they the words of God confessing but rather of God reproaching: *Behold, Adam has become like one of us*[^62]*.* Just as the Apostle, where he says, *Forgive me this wrong*[^63], surely wishes to be understood by way of irony—if a learned reader is present, not an unlearned caviler.

## Since rational souls live from wisdom, and their death is folly, for the sake of signifying this there was the tree of life in paradise.

**15. 24.** Moreover, whoever is displeased that the sinner was forbidden the tree of life—what does he want but to live wickedly with impunity? Nor was it a great thing for God to take away life in some other way from a man whom he had not wished to live; but since rational souls live from wisdom, and their death is folly, for the sake of signifying this matter he would not permit man, by the fruit of the tree of life in paradise, to die even in body. The fact, then, that, separated from it, he was handed over to death, to be consumed by age[^64]—which would by no means have befallen him if he had always enjoyed that same food—signified that his soul had first been shut out from the spiritual tree of life on account of sin and was already dead by a certain inner death of its own. For of wisdom it is written, *It is a tree of life to those who embrace it*[^65]. This man, not understanding it, says: "How then, before the curse, could an immortal man live forever, when he had not yet taken food from this tree?"—as though someone had told him, or he had read somewhere in that book, that Adam had not yet taken of the food of the tree of life. Rather, he is to be understood thus: since from it perpetual life in the body was available to him, that he should not be consumed by the age of long years, he was forbidden it for this end—that from the punishment of sin there should now be for him the necessity of death.

## By the tree of life wisdom was signified.

**15. 25.** "How," he says, "did he begin to die from God's curse, when life itself never took its beginning from God?"—as though God had wished death upon him, as one man does to another, and as though the words of God did not pertain to the sentence of one punishing rather than to the anger of one cursing. But to punish with bodily death was to separate him from the tree of life, when he had already been spiritually dead, separated in mind from the nourishment of wisdom. God, therefore, wished to signify what had befallen him in his mind by separating him from the tree of life, by which wisdom was signified.

## To be with Christ is to be with the tree of life.

**15. 26.** "But this tree," he says, "which in paradise bore the fruits of life—whom did it profit?" Whom, if not first those first human beings, the male and the female, who had been established in paradise[^66]? Then, after they were cast out of paradise for the merit of their iniquity, it remained as a reminder, to signify the spiritual tree of life: which is, as we have said, wisdom itself, the unchangeable food of blessed souls. But whether anyone now feeds on that food—except perhaps Enoch and Elijah[^67]—I do not think should be rashly asserted; yet unless the souls of the blessed were nourished by that tree of life which is in the spiritual paradise, we would not read that, as a gift for his devotion and most faithful confession, paradise was granted that same day to the soul of the thief who believed in Christ: *Amen,* he said, *I say to you, today you will be with me in paradise*[^68]. But to be there with Christ is to be with the tree of life. For he is wisdom, of which, as I recalled above, it is written, *It is a tree of life to those who embrace it*[^69].

## Holy Scripture wholesomely commends not only God's goodness but also his severity, since God is both usefully loved and feared.

**16. 27.** Must we also refute this, which he derides as cleverly, so it seems to him? First, "that God did not foreknow what happened"; then, "that he could not accomplish what he had greatly purposed"; third, "that, being overcome, he turned to cursing." Whence does he know that God did not foreknow what happened? Is it because it happened? On the contrary, if it had not happened, he would in no way have foreknown it as future, because it would not have been future. Or if he thinks that for this reason God did not foreknow it—because, if he had foreknown it, he would have provided that it should not happen—he can say this also of Christ, who gave a talent to a man who would acquire nothing[^70]; which he had surely given for this end, that his money—whatever it is that the money signifies—might be increased. So because it happened that the man by his laziness acquired nothing, did the one who gave it not foreknow this? He can also say that Christ did not accomplish, regarding this gain, what he had greatly purposed. He can also say that third thing—that "being overcome he turned to cursing"—since he said, *Bind his hands and feet and cast him into the outer darkness*[^71]. Just as it was said of Adam, that, separated from the tree of life, he should also be punished with the death of the body[^72]. For to this eloquent man the command of one who is powerful seems the curse of one who is failing. Let him therefore call Christ powerless, since he could not accomplish what he desired regarding the spiritual money to be acquired. Let him call him envious and malicious, because he begrudged his servant light and salvation, the servant whom he ordered to be cast into the darkness, where there would be weeping and gnashing of teeth[^73]. But if he does not say these things of Christ, lest in this way he show that he is not a Christian, why does he dare to say of the creator of man and the condemner of him for the merit of sin what he does not dare to say of the redeemer of man[^74] and—if anyone despises his precepts—the avenger by the punishment of eternal death, that very same one, namely? For against whom but Christ does this man, in his ignorance, hurl these curses? Since Christ himself says, *If you believed Moses, you would believe me also; for he wrote about me*[^75]. For what did the Father do then, or ever do, without the Son? If, then, holy Scripture wholesomely commends not only God's goodness but also his severity, since God is both usefully loved and feared—whence the Apostle in that same place mentions both, saying, *You see therefore the goodness and the severity of God*[^76]—how is it that this insane and headlong man, while he boasts that he is a Christian, reprehends in the God of the Prophets what he finds in the God of the Apostles, since it is the same God of these and of those?

## God is said to be neither improvident of the future, nor powerless, nor envious and malicious.

**16. 28.** But what I recalled about this servant whom the severity of God sent, lazy, into the outer darkness[^77]—where God is said to be neither improvident of the future, since he entrusted his money to such a one; nor powerless, since to make him act well he did not himself rule him, but he did himself correct him; nor envious and malicious, in that he sent into the darkness one separated from the light—this the faithful reader ought to observe regarding all the punishments of human beings which we read were inflicted on sinners in the prophetic books. This, then, also concerning the flood. For the Lord Jesus did indeed foretell that something of the kind would come at his advent, when he said: *As in the days of Noah: they were eating, drinking, planting, building, marrying, taking wives; the flood came and destroyed them all: so will be the advent of the Son of man also*[^78]. This also concerning the hardening of Pharaoh's heart[^79]. For the writings of the New Testament do indeed say of certain people: *God handed them over to a reprobate mind, to do what is not fitting*[^80]*.* This concerning the lying spirit, which God, who uses even evils well, by a most just judgment sent to deceive an impious king, as the prophet Micaiah testifies was shown to him in a prophetic vision[^81]. For the apostle Paul did not hesitate to say something of the kind, since he knew he was saying it most truly, where he says: *God will send them a working of error, so that they may believe a lie, and all may be judged who have not believed the truth but consented to iniquity*[^82]*.* This concerning the deed done through Moses, to whom God said, *Take all the leaders of the people, and offer them as victims to the Lord against the sun*[^83]—that is, openly, by day. Or that Moses so avenged the idol that was made that no one spared even his neighbor, slaying the impious with the sword[^84]. For the Lord Jesus too said: *But those who did not want me to reign over them, bring here and slay before me*[^85]. Where surely, because it signifies the death of souls, that death is certainly more to be dreaded and feared by the faithful than the death of bodies. Hence the same Lord says: *Do not fear those who kill the body but cannot kill the soul; but rather fear him who can destroy both soul and body in hell*[^86].

## Of both Testaments there is one God, always merciful.

**16. 29.** If anyone considers, with a faithful mind, as is fitting, deaths of this kind—through which souls are sent into hell—he will reckon as very cheap any slaughter, however vast, and rivers of blood of mortal bodies that are going to die in any case in some way at some time. These slaughters this man heaps up and, with a schoolman's vanity, describes, in order to blaspheme God, who with such deaths scourged those for whom terror of this sort was useful; he strikes horror into mortal senses and thinks he is accomplishing something by kicking his heels against the goad[^87], so that, while he accuses God's providence concerning the death of the flesh, he may by the death of the heart be sent into hell. But what human being of either sex would not rather be cut down by the sword—even in that manner in which the priest Phinehas cut down the fornicators in the very embrace of nefarious pleasure, setting a terrible example of vengeance against execrable lusts[^88], on account of which especially he pleased God—who, I say, would not rather be killed by such a kind of death, who would not finally rather be consumed by fire or torn through the very private parts by the bites of beasts, than be sent into the hell of eternal fire[^89]? Why, then, does the God of Christians punish sinners with such deaths, so that after the transitory destruction of the body there follows in hell a punishment without end—except because of both Testaments there is one God? For the Jews could say against this man's impiety, however much he heaps up wars, slaughters, wounds, deaths, blood, that they have a God incomparably milder than our God—punishing, namely, far more mildly with the transitory deaths of bodies than with the perpetual flames of hells.

## Religion and devotion profit not God but the people.

**16. 30.** But indeed "for this reason," it seems to this man, "the God of the Law and the Prophets," who is the one and true God, "is to be charged with the crime of cruelty, because for the most trifling causes, or even shameful ones, he inflicted the penalty of death, even of bodily death: that David had numbered the people[^90]; that the infant sons," as this man says, "of Eli the priest had tasted something from the pots or pans prepared for God[^91]." On this matter I do not discuss how great and how pernicious a vice of pride crept upon so holy a man, that he wished to number the people of God, and how he was scourged by the deaths—not eternal, but already at hand by the human condition and quickly to pass—of those whose multitude had puffed him up. Nor do I say that the sons of Eli were not infants, as this man says, not knowing what he says, but of an age at which they could and should, for the sacrilegious presumption by which they preferred themselves to the Lord God in the sacrifices, have been restrained by a fitting coercion; which, being neglected, God avenged even by war, not consulting for himself but for the people, whom religion and devotion would have profited: where those who were to live could increase the fear of God through the deaths of those who, even if they grew old, were after no long spaces of time going to die. For we read that others too died by the deaths of bodies on account of sins not their own but others'[^92]: where there is more in the grief of heart, the wound of the living, than in the dissolution of the flesh, the punishment of the dying; where souls going out from bodies have their own causes, either evil or good, not weighed down because stripped; but by the death of souls no one is punished in another's stead. But this I say: how great a sin this man would reckon it to have been that a man was found at the wedding feast not having a wedding garment[^93]: I think that, if we measure these things by human consideration, it should have sufficed for this man to blush a little; and at most, that the indignation of the inviter should be stretched so far as to compel him to change his garment. And yet it was said: *Bind his hands and feet and cast him into the outer darkness; there will be weeping and gnashing of teeth*[^94]. But he will say: To have lacked the wedding garment signifies no light fault, since these small things are signs of great things. So, then, the visible sacrifices too, although in earthly things they are slight, since they are signs of great things—divine things—in which the sons of the priest preferred themselves to God, whose honor is regarded in the sacrifices. But that guest did not prefer himself to the bridegroom; only, the one who had no wedding tunic did not match the occasion. Nevertheless, amid the very punishments by which both were avenged, he notices what difference there is, and understands how incomparably the latter surpasses the former, who prefers spiritual and everlasting things to bodily and temporal ones.

## Of both Testaments there is one God, faithful both there and here, nowhere cruel.

**16. 31.** But what need is there to wish to drive home, to carnal senses, an unsuitable discourse about the mystical significations of the sacrifices and of the wedding garment? Behold, let us bring forward what is more manifest. The Lord, comparing the Gospel to the old Law and testifying that not what men had learned before was evil, but that what he himself was teaching was more perfect, says: *You have heard that it was said to the ancients: You shall not kill, and whoever kills shall be liable to judgment. But I say to you: whoever is angry with his brother shall be liable to judgment; whoever says Raca shall be liable to the council; but whoever says You fool shall be liable to the hell of fire*[^95]. What is so small among sins as to say to one's brother, You fool? What is so great among punishments as the hell of fire? If in the Law and the Prophets this man had found someone ordered by God to be stoned because he had said to his brother, You fool, of how great a cruelty would he charge God? But who would not rather—I do not say be stoned, but be torn apart limb by limb, indeed piece by piece of the body, little by little and bit by bit, alive and feeling, and be consumed—than be handed over to the fires of hell? Far be it, however, that anyone should call the God of the Gospel crueler than the God of the Law, understanding that of both there is one and the same God, in the Law terrifying with bodily punishments, in the Gospel with spiritual ones, and both there and here faithful, nowhere cruel.

## It is no wonder that for such great barrenness, as of unfruitful trees, the punishment of eternal fire is prepared.

**16. 32.** What of this? If he found in Christ a tongue alien to him and blasphemous against him, and—such as his is—condemnable by its impious garrulity, would it not be assailed more harshly and more bitterly than the fact that this man is so indignant that a bodily and temporal punishment was imposed for the sacrilege of food from the sacrifice tasted beforehand—where the Lord, threatening that he will come, says he will say to the gentiles set on the left: *Go into the eternal fire, which is prepared for the devil and his angels*[^96]? Do you ask the cause of so great a punishment? *I was hungry,* he says, *and you did not give me to eat*[^97]*.* Behold, for food temporal not taken away but not given, he threatens an eternal and horrendous punishment. And rightly, if you consult the truth. For very small is what is given in alms; but when it is given devoutly, an eternal merit is thereby gained. And therefore precisely because what is given is small, it is by great impiety not given. Hence it is no wonder that for such great barrenness, as of unfruitful trees, the punishment of eternal fire is prepared. But if you consult man, that he may answer you out of himself: *Every man is a liar*[^98]—he makes light of the fault, he exaggerates the punishment; for he does not see the former with his carnal mind, while he shudders at the latter in his mortal flesh. Such is this man in regard to all the human beings who are punished or corrected in the Old Testament with bodily punishments far milder than those we read of in the Gospel. For what flood can be compared to the eternal fires? What slaughters, what wounds, what deaths of bodies to everlasting torments? Twenty-four thousand[^99] falling—the madman puffs up with so great a noise of his cheeks, as though innumerable thousands did not die daily in the whole world? But this death of the body is transitory: who indeed could reckon how many thousands from all the gentiles will stand on the left, to be condemned to everlasting fires?

## He offends against Christ himself who accuses the God of the Prophets.

**16. 33.** Let this man go and cry out with open mouth and closed eyes, as though "God confessed his own cruelty, because he says through the prophet: *I will sharpen my sword like lightning, I will make my arrows drunk with blood, and my sword will devour flesh from the blood of the wounded*[^100]." From these words this man so accuses "God," as though he were "always hungering for human blood," as if he had said, "I will make myself drunk with blood," or, "I will devour flesh from the blood of the wounded." But however much this useful "threat of God, as though greedy for crime and," as he says, "glorying only in cruelty toward the evil," the empty and frenzied man may shudder at it—what or how much does it amount to, compared with the words of him who says, *Depart from me, accursed, into the eternal fire, which is prepared for the devil and his angels*[^101]? There the arrows will not be made drunk with blood, but the flames will not be sated by all the limbs; nor will the sword devour flesh, taking away the sense of pain from the dead more quickly than inflicting it on the wounded; rather, no one is withdrawn from the torments even by dying, lest in the dying the punishment itself should also die. Why does he not say here: Shall we say that this one is to be worshiped, or rather to be execrated and fled? Or does he fear to say these things of Christ, lest he not escape the punishment of that same eternal fire into which Christ prepares to send the impious—and the wretch does not know that by saying these things of the God of the Prophets he says them of that very one whose so dreadful severity in the Gospel he is afraid to offend?

## In the Old Testament we understand Christ promised; in the New we receive him rendered.

**17. 34.** He also reproaches the fact that "God says the punishments of the impious, signified by the grape of gall and the cluster of bitterness and the fury of dragons and asps, are gathered up by him and sealed in his treasuries, to be repaid in time, when their foot has slipped[^102]"—not knowing that here "treasuries" are the names given to the hidden dispositions of God, who arranges to repay each according to his works. Hence the Apostle says: *But you, according to the hardness of your heart and your impenitent heart, are storing up for yourself wrath in the day of wrath and of the revelation of the just judgment of God, who will repay each according to his works*[^103]. With whom does the impenitent heart store up wrath for itself but with him who *is to judge the living and the dead*[^104]? For those old books too are not ignorant of a desirable treasure, of which it is there written that it rests in the mouth of the wise[^105]. And in *Proverbs* it is read that *God stores up salvation for those who love him*[^106]; and the prophet Isaiah says: *In the treasuries is our salvation; there is wisdom and instruction and devotion toward the Lord. These are the treasuries of righteousness*[^107]. But the talkers of vanity and seducers of minds, opposing the sacred writings, which they are unwilling to understand, choose out of them the harsh things which are read there to commend the severity of God, and out of the writings of the Gospel and the apostles the gentle things which are read there to commend the goodness of God, and among unskilled people from the one source they instill horror, from the other they seek favor; as though it were difficult for anyone similarly blasphemous and impious to oppose the New Testament in the manner in which this man, carping at the Old, opposes the Old—from the Old citing the things by which God's goodness is commended, and on the contrary from the New the things by which God's severity is commended—and to cry out, envious and swollen-veined: Behold the God who is to be worshiped, *merciful and compassionate, long-suffering and of great mercy; who will not be angry forever nor be indignant for eternity; who has not done to us according to our sins, nor repaid us according to our iniquities; but as far as the east is from the west, so far has he put our sins from us*; who, *as a father pities his children, so pities those who fear him*[^108]; who says, *I do not will the death of the impious, but rather that he turn back and live*[^109]. Not the one who, for greed of gains, ordered even that servant who did not lose the talent he had received—only because he did not acquire more—to be cast, with hands and feet bound, into the outer darkness, where there would be for him weeping and gnashing of teeth[^110]; and who takes from his feast the man not having a wedding garment, and, likewise bound, punishes him with a like punishment[^111]; and who, to those coming to him and knocking and saying, *Lord, open to us,* answers, *I do not know you*[^112]*,* only because they did not bring with them oil to pour into their lamps; and who, for one insulting word, sends into hell[^113]; and who, for temporal food not given, condemns to eternal fire[^114]. If anyone with a frenzied, sacrilegious mind should gather such things and the like—gentle ones from the one source, harsh ones from the other—and so try to turn away from Christ, as though savage and cruel, people unskilled in both writings, and to convert them to the merciful and gentle God of the Prophets, will not this very man, who does to the Old Testament what could be done likewise to the New by a man similarly sacrilegious, appear impure and impious? But whoever rightly worships God indeed finds one God of both Testaments and loves the goodness of the same one God in both, fears his severity in both, understanding in the one Christ promised, receiving in the other Christ rendered.

## Whoever gathers from the New things as though contrary to the Old Testament either is ignorant of both writings or, that he may deceive the unskilled, pretends not to know them.

**17. 35.** Was it not first read in those old books that evil is not to be repaid for evil, where it is commanded that, if anyone finds his enemy's beast straying, he should bring it back to its owner, and if it has fallen on the road, he should not pass by but lift it up with him[^115]? Was it not first written there what the Apostle sets down: *If your enemy is hungry, feed him; if he thirsts, give him drink*[^116]? Was it not there first that the man of God says to his God what he surely knows is pleasing to him: *Lord my God, if I have done this, if there is iniquity in my hands, if I have repaid those who repaid me with evils*[^117]? Was it not there first that the prophet Jeremiah describes the patience of a holy man offering *his cheek to the one striking him*[^118]? Was it not there first commanded, through another prophet, that each one should not remember the malice of his brother[^119]? What is it, then, that this blasphemer gathers these things from the New as though contrary to the Old Testament—either being ignorant of both writings, or, that he may deceive the unskilled, pretending to know them? Yet if it be asked of him whether one does not repay evil for evil who, for food not received, sends into eternal fire[^120], he will surely be disturbed and admonished that it is incomparably milder to take from a man eye for eye, tooth for tooth[^121], where the measure of the vengeance does not pass beyond the measure of the injury, than to reprehend so great a severity, on account of kindness not shown, where the fault is transitory and the punishment has no end; and so he will learn, if he is not obstinate, that in each Testament of the one God both goodness is to be loved and severity is to be feared. For although in the Old Testament, on account of the promise of temporal goods and the threat of evils, the temporal Jerusalem brings forth slaves; while in the New, where faith obtains love, by which the Law can be fulfilled not so much by fear of punishment as by love of righteousness[^122], the eternal Jerusalem brings forth free children[^123]—nevertheless even in those times there were spiritual righteous men, whom the commanding letter did not kill, but the helping spirit gave life[^124]. Hence also the faith of the Christ to come dwelt indeed in the Prophets foretelling the Christ to come[^125]; and now there are very many carnal people who either make heresies by not understanding the Scriptures, or, in the Catholic Church itself, are either still as little ones nourished with milk, or, like persisting chaff, are prepared for the future fires[^126]. But just as the one true God is the creator of good things both temporal and eternal, so the same one is the author of both Testaments, since the New was prefigured in the Old and the Old was revealed in the New.

## How much more gravely he is punished who is, as it were, left unpunished.

**17. 36.** But the merciful gentleness of forgiving, on account of which it was said that sins must be forgiven a brother not only seven times but even seventy times seven, does not avail to this end—that iniquity should go unpunished, or that discipline should be sluggish and asleep, which would harm rather than a diligent and watchful vengeance. For Christ so gave the keys of the kingdom of heaven to the Church that he not only said, *What you loose upon earth shall be loosed also in heaven*—where most openly the Church repays good, not evil for evil—but also added, *What you bind on earth shall be bound in heaven*[^127], because the justice of avenging is also good. For that which he says, *If he will not hear even the Church, let him be to you as a heathen and a tax collector*[^128], is graver than if he were struck with the sword, consumed by flames, exposed to wild beasts. For there too he added, *Amen I say to you, what you bind upon earth shall be bound also in heaven*[^129], so that it might be understood how much more gravely he is punished who is, as it were, left unpunished. Here let this man say, if he pleases: Is it thus that we have received the commands of the Savior, who orders, *Repay no one evil for evil; but if anyone strikes you on one cheek, offer the other also*[^130]; and, *Forgive the wrongs done to your brothers*? Behold, by men who do not repay evil for evil a man is bound more bitterly and more unhappily by the keys of the Church than by any most grievous and hardest bonds of iron or adamant. "Far be it," he says, "that I should say this, since I am a Christian." If this were truly so, he would not have said those other things either. For the God of the Prophets, whose writings he blasphemes, is himself the God of the Apostles, whose writings he is afraid to blaspheme.

## By a singular and sole true sacrifice the blood of Christ was poured out for us.

**18. 37.** "But David," he says, "asked God and was not heard except when a sacrifice was offered, that the men who had not sinned might be spared; and therefore he is not to be believed the true God who is delighted by sacrifices"[^131]. Concerning the punishment of the men slain—men who were going to die at some time, whom that death did not harm, and yet by whose deaths the heart of the king, exalting himself, was deservedly beaten—we have answered above. But concerning the sacrifice this man understands nothing at all, and therefore is deceived, because the people of God no longer offer such sacrifices to God, after the one sacrifice came, of which all those were shadows[^132]—not disapproving this one, but signifying it. For just as one thing can be signified by many expressions and many languages, so the one true and singular sacrifice was signified beforehand by many figures of sacrifices. You see how long it would be to discuss these one by one here. Yet let the foolish, slow to understand and headlong to reprehend, know this one thing: that a demon would not demand a sacrifice for himself unless he knew it was owed to the true God[^133]. For a false god wishes to be honored by those whom he deceives in the manner in which the true God is honored by those for whom he provides; and most of all it is sacrifice that is owed especially to God. For the other homages rendered to the divinity men have dared, in the arrogance of pride, even to claim for themselves. But very few are recorded who, when they could by royal power, have dared to command that sacrifice be made to themselves. Yet whoever have dared, through this they have wished to be held as gods. But who does not know that God has no need of sacrifices? But neither does he need our praises. Rather, just as it is useful to us, not to him, to praise God, so it is useful to us, not to him, to offer sacrifice to God. Since by a singular and sole true sacrifice the blood of Christ was poured out for us[^134], therefore in the first times God commanded sacrifices of unblemished animals to be offered to him[^135], that this sacrifice might be prophesied by such significations—so that, just as those were unblemished by defects of body, so there might be hoped to be immolated for us the one who alone had been unblemished by sins[^136]. Which times are foretold through the prophet thus: *The God of gods, the Lord, has spoken and called the earth from the rising of the sun to its setting; out of Zion is the form of his beauty*[^137]. And a little after, in the same psalm: *Hear, my people, and I will speak to you; Israel, and I will testify to you: that I am God, your God. I will not reprove you over your sacrifices; but your holocausts are always in my sight. I will not take calves from your house, nor he-goats from your flocks. For all the beasts of the forest are mine, the cattle on the mountains and the oxen. I have known all the birds of the air, and the beauty of the field is with me. If I am hungry, I will not tell you. For mine is the world and its fullness. Shall I eat the flesh of bulls or drink the blood of he-goats? Offer to God a sacrifice of praise, and pay your vows to the Most High*[^138]. And again at the end of that psalm: *A sacrifice of praise,* he says, *will honor me, and there is the way by which I will show him the salvation of God*[^139]. Now concerning this salvation of God—that it is Christ himself—I have recalled and shown above. But what is more sacred than a sacrifice of praise in the giving of thanks? And for what are greater thanks to be given to God than for his grace through Jesus Christ our Lord[^140]? All of which the faithful know in the sacrifice of the Church, of which all the kinds of earlier sacrifices were shadows. But these talkers of vanity, these reprehenders of the Old Testament, even if they understand the rest that I have recalled from this psalm too little, let it suffice them, as far as concerns the present question, that the God of the Prophets, who is also the God of the Apostles, does not eat the flesh of bulls or drink the blood of he-goats. For the saints, who, full of his Spirit, said these things, knew him to be such. Hence what David offered, that the people might be spared, was a shadow of what was to come, by which it was signified that through the one sacrifice—of which that was the figure—the salvation of the people is spiritually spared. For he himself is Christ Jesus, *who was handed over,* as the Apostle says, *for our offenses, and rose again for our justification*[^141], on account of which he also says, *Christ our Pasch has been immolated*[^142]*.*

## Israel according to the flesh and Israel according to the spirit.

**19. 38.** But how this man tried to prove that "he evidently served demons who by sacrifices earned their favor," wishing this to be understood there of holy David, shows more evidently with how great a fraud he lies in wait for the minds of the unskilled. For he brought forward the Apostle as a witness, in that he said: *See Israel carnally: are not those who eat the victims partakers of the altar? What then? Do I say that an idol is anything? But those who sacrifice, sacrifice to demons.* Which is not written thus, but thus: *See Israel according to the flesh: are not those who eat of the sacrifices partners of the altar? What then? Do I say that something immolated to idols is anything, or that an idol is anything? But that the things which they immolate, they immolate to demons and not to God. I do not want you to become partners of demons*[^143]. Now it could happen that, according to the variety of translations—not in substance but in words—what I said, *according to the flesh,* other manuscripts have as *carnally;* and what I said, *those who eat of the sacrifices,* some have as *they eat the victims;* what he set down and what I have, *partners of the altar,* some have, *partakers of the altar;* and what I set down, *What then? Do I say that something immolated to idols is anything?* he set down less of, or his manuscript had less of, and therefore he set down only that "an idol is anything." But what follows pertains much to the matter, because he set it down otherwise. For the Apostle says, *But that the things which they immolate, they immolate to demons and not to God.* This man, however, said, *But those who sacrifice, sacrifice to demons*—as though all who sacrifice sacrifice to none but demons. For the Apostle does not say, those who sacrifice, but *the things which they sacrifice,* or, as it is set down by me, *immolate:* namely, those who worship idols, *the things which they sacrifice to demons and not to God, they sacrifice* or *immolate.* And therefore he added, *I do not want you to become partners of demons;* for he was forbidding them idolatry. On account of which he wished to show them that they became partners of demons in this way—if they ate the sacrifices offered to idols[^144]—in the way in which carnal Israel was a partner of the altar in the temple, the one who ate of the sacrifices. For he added *carnally* or *according to the flesh* precisely because there is an Israel spiritually, or according to the spirit, which no longer follows the old shadows but the truth that follows—the truth that was signified by those preceding shadows. For from this he had begun, so as to say this: *On account of which, my beloved, flee from the worship of idols*[^145]*.* Then, continuing, he showed to which sacrifice they ought now to belong, saying: *I speak as to the prudent; judge for yourselves what I say. The cup of blessing which we bless, is it not a communion of the blood of Christ? The bread which we break, is it not a communion of the body of the Lord? Because there is one bread, we, though many, are one body; for we all partake of the one bread*[^146]. And for this reason he added: *See Israel according to the flesh: are not those who eat of the sacrifices partners of the altar?*[^147] So that they might understand that they were now partners of the body of Christ in the way in which those are partners of the altar. And since, in order to say this, he was forbidding idolatry—from which this passage of his discourse, as I have recalled, took its beginning—lest they should think there was no need to be concerned if they ate of the sacrifices of idols, on the ground that an idol is nothing, supposing that these things did not harm them as being, so to speak, superfluous, he himself confirmed indeed that an idol is nothing, and that he was not forbidding these things for the reason that the things which have no sense are immolated to idols, but because *the things which they immolate,* he says—that is, the worshipers of idols—*they immolate to demons and not to God. I do not want you to become partners of demons*[^148]*.* The truth itself declares this sense, since in the temple which carnal Israel served, an idol was certainly not worshiped. For if the sacrifices which in that temple were offered to God according to the old Law were condemned as sacrifices of idols, or as immolated to demons, the Lord Christ himself would in no way have said to the leper whom he had cleansed: *Go, show yourself to the priest, and offer the gift which Moses commanded, as a testimony to them*[^149]*.* For he had not yet given, for all those sacrifices, the sacrifice of his own body, had not yet raised up the temple of his body[^150]. Nor, casting out of that temple those who sold oxen and doves, would he have said: *My house shall be called a house of prayer, but you have made it a den of thieves*[^151].

## For this Church is Israel according to the spirit.

**20. 39.** To be sure, this man set down testimonies from apocryphal writings, which were composed under the names of the apostles Andrew and John. If these were theirs, they would have been received by the Church, which from their times, through most certain successions of bishops, persists up to our own and subsequent times and immolates to God, in the body of Christ, a sacrifice of praise[^152], from which *the God of gods has spoken and called the earth from the rising of the sun to its setting*[^153]*.* For this Church is Israel according to the spirit; from which is distinguished that Israel according to the flesh, which served in the shadows of sacrifices, by which was signified the singular sacrifice which Israel according to the spirit now offers—to which it was said and foretold: *Hear, my people, and I will speak to you; Israel, and I will testify to you*[^154], and the rest which I have already recalled above. For from this Israel's house he does not take calves, nor he-goats from its flocks[^155]. This one immolates to God a sacrifice of praise, not according to the order of Aaron but according to the order of Melchizedek[^156]. This is set down in that psalm which the Lord Jesus in the Gospel testifies was written about him, asking of the Jews—who answered that Christ was the son of David, which alone they knew of him carnally—how David in the spirit called him Lord. For then he recalled the beginning of this psalm: *The Lord said to my Lord: Sit at my right hand, until I place your enemies as a footstool for your feet*[^157]. For there too this was said: *The Lord has sworn, and it will not repent him: You are a priest forever according to the order of Melchizedek*[^158]*.* Those who read know what Melchizedek brought forth when he blessed Abraham[^159]; and if they are now partakers of him, they see that such a sacrifice is now offered to God in the whole world. But God's swearing is the rebuke of unbelievers. And that it will not repent God is a signification that he will not change this priesthood. For he did change the priesthood according to the order of Aaron. Hence another prophet says to the one who is Israel carnally: *I have no pleasure in you, says the Lord almighty, and I will not accept an offering from your hands*[^160]. Behold, this is what is according to the order of Aaron. But why he does not accept this, he adds and says: *Because from the rising of the sun to its setting my name is glorified among the gentiles, and in every place incense is offered to my name, and a pure offering; because great is my name among the gentiles, says the Lord almighty*[^161]. Behold, this is what is according to the order of Melchizedek. For the incense which in Greek is  
—as John explains in the *Apocalypse*—is the prayers of the saints[^162]. For that God, as is sung in the psalm, who *called the earth from the rising of the sun to its setting,* to which earth—that is, to which people, spread from the rising of the sun to its setting—would say: *I will not take calves from your house; offer to God a sacrifice of praise*[^163]. He himself, through this prophet, foretelling as though already done what was most certainly to be, says: *From the rising of the sun to its setting my name is glorified among the gentiles, and in every place incense is offered to my name, and a pure offering; because great is my name among the gentiles*[^164].

## God, therefore, when it repents him, is not changed yet changes; when he is angry, is not moved yet avenges; when he is merciful, does not grieve yet delivers; and when he is jealous, is not tormented yet torments.

**20. 40.** For it does not repent God as it does a man, but as it does God; just as he is not angry as a man is, nor merciful as a man is, nor jealous as a man is, but all as God is. The repentance of God is not after error; the anger of God does not have the heat of a disturbed mind; the mercy of God does not have a heart that suffers with the wretched—whence in the Latin tongue it received its name—the jealousy of God does not have the envy of the mind. But the repentance of God is the change, unexpected to human beings, of things set in his power; the anger of God is the vengeance of sin; the mercy of God is the goodness of helping; the jealousy of God is the providence by which he does not allow those whom he has as subjects to love with impunity what he forbids. Hence let this man, who so loquaciously stirred up the repentance of God, first learn that scarcely anything is found which can be worthily said of God, but that we say very many things—indeed almost all—about him by the necessity of speech, which human beings measure rather from human beings, while how they are to be understood of him is understood scarcely by a few and spiritual people. On account of which divine Scripture, most providently speaking of that ineffable one, descends even to certain words which to human beings, and to carnal ones at that, when there is a discourse about God, seem absurd and unworthy: so that, when it is feared that these are taken in the way they are wont to be in human beings, and it is discussed how they can be well taken of God, there may there be learned also that those things which to human senses seemed in those same Scriptures worthy of God ought not to be understood or believed according to human customs. For it quickly seems that repentance, as it is enacted by human beings, does not befall God; but it does not quickly seem that mercy too, as human beings are merciful, does not befall God. From that, then, which he confesses must be inquired into, a man learns to inquire also into this, which he was already supposing to suffice. So, then, when it repents him, he is not changed yet changes; just as when he is angry, he is not moved yet avenges; and when he is merciful, he does not grieve yet delivers; and when he is jealous, he is not tormented yet torments.

## God is ineffable, of whom nothing can be said by man or to man sufficiently worthily and sufficiently fittingly.

**20. 41.** But are these words lacking in the books of the New Testament—words which, if they are taken as they are wont to be understood in human beings, in no way befit the divinity and beget a grave offense? For while the evangelist says most truly of Christ that *he had no need that anyone should bear witness to him about man*[^165]—for he himself knew what was in man—why does he himself say to certain people, *I do not know you*[^166]? Finally, since he foreknew and chose his own saints before the foundation of the world[^167], what is it that the Apostle says, *But now knowing God, or rather being known by God*[^168]—as though he now knew those whom before he did not know? But that he says, *Do not quench the Spirit*[^169], as though the Spirit could be quenched—who would bear it but one who understands these things prudently? Is it not written in the Gospel: *He who believes in the Son has eternal life; but he who is unbelieving toward the Son will not see life, but the wrath of God remains upon him*[^170]? Let this blasphemer therefore cavil at this word too and say: What sort of being is he who is angry, when it is written, *The wrath of a man does not work the righteousness of God*[^171]? Let him cavil in this way also at the Apostle, who says: *Is God unjust, who inflicts wrath?*[^172] Moreover, if anyone should say: Christ will be confounded—and that at the time when he is to judge the living and the dead—what Christian would patiently hear him? Yet in the Gospel he says: *Whoever is confounded of me and of my words in this adulterous and sinful generation, of him the Son of man will be confounded also when he comes in the glory of his Father with the holy angels*[^173]*.* But why, when we pray, do we say, *Hallowed be your name*[^174], if it is always holy, except because what is written of certain people is also true—that *they polluted the name of the Lord their God*[^175]? And why was it said to the Lord, *Remember me when you come into your kingdom*[^176], if he forgets nothing, except because it is said to him not foolishly but understandingly, *You forget our want and our tribulation*[^177]? Therefore he both knowingly does not know; and what he has always known, he at some time comes to know; and when he is quenched by those who deny, he remains unquenchable; and he is tranquilly angry; and he cannot be confounded, even when he is confounded; and his name cannot be polluted, even when it is polluted; and he cannot forget, even when he forgets; and he remembers, even when he is reminded. Thus he is ineffable. For these things are said of him of whom nothing can be said by man or to man sufficiently worthily and sufficiently fittingly. Since these things are so, what devout person would not blow this man away like the *dust which the wind casts from the face of the earth*[^178]—this man who, swelling and puffed up and rushing into the eyes of the weak and disturbing them, seems to himself to be saying something, by reproving words in the Old Testament which he does not understand, and by not regarding those in the New which he does understand?

## On the repentance of God.

**20. 42.** But on this matter of the repentance of God—whence it came about that we should speak of it, when we were recalling the prophecy about Christ, where it was said, *The Lord has sworn and it will not repent him, you are a priest forever according to the order of Melchizedek*[^179], to commend the saving sacrifice in which the sacred blood was poured out for us, of which sacrifice the sacrifices were shadows[^180] that were commanded to be immolated from unblemished cattle[^181]—on this matter, then, of the repentance of God, lest he should think it must be so taken that, from what he does not understand, he might rage rabid into the barkings of blasphemies, he had close at hand something by which he might be admonished. For he himself recalled that God had said, *It repents me that I anointed Saul as king*[^182]. But this is written to have been said to holy Samuel, through whom God rebuked Saul because he had spared a man whom God had ordered to be killed, displaying a kind of mercy by despising obedience; as though he knew better what ought to be done with the man than the one who made the man. Here we learn what is most wholesome: that the divine precept should always conquer in us the human affection. Yet the same Samuel, to whom God had said, *It repents me that I anointed Saul as king,* says evidently that it does not repent God. For it is written thus: *And the word of the Lord came to Samuel, saying: It repents me that I appointed Saul as king, since he has turned away from me and has not kept my words*: and a little after, Samuel himself said to Saul: *The Lord has torn the kingdom of Israel from your hand today and will give it to your neighbor, better than you, and Israel will be divided in two and will not turn back, nor will it repent him, since he is not like a man, that it should repent him*[^183]. Behold one who knew how to understand God being merciful without misery, angry without anger, jealous without jealousy, forgetful without forgetfulness, ignorant without ignorance, repentant without repentance; not this man, who, by not speaking according to the word of God, and not considering his writings, and not heeding their voices, has become a mute barker, a blind reader, a deaf hearer.

## God willed to be reminded, although he had not forgotten, just as Christ willed to be taught where Lazarus had been laid, although he was not ignorant of it.

**20. 43.** "The forgetful God," he says, "and dead in memory, set a bow in the clouds, which is called the rainbow, that he might be reminded not to destroy the human race any further by a flood[^184]—not knowing at all what he is doing, one who deservedly needs a constant monitor." But this man, not in memory but dead in soul, not knowing what he says, if he cavils at more open matters, how much more does he grow dim in the clouds and is driven to rave? Yet, that I may answer him quickly, I say thus: that God willed to be reminded, although he had not forgotten, just as Christ willed to be taught where Lazarus had been laid, although he was not ignorant of it[^185]. For I do not wish to say what that bow signifies, shining from the clouds with brightness and rays of light, by which the dewy darkness is illumined, answering in a kind of pleasing confession; and how God does not destroy the world by a spiritual flood, while he is mindful of those whose figure the illumined clouds bear—for the names of these are written in heaven[^186], that the Father who is in heaven may be mindful of them, since they know that they shine not from their own light but from the sun of righteousness, as those clouds shine from the visible sun. But from what I have recalled he must be pressed: how he understands the Lord saying about Lazarus, *Where have you laid him?*[^187] And the place is shown to him, as though he did not know. For unless we acknowledge that he signified something by that inquiry in which he seemed not to know, how do we preach that Christ not only knew present things but also foreknew future ones? Especially since this man hurled himself, by a marvelous blindness, into this opinion, that he said: "No one but one who is ignorant asks a question." Here he could not consider how often Christ asked questions. Does he not ask a question who says, *What do you think about Christ? Whose son is he?*[^188] What is more manifest than this testimony? But if he is too obstinate, will he deny even there that Christ asked a question, where he himself testifies that he asks, saying: *I too will ask you one word: which, if you tell me, I also will tell you by what power I do these things: the baptism of John, whence was it? From heaven or from men?*[^189] Where now will he hide himself, this most loquacious and most desperate disputant against God? Where is what he says, "No one but one who is ignorant asks a question"? Behold, Christ is not ignorant, and yet he asks. With these eyes, indeed, he reproaches the God of the Prophets—the eyes with which he does not see Christ. But in such questions Christ is most openly a teacher. But in these, when he says, *Where have you laid him?*[^190] And, *Who touched me?*[^191] And whatever of this kind is read to have been said, he seems to wish to be taught what he does not know, and yet he knows. So, then, in those books too, God, as though forgetful, is reminded; but yet far be it that he should ever forget anything.

## In certain writings God, as though forgetful, is reminded; but yet far be it that he should ever forget anything.

**20. 44.** What of that very thing which the Lord said to his disciples: *Rejoice, because your names are written in heaven*[^192]; does it not seem that, as though from that bow which shines back from the clouds, God is reminded as though by certain letters in heaven? Unless these things are taken devoutly, until faith obtains that they be understood, are they not laughed at as fabulous? By whom laughed at, except by the foolish, and by that very thing in which they seem to themselves wise becoming more and more demented? For who considers how those who follow the Lord are written in heaven to God's memory, while those who forsake the Lord are written on the earth, of whom the prophet Jeremiah says: *All who forsake you will be confounded; those who depart will be written on the earth*[^193]? Whom Jesus is well understood to have signified when the Jews, conquered and confounded, having heard, *Let him who is without sin first cast a stone at her,* withdrew one after another. But he then, showing of what number they were, was writing with his finger on the earth[^194].

## The mystery of the flood.

**21. 45.** "If we suppose," he says, "that human beings received the sentence of the flood for the merit of their conduct, and that the righteous Noah was saved for the restoration of a better creation[^195], why thereafter are worse ones born, and why even now is the birth of the human race rolled round again into the same act of a filthy way of life?" He says this as though he had lived with those who perished in the flood and from there knew that worse ones are now born. But whether the human race after the flood is engaged in a worse, or the same, or a better act, I think it should be left to the judgment of God, who knows how to repay each according to his merits—not to this rabid dog barking against his Lord, nor to this foolish ass kicking against the goad. The Apostle cries out: *O depth of the riches of the wisdom and knowledge of God! How inscrutable are his judgments and unsearchable his ways! For who has known the mind of the Lord, or who has been his counselor?*[^196] And this man dares to be an adversary to him to whom there is no counselor. But what difference does it make to all who are going to die, as far as concerns the death of the body, whether they all die one by one or together and equally—except that, when they die one by one, all suffer both death and grief over the dead; but when one destruction snatches all together, grief is at least left to no one? But indeed there was in that flood a counsel of God deeper than the heart of unbelievers either knows or grasps. But I do not want—let this man rather hear the apostle Peter saying: *In the days of Noah, when the ark was being built, eight souls were saved through water; and that,* he says, *in a like form, baptism saves you—not the putting off of the filth of the flesh, but the appeal of a good conscience to God through the resurrection of Jesus Christ*[^197]. Behold, he has the mystery of the flood explained. Where for this reason there is added, *through the resurrection of Jesus Christ,* so that we might understand the eighth day, which the number of human beings in the ark signified: for on the eighth day, that is, after the seventh of the Sabbath, the Lord rose again. Thus those things which are recalled as done were also, if one understands, prophecies. But this man, established outside the ark—that is, outside the Church—was submerged by the flood, not washed.

## That is called a worst seed which is born of an impious will and sprouts up with execrable morals.

**22. 46.** He also cavils and blasphemes concerning the testimony of the prophet Isaiah, in that he said: "I have begotten and exalted sons, but they have spurned me; and a little after he called these same ones a worst seed[^198], as though showing himself the begetter of the evil, whose sons are a worst seed"—not knowing that they are called a worst seed for this reason: because, by sinning, they became degenerate from the grace of God by which they had been made sons, becoming the sons of those whose imitators they wished to be. Hence it is said to them in another place: *Your father is the Amorite and your mother the Hittite*[^199]—nations whose impiety and malice they followed, though they did not draw their fleshly origin from them. But let this man resolve the gospel question, where the Lord says: *If you, being evil, know how to give good gifts to your children, how much more will your Father who is in heaven give good things to those who ask him?*[^200] Let him say how God is the good father of the evil; for the Truth said both. Were they not evil to whom he said, *If, then, you, being evil*[^201]? Did they not have God as a good father, to whom he said, *How much more will your Father who is in heaven give good things to those who ask him*[^202]? Moreover, if they were called evil on account of sins—without which, in this weakness of mortals, even the life of any of the righteous does not exist—how much more fittingly would that be called a worst seed which was born of an impious will and sprouted up with execrable morals?

## A man can now be converted, by a changed will, from a bad tree into a good tree.

**22. 47.** But "this God," he says, "the Lord Christ called a bad tree making bad fruits." On the contrary, this very blasphemous discourse of this man, who thinks such things, is, as it were, the bad fruit of a bad tree. For that the Lord called a bad man a bad tree, whose bad fruits were bad works, and a good man a good tree, whose good fruits were good works[^203]—that is, that the very wills of human beings, whether the bad will of a bad man or the good will of a good man, are different trees bearing different fruits—he himself testifies clearly enough, saying: *A good man out of the good treasure of his heart brings forth good things, and an evil man out of the evil treasure of his heart brings forth evil things*[^204]. But how would he say, *Either make the tree good and its fruit good, or make the tree bad and its fruit bad*[^205], unless a man could be converted, by a changed will, now into this, now into that?

## The severity of God, because it is just, is found good in another way; for everything just is good.

**23. 48.** "But God himself," he says, "through the same prophet confesses and says: *I am the God who makes good things and creates evils*[^206]." Quite so: for he is the God of whom the Apostle says, *You see therefore the goodness and the severity of God*[^207]. But this severity of his is evil to the damnable, because it brings them the evil of damnation. For because it is just, it is found good in another way; for everything just is good. But how elegantly this man thinks he sifts and distinguishes words, not knowing what he says! Since this very thing—that the testimony was so set down that it was not said, "making good things and evils," or "creating good things and evils," or "creating good things and making evils," but, *Making good things and creating evils*[^208]—he wishes to turn into a charge, and tries to show that what is made is made externally, but that what is created is, in himself, the creator, and proceeds from him; so that, namely, the God of the Prophets might seem at some time to have been the maker of a good external to himself, but the creator of evil as being by nature evil, bringing forth from himself what he created. If we consider these words from the custom of human speech, both to be made and to be created are said not only of the sons whom anyone begets from himself, but also of magistrates and cities and whatever other things which do not come out of the begetter but are made externally. But if we examine how the holy Scriptures, at which this man lies in wait, are wont to speak, either to make is the same as to create—from which, however, to beget is distinguished—and for the sake of varying the word, not for any difference in things, it could be said, *Making good things and creating evils,* when it could also be said, "creating good things and making evils"; or, if the prophetic Spirit had wished there to be any distinction here, these words would much more fittingly be so taken that we should understand that to be made which, if it were not made, would simply not exist, but to be created that something be founded and established out of what already was, as we said that magistrates and cities are created. For both those men, from among those who were already human beings, are established in honors when magistrates are created; and the wood and stones from which cities are constructed already existed, surely, but had not yet come, by a certain order and arrangement, into that appearance of things which we see in cities, and when this happens cities are said to be created. For what the Greeks call  
, our people sometimes render "to create," sometimes "to establish," sometimes "to found"—which very often signifies the same in those writings as does "to make." For we read both *God made man to the image of God*[^209] and *God created man imperishable*[^210]; and if it is sometimes said with some difference, that distinction which I have stated can more rightly hold: that to make is what was not at all, but to create is to establish something out of what already was, by ordering it. And therefore in this place God was said to be *creating evils,* since for sinners he turns into an evil, by the disposition of his severity, the things which were made good by the bounty of his goodness. Hence the apostle Paul says: *We are the good odor of Christ in every place, both among those who are being saved and among those who are perishing. To some, indeed, an odor of life unto life, but to others an odor of death unto death.* But since there immediately follows and he says, *And for these things who is sufficient?*[^211]—are we not, in a way, importunate to thrust these things upon carnal people who are not studious but contentious, in no way fit to grasp the things which—would that they at least ceased to carp at!

## That the God of the Prophets is read to be the creator of good things.

**23. 49.** For how great a conflict was stirred up against the Arians, lest they should say that the only-begotten Son is a creature—thinking that "created" was the same as "begotten"—this man perhaps does not know. But that his fallacious and distorted rule may be struck and broken by the very prophetic and gospel testimony which he himself set down, God speaks thus through the prophet: *I, creating light and making darkness, making peace and creating evils*[^212]. Which this man set down neither in full nor as it is read there. And that, indeed, must be easily passed over—that for "peace" he put *good things,* since peace is a good. But this must not be passed over: that he was able by guile to pass over the upper part of this sentence, so as not to say *creating light,* because, since light is—even by his own confession—surely a good, he was unwilling to confess it created by him whom he wishes to create nothing but evils. We ought, then, rather to take it that "create" and "make" are set down indifferently; yet the rule of the distinction which this man made for himself is broken, since the God of the Prophets, whom he blames over a word he does not understand, is there read to be the creator of good things, which he denies. And likewise from the Gospel. Indeed, as though he were favoring his own side greatly, he set against us what the Lord says: *A good tree makes good fruits, and a bad tree makes bad fruits*[^213]*.* Why, then, according to this man, did he say "makes," not "creates," if his distinction is true—the one by which he distinguishes the maker from the creator, saying that what is made is alien to the maker, since it accedes from without, but that what is born is the proper offspring of him who begets? For from this he supposes that God is the begetter of evils, because it was said *creating evils,* thinking—as the Arians too thought—that in those Scriptures it makes no difference whether something is said to be begotten or created. But surely in this, that the Lord said the good tree does not "create" but "make" good fruits, and the bad tree bad ones, let this man see his rule broken and restrain his tongue. For what is more foolish than to say that the God of the Prophets is a bad tree, and to want this understood in what the Lord says, *A bad tree makes bad fruits,* and again to say, "He does not make evils but creates them; for if he made them, they would be alien to him and would accede to him from without; but when he creates, he himself begets them, as it were by the root, from himself"? Therefore the Lord did not say of this God, *A bad tree makes bad fruits,* since, according to this man, God creates evils, he does not make them. Behold one who dares to accuse the Prophets, who is refuted by the witnesses he himself has brought forward from the Gospel.

## The Spirit of God may also, through the horror of suffering evils, strike in the fear of doing evil.

**24. 50.** Likewise, the impure man, shuddering at certain words from the book of *Deuteronomy* as though they were impure, stirs them up, as though God ought to have been ashamed to impose or to foretell shameful torments upon the impious, and ought not to have threatened in such a way as to say: *The woman among you who is soft and too tender, whose foot has not attempted to walk upon the earth for tenderness and softness, will look with grudging eye upon her husband and her son and her daughter and her afterbirth and what comes out from between her thighs, and will eat it*[^214]. On the contrary, the more horrible it is, the more terrible it is. For it was said by the prophet not by way of admonishing but of threatening, not that human beings should do these things, but that they should not do those things which a perverse sense practices, and arrive at these things which the human sense shudders at. But who could worthily express how much more execrable is the foulness of a mind that shudders at the punishments of misdeeds and does not beware of the misdeeds that earn punishments? Let the Holy Spirit, surely, untainted and untaintable, say what the unclean soul refuses to hear and does not refuse to be. For it turns away from uncleanness of the flesh by the offended senses of the flesh, and loves its own uncleanness with the senses of the heart extinguished. Let the Spirit of God say these things and, through the horror of suffering such evils, strike in the fear of doing evil.

## For God grants that there profit us not only what truth teaches but also what vanity clamors against it.

**24. 51.** For the same Spirit, speaking also through the Apostle, was not ashamed to offend impious senses while wishing to instruct devout ones, where, having recalled the impiety of certain people by which *they worshiped and served the creature rather than the Creator*[^215], he added and said: *For this reason God handed them over to passions of dishonor. For their women exchanged the natural use for that use which is against nature. And likewise the males too, abandoning the natural use of the woman, burned in their lust toward one another, males working shamefulness upon males and receiving in themselves the mutual recompense which was due for their error*[^216]*.* If any enemy of the Apostle wished to say, from these words, such things as this blasphemer says from certain passages of the old books, would he not have ample material in which loquaciously to rage, and the more eloquent he seemed to himself, the more detestable would be the curses he hurled—especially because it was said, *Receiving in themselves the mutual recompense which was due for their error*? For the Apostle was not afraid to say that it was "due" that those who *served the creature rather than the Creator* should receive *the recompense of their error*[^217] by suffering these shameful things not unwillingly but by doing them gladly—by the judgment, namely, not of some most unclean man whom such things would delight, but of the most just God, who *handed them over to passions of dishonor,* so that crimes might be avenged by crimes and the punishments of sinners might be not torments but increases of vices. But the wise man, when he hears these things, rather fears in this life that very wrath of God by which a man does not suffer what he bitterly grieves over but does what he shamefully delights in; and he despises the insane words of the one to whom such judgments are displeasing, because in this man too he recognizes the punishment of Pharaoh—that is, of a hardened heart[^218]. For if God handed over certain people who *did not approve of having God in their knowledge to a reprobate mind, so that they do what is not fitting*[^219], what wonder if he handed over this man too, who blasphemes the divine words, to a reprobate mind, so that he says what is not fitting? For thus *it is fitting that there be heresies, so that the approved*[^220], says the Apostle, may be made manifest among you. Thus the vessels of wrath are arranged in fitting places and times, so that even from them God may make known *the riches of his glory upon the vessels of mercy*[^221], which are made out of the mass of the same damnation, unto honor by his grace, not by their own merits. For he grants that there profit us not only what truth teaches but also what vanity clamors against it, so that, when reply is made to most restless vanity, attention may be given to most sincere truth.

## Why God, seeking the beauty and cleanness of morals rather than of words, said something shameful not shamefully but threateningly, so that it should not be committed.

**24. 52.** Vanity reproaches a shameful curse; but that it is not shameful—by naming certain shameful things, to show that a shamefulness must be avoided—merciful truth declares, and senseless vanity is conquered. For concerning a shameful curse even the apostle Paul can be reprehended by verbose impious men, where he says, *Would that those who are disturbing you might even be cut off*[^222]—which, even though to those who understand well it appears rather a blessing, that they might become eunuchs for the kingdom of heaven[^223], garrulous blindness can nevertheless reprehend even in the Apostle, contending that he ought not to have stated an honorable matter with a shameful word. They can reprehend even the Lord himself, who, commending the gift of the same continence, says: *There are those who have castrated themselves for the kingdom of heaven*[^224]. For these little scribblers—for so, rather than learned men, are they to be called who by reading have learned to be learned men knowing nothing—these, then, read in Cicero something of the sort by which they think they learnedly blame the words of Christ, ruined rather than skilled. For he, when teaching that obscenity must be avoided in the figurative use of words, says: *I do not want it said that by the death of Africanus the republic was castrated*[^225]*.* But if he did not himself avoid the very word he wished to be avoided—so as to show it must be avoided—and was compelled to say what he did not want said, how much more is a thing which is rightly signified by that same word, so that it can be understood by the hearer, stated by its own word? And, to return to what this man reprehends in *Deuteronomy*[^226]: if Cicero, a most eloquent man and a most vigilant weigher and measurer of words, said what he did not want said, in order that it should not be said, how much better did God, seeking the beauty and cleanness of morals rather than of words, say something shameful not shamefully but threateningly, so that this might be shuddered at, lest something else be committed, on account of which one would come to those things which it is a horror to hear? And yet, when it is read, unbelief closes its ears, turns away its face, hardens its countenance, darts its tongue, hurls blasphemies. See whether these people are not of that kind of men who, when Christ was speaking of the sacrament of his body and blood, said: *This is a hard saying; who can hear it?*[^227] Except that more excusable are those who could not bear the divine words they did not understand as horrible—not in a curse, but in a blessing. For it is no wonder if a curse is shuddered at when it is heard; nor is it to be required that what is said for this end—that it may be feared by one who shudders—be said in words not to be shuddered at. But the Lord was saying such things as he commanded to be loved, not feared; and yet such as unbelief cannot bear: *My flesh is truly food, and my blood is truly drink*; and, *Unless you eat my flesh and drink my blood, you will not have life in you*[^228]? If, then, the wisdom of God, feeding the believing soul with words fitting for the sacrament, took no heed of the nauseating folly, how much more did that same wisdom, where there had been a place and time for fear, not for love, wishing to bring in a wholesome terror, take no heed of the foolish man's error, although it foresaw his horror? But who of these people knows how to shudder at the spiritual foulness of the soul, when, as if by a kind of hunger and want of its own, it is compelled to eat the things which come out, as from its thighs, from its carnal thoughts[^229]? For the effect of that curse, which this man reprehends as shameful, is rare; for scarcely ever is the scourge of hunger so great that it compels to those unspeakable deeds; but with that hunger by which the wretched souls of those destitute of truth eat, in place of truth, the things which their carnal senses bring forth, all things everywhere are full—so much the more unhappily as it harms the more and is shuddered at the less.

## The conclusion of this book.

**24. 53.** But I think that I ought to answer the one book of the man you sent with more than one of my own, lest it be too long. Accordingly, let us here make the end of this book, so that, from the matters which remain to be sifted, another may be begun. For somehow the reader's attention is refreshed by the ending of a book, just as the traveler's labor is by an inn.
