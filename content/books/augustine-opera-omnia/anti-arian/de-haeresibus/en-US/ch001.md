# Letters

## Letter 221

> *Written about the same time.*

> *Quodvultdeus urgently asks Augustine to write a catalogue of all the heresies that have sprung up against the Christian faith, setting out their errors and rites in brief (nn. 1–2), and stating the opposing positions concisely so as to help the unlearned (nn. 3–4).*

TO THE LORD DESERVEDLY VENERABLE AND TRULY MOST BLESSED FATHER AUGUSTINE THE BISHOP, FROM QUODVULTDEUS THE DEACON.

**1.** For a long time I was anxious, and more than once I put off these requests of mine, daring to make them only with hesitation; but what chiefly emboldened me, as the saying goes, was your Blessedness's kindness, which everyone has experienced. As I consider it, I came rather to fear that the Lord might judge me proud for not asking, slothful for not seeking, and lazy for not knocking. I would have believed it enough merely to have had this kind of wish, were I unable to obtain the fruit; but since I know for certain that the heavenly grace has opened to your Reverence a door for the word of God[^1], and that your holy mind, which Christ possesses, is ready not only to open it to all who are willing, but even to persuade the unwilling not to balk at entering, I will not, with superfluous wordiness, delay your busy Reverence, but will state the desire of my entreaty briefly.

**2.** That there are some in the clergy even of this very great city who are unskilled, I infer from my own case; and how much profit my request would bring to that whole order, I lay before the consideration of your Holiness to weigh. For I presume that I too, unworthy as I am, will obtain the privilege granted to all who earn it from your spiritual labors, my deservedly venerable lord and truly most blessed father. I therefore beg your Devotion that you deign to set forth: from the time the Christian religion received the name of the promised inheritance, what heresies there have been and now are; what errors they introduced and still introduce; what they held and still hold against the catholic Church concerning faith, the Trinity, baptism, penance, Christ as man, Christ as God, the resurrection, and the New and Old Testament; and absolutely everything in which they dissent from the truth; which of them have baptism and which do not; after which of them the Church baptizes yet does not rebaptize; how it receives those who come over; and what answer, by law, authority, and reason, is to be given to each.

**3.** Let not your Blessedness think me so foolish as not to see how many and how vast the volumes would have to be in order to resolve these matters fully. But this is not what I am asking be done; for I do not doubt that it has been done many times over. Rather, I ask that the opinions of each heresy be set out briefly, concisely, and summarily, and that what the catholic Church holds against them be appended, only so far as is enough for instruction—so that, as it were from a single handbook drawn from them all, anyone who wishes to know some objection or refutation more amply, fully, and plainly may be referred to the rich and magnificent volumes which various authors, and especially your Reverence, are known to have produced for this very purpose. Such a guide will suffice, I think, for the learned and the unlearned, the leisured and the busy, and for those promoted from anywhere to a ministry of the Church of whatever rank; for the man who has read much will recall it briefly, and the ignorant man will be instructed in summary, so that he may know what to hold, what to avoid, what to shun lest he do it, and what to attain that he may do it. Perhaps too, if what I judge is true, this little work will not, amid your other magnificent ones, fail to win the testimony of your crown even against the malicious minds and crafty lips of slanderers[^2]—so that those for whom a wider field of slander lies open may be hemmed in by the strong and wholly enclosed bounds of faith, surrounded on every side by all kinds of the weapons of truth, and, suddenly laid low by a single many-pronged dart, may not dare to breathe out their dead spirits.

**4.** I see how burdensome I am, when you have better things to think of and greater things to arrange, a holy old man bearing also the complaints of the body. But through Christ the Lord I ask—he who granted you without envy to share in his wisdom—that you grant this favor to the unlearned of the Church, you who acknowledge yourself a debtor to the wise and the foolish[^3], and who may deservedly and rightly say: *See that I have not labored for myself alone, but for all who seek the truth*[^4]. I could still have offered humble and manifold prayers and enlisted with me a host of the unlearned; but I preferred now to hear you dictate rather than to keep you reading any longer.

## Letter 222

> *Written a little after the preceding one.*

> *Augustine, excusing himself to Quodvultdeus on account of the difficulty of the catalogue—attempted by the bishops Philaster and Epiphanius, each in a different way (nn. 1–2)—commends the bearer of the letter (n. 3).*

TO HIS MOST BELOVED SON AND FELLOW DEACON QUODVULTDEUS, FROM AUGUSTINE THE BISHOP.

**1.** On receiving the letter of your Charity, in which with the most ardent zeal you asked me to write something briefly about all the heresies that have sprung up against the Savior's teaching since the coming of the Lord, I wrote back at once, as soon as an occasion presented itself, by my son Philocalus, a leading man of Hippo, telling you how difficult this is; but soon this other occasion too was offered, by which I now write back and briefly show the difficulty of the work.

**2.** A certain Philaster, bishop of Brescia, whom I myself also saw at Milan together with holy Ambrose, wrote a book on this subject; he did not pass over even those heresies that existed among the Jewish people before the coming of the Lord, and he listed twenty-eight of them, and after the coming of the Lord, one hundred twenty-eight. Epiphanius, a bishop of Cyprus, praiseworthily renowned in the teaching of the catholic faith, also wrote on this subject in Greek; but he, gathering the heresies of both periods, comprised eighty. Since, then, both wished to do what you ask of me, you see nonetheless how much they differ between themselves about the number of the sects—which surely would not have happened unless one of them counted as a heresy what the other did not. For it is not to be supposed that Epiphanius was unaware of any that Philaster knew, since we have found Epiphanius far more learned than Philaster; so that we should rather say that very many escaped Philaster, if the one comprised so many more and the other fewer. But beyond doubt, in that question where it is debated what a heresy is, the two did not see the matter alike; and indeed this is altogether difficult to define. Therefore caution is needed, when we try to bring them all into a count, lest we pass over some, although they are heresies, or count some, when they are not heresies. See, then, whether perhaps I ought to send you the book of holy Epiphanius; for I judge that he spoke on this subject more learnedly than Philaster, and his work could be translated into the Latin tongue at Carthage more easily and conveniently, so that you might rather supply us with what you seek from us.

**3.** I commend the bearer warmly. He is a subdeacon of our diocese, from the estate of the eminent Orontius, who is very dear to us. On his behalf, and on behalf of his father, by whom he was adopted, I have also written to Orontius himself; and when your Christian Kindness has read that letter, I ask that you deign to support it before the said man by your intercession. I have also sent with him a man of the Church, so that his access to your Holiness might not be difficult; for I am not a little anxious about him, and from this anxiety the Lord, as I hope, will free me through the urging of your love. I ask too that you not find it a burden to write back to me how that Theodosius stands in the catholic faith, the man through whom certain Manicheans were exposed; and likewise about those whom we suppose, having been exposed by him, to have been corrected. If you have perhaps heard anything also about the departure of the holy bishops, let me know. May you live in God.

## Letter 223

> *Perhaps written in 428.*

> *Quodvultdeus again presses Augustine for the little work on the heresies (nn. 1 and 3), one that would indeed be far different from those of Philaster and Epiphanius (n. 2).*

TO THE LORD DESERVEDLY VENERABLE AND TRULY MOST BLESSED HOLY FATHER AUGUSTINE, FROM QUODVULTDEUS THE DEACON.

**1.** I have indeed received one handbook of your Reverence, which you deigned to send by a man of the Church. For the one that your Blessedness indicated had been sent earlier, by the honorable Philocalus, has not yet reached me. But although I have always been conscious of my own sins, I have now plainly recognized that, in obtaining for myself the gift I begged, my own person is an obstacle to the whole Church. Yet I am wholly confident that he who through the grace of his Only-begotten deigned to blot out the crimes of the human race will not allow my own to prevail unto the ruin of all, but rather, where sin abounded, will make grace abound the more[^5], my deservedly venerable lord and truly most blessed holy father. The difficulty of the work which I have humbly begged be supplied through your kindness, for the instruction of us most unlearned men, I myself was not ignorant of and foretold beforehand; but from the abundance of the divine fountain which the Lord has bestowed on you, I presumed with a truthful heart.

**2.** For although the venerable bishops Philaster and Epiphanius are remembered to have written something of this kind—which, beyond doubt, like everything else, is unknown to me among the rest—I do not think they took this care and diligence: to attach to each of the several opinions the contrary opinions as well, and to add the rites; and then, finally, whatever that work of theirs may be, it perhaps does not have the brevity I desire. In vain, too, is Greek eloquence delegated to a man who has not learned Latin; for I asked not only for counsel but also for help. And why should I remind your Reverence not only of the difficulty but also of the obscurity of translators, when you yourself judge this better and fully? It is also relevant that some heresies are said to have emerged even after the death of those men, of which they made no mention.

**3.** Therefore I take refuge in the special protection of your fatherly love, and with my own voice—but with the desire of all—I appeal to your sacred breast, ever ready for mercy; setting aside foreign relishes, and considering the text of my earlier letter, do not deny African bread, which our province usually has as its special fare, seasoned too with heavenly manna, to one who knocks late[^6] and suffers hunger. For surely I too will not cease to knock until you yourself grant it; so that what no privilege obtains, since I have no merits, my tireless importunity may at least deserve.

## Letter 224

> *Written after the preceding one.*

> *Augustine, promising Quodvultdeus that he will write the catalogue if his other occupations permit—since he is most busy refuting Julian's later books and composing the Revisions (nn. 1–2)—again commends the bearer (n. 3).*

TO THE LORD MOST SINCERELY BELOVED, HIS BROTHER AND FELLOW DEACON QUODVULTDEUS, FROM AUGUSTINE THE BISHOP.

**1.** When this occasion for writing was offered me through the presbyter of Fussala, whom I commend to your love, I reviewed your letter, in which you ask that I write something about the heresies that have been able to arise since the coming of the Lord began to be announced in the flesh. I did this in order to see whether I ought now to undertake the work itself and send you something from it, where you might consider that it is the more difficult the briefer you want it made. But I could not even do this, hindered by such cares coming upon me as I could by no means disregard; for they have turned me away even from what I had in hand.

**2.** This is the task in which I am answering the books of Julian, the eight he has published after the four to which I replied earlier. For when brother Alypius received these at Rome, he had not yet copied them all, when he chose not to let slip the occasion offered, through which he sent me five, promising that he would soon send the other three, and strongly insisting that I not delay my reply. Compelled by his insistence, I was obliged to carry on more slackly what I was doing, so as not to fail either work—the one by day, the other by night, whenever I was spared by the other occupations that did not cease to come upon me from this side and that.

I was engaged, indeed, in a most necessary task: for I was revising my little works; and if anything in them offended me, or could offend others, I worked at it, partly censuring, partly defending what ought to and could be read. And I had already completed two volumes, having revised all my books, whose number I did not know; and I found them to be two hundred thirty-two. There remained the letters, and then the discourses to the people, which the Greeks call homilies. And I had already read very many of the letters, but had as yet dictated nothing from them, when these books of Julian too began to occupy me, to the fourth of which I have now begun to reply. When, therefore, I have finished this, and replied to the fifth, if the three do not arrive, I plan (if God wills) to begin also what you ask, carrying on both at once—namely, this, and that revision of my little works—the night and day hours being apportioned to each.

**3.** I have made this known to your Holiness so that, as great as your desire is to receive what you ask, so much the more ardently you may ask the Lord's help for me, that I may serve your praiseworthy zeal and the benefit of those whom you judge it will profit, my most sincerely beloved brother. I again commend the bearer, and the business for which he has set out; if you learn before whom it is to be transacted, I ask that you not be loath to help. For we cannot abandon the needs of men of this sort, who are not our tenants, but—what is greater—our brothers, and who in the love of Christ belong to our care. May you live in God.

[^2]: See Ps 12:3-4 (11:3-4).
[^3]: Rom 1:14.
[^4]: Sir 24:34; 33:18.
[^5]: See Rom 5:20.
[^6]: See Luke 11:5-8.
