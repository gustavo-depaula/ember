# Preface

**1.** What you ask very often and most insistently, holy son Quodvultdeus—that I write something about the heresies worth reading by those who desire to avoid the doctrines contrary to the Christian faith and deceptive under the shadow of the Christian name—know that long ago, far before you asked, I had thought to do it, and would have done it, had I not, carefully considering of what kind and how great a thing it was, felt it to be beyond my powers. But since I confess that no one has pressed me with asking as you have, in your very troublesome insistence I noted your name too and said, "I will undertake it and do what God wills." For I am confident that God wills this: if by his merciful favor he brings me to the end of this work, that through the ministry of my tongue the great difficulty of the matter may either be merely shown, or, with his fuller help, removed. Of these two, the one I put first I have long turned over and over in my mind and contemplate in a kind of meditation. But the one I named afterward I confess I have not received. And whether, while I do this in order to accomplish it, while I ask, while I seek, while I knock, I am perhaps going to receive it, I do not know. Yet I know that I shall neither ask nor seek nor knock as much as is enough, unless I have received this disposition too as a gift of divine inspiration.

**2.** In this work, then, which at your vehement urging I have undertaken in the will of God, you see that, in order to carry it through, I need not so much to be compelled by your ever more frequent requests to me, as to be helped by the devout prayers to God—not only yours, but also those of the other brothers whom you may be able to find as faithful associates in this matter. That this may come about, I have taken care to hasten the sending, with the Lord's help, of these very first parts of my labor, where this preface stands, to your Charity, so that all of you who can know that I have already undertaken this so great a task which you desire to be fulfilled by me may know how much you ought to pray for me.

**3.** You ask, then, as your letters indicate—those you sent me when you first began to ask these things of me—that I set out: "Briefly, concisely, and summarily, from the time the Christian religion received the name of the promised inheritance, what heresies there have been and now are; what errors they introduced and still introduce; what they held and still hold against the catholic Church concerning faith, the Trinity, baptism, penance, Christ as man, Christ as God, the resurrection, and the New and Old Testament." But when you saw that these inquiries of yours were proceeding without measure, you thought a summary of a certain general kind should be employed, and said, "And absolutely everything in which they dissent from the truth." Then you added, "Which of them have baptism and which do not; after which of them the Church baptizes yet does not rebaptize; how it receives those who come over; and what answer, by law, authority, and reason, is to be given to each."

**4.** When you ask that all these things be set out by me, I marvel that your brilliant mind both thirsts for the truth in so many and such great matters and, already dreading tedium, asks for brevity. But you yourself also saw what I could think at this point about this letter of yours, and as if watchfully you met my thought, saying, "Let not your Blessedness think me so foolish as not to see how many and how vast the volumes would have to be in order to resolve these matters. But this is not what I am asking be done; for I do not doubt that it has been done many times over." And as if suggesting a plan by which both brevity might be kept and the truth disclosed, you add the words I have already set down a little above, and say, "But I ask that the opinions of each heresy be set out briefly, concisely, and summarily, and that what the catholic Church holds against them be appended, only so far as is enough for instruction." See, again you send me off into length—not because these things cannot or should not be said briefly, but because they are so many that, however briefly they are to be said, they demand many pages. But you say, "So that, as it were from a single handbook drawn from them all, anyone who wishes to know some objection or refutation more amply, fully, and plainly may be referred to the rich and magnificent volumes which various authors, and especially," you say, "your Reverence, are known to have produced for this very purpose." In saying this you indicate that you desire, as it were, a single handbook about all these things. Hear, then, from where you may be reminded what you are asking.

**5.** A certain Celsus set out, in six not small volumes, the opinions of all the philosophers who founded the various sects down to his own times—for he could not go further. Nor did he refute anyone, but only disclosed what they held, with such brevity of speech that he employed only as much eloquence as sufficed for a matter neither to be praised nor blamed, neither affirmed nor defended, but only disclosed and indicated; though he named nearly a hundred philosophers, not all of whom established heresies of their own, since he did not think that even those should be passed over in silence who followed their teachers without any disagreement.

**6.** But our own Epiphanius, bishop of Cyprus, taken from human affairs not long ago, speaking of eighty heresies, himself also composed six books, recounting everything in a historical narrative, contending in no disputation against falsehood on behalf of the truth. These little books are indeed brief, and if they were brought together into one book, even that would not be comparable in length to certain books of ours or of others. If I imitate his brevity in recounting the heresies, you will have nothing briefer to demand or expect of me. But the sum of this labor of mine does not lie there, as will be able to appear to you, either as I demonstrate it or even as you run ahead of me, when I have done so. For you will see, in what the aforesaid bishop did, how much is lacking to the work that you yourself want done—how much more, then, to what I want. For you, although you want it "briefly, concisely, and summarily," nevertheless also want the heresies recounted to be answered, which he did not do.

**7.** But I would rather do this, if God too wills, whereby every heresy, both the one that is known and the one that is unknown, may be avoided, and whereby whatever becomes known may be rightly judged. For not every error is a heresy, although every heresy, which is reckoned among the vices, cannot be a heresy without some error. What it is, then, that makes someone a heretic can, as I judge, either not be comprised at all in a regular sort of definition, or be so only with the greatest difficulty. This will be made clear in the course of this work, if God guides it and brings my plan to what I intend. But what use the inquiry itself may have, even if we are unable to comprise how a heretic is to be defined, is to be seen and stated in its own place. For if this can be comprised, who does not see how great the usefulness is? The first parts, then, of this work will be about the heresies that arose against Christ's teaching after his coming and ascension, and that have been able in some way to become known to us.
