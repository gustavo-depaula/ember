# Revisions II

## Chapter 15 (42): *On the Trinity*, in fifteen books[^1]

**1**. I wrote fifteen books over a number of years on *The Trinity*, who is God. But when I had not yet finished the twelfth of them, and was holding on to them longer than those who were eager to have them could bear, they were taken from me less corrected than they ought and were able to be, when I should have wished to publish them.

After I found this out—since other copies of them had also remained with us—I had decided that I myself would not now publish them, but would keep them so as to tell in some other little work of mine what had happened to me concerning them.

Yet at the urging of the brothers, whom I had not the strength to resist, I corrected them as much as I thought they needed correcting, and I completed and published them, adding at the head a letter that I had written to the venerable Aurelius, bishop of the Church of Carthage,[^2] in which, as in a kind of prologue, I set out both what had happened and what I had intended to do in my own design, and what I had done under the compelling love of the brothers.[^3]

**2**. In the eleventh book of these works, when I was dealing with the visible body, I said: *and so to love it is to become estranged*.[^4] This was said with reference to that love by which a thing is so loved that the one who loves it thinks himself blessed in enjoying it. For it is not to become estranged, but rather to praise the Creator, to love bodily form, so that anyone enjoying the Creator himself may be truly blessed.

Likewise, in the same book, where I said: *nor do I remember a four-footed bird, because I have not seen one, but I picture such a thing very easily by adding to some winged form such as I have seen two other feet such as I have likewise seen*,[^5] in saying this I failed to recall the four-footed flying creatures that the law mentions.[^6] For it does not count among the feet the two hind legs by which locusts leap, which it calls clean and so distinguishes from those unclean flying creatures, such as beetles, that do not leap with such legs. Indeed all flying creatures of this kind are called four-footed in the law.

**3**. In the twelfth book,[^7] the supposed *exposition of the words of the Apostle, where he says: "every sin that a man commits is outside the body,"* does not satisfy me; nor do I think that what is said—*but he who fornicates sins against his own body*—is to be understood as if a person did this who does something in order to obtain what is perceived through the body, so as to set the goal of his good in these things. For this embraces far more sins than that fornication which is committed in unlawful intercourse,[^8] about which the Apostle clearly was speaking when he said this.

This work, apart from the letter that was afterward attached to its head, begins thus: *whoever is going to read what we discuss concerning the Trinity*.

[^1]: Augustine began work on the *De Trinitate* around 400 and completed it in the early 420s.
[^2]: Letter 174. Aurelius, a long-time friend of Augustine and a person of great importance in the African church, was bishop of Carthage from the early 390s until his death in 430.
[^3]: *Fratrum caritate compellente*: "out of a compelling love for the brothers." *Fratrum* is translated here as an objective genitive, but it could also be translated as a subjective genitive, in which case it could be rendered "as a result of the brothers' loving urging."
[^4]: *On the Trinity* 11.5.9.
[^5]: *On the Trinity* 11.10.17.
[^6]: See Lev 11:20–23.
[^7]: *On the Trinity* 12.10.15.
[^8]: See *Revisions* 1.19.6; *Homily* 162.
