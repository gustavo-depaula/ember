# Book VII: Augustine returns to the question: whether any person of the Trinity is Wisdom in itself.

**1.** 1. Now let us inquire more carefully, as far as God grants, what we deferred a little before: whether each single person in the Trinity can also be called, in itself and not with the other two, God, or great, or wise, or true, or almighty, or just, and whatever else can be said of God, not relatively, but in relation to itself; or whether these things are not said except when the Trinity is understood.

For this makes the question, because it is written, *Christ the power of God and the wisdom of God*: whether he is so the father of his wisdom and power that he is wise by this wisdom which he begot, and powerful by this power which he begot; and because he is always powerful and wise,[^1] he always begot power and wisdom. For we had said, if it is so, why is he not also the father of his magnitude by which he is great, and of the goodness by which he is good, and of the justice by which he is just, and of others if there are any?

Or if all these are understood under several words in the same wisdom and power, so that the magnitude is that which is power, the goodness that which is wisdom, and again the wisdom that which is power, as we have already treated; let us remember that, when I name any one of these, it is to be so taken as if I mentioned them all.

It is asked, then, whether the Father even singly is wise, and is himself wisdom to himself, or whether he is wise in the way that he is speaking. For he is speaking by the Word which he begot;[^2] not by a word that is uttered, and sounds, and passes away; but by the Word which *was with God, and the Word was God, and all things were made through him*: by a Word equal to himself, by which he always and unchangeably speaks himself. For he is not himself the word, just as he is not the son, nor the image. But speaking: excepting those temporal voices of God which come about in the creature; for they sound and pass away:[^3] speaking, then, by that coeternal Word, he is understood not singly, but with the Word itself, without which he is certainly not speaking.

Is he then also wise as he is speaking, so that he is wisdom in the way that he is the Word, and this is to be the Word which is to be wisdom; this is also to be power, so that power and wisdom and Word are the same, and it is said relatively, as Son and image: and he is not singly powerful or wise, but with the very power and wisdom which he begot; as he is not singly speaking, but by that Word, and with that Word which he begot; and so great by that and with that magnitude which he begot?

And if he is not great by one thing and God by another, but is great by that whereby he is God, because for him to be great is not one thing and to be God another; it is consequent that he is not God singly either, but by that and with that deity which he begot, so that the Son is the deity of the Father in the way that he is the wisdom and power of the Father, and as he is the Word and image of the Father.[^4]

And because for him to be is not one thing and to be God another, so the Son is also the essence of the Father, just as he is his Word and image. And through this, even excepting that which the Father is, the Father is not anything except because he has a Son: so that not only is that which is called Father (which is manifestly said of him not in relation to himself, but relatively to the Son, and therefore Father because he has a Son); but altogether, that he is what he is in relation to himself, he is for this reason, that he begot his own essence. For just as he is great only by that magnitude which he begot, so he also is only by that essence which he begot; because for him to "be" is not one thing, and to be great another.

Is he, then, the father of his own essence, as he is the father of his own magnitude, as he is the father of his own power and wisdom? For the same is his magnitude which is his power, and the same essence which is his magnitude.

## The proposed question is resolved: the Son is wisdom of wisdom as he is light of light.

**1.** 2. This discussion arose from what is written, that *Christ is the power of God and the wisdom of God*.

Wherefore our speech is constrained into these straits, when we desire to utter the unutterable, so that we must either say that *Christ* is not *the power of God and the wisdom of God*, and so impudently and impiously resist the Apostle;

or confess that *Christ* is indeed *the power of God and the wisdom of God*, but that his Father is not the father of his own power and wisdom, which is no less impious; for thus he will not even be the Father of Christ, because *Christ is the power of God and the wisdom of God*;

or that the Father is not powerful by his own power, nor wise by his own wisdom; which who would dare to say?

or that in the Father to be is one thing and to be wise another, so that he is not by the very thing by which he is wise; which is wont to be understood of the soul, which is at one time unwise, at another wise, as a nature changeable, and not supremely and perfectly simple;

or that the Father is not anything in relation to himself, and that not only that which is the Father, but altogether that which he is, is said relatively to the Son. How, then, is the Son of the same essence of which the Father is, since, in relation to himself, the Father is neither essence, nor is at all in relation to himself, but even "to be" is for him in relation to the Son?

But rather much more of one and the same essence, because one and the same essence is the Father and the Son; since for the Father his very "being" is not in relation to himself, but in relation to the Son, whose essence he begot, and by which essence he is whatever he is. Neither, then, is in relation to himself, and each is said relatively in relation to the other.

Or is the Father alone said not only that which is called Father, but altogether whatever is said relatively in relation to the Son; while that one is said also in relation to himself? And if it is so, what is said in relation to himself? Is it the essence itself? But the essence of the Father is the Son, as the power and wisdom of the Father, as the Word of the Father and the Image of the Father.[^5]

Or if the Son is said essence in relation to himself, but the Father is not essence, but the begetter of essence, and is not in relation to himself, but by that very essence which he begot, as he is great by that very magnitude which he begot; then the Son is also said magnitude in relation to himself, then also power, and wisdom, and Word, and Image. But what is more absurd than that an image be said in relation to itself?

Or if Image and Word are not the same as power and wisdom, but those are said relatively, while these in relation to himself, not to another; then the Father begins to be wise not by that wisdom which he begot, because he cannot be said relatively in relation to it, and it not be said relatively in relation to him. For all things that are said relatively are said in relation to one another.

It remains, then, that the Son too be said essence relatively in relation to the Father. From which results a most unexpected sense, that the essence itself is not essence; or at least that when essence is said, not essence, but a relative is indicated. As when a master is said, not essence is indicated, but a relative which is referred to a servant; but when a man is said, or anything of the sort, which is said in relation to himself, not to another, then essence is indicated. A man, then, when he is called a master, the man himself is essence, but master is said relatively; for man is said in relation to himself, master in relation to a servant. But in the matter we are treating, if the essence itself is said relatively, the essence itself is not essence.

There is added to this, that every essence that is said relatively is also something apart from the relative; as a man master, and a man servant, and a horse a beast of burden,[^6] and a coin a pledge; man and horse and coin are said in relation to themselves, and are substances or essences; but master and servant and beast of burden and pledge are said relatively in relation to something.

But if there were not a man, that is, some substance, there would not be one who might be called master relatively: and if a horse were not a certain essence, there would not be what might be called a beast of burden relatively; so if a coin were not some substance, neither could a pledge be said relatively.

Wherefore, if the Father too is not anything in relation to himself, there is altogether no one who might be said relatively in relation to something.[^7]

For it is not as the color of a colored thing is referred to it, and color is not at all said in relation to itself, but is always of something colored; while that of which it is the color, even if, by being called colored, it is referred to the color, yet what is called body is said in relation to itself: in no way is it to be thought that the Father is said nothing in relation to himself, but that whatever is said is said in relation to the Son; while the same Son is said both in relation to himself and in relation to the Father, when he is called a great magnitude and a powerful power, certainly in relation to himself, and the magnitude and power of the great and powerful Father, by which the Father is great and powerful. Not so, then, but each is substance, and each is one substance.

But just as it is absurd to say that a white thing is not whiteness, so it is absurd to say that a wise one is not wisdom; and just as whiteness is said white in relation to itself, so wisdom too is said wise in relation to itself. But the whiteness of a body is not essence; since the body itself is essence, and that its quality: whence also a body is called white from it, for which to be is not the same as to be white. For there form is one thing, and color another; and both are not in themselves, but in some mass, which mass is neither form nor color, but formed and colored. But wisdom is both wise, and is wise by itself. And since whatever soul becomes wise by participation in wisdom, if it again become unwise, yet wisdom remains in itself, nor, when the soul has been changed into folly, is it changed.

It is not so in him who becomes wise from it, as whiteness in a body which is white from it. For when the body has been changed into another color, that whiteness will not remain, but will altogether cease to be. But if the Father too, who begot wisdom, becomes wise from it,[^8] and for him to be is not the same as to be wise, then the Son is his quality, not his offspring, and there will then not be there the highest simplicity. But far be it that it should be so; because there is truly there a supremely simple essence: this, then, is there to be which is to be wise. But if this is there to be which is to be wise, the Father is not wise by that wisdom which he begot; otherwise it is not he who begot it, but it that begot him.

For what else do we say, when we say, for him to be is the same as to be wise, except, he is by that whereby he is wise? Wherefore that which is the cause to him of being wise is itself the cause to him of being: accordingly, if the wisdom which he begot is the cause to him of being wise, it is also the cause to him of being. Which cannot come about except by begetting or making him. But neither begetter nor founder of the Father would anyone in any way call wisdom. For what is more insane?

Therefore the Father himself too is wisdom: and the Son is called the *Wisdom* of the Father in the way that he is called the *Light* of the Father;[^9] that is, so that, as light of light, and both one light, so wisdom of wisdom is understood, and both one wisdom: therefore also one essence; because this is there to be which is to be wise. For what it is for wisdom to be wise, and for power to be able, for eternity to be eternal, for justice to be just, for magnitude to be great; this it is for essence to be itself. And because in that simplicity to be wise is not other than to be, the same there is the wisdom which is the essence.

## The Father and the Son are one essence, yet not together one Word.

**2.** 3. The Father, therefore, and the Son together are one essence, and one magnitude, and one truth, and one wisdom. But the Father and the Son together are not both one Word, because they are not together both one Son. For just as the Son is referred to the Father, and is not said in relation to himself; so the Word too is referred to him whose Word it is, when it is called Word. For he is Son by that whereby he is Word, and Word by that whereby he is Son. Since, therefore, the Father and the Son together are certainly not one Son, it is consequent that the Father and the Son together are not both one Word.

And therefore he is not Word by that whereby he is Wisdom; because Word is said not in relation to himself, but only relatively in relation to him whose Word it is, as the Son in relation to the Father: but Wisdom by that whereby he is essence. And therefore, because there is one essence, there is one Wisdom. But since the Word too is Wisdom, yet not Word by that whereby Wisdom (for Word is understood relatively, Wisdom essentially), let us take it that what is said when Word is said is as if it were said, *born Wisdom*,[^10] so that he is both *Son and Image*.[^11]

And when these two things are said, that is, *born Wisdom*, in one of them, by that which is *born*, both Word, and Image, and Son are understood, and in all these names essence is not shown, because they are said relatively: but in the other, which is "Wisdom," since it is also said in relation to itself (for it is wise by itself), essence too is demonstrated, and that for it to be is to be wise.[^12]

Whence the Father and the Son together are one wisdom, because one essence, and singly wisdom of wisdom, as essence of essence. Wherefore, not because the Father is not the Son, and the Son is not the Father, or that one is unbegotten and that one begotten, are they therefore not one essence: because by these names their relatives are shown. But each together is one wisdom and one essence, where to be is the same as to be wise: but not each together is Word or Son, because to be is not the same as to be Word or to be Son: as we have already sufficiently shown that these are said relatively.

## In the Scriptures Wisdom is shown to be begotten.

**3.** 4. Why, then, is nothing almost ever said in the Scriptures about *Wisdom*, except to show it *begotten* or *created* by God?[^13] Begotten, namely, that *through* which *all things were made*;[^14] but created or made, as in men, when they are converted and illumined to that wisdom which is not created and made, but begotten; for in them there comes about something that may be called their wisdom; or that which the Scriptures foretell or narrate, that *the Word was made flesh and dwelt among us*; for in this way Christ *was made wisdom*,[^15] because he was made man.

Or is it for this reason that wisdom does not speak in those books, or that anything is said of it, except what shows it born of God, or made, although the Father too is wisdom itself, because that wisdom had to be commended and imitated by us, by whose imitation we are formed?[^16] For the Father speaks it, so that it may be his Word; not as a sounding word is uttered from the mouth, or thought before its utterance: for that is completed in spaces of time, but this is eternal, and by illumining it tells us both of itself and of the Father, what must be told to men.

And therefore he says, *no one knows the Son but the Father; and no one knows the Father but the Son, and he to whom the Son shall will to reveal him*: because through the Son the Father reveals, that is, through his Word. For if this word which we utter, temporal and transitory, shows both itself and that of which we speak, how much more the *Word of God through whom all things were made*?[^17]

Which so shows the Father as the Father is: because it too so is, and is that which the Father is, according as it is wisdom and essence. For according as it is Word, it is not that which the Father is: because the Word is not the Father, and Word is said relatively, as Son, which is certainly not the Father.

And therefore *Christ is the power and Wisdom of God*, because of the Father, who is power and Wisdom, he too is power and wisdom, as light of the Father, who is light, and the *fountain of life*[^18] with God the Father, who is certainly the fountain of life. *For with you*, he says, *is the fountain of life, in your light we shall see light*: because, *as the Father has life in himself, so he gave to the Son to have life in himself*; and *he was the true light that enlightens every man coming into this world*, and this light *was the Word with God*; but *the Word also was God*. But *God is light, and in him there is no darkness*: light, however, not corporeal, but spiritual: nor so spiritual that it was made by illumination, as it was said to the apostles, *you are the light of the world*; but the *light that enlightens every man*, that very and supreme wisdom God, of which we now treat.[^19]

The Son, then, is Wisdom of the Father, who is Wisdom, as *light of light*, and *God of God*, so that both the Father singly is light, and the Son singly is light; and the Father singly is God, and the Son singly is God: therefore both the Father singly is Wisdom, and the Son singly is Wisdom. And just as both together are one light, and one God, so both are one Wisdom. But the Son *was made for us wisdom from God, and righteousness, and sanctification*, because in time we are converted to him, that is, from some time, that we may remain with him forever. And he himself from some time *was made flesh, the Word, and dwelt among us*.

## That Wisdom, without an example, is an example to us.

**3.** 5. Therefore, when in the Scriptures anything is pronounced or narrated about wisdom, whether it itself speaks or something is said about it, the Son is chiefly intimated to us. By the example of whose Image let us too not depart from God, because we too are the *image of God*: not indeed equal, since it was made by the Father through the Son, not born of the Father as that one;

and we, because we are enlightened by light; but it, because it is the enlightening light: and therefore it, without an example, is an example to us. For it does not imitate anyone going before it toward the Father, from whom it is never at all separable, because it is the very same as that one from whom it is.

But we, striving, imitate the one remaining, and follow the one standing, and walking in him tend toward him: because he was made for us a temporal way through humility, who is for us an eternal dwelling through divinity.[^20]

For since to the pure intellectual spirits, who have not fallen through pride, he offers an example *in the form of God* and as *equal to God* and God: in order that he might offer the same example of returning even to fallen man, who, on account of the uncleanness of sins and the punishment of mortality, could not see God, *he emptied himself*, not by changing his divinity, but by assuming our changeableness; and *taking the form of a servant, he came* to us *into this world*, who *was in this world*, because *the world was made through him*; that he might be an example to those who look up and see God, an example to those who look down and marvel at man, an example to the sound for remaining, an example to the weak for recovering, an example to the dying for not fearing, an example to the dead for rising again, *himself holding the primacy in all things*. For because man ought to follow toward blessedness none but God, and could not perceive God, by following God made man, he would follow at once both him whom he could perceive and him whom he ought to follow.

Let us, then, love him and cleave to him, with love *poured out in our hearts through the Holy Spirit who has been given to us*. It is no wonder, then, if, on account of the example which the *image equal to the Father*[^21] offers us, that we may be reformed to the image of God, when Scripture speaks of Wisdom, it speaks of the Son, whom we follow by living wisely: although the Father too is wisdom, as he is light and God.

## The Holy Spirit, together with the Father and the Son, one Wisdom.

**3.** 6. The Holy Spirit too, whether he be the supreme love joining both and subjoining us, which is not unworthily said because it is written, *God is love*; how is he not also himself wisdom, since he is light, because *God is light*? Or whether the essence of the Holy Spirit is to be named in another way, singly and properly: since he is God, he is certainly light: and since he is light, he is certainly wisdom. But that the Holy Spirit is God, Scripture cries out in the Apostle, who says, *do you not know that you are the temple of God?* And he straightway added, *and the Spirit of God dwells in you*. For God dwells in his temple.[^22] For the Spirit of God does not dwell in the temple of God as a minister, since in another place he says more evidently: *do you not know that your bodies are the temple within you of the Holy Spirit, whom you have from God, and you are not your own? For you were bought with a great price: glorify, then, God in your body*.

But what is wisdom except spiritual and unchangeable light? For this sun too is light, but it is corporeal; and the spiritual creature is light, but not unchangeable. Light, then, is the Father, light the Son, light the Holy Spirit: but together not three lights, but one Light. And therefore wisdom the Father, wisdom the Son, wisdom the Holy Spirit; and together not three wisdoms, but one Wisdom; and because this is there to be which is to be wise, one essence is the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit. Nor is to be there anything other than to be God: *one God*, then, *the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit*.[^23]

## One essence, three persons.

**4.** 7. And so, for the sake of speaking about unutterable things, that we might in some way utter what we can in no way express, it was said by our Greeks *one essence, three substances*: but by the Latins, *one essence* or *substance*, *three persons*;[^24] because, as we have already said, in our speech, that is, Latin, "essence" is not wont to be understood otherwise than "substance." And provided that what is said be understood at least *in a riddle*,[^25] it was thought good so to say, that something might be said when it was asked "what three" they are, which three the true faith pronounces to be, since it both says the Father is not the Son, and says that the Holy Spirit, who is the *gift of God*,[^26] is neither the Father nor the Son.

When, then, it is asked what three, or "what three persons," we betake ourselves to finding some special or general name by which we may embrace these three, and none occurs to the mind, because the supereminence of the divinity exceeds the capacity of customary speech.[^27] For God is more truly thought than spoken, and more truly is than thought.

For when we say that Jacob is not the same as Abraham, but that Isaac is neither Abraham nor Jacob, we certainly confess that there are three, Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. But when it is asked "what three," we answer "three men," calling them plurally by a special name; but by a general one, if we say "three animals": for man, as the ancients defined him, *is a rational, mortal animal*;[^28] or, as our Scriptures are wont to speak, "three souls," when it is pleasing to call the whole by the better part, that is, by the soul, both the body and the soul, which is the whole man. For so it was said that seventy-five souls went down into Egypt with Jacob, for so many men.[^29]

Likewise, when we say that your horse is not the one that is mine, and that a third belonging to someone else is neither mine nor yours, we confess that there are three: and to one asking "what three," we answer "three horses" by a special name, but by a general one "three animals." Likewise, when we say that an ox is not a horse, but that a dog is neither an ox nor a horse, we say certain three things: and to those inquiring "what three," we do not now by a special name say three horses, or three oxen, or three dogs, because they are not contained under the same species; but by a general one, three animals; or by a higher genus, three substances, or three creatures, or three natures.

But whatever things are enunciated plurally by one special name, can also be enunciated generally by one name. But not all things that are called generally by one name can we also call specially by one name. For three horses, which is a special name, we call also three animals: but a horse and an ox and a dog we call only three animals, or substances, which are general names, and whatever else can be said generally of them; but three horses, or oxen, or dogs, which are special words, these we cannot say. For we enunciate by one name, though plurally, those things that have in common that which is signified by that name. For Abraham and Isaac and Jacob have in common that which is man; and so they are called three men: a horse too and an ox and a dog have in common that which is animal; they are called, therefore, three animals. So three laurels we call also three trees; but a laurel and a myrtle and an olive, only three trees or three substances, or natures: and so three stones, also three bodies; but a stone and wood and iron, only three bodies, or if they can also be called by some higher general name.

The Father, then, and the Son, and the Holy Spirit, since they are three,[^30] let us ask what three they are, what they have in common. For that which is the Father is not common to them, so that they should be fathers to one another; as friends, since they are said relatively in relation to one another, can be called three friends, because they are so to one another. But not so there, because there only the Father is father; nor father of two, but of the only Son. Nor three sons, since the Father there is not the Son, nor the Holy Spirit. Nor three holy spirits, because the Holy Spirit too, in the proper signification by which he is also called the *gift of God*,[^31] is neither Father nor Son. What, then, are the three? For if they are *three persons*, that which is person is common to them.

Therefore this is a special or general name for them, if we regard the custom of speaking. But where there is no diversity of nature, several things are so enunciated generally that they can also be enunciated specially. For difference of nature makes it that a laurel and a myrtle and an olive, or a horse and an ox and a dog, are not called by a special name, these, three laurels; or those, three oxen: but by a general one, both these, three trees; and those, three animals. But here, where there is no diversity of essence, it is fitting that these three have also a special name, which nonetheless is not found. For person is a general name, so much so that even a man can be so called, though there is so great a difference between man and God.

## Scripture nowhere says three persons in God.

**4.** 8. Then, in the general word itself, if we say three persons because that which is person is common to them (otherwise they can in no way be so called, just as they are not called three sons, because that which is son is not common to them); why do we not also say three gods? For certainly, because the Father is a person, and the Son a person, and the Holy Spirit a person, therefore three persons: because, then, the Father is God, and the Son is God, and the Holy Spirit is God, why not three gods? Or, since on account of the ineffable conjunction these three together are one God; why not also one person, so that we cannot say three persons, although we call each single one a person, just as we cannot say three gods, although we call each single one God, whether Father, or Son, or Holy Spirit?

Is it because Scripture does not say three gods? But neither do we find that Scripture anywhere mentions three persons. Is it because Scripture says these three to be neither three nor one person (for we read *the person of the Lord*, not the person the Lord), that therefore it was permitted, by the necessity of speaking and disputing, to say three persons, not because Scripture says it, but because Scripture does not contradict it: whereas, if we said three gods, Scripture would contradict, saying, *hear, O Israel: the Lord your God is one God*?

Why, then, is it not also permitted to say three essences, which similarly Scripture, as it does not say, so neither contradicts? For if essence is a special name common to the three, why should they not be called three essences, as Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob are three men, because man is a special name common to all men? But if essence is not a special but a general name, because man, and beast, and tree, and star, and angel is called essence; why are these not called three essences, as three horses are called three animals, and three laurels are called three trees, and three stones three bodies? Or if, on account of the unity of the Trinity, they are not called three essences, but *one essence*;[^32] why, on account of the same unity of the Trinity, are they not called not three substances or three persons, but one substance and one person? For as the name of essence is common to them, so that each single one is called essence, so common to them is the word substance or person. For what we have said of persons according to our custom, the same must be understood of substances according to the custom of the Greeks. For they say three substances, one essence, in the way that we say three persons, one essence or substance.

## These words were brought forth by the necessity of speaking.

**4.** 9. What, then, remains, except that we confess that these words were brought forth by the necessity of speaking, when there was need of copious disputation against the snares or errors of the heretics?[^33] For when human poverty tried to bring forth in speech to the senses of men what it holds, according to its capacity, in the secret place of the mind concerning the Lord God its Creator, whether through pious faith or through whatever understanding, it feared to say three essences, lest in that supreme equality any diversity be understood.

Again, it could not say that there are not certain three things, which, because Sabellius said it, he fell into heresy.[^34] For it is most certainly known both from the Scriptures what must be piously believed, and is grasped by the gaze of the mind with undoubted perception, that there is both a Father, and a Son, and a Holy Spirit; and that the Son is not the same as the Father, nor the Holy Spirit the same as the Father or the Son.

It sought what to call the three: and it said substances or persons, by which names it wished not diversity to be understood, but singularity to be refused; so that not only may unity there be understood from the fact that one essence is said, but also Trinity from the fact that three substances or persons are said.[^35]

For if for God to be is the same as to subsist, three substances were no more to be said than three essences are said: just as, because for God to be is the same as to be wise, as we do not say three essences, so neither do we say three wisdoms. For thus, because for him to be God is the same as to be, it is not lawful to say either three essences or three gods.

But if for God to be is one thing, to subsist another, as for God to be is one thing, to be Father or to be Lord another; for what he is, is said in relation to himself, but Father is said in relation to the Son and Lord in relation to the serving creature: then he subsists relatively, as he begets relatively and rules relatively. So now substance will not be substance, because it will be a relative.

For just as from that which is to be, essence is named, so from that which is to subsist, we say substance. But it is absurd that substance be said relatively: for every thing subsists in relation to itself; how much more God?

## In God substance is said improperly, essence properly.

**5.** 10. If indeed it is worthy that God be said to subsist: for it is rightly understood of those things in which, as subjects, are those that are said to be in some subject, as color or form in a body. For a body subsists, and therefore is a substance: but those things which are in a subsisting and subject body, which are not substances, but in a substance; and therefore, if either that color or that form cease to be, they do not take from the body its being a body, because for it to be is not the same as to retain this or that form or color. Changeable, then, and not simple things are properly called substances. But God, if he subsists so that he can properly be called substance, there is in him something as in a subject, and he is not simple, for whom to be is the same as whatever else is said of him in relation to himself, as great, almighty, good, and anything of the sort that is not incongruously said of God: but it is impious to say that God subsists under and is subject to his own goodness, and that goodness is not substance or rather essence, nor God himself his own goodness, but it is in him as in a subject: whence it is manifest that God is called substance improperly, so that by a more usual name essence be understood, which is truly and properly said; so that perhaps God alone ought to be called essence. For he alone truly is, because he is unchangeable, and this his name he declared to his servant Moses, when he said, *I am who am*; and, *you shall say to them: 'He who is has sent me to you.'* But whether essence[^36] be said, which is properly said, or substance,[^37] which is said improperly; each is said in relation to himself, not relatively in relation to something.[^38] Whence for God to be is the same as to subsist, and therefore, if the Trinity is one essence, it is also one substance.

## Why in the Trinity one person and three essences are not said.

**6.** 11. Perhaps, then, three persons are more suitably said than three substances.[^39] But lest I should seem to be pleading our own case, let us inquire into this too. Although they too, if they wished, just as they say three substances, τρεῖς ὑποστάσεις, could say three persons, τρία πρόσωπα. But they preferred that other, which perhaps, according to the custom of their language, was more aptly said.

For in persons too the same reasoning holds: for to God to be is not one thing, and to be a person another, but altogether the same. For if to be is said in relation to himself, but person relatively; so we would say three persons, Father and Son and Holy Spirit, in the way that some are called three friends, or three kinsmen, or three neighbors, because they are so in relation to one another, not because each of them is so in relation to himself. Wherefore any one of them is the friend of the other two, or kinsman or neighbor, because these names have a relative signification.

What then? Does it please us to say that the Father is the person of the Son and the Holy Spirit, or the Son the person of the Father and the Holy Spirit, or the Holy Spirit the person of the Father and the Son? But neither is person wont to be so said anywhere, nor, in this Trinity, when we say the person of the Father, do we say anything other than the substance of the Father. Wherefore, just as the substance of the Father is the Father himself, not by that whereby he is Father, but by that whereby he is; so the person of the Father too is nothing other than the Father himself: for person is said in relation to himself, not in relation to the Son or the Holy Spirit; just as in relation to himself he is said God, and great, and good, and just, and anything else of the sort. And just as for him to be is the same as to be God, as to be great, as to be good; so for him to be is the same as to be a person.

Why, then, do we not say these three together one person, as *one essence* and one God, but say *three Persons*, when we do not say three gods or three essences; unless because we wish some one word to serve this signification by which the Trinity is understood, lest we should be altogether silent when asked, what three, when we confessed that there were three? For if essence is a genus, but substance or person a species, as some think, I omit what I have already said, that there ought to be called three essences, as there are called three substances or persons, just as three horses are called, and the same three animals, since horse is a species, animal a genus. For there the species is not said plurally, and the genus singularly, as though it were said three horses one animal: but as three horses by a special name, so three animals by a general name. But if they say that by the name of substance or person not a species is signified, but something singular and individual; so that substance or person is not said as man is said, which is common to all men, but as this man is said, such as Abraham, such as Isaac, such as Jacob, or anyone else who can even be pointed out present with the finger: then the same reasoning will follow upon them too. For just as Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob are called three individuals, so three men, and three souls. Why, then, are not the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit too, if we discuss these matters according to genus and species and individual, so called three essences, as three substances or persons? But this, as I said, I omit.

This I say: if essence is a genus, one essence now has no species; just as, because animal is a genus, one animal now has no species. The Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit are not, then, three species of one essence. But if essence is a species, as man is a species, while those three which we call substances or persons have the same species in common, in the way that Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob have in common the species which is called man; not as man is subdivided into Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, so can one man be subdivided also into several single men: for he altogether cannot, because one man is already a single man. Why, then, is one essence subdivided into three substances or persons? For if essence is a species, as man, then one essence is as one man:

or, as we say that some three men of the same sex, of the same constitution of body, and of the same mind, are one nature; for there are three men, but one nature:[^40] so also there do we say three substances one essence, or three persons one substance or essence? This indeed is in some way similar, because the ancients too who spoke Latin, before they had those names, which it is not long since they came into use, that is, essence or substance, used to say "nature" for these.[^41] We do not, then, say these things according to genus and species; but as it were according to a common and the same matter. As, if three statues were made from the same gold, we would say three statues one gold, yet we would not say gold a genus, and statues species; nor gold a species, and statues individuals. For no species transgresses its individuals, so as to comprehend something outside. For when I have defined what man is, which is a special name, all the single men, which are individuals, are contained in the same definition, nor does anything pertain to it which is not man. But when I have defined gold, not only statues, if they be golden, but rings too, and anything else made of gold, will pertain to gold, and even if nothing be made of it, it is called gold, because, even if they be not golden, they will not on that account not be statues. Likewise no species exceeds the definition of its genus. For when I have defined animal, since horse is a species of this genus, every horse is an animal; but not every statue is gold. Therefore, although in three golden statues we rightly say three statues one gold; yet we do not say it so as to understand gold a genus, but statues species.

Nor, then, do we so call the Trinity three Persons or substances, one essence and one God, as though three things subsisted out of one matter, even if whatever that is, be unfolded in these three. For there is nothing else of that essence besides this Trinity: yet we say three Persons of the same essence, or three Persons one essence: but we do not say three persons out of the same essence, as though there were one thing there which is essence, another which is person; as we can say three statues out of the same gold; for there to be gold is one thing, to be statues another. And when three men are said to be one nature, or three men of the same nature, they can also be said three men out of the same nature, because out of the same nature three other such men can also exist; but in that essence of the Trinity, in no way can any other person whatever exist out of the same essence. Then, in these things, one man is not as much as three men together; and two men are something more than one man: and in equal statues there is more gold in three together than in single ones, and less gold in one than in two. But in God it is not so: for the Father and the Son together are not a greater essence than the Father alone or the Son alone; but those three substances or persons together, if they are so to be called, are equal to the single ones: which *the animal man does not perceive*. For he cannot think except masses and spaces, whether small or great, with phantasms as images of bodies flitting about in his mind.

## Until the animal man is purified, let him believe; man to the image and the image of God.

**6.** 12. Until he is purified from this uncleanness, let him believe in the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit, one God, alone, great, almighty, good, just, merciful, the maker of all things visible and invisible,[^42] and whatever can be said of him worthily and truly according to human capacity. Nor, when he hears that the Father alone is God, let him separate from him the Son or the Holy Spirit:[^43] for with him he is the only God, with whom he is also one God, because, when we hear that the Son too is the only God, we must take it without any separation of the Father or the Holy Spirit. And so let him say one essence, so as not to suppose that one is greater than, or better than, or in any part diverse from, another. Yet not so that the Father himself be the Son and the Holy Spirit, and whatever else the single ones are said in relation to one another; as Word, which is said only of the Son, or Gift, which is said only of the Holy Spirit:[^44] on account of which they also admit the plural number, as it is written in the Gospel: *I and the Father are one*. And he said both *one* and *we are*; *one*, according to essence, because the same God; *we are*, according to relation, because that one is Father, this one Son.

Sometimes the unity of essence is even passed over in silence, and the relatives alone are mentioned plurally: *we will come to him*, I and the Father, *and will make our dwelling with him*. *We will come* and *will make our dwelling* is a plural number, because it was said before, *I and the Father*, that is, the Son and the Father, which are said relatively in relation to one another. Sometimes altogether latently, as in Genesis: *let us make man to our image and likeness*. Both *let us make* and *our* were said plurally, and ought not to be taken except from relatives. For not that gods might make, or to the image and likeness of gods; but that the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit might make, *to the image* of the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit, so that man might subsist as the image of God. But God is Trinity.[^45]

But because that *image of God*[^46] was not made altogether equal, as not born of him but created by him, for the sake of signifying this, it is so an image that it is to the image: that is, it is not equated by parity, but approaches by a certain likeness.[^47] For not by intervals of places, but by likeness one approaches God, and by unlikeness one withdraws from him. For there are those who so distinguish that they wish the Image to be the Son, but man to be not an image, but "to the image."[^48]

But the Apostle refutes them, saying: *a man indeed ought not to veil his head, since he is the image and glory of God*. He did not say, "to the image"; but *image*. Which image, however, when it is said elsewhere, "to the image," is not said as it were "to the Son," which image is equal to the Father;[^49] otherwise he would not say, *to our image*. For how *our*, when the Son is the image of the Father alone?

But on account of the unequal likeness, as we have said, man was said to be to the image: and therefore "our," so that man might be the image of the Trinity; not equal to the Trinity as the Son to the Father, but approaching, as has been said, by a certain likeness; as in distant things a certain nearness is signified, not of place, but of a certain imitation.

For to this end it is also said, *be reformed in the newness of your mind*: to whom likewise he says, *be therefore imitators of God, as most dear sons*. For to the new man it is said: *who is renewed unto the knowledge of God, according to the image of him who created him*.

Or if it now please, on account of the necessity of disputing, even apart from the relative names, to admit the plural number, so that one name may be answered when it is asked, "what three," and to say three substances or three persons; let no masses or intervals be thought of, no distance of however small an unlikeness, where one may be understood to be even a little less than another, in whatever way one can be less than another: so that there may be neither a confusion of the persons, nor such a distinction as that anything be unequal.

But if it cannot be grasped by the understanding, let it be held by faith, until he shine in the hearts[^50] who said through the prophet, *unless you believe, you will not understand*.
