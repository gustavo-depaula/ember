# Book XIV: The Worship of God Is Wisdom

**1.** 1. Now we must discuss wisdom: not that wisdom of God, which beyond doubt is God—for the Wisdom of God is called his only-begotten Son[^1]—but we shall speak of the wisdom of man, true wisdom nevertheless, which is according to God, and is his true and chief worship, which by one name is called in Greek *theosebeia*. This name our own writers, as we have already mentioned, wishing themselves too to render by one name, called "piety," although piety among the Greeks is more usually named *eusebeia*; but *theosebeia*, since it cannot be perfectly rendered by one word, is better rendered by two, so that it is rather called "the worship of God." That this is the wisdom of man—which we have already set down in the twelfth volume of this work[^2]—is shown by the authority of Holy Scripture, in the book of God's servant Job, where it is read that the Wisdom of God said to man, *behold, piety is wisdom; but to abstain from evils is knowledge*[^3];

or even, as some have rendered from the Greek *episteme*, *discipline*—which surely received its name from learning (*discere*), whence it can also be called knowledge. For to this end each thing is learned, that it may be known.[^4] Although, in another notion, in the evils which each one suffers for his sins that he may be corrected, it is wont to be called *discipline*. Whence comes that passage in the Letter to the Hebrews: *for what son is there to whom his father does not give discipline*[^5]? and that more evident one in the same: *for all discipline for the time seems to be not of joy, but of sadness: but afterward it will render the peaceable fruit of justice to those who have contended through it*[^6].

God himself, therefore, is the highest Wisdom; but the worship of God is the wisdom of man, of which we now speak. For *the wisdom of this world is foolishness with God*[^7]. According to this wisdom, then, which is the worship of God,[^8] the Holy Scripture says, *a multitude of the wise is the health of the world*[^9].

## The Philosopher Is a Lover of Wisdom

**1.** 2. But if to discuss wisdom belongs to the wise,[^10] what shall we do? Shall we dare to profess wisdom, lest our discussion of it be impudent? Shall we not be deterred by the example of Pythagoras? who, when he had not dared to profess himself wise, answered rather that he was a philosopher, that is, a lover of wisdom: from whom that name arose, and so pleased posterity thereafter, that however great a doctrine anyone might seem, to himself or to others, to excel in concerning matters pertaining to wisdom, he was called nothing but a philosopher.[^11]

Or was it for this reason that none of such men dared to profess himself wise, that they thought the wise man to be without any sin? But our Scripture does not say this, which says, *rebuke a wise man, and he will love you*[^12]. For surely it judges him to have sin whom it deems to be in need of rebuke. But I, even so, do not dare to profess myself wise: it is enough for me—what even they themselves cannot deny—that it belongs even to a philosopher, that is, to a lover of wisdom, to discuss wisdom. For they did not cease to do this, who professed themselves to be lovers of wisdom rather than wise.

## Knowledge and Wisdom; What the Knowledge of Faith Is

**1.** 3. But discussing wisdom, they defined it, saying: *wisdom is the knowledge of things human and divine*[^13]. Whence I too, in the previous book, did not keep silent that the cognition of both kinds of things, that is, *of divine and human* things, can be called both wisdom and knowledge.[^14] But according to that distinction by which the Apostle said, *to one is given the utterance of wisdom, to another the utterance of knowledge*[^15], this definition is to be divided, so that the knowledge of divine things is properly called wisdom, but that of human things properly holds the name of knowledge: of which I discussed in the thirteenth volume, attributing to this knowledge not, indeed, whatever can be known by man in human things—where there is very much superfluous vanity and harmful curiosity—but only that by which the most wholesome faith, which leads to true blessedness, is begotten, nourished, defended, strengthened: with which knowledge very many of the faithful are not strong, although they are very strong in faith itself.

For it is one thing to know only what a man ought to believe for the sake of attaining the blessed life, which is none other than eternal: but another to know in what way this very thing both aids the pious and is defended against the impious,[^16] which the Apostle seems to call by the proper word "knowledge."[^17] Of which, when I spoke before, I took care especially to commend faith itself, briefly distinguishing eternal things from temporal beforehand, and there discussing temporal things; but deferring eternal things to this book,[^18] I showed that, even concerning eternal things, a temporal faith—dwelling temporally in the hearts of believers—is nevertheless necessary for the attaining of those eternal things themselves.[^19]

I also discussed that faith concerning temporal things—which the Eternal One did and suffered for us in the man whom he bore in time, and brought forward to eternal things—is profitable for the attaining of those same eternal things: and that the virtues themselves, by which one lives prudently, bravely, temperately, and justly in this temporal mortality, unless they are referred to that same faith, temporal though it be, which nevertheless leads to eternal things, are not true virtues.

## A Certain Trinity in Faith, but Not Yet the Image of God

**2.** 4. Wherefore, since, as it is written, *as long as we are in the body, we are away from the Lord; for we walk by faith, not by sight*[^20], surely, as long as *the just man lives by faith*[^21], although he lives according to the inner man,[^22] and though by that same temporal faith he strives and tends toward the eternal truth,[^23] yet in the retaining, the contemplation, the love of that same temporal faith, there is not yet such a trinity as is to be called the image of God: lest it should seem to be set among temporal things, which is to be set among eternal things.

For the human mind, when it sees its own faith, by which it believes what it does not see, sees nothing everlasting. For this will not be forever, which surely will not be when, this pilgrimage being ended in which we are away from the Lord, so that we must needs walk by faith, that sight will succeed by which we shall see *face to face*[^24]: just as now, not seeing, yet because we believe, we shall deserve to see, and we shall rejoice that we have been led through faith to sight.[^25] For it will no longer be faith, by which things not seen are believed; but sight, by which the things that were believed are seen.

Then, therefore, even if we remember this mortal life now passed, and recall by memory that we once believed what we did not see, this faith will be reckoned among things past and gone, not among present things ever remaining: and through this, even that trinity which now consists in the memory, beholding, and love of that same present and remaining faith, will then be found to be passed and gone, not abiding.

From which it is gathered that, if this trinity is already the image of God, then it too must be held to be not among the things that are always, but among things that pass away.

## A Difficulty Is Resolved

**3.** 4. But far be it that, since the nature of the soul is immortal, and from the beginning in which it was created never thereafter ceases to be, that which it has as nothing better should not endure together with its immortality. But what better was created in its nature than that it was made *to the image* of its Creator?[^26] Therefore not in the retaining, the contemplation, the love of faith, which will not be forever, but in that which will be forever, is to be found what ought to be called the image of God.

**3.** 5. Or shall we still search out, somewhat more diligently and more deeply, whether the matter stands thus? For it can be said that this trinity does not perish even when faith itself has passed away: because, just as now we both hold it in memory, and discern it by thought, and love it by will, so also then, when we shall hold in memory that we had it, and shall recall it, and shall join both these by a third will, the same trinity will abide. For if it leaves in us no trace, as it were, in passing, surely we shall have nothing of it in our memory to which we may return, recalling it as past, and joining both these by a third intention—both, namely, what was in the memory while we were not thinking of it, and what is formed from it by thought. But he who says this does not distinguish that there is one trinity now, when we hold, see, love a present faith in us; and another that will be then, when we shall behold by recollection, not faith itself, but as it were an imaginary trace of it stored up in the memory, and shall join these two—that is, what was in the memory of the one retaining it, and what is impressed from it on the gaze of the one recollecting—by a third will.

That this may be understood, let us take an example from corporeal things, of which we spoke enough in the eleventh book.[^27] For surely, ascending from lower things to higher, or entering from outer to inner, we first find a trinity in the body that is seen, and in the gaze of the seer which, when it sees, is formed from it, and in the intention of the will which joins the two.

Let us set up one like this trinity, when the faith which is now in us, just as that body in its place, is so set in our memory, from which the thought of the one recollecting is informed, as the gaze of the beholder is informed from that body: to which two, that the trinity may be completed, is reckoned a third, the will, which connects and joins the faith set in the memory and a certain effigy of it impressed on the beholding of recollection; just as, in that corporeal trinity of vision, the intention of the will joins the form of the body that is seen and the conformation that comes about in the gaze of the one discerning.

Let us, then, suppose that body which was discerned to have perished, fallen away, and that nothing of it remained in any place to which the gaze might return for seeing: surely, because the image of the corporeal thing now passed and gone remains in the memory, from which the look of the one thinking is informed, and both these are joined by a third will, is the same trinity to be called which there was when the appearance of the body set in its place was seen? Surely not, but altogether another: for besides the fact that the former was outside, this is inside; the former, surely, the appearance of a present body made; this, the image of a past one.

So too in this matter of which we now treat, and for the sake of which we thought that example ought to be applied: the faith which is now in our mind, like that body in its place, while it is held, beheld, loved, makes a certain trinity: but it will not be the same one, when this faith will no longer be in the mind, as that body will no longer be in its place.

But that which will be then, when we shall recall that it was in us, but is not, will surely be another. For this one, which is now, the thing itself, present and fixed to the mind of the believer, makes; but that one, which will be then, the imagining of the past thing, left behind in the memory of the one recollecting, will make.

## The Image of God Is to Be Found in the Soul of the Rational Man, Where It Is Implanted Immortally in His Immortality

**4.** 6. Neither, therefore, will that trinity, which now is not, be the image of God; nor is this one the image of God, which then will not be: but it is to be found in the soul of man, that is, the rational or intellectual soul, an image of the Creator which is implanted immortally in its immortality.

For just as the immortality of the soul itself is spoken of according to a certain manner—for the soul too has its own death, when it lacks the blessed life, which is to be called the soul's true[^28] life; but it is named immortal because, with a life of whatever kind, even when it is most wretched, it never ceases to live—so, although reason or intellect is now dormant in it, now appears small, now great, the human soul is never anything but rational and intellectual; and through this, if it was made *to the image of God*[^29] according to this, that it can use reason and intellect to understand and behold God, surely from the beginning in which this so great and wonderful nature began to be, whether this image be so worn away as to be almost none, or obscure and deformed, or clear and beautiful, it always is.

Finally, pitying the deformity of its dignity, the divine Scripture says, *although man walks in an image, yet he is troubled in vain: he heaps up treasure, and knows not for whom he gathers it*[^30]. It would not therefore attribute vanity to the image of God, unless it discerned that it had been made deformed. And that this deformity does not avail so much as to take away the fact that it is an image, it shows well enough by saying, *although man walks in an image*. Wherefore this sentence can be truly pronounced from both sides, so that, as it was said, *although man walks in an image, yet he is troubled in vain*, so it may be said, "although man is troubled in vain, yet he walks in an image." For although it is a great nature, yet it could be vitiated, because it is not the highest: and although it could be vitiated, because it is not the highest, yet because it is capable of the highest nature, and can be a partaker of it, it is a great nature.

Let us, then, seek in this image of God a certain trinity of its own kind, with the help of him who made us to his image.[^31] For not otherwise can we wholesomely investigate these things, and find something according to the wisdom which is from him: but if the things which, in the earlier books, and especially in the tenth, we said about the human soul or mind are either held and recalled by the reader's memory, or reviewed by his diligence in the very places in which they were written down, he will not here require a more lengthy discourse on the inquiry into so great a matter.

**4.** 7. Among other things, then, in the tenth book we said that the mind of man knows itself.[^32] For nothing does the mind so know as that which is present to it: nor is anything more present to the mind than itself to itself. And we brought forward other proofs, as far as seemed sufficient, by which this might be most certainly demonstrated.

## Is the Mind of an Infant Too to Be Believed to Know Itself?

**5.** 7. What, then, is to be said of the mind of an infant, still so little and sunk in so great an ignorance of things, that the mind of a man who knows something shudders at the darkness of that mind? Is it too to be believed to know itself, but, too intent on those things which it has begun to perceive through the body's senses with a delight all the greater the newer it is, able not to be ignorant of itself, but unable to think of itself?

Moreover, with how great an intention it is borne toward those sensible things which are without can be conjectured from this alone, that it is so greedy to drink in this light, that if anyone less cautious, or not knowing what may thence happen, has placed a night-light where the infant lies, in that part toward which the eyes of the one lying can be turned back, and the neck cannot be bent, his gaze is so not removed from it, that we have known some to become even cross-eyed from this, the eyes holding to that form which habit has in a certain way fixed in tender and soft members.

So too toward the other senses of the body, as far as that age allows, the souls of little ones, as it were, confine themselves by intention, so that whatever offends or allures through the flesh, this alone they vehemently abhor or seek: but their own inner things they do not think of, nor can they be admonished to do this; because they do not yet know the signs of the one admonishing, where words hold the chief place, which, like other things, they utterly do not know. But that it is one thing not to know oneself, another not to think of oneself, we have already shown in that same volume.[^33]

**5.** 8. But let us pass over this age, which cannot be asked what goes on within it, and of which we ourselves have greatly forgotten. Let it suffice that we are certain of this alone: that when a man has been able to think of the nature of his own mind, and to find what is true, he will find it nowhere but with his own self. But he will find, not what he did not know, but that of which he was not thinking. For what do we know, if we do not know what is in our own mind, since everything we know we cannot know except by the mind?

## In the Mind Thinking of Itself a Certain Trinity Comes to Be

**6.** 8. So great, nevertheless, is the power of thought, that not even the mind itself in a certain way sets itself in its own sight, except when it thinks of itself: and through this, so far is nothing in the sight of the mind except that of which it thinks, that not even the mind itself, by which is thought whatever is thought, can be otherwise in its own sight, except by thinking of itself.

But how, when it does not think of itself, it is not in its own sight, since it can never be without itself, as though it were one thing and its sight another, I cannot find. For this is not absurdly said of the eye of the body: for the eye itself is fixed in its place in the body, but its gaze is directed to the things that are outside, and is extended even to the stars. Nor is the eye in its own sight; since it does not behold itself, except by a mirror set before it, of which we have already spoken:[^34] which surely does not happen when the mind sets itself in its own sight by thinking of itself. Does the mind, then, see another part of itself by another part of itself, when it beholds itself by thinking, as by other members of ours, which are the eyes, we behold other members of ours, which can be in our sight? What more absurd can be said or thought? Whence, then, is the mind taken away, except from itself? And where is it set in its own sight, except before itself?

It will not, therefore, be there where it was, when it was not in its own sight; because, set here, it was taken away from there. But if it has migrated to be beheld, where will it abide to behold? Or is it as it were doubled, so that it is both there and here, that is, both where it can behold and where it can be beheld; so that in itself it is beholding, before itself beheld? None of these things does truth, when consulted, answer us: since when we think in this way, we think only of fictitious images of bodies, which the mind is not—a thing most certain to the few minds by which truth can be consulted about this matter.

Accordingly, it remains that something pertaining to its nature is its sight, and into it, when it thinks of itself, it is recalled, not as it were through an interval of place, but by an incorporeal turning:[^35] but when it does not think of itself, it is indeed not in its own sight, nor is its look formed from it, but yet it knows itself as though it were itself a memory of itself.

Just as a man skilled in many disciplines—the things he knows are contained in his memory, nor is there anything thence in the sight of his mind, except that of which he thinks; the rest are stored up in a certain hidden acquaintance, which is called memory. Therefore we commended the trinity in such a way as to set in the memory that from which the look of the one thinking is formed; but the conformation itself, as it were an image which is impressed from it; and that by which both are joined, love or will.

The mind, therefore, when by thought it beholds itself, understands and recognizes itself: it begets, then, this understanding and cognition of itself. For an incorporeal thing is beheld when understood, and is known by being understood. Nor indeed does the mind so beget this acquaintance with itself, when by thinking it beholds itself understood, as though it had before been unknown to itself: but it was so known to itself as those things are known which are contained in the memory, even if they are not thought of: since we say that a man knows letters, even when he is thinking of other things, not of letters. But these two, the begetter and the begotten, are coupled by love as a third, which is nothing other than the will seeking or holding something to be enjoyed. And therefore we thought the trinity of the mind ought to be conveyed by those three names too: memory, understanding, will.[^36]

## It Is One Thing to Know Oneself, Another to Think of Oneself: the Mind Always Remembers Itself, Always Knows and Loves Itself

**6.** 9. But since, near the end of that same tenth book, we said that the mind always remembers itself, and always understands and loves itself, although it does not always think of itself as distinct from those things which are not what it is:[^37] it must be asked in what way understanding pertains to thought; but the acquaintance with each thing, which is in the mind even when one does not think of it, is said to pertain to memory alone. For if this is so, it did not have these three—to remember itself, to understand itself, and to love itself—but only remembered itself, and afterward, when it began to think of itself, then it understood and loved itself.

**7.** 9. Wherefore let us consider more diligently that example which we applied, where it was shown that it is one thing not to know any given thing, another not to think of it; and that it can happen that a man knows something of which he is not thinking, when he thinks of something else, not of it. He who is skilled, then, in two or more disciplines, when he thinks of one, even if he does not think of the other or the others, yet knows them.

But can we rightly say, "this musician indeed knows music, but now he does not understand it, because he does not think of it; he understands now geometry, for this he now thinks of"? That sentence is, so far as appears, absurd. What too of this, if we say, "this musician indeed knows music, but now he does not love it, since he does not think of it; he loves now geometry, since he now thinks of it"? Is it not similarly absurd?

But most rightly we say: "this man whom you observe disputing about geometry is also a finished musician; for he both remembers that discipline, and understands it, and loves it: but although he knows and loves it, he does not now think of it, since he thinks of geometry, of which he disputes."

Hence we are admonished that there are in the secret place of the mind certain acquaintances with certain things, and that these in a certain way come forth into the open, and are set, as it were more openly, in the sight of the mind, when they are thought of: for then the mind finds that it both remembers, and understands, and loves itself, even in that of which it was not thinking, when it was thinking of something else. But that of which we have not thought for a long time, and of which we cannot think unless reminded, this, in some same wonderful way, if it can be said, we do not know that we know.

In short, rightly is it said by the one who reminds, to the one whom he reminds, "you know this, but you do not know that you know it; I will remind you, and you will find yourself knowing what you had thought you did not know."[^38]

This too the writings do, which have been composed about those things which the reader, with reason as guide, finds to be true: not those which he believes to be true for the one who wrote them, as a history is read; but those which he himself too finds to be true, whether within himself, or in the very truth that guides the mind.[^39]

But he who, even when reminded, is unable to behold these things, is sunk more deeply, by a great blindness of heart, in the darkness of ignorance, and needs a more wonderful divine help, that he may be able to come to true wisdom.

**7.** 10. For this reason, then, I wished to bring forward some proof concerning thought, by which it could be shown how, from those things contained in the memory, the gaze of the one recollecting is informed, and how something is begotten, when a man thinks, like that which was in him when, before thought, he remembered: because that is more easily discerned which comes in time, and where the parent precedes the offspring by an interval of time.

For if we refer ourselves to the inner memory of the mind, by which it remembers itself, and the inner understanding by which it understands itself, and the inner will by which it loves itself, where these three are always together, and always have been together from the time when they began to be, whether they were thought of or not thought of, the image of that Trinity will indeed seem to pertain to memory alone: but because there a word cannot be without thought (for we think everything we say, even by that inner word which belongs to the language of no nation), this image is rather recognized in those three, namely, memory, understanding, will. But I now call understanding that by which we understand when thinking, that is, when, those things being found which were present to the memory but were not thought of, our thought is formed; and I call will, or love, or affection, that which joins this offspring and parent, and is in a certain way common to both.

Hence it came about that, even through outward sensible things which are seen through the eyes of the flesh, I led on the slowness of readers, namely, in the eleventh book;[^40] and thence, when I entered with them into that power of the inner man by which it reasons about temporal things, distinguishing that principally dominant power by which it contemplates eternal things: and this I did in two volumes, in the twelfth distinguishing the two, of which the one is higher, the other lower, which ought to be subject to the higher; but in the thirteenth I discussed, with as much truth and brevity as I could, the function of the lower, in which is contained the wholesome knowledge of human things, that in this temporal life we may do that by which we attain the eternal: since a matter so manifold and copious, celebrated by many great disputations of many great men, I enclosed compactly in one volume, showing in it too a trinity, but not yet one to be called the image of God.

## The Image of God Is to Be Sought in the Principal Part of the Human Mind. The Mind Is the Image of God by the Very Fact That It Is Capable of Him

**8.** 11. But now we have come at last to that discussion where we have undertaken to consider the principal part of the human mind, by which it knows God or can know him, that in it we may find the image of God.[^41] For although the human mind is not of that nature of which God is, yet the image of his nature—than which nature none is better—is to be sought and found in us in that by which even our nature has nothing better. But the mind is first to be considered in itself, before it is a partaker of God, and in it his image is to be found. For we said[^42] that, even when, the participation in God being lost, it is worn away and deformed, it nevertheless remains the image of God. For by the very fact that it is capable of him, and can be a partaker of him, it is his image; which so great a good it cannot be except through this, that it is his image.

Behold, then, the mind remembers itself, understands itself, loves itself: if we discern this, we discern a trinity; not yet God indeed, but already the image of God. The memory did not receive from without what it should hold, nor did the understanding find without what it should look upon, as the eye of the body does; nor did the will join from without these two—as it were the form of the body and that which is made from it in the gaze of the beholder; nor did thought, when it was turned to it, find the image of a thing seen without, as it were snatched and stored in the memory, and from it was the look of the one recollecting informed, with a third will joining the two; as we were showing happens in those trinities which were found in corporeal things, or were in a certain way drawn inward from bodies through the sense of the body, concerning all of which we discoursed in the eleventh book:[^43]

nor as it was happening or appearing, when we were discoursing about that knowledge, now set among the riches[^44] of the inner man, which had to be distinguished from wisdom; whence the things that are known are, as it were, adventitious in the mind—either brought in by historical cognition, as are deeds and sayings, which are carried out in time and pass away, or are set in their own places and regions in the nature of things; or in the man himself things arise which were not there, either by others teaching or by his own thoughts, such as faith, which we greatly commended in the thirteenth book;[^45] such as the virtues, by which, if they are true, one lives well in this mortality to this end, that one may live blessedly in that immortality which is divinely promised.

These and such things have their own order in time, in which the trinity of memory, vision, and love appeared to us more easily. For some of them precede the cognition of those learning them. For they are knowable, and even before they are known, they beget a cognition of themselves in those who learn them. But they are either in their own places, or are things that have passed away in time: although the things that have passed away are not themselves, but certain signs of those past things, by seeing or hearing which they are known to have been and to have passed by. Which signs are either set in places, like the monuments of the dead, and whatever is similar; or in writings worthy of faith, as is all history of grave and approvable authority; or in the minds of those who already know them; for, already known to these, they are surely knowable to others as well, whose knowledge they have preceded, and who can know them, with those to whom they are known teaching them.

All which things, when they are learned too, make a certain trinity: by their own appearance, which was knowable even before it was known, and the cognition of the learner joined to it, which then begins to be when it is learned, and a third, the will, which joins the two. And when they have been known, another trinity, while they are recalled, comes to be now within, in the mind itself, from those images which were impressed on the memory when they were learned, and from the informing of thought, the gaze of the one recollecting being turned to them, and from the will which joins these two as a third.

But those things which arise in the mind where they were not, such as faith and the rest of this kind, even if they seem adventitious when they are implanted by teaching, are nevertheless not set without or carried out without, like those things which are believed; but they began to be wholly within, in the mind itself. For faith is not that which is believed, but that by which it is believed: and the former is believed, the latter is beheld.

Yet because it began to be in the mind, which was already a mind before this began to be in it, it seems something adventitious, and will be held among things past, when, sight succeeding, it shall have ceased to be: and now it makes one trinity by its presence, being retained, beheld, loved; another it will then make through a certain trace of itself, which in passing it will leave behind in the memory, as has already been said above.[^46]

## Will the Virtues Cease to Be When They Have Led Us to Eternal Things?

**9.** 12. But whether then even the virtues, by which one lives well in this mortality—since they too begin to be in the mind, which, when it was previously without them, was nevertheless a mind—will cease to be when they have led to eternal things, is some question. For to some it has seemed that they will cease: and concerning three of them indeed, prudence, fortitude, temperance, when this is said, something not nothing seems to be said; but justice is immortal, and will then be more perfected in us than it will cease to be.[^47] Yet concerning all four "the great author of eloquence, Tully"[^48] says, disputing in the dialogue *Hortensius*:

*If it were permitted us, when we have departed from this life, to pass an immortal age in the isles of the blessed, as the fables tell, what need would there be of eloquence, since no trials would take place; or even of the virtues themselves? For we would not need fortitude, with no toil or danger set before us; nor justice, since there would be nothing belonging to another that might be coveted; nor temperance, which governs the lusts that would be none; nor indeed would we need prudence, with no choice set before us of goods and evils*[^49].

*We would therefore be blessed by the one cognition and knowledge of nature, by which alone even the life of the gods is praiseworthy. From which it can be understood that the rest are of necessity, this one alone of the will*[^50].

So that so great an orator, when he was extolling philosophy, recalling the things he had received from the philosophers, and setting them forth splendidly and pleasantly, said that all four virtues are necessary only in this life, which we see to be full of hardships and errors: but none of them, when we shall have departed from this life, if it be permitted to live there where one lives blessedly; but that good minds are blessed by cognition and knowledge alone, that is, by the contemplation of that nature than which nothing is better or more lovable; that is the nature which created all the other natures and established them. To be subject to which, ruling, if it belongs to justice, then justice is altogether immortal: nor will it cease to be in that blessedness, but will be such and so great that it cannot be more perfect or greater.

Perhaps the other three virtues too—prudence without any danger now of error, fortitude without the trouble of evils to be endured, temperance without the resistance of lusts—will be in that happiness: so that it belongs to prudence to set no good before God or equal to him; to fortitude, to cleave to him most firmly; to temperance, to be delighted by no harmful defect. But now what justice does in coming to the aid of the wretched, what prudence does in guarding against snares, what fortitude does in enduring troubles, what temperance does in restraining depraved delights, will not be there, where there will be no evil at all. And through this, those works of the virtues, which are necessary to this mortal life, like the faith to which they are to be referred, will be held among things past: and now they make one trinity, when we hold, look upon, love them as present; another they will then make, when we shall find that they are not, but were, through certain traces of them which in passing they will leave behind in the memory; because then too there will be a trinity, when that trace of whatever kind will be both retained by memory, and truly recognized, and these two joined by a third will.

## The Trinity of the Mind Is Not Adventitious

**10.** 13. In the knowledge of all those temporal things which we have mentioned, certain knowable things precede cognition by an interval of time; such as those sensible things which already were among things before they were known; or all those things which are known through history: but certain things begin to be at the same time; as, for instance, if something visible, which was not at all, should arise before our eyes, it surely does not precede our cognition; or if something sounds where a hearer is present, surely both the sound and the hearing of it begin to be at the same time, and at the same time cease. Nevertheless, whether preceding in time or beginning to be at the same time, knowable things beget cognition, are not begotten by cognition. But cognition having been made, when the things we have known, set in the memory, are revisited by recollection, who does not see that the retention in the memory is prior in time to the vision in recollection, and the joining of these two by a third will?

But in the mind it is not so: for neither is it adventitious to itself, as though to itself, which already was, the same self, which was not, had come from elsewhere; or had not come from elsewhere, but in itself, which already was, had been born that same self which was not; as in the mind which already was, faith which was not arises:

or as if, after the cognition of itself, by recollecting it sees itself as it were set in its own memory, as though it had not been there before it knew itself; when surely, from the time it began to be, it has never ceased to remember itself, never to understand itself, never to love itself, as we have already shown.

And through this, when it is turned to itself by thought, a trinity comes to be, in which now even a word can be understood: for it is formed from that thought itself, the will joining the two. There, then, the image we seek is rather to be recognized.

## Is There a Memory Also of Present Things?

**11.** 14. But someone will say: "this is not memory, by which the mind is said to remember itself, which is always present to itself. For memory is of past things, not of present ones": for some, when they treated of the virtues—among whom is also Tully[^51]—divided prudence into these three: memory, understanding, foresight; attributing memory, namely, to past things, understanding to present, foresight to future things, which they do not have as certain unless they foreknow the future; which is not the gift of men, unless it be given from above, as to prophets. Whence Scripture, speaking of men in the book of Wisdom, says: *the thoughts of mortals are timid, and our foresight uncertain*[^52]. But memory is certain of past things, and understanding of present things, but of present incorporeal things, surely: for corporeal things, of corporeal things, are present to the gaze of the eyes.

But let him who says that memory is not of present things attend to how it was said in those very secular writings, where there was greater care for the correctness of words than for the truth of things: *nor did Ulysses, suffering such things, the Ithacan forget himself in so great a peril*[^53].

For when Virgil said that Ulysses did not forget himself, what else did he wish to be understood than that he remembered himself? Since, then, he was present to himself, he could in no way have remembered himself, unless memory pertained to present things. Wherefore, just as in past things that is called memory by which it comes about that they can be recalled and remembered, so in a present thing, which the mind is to itself, that may without absurdity be called memory by which it is present to itself, so that by its own thought it can be understood, and both joined by love of itself.

## The Trinity of the Mind Is the Image of God Because It Can Also Remember, Understand, and Love God; Which When It Does, It Becomes Itself Wise

**12.** 15. This trinity of the mind, then, is not the image of God for this reason, that the mind remembers itself, and understands and loves itself: but because it can also remember, and understand, and love him by whom it was made. And when it does this, it becomes itself wise. But if it does not, even when it remembers, and understands and loves itself, it is foolish. Let it then remember its God, to whose image it was made,[^54] and understand and love him. To say this more briefly: let it worship the uncreated God, of whom it was made capable by him, and of whom it can be a partaker; on account of which it is written: *behold, the worship of God is wisdom*[^55]: and it will be wise not by its own light, but by participation in that highest light, and where there are eternal things, there it will reign blessed. For this wisdom of man is so called that it is also God's. For then it is true: for if it is human, it is vain. But not so God's, by which God is wise. For he is not wise by participation in himself, as the mind by participation in God.

But just as there is said to be also a justice of God—not only that by which he himself is just, but that which he gives to man when he justifies the ungodly, commending which the Apostle says of some, *for being ignorant of the justice of God, and wishing to establish their own justice, they were not subject to the justice of God*[^56]: so it can be said also of some: "being ignorant of the wisdom of God, and wishing to establish their own, they were not subject to the wisdom of God."

**12.** 16. There is, then, an uncreated nature, which made all the other natures, great and small, without doubt more excellent than those it made, and through this more excellent also than this one of which we speak, the rational and intellectual nature, which is the mind of man, made to the image of him who made it. But that nature more excellent than the rest is God. And indeed *not set far from each one of us*, as the Apostle says; adding, *for in him we live, and move, and are*[^57]. And if he said this according to the body, it could be understood even of this corporeal world. For in it too, according to the body, we live, move, and are.

Whence, according to the mind which was made to his image, this ought to be received in a certain more excellent, and that not visible but intelligible, manner. For what is not in him, of whom it is divinely written, *since from him, and through him, and in him are all things*[^58]? Accordingly, if in him are all things, in what at last can the things that live, live, and the things that move, move, except in him in whom they are?[^59] Yet not all are with him in that manner in which it was said to him, *I am always with you*[^60]. Nor is he with all in that manner in which we say, "the Lord be with you."[^61] Great, therefore, is the misery of man not to be with him without whom he cannot be. For in him in whom he is, beyond doubt he is not without him: and yet, if he does not remember him, and does not understand and love him, he is not with him. But that which anyone utterly forgets, he surely cannot even be reminded of.

## How the Mind Can Forget and Remember God

**13.** 17. From visible things let us take an example for this matter. Someone whom you do not recognize says to you, "you know me"; and to remind you, he says where, when, how he became known to you: and, with all the signs applied by which you may be recalled to memory, if you do not recognize him, you have so forgotten that all that acquaintance has been utterly erased from your mind; and nothing else remains except that you believe him who says this to you, that you once knew him; or not even this, if the one who speaks does not seem to you to be worthy of faith. But if you remember, surely you return into your own memory, and in it you find what had not been utterly erased by oblivion.

Let us return to that for the sake of which we applied the example of human society. Among other things the ninth Psalm says: *let the sinners be turned into hell, all the nations that forget God*[^62]. But the twenty-first[^63] says: *all the ends of the earth shall remember, and shall be turned to the Lord*[^64]. These nations, therefore, had not so forgotten God as not to remember him even when reminded. But by forgetting God, as it were by forgetting their own life, they had been turned into death, that is, into hell. But, reminded, they are turned to the Lord, as it were coming back to life by remembering their own life, of which oblivion had hold. Likewise it is read in the ninety-third: *understand now, you who are unwise among the people; and you fools, be wise at last. He who planted the ear, shall he not hear*[^65]? and the rest. For it was said to those who, by not understanding God, said vain things about him.

## The Mind Cannot Rightly Love Itself Except by Loving God

**14.** 18. But of the love of God more testimonies are found in the divine eloquences. For there those two things too are consequently understood, that no one loves him whom he does not remember, and what he utterly does not know. Whence comes that most well-known and chief precept: *you shall love the Lord your God*[^66].

The human mind, then, was so founded that it never does not remember itself, never does not understand itself, never does not love itself. But since he who hates someone is eager to harm him, not undeservedly is the mind of man, when it harms itself, said to hate itself. For unknowingly it wills evil to itself, while it does not think that what it wills is against itself: but yet it wills evil to itself, when it wills that which is against itself; whence it is written, *he who loves iniquity hates his own soul*[^67].

He, then, who knows how to love himself, loves God:[^68] but he who does not love God, even if he loves himself—which is naturally implanted in him—yet is not unfittingly said to hate himself, when he does that which is against himself, and pursues his own self as though he were his own enemy. Which is surely a horrendous error: that, although all wish to profit themselves, many do nothing but what is most pernicious to them. When the poet described a like disease of the dumb animals, he said: *the gods grant better things to the pious, and that error to the enemy! With bared teeth they tore their own torn limbs*[^69].

Since that was a disease of the body, why did he call it an error, except that, since every animal is so reconciled to itself by nature that it guards itself as much as it can, that disease was such that they tore their own members, whose health they were seeking?[^70]

But when the mind loves God, and, as was said, consequently remembers him, and understands him, it is rightly enjoined concerning its neighbor to love him as itself. For now it loves itself not perversely, but rightly, when it loves God, by participation in whom that image not only is, but also is renewed from its oldness, reformed from its deformity, made blessed from its unhappiness. For although it so loves itself that, if one or the other were set before it, it would rather lose all the things it loves below itself than perish: yet, by deserting the higher one—to whom alone it could keep its strength, and enjoy its own light, the one to whom it is sung in the Psalm, *I will keep my strength to you*[^71]; and in another, *draw near to him, and be enlightened*[^72]—it has been made so weak and dark that it slips, even from itself, more unhappily into the things that are not what it is, and over which it is itself higher, through loves which it cannot overcome, and errors from which it does not see by what way to return.[^73]

Whence now, with God showing mercy, repenting it cries out in the Psalms: *my strength has deserted me, and the light of my eyes, it too is not with me*[^74].

## If the Mind Deserts God, It Becomes Weak and Dark; Yet the Image Remains

**14.** 19. Yet amid these so great evils of weakness and error it could not lose the natural memory, understanding, and love of itself: on account of which it could deservedly be said what I mentioned above:[^75] *although man walks in an image, yet he is troubled in vain. He heaps up treasure, and knows not for whom he gathers it*[^76]. For why does he heap up treasure, except that his strength *has deserted him*,[^77] by which, having God, he would lack nothing? And why does he *not know for whom he gathers it*, except that the light of his eyes is not with him? And therefore he does not see what the Truth says: *fool, this night they require your soul of you; these things which you have prepared, whose shall they be*[^78]?

But nevertheless, since even such a man walks in an image, and the mind of man has memory, and understanding, and love of itself: if it were made plain to him that he could not have both, and he were permitted to choose one of two, about to lose the other—either the treasures he has gathered, or the mind—who is so far without a mind as to prefer to have the treasures rather than the mind? For treasures can often overturn the mind: but the mind which is not overturned by treasures can live more easily and freely without any treasures. But who will be able to possess any treasures except through the mind? For if an infant child, though born most wealthy, since he is the owner of all that is his by right, possesses nothing while his mind is dormant; in what way, finally, will anyone possess anything with his mind lost? But why do I speak of treasures, since, if such a choice were set before him, any man would rather lack them than his mind; while no one prefers them to, no one compares them with, the lights of the body, by which not, like gold, only this or that rare man, but every man possesses the sky—for through the lights of the body each one possesses whatever he gladly sees?

Who, then, if he could not keep both, and were forced to lose one or the other, would not rather lose his treasures than his eyes? And yet if he were asked, on a like condition, whether he would rather lose his eyes or his mind, who would not see with his mind that he would rather lose his eyes than his mind? For a mind without the eyes of the flesh is human, but the eyes of the flesh without a mind are bestial. And who would not rather be a man, even blind in the flesh, than a beast that sees?

**14.** 20. I have said these things that even the slower ones, into whose eyes or ears these writings shall come, might be admonished by me, however briefly, how much the mind loves itself even when weak and erring, by loving and pursuing evilly the things that are below itself. But it could not love itself, if it were wholly ignorant of itself, that is, if it did not remember itself; nor would it understand itself: in which image of God in itself it is so powerful that it can cleave to him whose image it is. For it has been so ordered, by the order of natures, not of places, that above it there is none but he. Finally, when it has cleaved wholly to him, it will be one spirit: to which the Apostle bears witness, saying, *but he who cleaves to the Lord is one spirit*[^79]: this mind, indeed, coming to a participation in his nature, truth, and blessedness, yet he not growing in his own nature, truth, and blessedness. In that nature, then, when it has happily cleaved, it will live immutably, and will see immutable everything it sees. Then, as the divine Scripture promises it, its desire will be satisfied with good things,[^80] with immutable good things, with the very Trinity, its God, whose image it is: and lest it ever thereafter be violated, it will be in the hidden place of his face,[^81] filled with so great an abundance of him[^82] that it will never delight to sin. But now, when it sees itself, it sees nothing immutable.

## Even the Sinner Is Enlightened by the Light of Justice

**15.** 21. This, certainly, it does not doubt, since it is wretched, and desires to be blessed: nor does it hope that this can come about for any other reason than that it is mutable. For if it were not mutable, just as it could not become wretched from blessed, so it could not become blessed from wretched. And what would have made it wretched, under an omnipotent and good Lord, except its own sin and the justice of its Lord? And what will make it blessed, except its own merit and the reward of its Lord? But even its merit is the grace of him whose reward will be its blessedness.[^83] For it cannot give itself the justice which, lost, it does not have. For this it received when man was founded; and by sinning, surely, it lost it. It receives, then, justice, on account of which it may deserve to receive blessedness. Whence it is truly said to it by the Apostle, as it were beginning to be proud of its own good: *for what do you have that you did not receive? But if you received it, why do you glory as though you had not received it*[^84]?

But when it well remembers its Lord, his Spirit being received, it perceives wholly—for this it learns by an inmost teaching—that it can rise only by his gratuitous working,[^85] and that it could fall only by its own voluntary defect. It does not, indeed, remember its blessedness: for that was and is not, and it has utterly forgotten it; and therefore it cannot even be reminded of it. But it believes about it on the strength of the writings of its God, worthy of faith, composed by his prophets,[^86] who tell of the happiness of paradise, and indicate by historical tradition that first good and evil of man. But of the Lord its God it remembers.[^87] For he always is, and was not and is not, nor is and was not: but as he will never not be, so he never was not. *And he is wholly everywhere*:[^88] on account of which it both lives, and moves, and is in him;[^89] and therefore it can remember him.

Not that it recalls this, that it knew him in Adam, or somewhere else before the life of this body, or when it was first made to be implanted in this body: for it remembers none of these things at all; whatever any of these is, has been erased by oblivion. But it is reminded, that it may be turned to the Lord,[^90] as to that light by which, even when it was turned away from him, it was in a certain way touched. For hence it is that even the impious think of eternity, and rightly blame and rightly praise many things in the manners of men.[^91]

By what rules, then, do they judge, except those in which they see how each one ought to live, even if they themselves do not live in the same way? Where do they see them? For not in their own nature, since these things are surely seen by the mind, and it is agreed that their minds are mutable, but whoever has been able to see this too in them sees these rules to be immutable; nor in the habit of their own mind, since those rules are rules of justice, but it is agreed that their minds are unjust.

Where, then, are these rules written, where the unjust man recognizes what is just, where he discerns that he ought to have what he himself does not have? Where, then, are they written, except in the book of that light which is called truth? whence every just law is copied, and is transferred into the heart of the man who works justice, not by migrating, but as it were by being impressed; as the image passes from a ring even into wax, and yet does not leave the ring?[^92]

But he who does not work justice, and yet sees what is to be done, he is the one who is turned away from that light, by which nevertheless he is touched. But he who does not even see how he ought to live, sins indeed more excusably, because he is not a transgressor of a law unknown to him:[^93] but even he is sometimes touched by the splendor of the everywhere-present truth, when, admonished, he confesses.[^94]

## How the Image of God Is Reformed in Man

**16.** 22. But those who, reminded, are turned to the Lord from that deformity by which, through worldly desires, they were conformed to this age, are reformed from it, hearing the Apostle saying, *do not be conformed to this age, but be reformed in the newness of your mind*[^95]: that that image may begin to be reformed by him by whom it was formed. For it cannot reform itself, as it could deform itself. He says also elsewhere, *be renewed in the spirit of your mind, and put on the new man, who was created according to God in the justice and holiness of truth*[^96]. What he says, *created according to God*, is said in another place, *to the image of God*.[^97] But by sinning, it lost the justice and holiness of truth; on account of which this image was made deformed and discolored: this it recovers when it is reformed and renewed.

But by saying, *the spirit of your mind*,[^98] he did not wish two things to be understood there, as though the mind were one thing, and the spirit of the mind another: but because every mind is a spirit, but not every spirit is a mind.[^99] For God too is Spirit,[^100] who cannot be renewed, because he cannot grow old.

Spirit is also said of something in man which is not the mind, to which belong the imaginings like bodies: of which he speaks to the Corinthians, where he says, *but if I pray in a tongue, my spirit prays, but my mind is unfruitful*[^101]. For he says this when what is said is not understood: because it cannot even be said, unless images of corporeal voices precede, by the thought of the spirit, the sound of the mouth.

The soul of man too is called spirit: whence it is in the Gospel, *and bowing his head, he gave up the spirit*[^102]; by which the death of the body was signified, the soul going out.

Spirit is said also of a beast, which is most plainly written in the book of *Ecclesiastes* of Solomon, where he says: *who knows the spirit of the sons of men, whether it goes upward, and the spirit of the beast, whether it goes downward into the earth*[^103]? It is also written in *Genesis*, where it says that by the flood all flesh died *which had in it the spirit of life*[^104].

Spirit is said also of the wind, a most plainly corporeal thing: whence comes that passage in the Psalms: *fire, hail, snow, ice, the spirit of the tempest*[^105].

Since, then, spirit is said in so many ways, by *the spirit of the mind* he wished to mean that spirit which is called the mind. Just as the same Apostle also says: *in the stripping off of the body of the flesh*[^106]. He did not, surely, wish two things to be understood, as though the flesh were one thing, the body of the flesh another: but because "body" is the name of many things, none of which is flesh (for there are many bodies besides flesh, both celestial bodies and terrestrial bodies), he said "the body of the flesh," the body which is flesh. So, then, he said "the spirit of the mind," that spirit which is the mind.

Elsewhere too he even more openly named the image, namely, enjoining the same thing in other words: *stripping off*, he says, *the old man with his deeds, put on the new man, who is renewed in the knowledge of God according to the image of him who created him*[^107]. What, then, is read there, *put on the new man, who was created according to God*; is, in this place: *put on the new man, who is renewed according to the image of him who created him*.[^108] But there he says, *according to God*; here, *according to the image of him who created him*. And for what he there set down, *in the justice and holiness of truth*; he set down here, *in the knowledge of God*. This renewal and reformation of the mind, then, comes about according to God, or according to the image of God.

But it is said *according to God*, lest it be thought to come about according to some other creature; and *according to the image* of God, that this renewal may be understood to come about in that thing where the image of God is, that is, in the mind. Just as we say, "dead according to the body," not according to the spirit, of one who, faithful and just, departs from the body. For what do we mean by "dead according to the body," except dead in body or in the body, not in soul or in the soul? Or if we say: "he is handsome according to the body"; or: "brave according to the body, not according to the mind"; what is this other than: "he is handsome or brave in body, not in mind"? And we speak thus in countless cases. Let us not, then, so understand *according to the image of him who created him*, as though the image according to which it is renewed were one thing, and not the very thing which is renewed.

## How the Image of God Is Renewed, by Daily Increments

**17.** 23. Surely this renewal does not come about in the one moment of conversion itself, as that renewal in Baptism comes about in one moment by the remission of all sins:[^109] for there does not remain even one thing, however small, that is not remitted. But just as it is one thing to be free of fevers, another to recover from the weakness which the fevers have caused; and likewise it is one thing to draw out a weapon fixed in the body, another to heal by a second treatment the wound which it made: so the first treatment is to remove the cause of the languor, which comes about through the forgiveness of all sins; the second is to heal the languor itself, which comes about by advancing little by little in the renewal of this image: which two things are shown in the Psalm, where it is read, *who is merciful to all your iniquities*; which comes about in Baptism; then follows, *who heals all your languors*[^110]; which comes about by daily increments, when this image is renewed. Of which matter the Apostle has spoken most openly, saying: *and though our outer man is corrupted, yet the inner is renewed from day to day*[^111]. But it *is renewed in the knowledge of God*, that is, *in the justice and holiness of truth*; as the apostolic testimonies which I mentioned a little before have it.

In the knowledge of God, then, and the justice and holiness of truth, he who, advancing from day to day, is renewed, transfers his love from temporal things to eternal, from visible to intelligible, from carnal to spiritual; and diligently presses on to bridle and diminish desire for the former, and to bind himself to the latter by love. But he does as much as he is divinely aided. For it is God's sentence, *without me you can do nothing*[^112]. In which advance and increase, when the last day of this life shall find each one holding the faith of the Mediator, he, to be led to the God whom he worshiped, and to be perfected by him, will be received by the holy angels, about to receive at the end of the age an incorruptible body, not for punishment, but for glory.

For in this image the likeness of God will then be perfect,[^113] when the vision of God will be perfect. Of which the Apostle Paul says: *we now see through a mirror in an enigma, but then face to face*[^114]. Likewise he says, *but we, beholding with unveiled face the glory of the Lord, are transformed into the same image from glory to glory, as by the Spirit of the Lord*[^115]: this is what comes about from day to day in those who advance well.

## The Full Likeness of the Image Is in the Vision, but We Shall Be Like the Son Also in the Immortality of the Body

**18.** 24. But the Apostle John says, *most beloved, now we are sons of God, and it has not yet appeared what we shall be: we know*[^116] *that when he shall appear, we shall be like him, for we shall see him as he is*[^117].

Hence it appears that the full likeness of God will then come to be in this image of God, when it shall have received the full vision of him. Although this may seem to have been said by the apostle John even of the immortality of the body.[^118] For in this too we shall be like God, but only like the Son, because he alone in the Trinity received a body, in which, having died, he rose again, and carried it up to the heights. For this image too is called the image of the Son of God, in which we shall, like him, have an immortal body, made conformed in this part not to the image of the Father or of the Holy Spirit, but only of the Son, because of him alone it is read, and received by a most sound faith, *the Word was made flesh*[^119].

On account of which the Apostle says, *those whom he foreknew, he also predestined to be conformed to the image of his Son, that he himself might be the firstborn among many brothers*[^120]. *Firstborn*, surely, *from the dead*[^121], according to the same Apostle, by which death his flesh was sown in dishonor, and rose again in glory. According to this image of the Son, to which we are conformed by immortality in the body, we also do that which the same Apostle says: *as we have borne the image of the earthly, let us also bear the image of him who is from heaven*[^122]: namely, that we who were mortal according to Adam should hold, by true faith and sure and firm hope, that we shall be immortal according to Christ. For so now we can bear that same image, not yet in vision, but in faith; not yet in fact, but in hope. For the Apostle was speaking of the resurrection of the body, when he said these things.[^123]

## The Image Which Is Renewed Inwardly in the Knowledge of God Will Be Perfected by Vision

**19.** 25. But that image, of which it was said, *let us make man to our image and likeness*[^124]—since it was not said, "to my," or, "to your"—we believe man was made to the image of the Trinity, and have grasped this with as much investigation as we could. And therefore according to this rather is that too to be understood which the apostle John says, *we shall be like him, for we shall see him as he is*[^125]: because he said it of him of whom he had said, *we are sons of God*[^126].

And the immortality of the flesh will be perfected in that moment of the resurrection of which the Apostle Paul says, *in the twinkling of an eye, at the last trumpet, and the dead shall rise incorruptible; and we shall be changed*[^127]. For in that very twinkling of an eye, before the judgment, the spiritual body, which is now sown in weakness, corruption, dishonor, an animal body, shall rise in power, in incorruption, in glory.[^128] But the image which is renewed in the spirit of the mind in the knowledge of God,[^129] not outwardly but inwardly *from day to day*, will be perfected by that very vision, which will then be, after the judgment, *face to face*; but *now* it advances *through a mirror in an enigma*.[^130]

On account of the perfection of which that saying is to be understood, *we shall be like him, for we shall see him as he is*[^131]. For this gift will then be given to us, when it shall have been said, *come, blessed of my Father, possess the kingdom prepared for you*[^132]. For then the impious one will be taken away, that he may not see the brightness of the Lord, when those on the left shall go into eternal punishment, those on the right going into eternal life.[^133] *But this is*, as the Truth says, *eternal life, that they may know you*, he says, *the one true God, and Jesus Christ whom you sent*[^134].

## Wisdom Is Perfected in Blessedness

**19.** 26. This contemplative wisdom—which I think is, properly, the wisdom called in the Holy Writings as distinct from knowledge, the wisdom, at least, of man, which is not his except from him by participation in whom the rational and intellectual mind can become truly wise—Cicero, commending it at the end of the dialogue *Hortensius*, says:

*For us who consider these things day and night, and sharpen the understanding, which is the edge of the mind, and take care that it does not at any time grow dull—that is, for us who live in philosophy—there is great hope, that either, if this which we feel and taste is mortal and perishable, our setting, when we have discharged our human duties, will be pleasant, and our extinction not troublesome, and as it were a rest of life: or, if, as it pleased the ancient philosophers, and those the greatest and by far the most renowned, we have eternal and divine souls, then it must be thought that the more these have always been in their own course, that is, in reason and the desire of investigating, and the less they have mingled and entangled themselves in the vices and errors of men, the easier will their ascent and return to heaven be*[^135].

Then, adding this very conclusion, and ending his discourse by repeating it, he says: *wherefore, that the speech may at length be brought to a close, if we wish either to be tranquilly extinguished, when we have lived amid these arts, or to migrate without delay from this home into another by no little better one, in these studies all our labor and care is to be placed*[^136].

Here I marvel that a man of so great a talent promises to men living in philosophy—which makes men blessed by the contemplation of truth—a pleasant setting, when they have discharged their human duties, "if this which we feel and taste is mortal and perishable": as though that should die and fall away which we did not love, or rather which we fiercely hated, so that its setting should be pleasant to us. But this he had not learned from the philosophers whom he extols with great praises; rather, this opinion smelled of that New Academy, where it pleased him to doubt even about the most manifest things.

But from the philosophers, as he himself confesses, the *greatest and by far the most renowned*, he had received that souls are eternal.[^137] For eternal souls are not unfittingly roused by this exhortation, that they may be found in their own course, when the end of this life shall have come, *that is, in reason and the desire of investigating*, and may *less mingle and entangle themselves in the vices and errors of men*, that their return to God may be easier for them. But this course, which is set in the love and investigation of truth, does not suffice for the wretched, that is, for all mortals who have this reason alone, without the faith of the Mediator: which, in the earlier books of this work, especially in the fourth and thirteenth, I have taken care, as far as I could, to demonstrate.
