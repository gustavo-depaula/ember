# Book XII: What the outer and the inner man are.

**1.** 1. Come now, let us see where there is, as it were, a certain boundary between the outer and the inner man. For whatever we have in our mind in common with the beast is rightly said still to pertain to the outer man. For the outer man will be reckoned to be not only the body, but with a certain life of its own joined to it, by which the framework of the body and all the senses flourish, with which it is equipped for sensing outer things: and when the images of these things sensed, fixed in the memory, are revisited by remembering, the matter still being enacted pertains to the outer man. And in all these things we do not differ from the beast, except that in the shape of body we are not prone but erect.[^1]

In which matter we are admonished by him who made us, that we be not, in the better part of ourselves, that is, the mind, like the beasts, from which we differ by the erectness of the body. Not that we should cast the mind into those things which are lofty among bodies; for to seek the rest of the will even in such things is to lay the mind low.[^2] But just as the body is naturally erect toward those things which are high among bodies, that is, toward the heavenly things; so the mind, which is a spiritual substance, is to be erected toward those things which are high among spiritual things, not by the elation of pride, but by the piety of justice.

## The perception of the eternal reasons.

**2.** 2. But beasts too can both sense bodily things from without through the senses of the body, and recall them, fixed in the memory, and in them seek what is useful, and flee what is harmful: but to note these things, and to retain not only what is naturally seized but also what is committed to memory on purpose, and to impress again, by remembering and thinking, things that are now and again slipping into forgetfulness; so that, just as thought is formed from what the memory carries, so this very thing that is in the memory may be strengthened by thought: to compose also feigned seeings by taking things recalled here and there and, as it were, stitching them together, and to inspect how, in this kind of thing, the verisimilar are distinguished from the true, not in spiritual things, but in the bodily things themselves: these and things of this kind, although they are enacted and engaged in among sensible things, and among those which the mind has drawn from them through the sense of the body, are nonetheless not devoid of reason, nor common to human beings and beasts.

But it is the part of a more sublime reason to judge of these bodily things according to incorporeal and eternal reasons: which, unless they were above the human mind, would surely not be unchangeable; and unless something of ours were joined to them, we could not judge of bodily things according to them.[^3] But we judge of bodily things from the reason of dimensions and figures,[^4] which the mind knows to abide unchangeably.

## Higher and lower reason in one mind.

**3.** 3. But that part of ours which is so engaged in the action of handling bodily and temporal things that it is not common to us with the beast, is indeed rational, but is, as it were, drawn from that rational substance of our mind by which we cling beneath to the intelligible and unchangeable truth, and is deputed to handling and governing lower things. For just as in all the beasts there was not found for the man a helper like him, unless one were formed for marriage out of him, drawn from him;[^5] so for our mind, by which we consult the supernal and inner truth, there is no helper like it for the use of bodily things, as far as suffices for the nature of a human being, out of the parts of the soul which we have in common with the beasts. And therefore something rational of ours, not separated to the divorce of unity, but as it were derived in aid of fellowship, is set apart for the office of its own work. And just as the flesh of the two, in male and female, is one, so our understanding and action, or counsel and execution, or reason and rational appetite, or by whatever other way they can be more significantly expressed, one nature of the mind embraces: so that, as it was said of those, *they shall be two in one flesh*;[^6] so of these it may be said: two in one mind.

## The Trinity and the image of God only in that which pertains to the contemplation of eternal things.

**4.** 4. When, therefore, we discourse on the nature of the human mind, we discourse on a certain one thing, nor do we double it into these two which I mentioned, except by reason of offices. And so when we seek a trinity in it, we seek it in the whole, not separating the rational action in temporal things from the contemplation of eternal things, so as to seek now some third thing by which the trinity may be filled out.

But the trinity must be found in the whole nature of the mind in such a way that, if the action of temporal things—for which work a helper is necessary, on account of which something of the mind is derived to administer these lower things—be lacking, the trinity may be found in the one mind, nowhere divided: and, this distribution being now made, in that alone which pertains to the contemplation of eternal things there may be found not only a trinity, but also the *image of God*;[^7] but in that which is derived into the action of temporal things, although a trinity can be found, yet the *image of God* cannot.

## Those who think the image of God is found in the union of male and female and their offspring do not seem to bring forward a probable opinion.

**5.** 5. Accordingly, those do not seem to me to bring forward a probable opinion who think that the trinity of the image of God in three persons, as far as concerns human nature, can be found in such a way that it is filled out in the union of male and female and in their offspring: in that the man himself, as it were, intimates the person of the Father; and that of the Son, in that which so proceeded from him as to be born; and so they say the third person, as it were the Spirit, is the woman, who so proceeded from the man as not herself to be a son or a daughter, although by her conceiving the offspring was born.[^8] For the Lord said of the Holy Spirit that *he proceeds from the Father*, and yet he is not a Son.

In the error of this opinion, then, this alone is brought forward probably: that in the origin of the woman who was made, it is sufficiently shown, according to the faith of holy Scripture, that not everything which so comes to be from some person as to make another person can be called a son; since the person of the woman came to be from the person of the man, and yet she was not called his daughter. The rest indeed are so absurd, or rather so false, that they are most easily refuted.

For I pass over what kind of thing it is to think the Holy Spirit the mother of the Son of God and the consort of the Father: for perhaps it might be answered that these things have an offense in carnal matters, when bodily conceivings and births are thought of. Although even these things they would think most chastely, to whom *to the pure all things are pure: but to the impure and unbelieving*, whose *mind and conscience are defiled*, so *nothing is pure*,[^9] that Christ, born according to the flesh of a Virgin, offends some of them too.[^10]

But nevertheless, in those highest spiritual things, where there is nothing violable or corruptible, nor anything born *in time*,[^11] nor formed from the formless, if any such things are spoken, to the likeness of which even these kinds of lower creation, although made far most remotely, were made, they ought not to disturb anyone’s sober prudence, lest, while he avoids an empty horror, he run into a pernicious error.

Let it grow accustomed to find in bodies the traces of spiritual things in such a way that, when from there it begins to ascend upward, reason leading, so as to arrive at that unchangeable truth itself *through which these things were made*, it may not drag along with itself to the highest things what it despises in the lowest.

For a certain man was not ashamed to choose wisdom as a wife for himself,[^12] because the name of wife suggests, to one thinking, the corruptible intercourse of begetting offspring: or, indeed, that wisdom itself is female in sex, because it is expressed by a word of the feminine gender both in the Greek and in the Latin tongue.

## Why that opinion is to be rejected.

**6.** 6. We do not, then, reject that opinion for this reason, that we fear to think of the holy and inviolable and unchangeable love, as it were the consort of God the Father, coming to be from him, but not as offspring, for the begetting of the Word *through whom all things were made*: but because divine Scripture evidently shows it to be false. For God said, *Let us make the human being to our image and likeness*: but a little after it is said, *and God made the human being to the image of God*. *Our* certainly, since it is plural in number, would not be rightly said if the human being were made to the image of one person, whether of the Father, or of the Son, or of the Holy Spirit: but because he was being made to the image of the Trinity, therefore it was said, *to our image*. But again, lest we should reckon that three gods are to be believed in the Trinity, since the same Trinity is one God, *and God made*, he says, *the human being to the image of God*: as if he said, *to his own image*.

**6.** 7. For there are such customary turns of speech in those Writings, which some, even if they assert the catholic faith, yet do not diligently notice, so that they suppose it was said, *God made to the image of God*, as if it were said, The Father made to the image of the Son: thus wishing to assert that in the holy Scriptures the Son too is called God, as if other most true and most manifest proofs were lacking, where the Son is called not only God, but even *true God*.[^13]

For in this testimony, while they intend to solve one thing, they so entangle themselves that they cannot get free. For if the Father made to the image of the Son, so that the human being is the image not of the Father but of the Son, then the Son is unlike the Father.

But if pious faith teaches, as it does teach, that the Son is, to the equality of essence, like the Father, then what was made *to the likeness* of the Son must necessarily have been made also to the likeness of the Father.

Then, if the Father made the human being not *to his own* image, but to the image of the Son, why did he not say, *Let us make the human being to* your *image and likeness*, but said, *our*;[^14] unless because the image of the Trinity was being made in the human being, so that in this way the human being might be the *image* of the one *true God*,[^15] because the Trinity itself is the *one true God*?

But there are innumerable such turns of speech in the Scriptures,[^16] but to have brought forward these will suffice. In the Psalms it is said thus, *Salvation is the Lord’s, and your blessing is upon your people*: as if it were said to another, not to him of whom he had said, *Salvation is the Lord’s*.

And again, *by you*, he says, *I shall be rescued from temptation, and in my God, hoping, I shall pass over the wall*: as if he had said to another, *by you I shall be rescued from temptation*.

And again, *the peoples shall fall under you in the heart of the king’s enemies*: as if he said, in the heart of your enemies. For he had said to that king, that is, to the Lord Jesus Christ, *the peoples shall fall under you*; whom he wished to be understood as king, when he said, *in the heart of the king’s enemies*.

These turns of speech are found more rarely in the writings of the New Testament. But nonetheless the Apostle, to the Romans, says *of his Son, who was made for him of the seed of David according to the flesh, who was predestined Son of God in power according to the Spirit of sanctification by the resurrection from the dead, Jesus Christ our Lord*: as though he were speaking above of another.

For what is *the Son of God predestined by the resurrection from the dead of Jesus Christ*, but that same[^17] Jesus Christ *who was predestined Son of God in power*? Therefore, just as here, when we hear, *the Son of God in the power of Jesus Christ*, or, *the Son of God according to the Spirit of sanctification of Jesus Christ*, or, *the Son of God by the resurrection from the dead of Jesus Christ*; when it could have been said in the usual way, *in* his *power*; or, *according to the Spirit of* his *sanctification*; or, *by the resurrection of* his *dead*, or his *dead*: we are not compelled to understand another person, but one and the same, namely of the Son of God, *our Lord Jesus Christ*.

So when we hear, *God made the human being to the image of God*: although it could have been said more in the usual way, *to his own image*;[^18] yet we are not compelled to understand another person in the Trinity, but that one and the same Trinity itself, which is *one God*, *to* whose *image the human being was made*.[^19]

**6.** 8. Since these things are so,[^20] if we were to take that same image of the Trinity to be not in one, but in three human beings, father and mother and son, then the human being was not *made to the image of God*[^21] before a wife was made for him, and before they propagated a son: because there was not yet a trinity.

Or does someone say: There was already a trinity, because, even if not yet in its own proper form, yet already in original nature both the woman was in the side of the man and the son was in the loins of the father? Why, then, when Scripture had said, *God made the human being to the image of God*, did it continue, saying, *God made him; male and female he made them, and blessed them*?

Or if it is to be distinguished thus, *and God made the human being*; so that then is added, *to the image of God he made him*; and there is a third clause subjoined, *male and female he made them*. For some have feared to say, *he made him male and female*, lest something monstrous, as it were, be understood, such as those whom they call hermaphrodites:[^22] although even so both could be understood, without falsehood, in the singular number, on account of what was said, *two in one flesh*.[^23]

Why, then, as I had begun to say, in the nature of the human being made to the image of God, does Scripture mention nothing besides male and female? For to fill out the image of the Trinity it ought to have added also the son, although still set in the loins of the father, just as the woman was in the side.[^24] Or had the woman perhaps already been made, and Scripture had bound it up in a brief summary, so as later to explain more diligently how it was done; and therefore the son could not be mentioned, because he was not yet born? As if even this the Holy Spirit could not have bound up in that brevity, intending later, in its place, to narrate the son born, just as it afterward narrated, in its place, the woman taken from the side of the man, and yet did not here pass over naming her.

## Why the woman too is not the image of God.

**7.** 9. We ought not, then, to understand the *human being made* to the image of the supreme Trinity, that is, *to the image of God*,[^25] in such a way that the same image is understood in three human beings: especially since the Apostle says that the man is the image of God, and therefore takes from him the covering of the head, which he advises is to be applied to the woman, speaking thus: *the man indeed ought not to veil his head, since he is the image and glory of God. But the woman is the glory of the man*. What, then, shall we say to these things? If the woman, in her own person, fills out the image of the Trinity, why is the man, after she was drawn from his side, still called the *image*?[^26] Or if even one person of the human being out of three can be called the *image of God*, as in the supreme Trinity itself each person too is God, why is the woman too not the image of God? For it is for this reason that she is commanded to veil her head, which he is forbidden because he is the *image of God*.

## How the saying of the Apostle, that the man is the image of God, but the woman the glory of the man, is to be understood figuratively and mystically.

**7.** 10. But we must see how it is not contrary, what the Apostle says, that not the woman, but the man is the image of God, to this which is written in Genesis, *God made the human being, to the image of God he made him; male and female he made them, and blessed them*. For he says that human nature itself was made *to the image of God*, which is completed in both sexes, nor does he separate the woman from the image of God to be understood. For having said that *God made the human being to the image of God*, he says, *he made him male and female*: or at least, by another distinction, *male and female he made them*.

How, then, did we hear through the Apostle that the man is the *image of God*, whence he is forbidden to veil his head, but the woman is not, and therefore she is bidden to do this?[^27] Unless, I believe, it be that which I have already said, when I was dealing with the nature of the human mind, that the woman together with her husband is the *image of God*,[^28] so that that whole substance is one image: but when she is set apart as a helper, which pertains to her alone, she is not the image of God; whereas what pertains to the man alone *is the image of God*,[^29] as full and whole as when the woman too is joined to him into one.

As we said of the nature of the human mind, that both if as a whole it contemplates the truth, it *is the image of God*; and when something is set apart from it, and by a certain intention is derived to the action of temporal things, nonetheless from that part by which it consults the beheld truth, it *is the image of God*; but from that by which it is intent on doing lower things, it is not the image of God. And since, the more it extends itself toward that which is eternal, so much the more is it thence formed[^30] *to the image of God*, and therefore it is not to be restrained, so as to hold itself back from there and to temper itself; therefore *the man ought not to veil his head*.

But because for that rational action which is engaged in bodily and temporal things, too great a progress into lower things is dangerous; *she ought to have a power upon her head*, which the veil indicates, by which it is signified that she is to be restrained.[^31] For a hallowed and pious signification is pleasing to the holy angels.

For God does not see in time, nor does anything new come about in his seeing and knowledge, when something is done temporally and transitorily, as the senses of living beings are affected thereby, whether the carnal senses of animals and human beings, or even the heavenly senses of the angels.

**7.** 11. That the apostle Paul figured the mystery of a certain more hidden thing in this manifest sex of male and female can be understood even from this, that whereas in another place he says that a true widow is one left desolate, without sons and grandsons, and yet that she ought to hope in the Lord, and to persist *in prayers night and day*, here he says that the woman, seduced and made in transgression, is saved *through the bearing of children*: and he added, *if they remain in faith, and love, and sanctification with sobriety*. As if, indeed, it could harm a good widow if she either has not had children, or those whom she has had have refused to remain in good conduct.[^32] But because the things that are called good works are, as it were, the children of our life, according to which it is asked of what life each one is, that is, how he carries on these temporal things, which life the Greeks call not ζωή but βίος; and these good works are wont to be performed especially in the offices of mercy; but works of mercy profit nothing, whether to Pagans, or to Jews who do not believe in Christ, or to any heretics or schismatics where faith and love and sober sanctification are not found:[^33] it is plain what the Apostle wished to signify; and figuratively and mystically, because he was speaking of veiling the woman’s head,[^34] which, unless it is referred to some secret of a mystery, will remain empty.

**7.** 12. For just as not only most true reason, but also the authority of the Apostle himself, declares that the *human being was made to the image of God*[^35] not according to the form of the body, but according to the rational mind.[^36] For the thought is basely empty which supposes that God is circumscribed and bounded by the lineaments of bodily members. Now further, does not the same blessed Apostle say, *be renewed in the spirit of your mind, and put on the new man, who was created according to God*; and elsewhere more plainly, *putting off*, he says, *the old man with his deeds, put on the new who is renewed unto the knowledge of God according to the image of him who created him*? If, then, we are renewed in the spirit of our mind, and it is the new man who is renewed unto the knowledge of God according to the image of him who created him; it is doubtful to no one that the *human being was made to the image of him who created him* not according to the body, nor according to any part of the soul, but according to the rational mind, where the knowledge of God can be.

But according to this renewal we are made also *sons of God* through the baptism of Christ, and, *putting on the new* man, we surely put on Christ *through faith*.[^37] Who is there, then, who would estrange women from this fellowship, since they are coheirs of grace with us; and in another place the same Apostle says, *for you are all sons of God through faith in Christ Jesus. For whoever of you were baptized in Christ have put on Christ. There is neither Jew nor Greek, there is neither slave nor free, there is not male and female: for you are all one in Christ Jesus*?

Have faithful women, then, lost the sex of the body? But because there they are *renewed to the image of God*, where there is no sex, there the *human being was made to the image of God*[^38] where there is no sex, that is, *in the spirit of* his *mind*.[^39]

Why, then, *ought the man not to veil his head, because he is the image and glory of God; but the woman* ought to, because she is *the glory of the man*, as though the woman were not *renewed in the spirit of* her *mind*,[^40] *which is renewed unto the knowledge of God according to the image of him who created him*?

But because she differs from the man in the sex of the body, that part of reason which is bent down to governing temporal things could fittingly be figured in her bodily veil; so that the image of God does not remain except in that part by which the mind of the human being clings to the eternal reasons to be beheld or consulted, which it is plain that not only males, but also females[^41] have.

## The falling-away from the image of God.

**8.** 13. In their minds, then, a common nature is recognized; but in their bodies the distribution of that one mind is figured.

And so, as we ascend inward by certain steps of consideration through the parts of the soul, from where something begins to present itself that is not common to us with the beasts, there reason begins, where now the inner man can be recognized.

And if this very man, through that reason to which the administration of temporal things has been delegated, by an immoderate progress slips too far into outer things, his head consenting with him—that is, that part which presides on the watchtower of counsel, as the virile portion, not restraining and curbing him—he grows old among his enemies,[^42] the demons envious of virtue, with their prince the devil:

and that vision of eternal things is withdrawn from the head itself too, eating, with its spouse, the forbidden thing, so that the light of his eyes is not with him;[^43] and so, both stripped of that illumination of the truth, and with the eyes of conscience opened to see how dishonorable and unseemly they have remained, like the leaves of sweet fruits, but without the fruits themselves, so without the fruit of good work they weave together good words, so that, living badly, they may, as it were by speaking well, cover their baseness.[^44]

**9.** 14. For the soul, loving its own power, slips down from the common universe to a private part: and by that apostatic *pride*, which is called *the beginning of sin*[^45]—when, in the universe of creation, following God as ruler, it could have been governed most excellently by his laws, but, desiring something more than the universe, and striving to govern it by its own law, because there is nothing more than the universe—it is thrust into a partial care, and so, by desiring something more, is diminished; whence also *avarice* is called *the root of all evils*:[^46]

and all that wherein it strives to do something of its own against the laws by which the universe is administered, it carries on through its own body, which it possesses partially; and so, delighted with bodily forms and motions, because it does not have them with itself within, it is wrapped up in their images, which it has fixed in the memory, and is basely defiled by a phantastic fornication, referring all its offices to those ends by which it curiously seeks bodily and temporal things through the senses of the body; or, by a swollen haughtiness, it strives to be more exalted than other souls given over to bodily senses, or it is plunged into the muddy whirlpool of carnal pleasure.

## The steps to the basest things.

**10.** 15. When, therefore, with a good will it consults either for itself or for others toward perceiving inner and higher things, which are possessed not privately, but in common by all who love such things, in a chaste embrace without any straitness or envy; and if it is deceived in something through ignorance of temporal things, because it carries this on temporally too, and does not keep the manner of acting which it ought, this is a *human temptation*.[^47] And it is a great thing to pass this life thus, which we travel as a road of those returning, so that *temptation does not lay hold of us except a human one*.

For this *sin is outside the body*,[^48] nor is it reckoned as fornication, and therefore it is most easily forgiven. But when, in order to obtain those things that are sensed through the body, on account of the desire of experiencing or excelling or handling, so as to set in them the end of its good, it does something—whatever it does, it does basely; and it *fornicates, sinning against its own body*, and, seizing inwardly the deceitful images of bodily things and composing them by empty meditation, so that nothing divine seems to it except such a thing, privately and avariciously it teems with errors, and privately and prodigally it is emptied of its strength.[^49] Nor would it leap forth at once, from the very outset, into so base and miserable a fornication; but, as it is written, *he who despises small things falls little by little*.

## When the human being wishes to be as God, he is driven to the lowest things, to those in which the beasts rejoice.

**11.** 16. For just as a serpent creeps not by open steps, but by the most minute efforts of its scales; so the slippery motion of falling away seizes the negligent little by little, and, beginning from a perverse appetite for the likeness of God, arrives at the likeness of the beasts.[^50] Hence it is that, stripped of the first robe,[^51] they earned *garments of skin*[^52] by mortality.[^53] For the true honor of the human being is the *image and likeness of God*,[^54] which is not kept except toward him by whom it is impressed. The more, therefore, one clings to God, the less is one’s own self loved.

But by the desire of experiencing its own power, it rushes, by a certain nod of its own, upon itself as upon a middle. And so, when it wishes to be like him, under no one, it is driven, as a punishment, even from its own middle position down to the lowest things, that is, to those in which the beasts rejoice: and so, since its honor is the likeness of God, but its disgrace the likeness of the beast, *the human being, set in honor, did not understand; he has been compared to the senseless beasts, and made like them*.

By what way, then, would it pass so far from the highest things to the lowest, except through its own middle? For when, the love of wisdom being neglected, which always abides in the same way, *knowledge* is desired from the experience of changeable and temporal things, it *puffs up*, it does not *build up*:[^55]

so the soul, weighed down as by its own weight, is expelled from blessedness, and through that experience of its own middle position it learns by its own punishment what difference there is between the good forsaken and the evil committed, nor can it return, its strength poured out and lost, except by the grace of its Maker, calling it to repentance and forgiving its sins. For *who* will *free* the unhappy soul *from the body of this death*, except *the grace of God through Jesus Christ our Lord*? Of which grace we shall discourse in its place, as far as he himself shall grant.

## A figurative interpretation of the first sin; a certain secret marriage in the inner man.

**12.** 17. Now let us carry through the consideration we have undertaken, as far as the Lord helps, concerning that part of reason to which knowledge belongs, that is, the cognition of temporal and changeable things necessary for carrying on the actions of this life. For just as in that manifest marriage of the two human beings who were first made, the serpent did not eat of the forbidden tree, but only persuaded it to be eaten: but the woman did not eat alone, but gave to her husband, and they ate together; although it was she alone who spoke with the serpent, and was alone seduced by him:[^56] so too in this which is enacted and discerned even in one human being, by a certain hidden and secret marriage, the carnal—or, so to speak, the sensual—movement of the soul, which is intent upon the senses of the body, which we have in common with the beasts, is sequestered from the reason of wisdom.

For by the sense of the body bodily things are sensed: but eternal and unchangeable things are understood by the reason of wisdom. But to the reason of knowledge appetite is near: since concerning the very bodily things which are sensed by the sense of the body, it reasons out that which is called the knowledge of action; if well, so that it refers that knowledge to the end of the highest good; but if badly, so that it enjoys them as such goods in which it rests in a false blessedness. When, therefore, to this intention of the mind, which is engaged, by liveliness of reasoning, in temporal and bodily things for the office of action, that carnal or animal sense brings in a certain allurement of enjoying itself, that is, as a certain private and proper good, not as a public and common one, which is the unchangeable good, then it is as though the serpent addresses the woman. But to consent to this allurement is to eat of the forbidden tree. But if this consent is content with the delight of thought alone, while by the authority of the higher counsel the *members* are so held that *they are not presented as weapons of iniquity to sin*; thus I reckon it must be held that the woman alone ate the forbidden food.

But if, in the consent of using badly the things that are sensed through the sense of the body, any sin is so decreed that, if there be power, it is even fulfilled in the body; then that woman is to be understood to have given to her husband the unlawful food to be eaten together with her. For a sin cannot be decreed in the mind, not only to be thought of sweetly, but even to be effectively perpetrated, unless that intention of the mind too, with which is the supreme power of moving the members to a work, or of restraining them from a work, yields and serves the evil action.

**12.** 18. Nor indeed, when the mind is delighted by unlawful things in thought alone, not indeed decreeing that they are to be done, yet holding and willingly turning over things which ought to have been spurned as soon as they touched the mind, is it to be denied that there is sin, but far less than if it were also resolved to be fulfilled in deed. And therefore for such thoughts too pardon must be sought, and the breast struck, and it must be said, *forgive us our debts*: and what follows must be done, and joined in prayer, *as we also forgive our debtors*.

For it is not as in those two first human beings, where each one bore his own person, and therefore, if the woman alone had eaten the unlawful food, she alone surely would have been struck with the punishment of death; so it can be said in one human being, if the thought alone is willingly fed on unlawful delights, from which it ought immediately to turn away, and evils are not decreed to be done, but only held sweetly in recollection, that the woman, as it were, can be condemned without the man: God forbid that we should believe this. For this is one person, it is one human being, and the whole will be condemned, unless these things which are felt to be the sins of thought alone—without the will of doing, yet with the will of delighting the mind in such things—are remitted through the grace of the Mediator.

**12.** 19. And so this discussion, in which we have sought, in the mind of each human being, a certain rational marriage of contemplation and action, with their offices distributed through certain particulars, yet the unity of the mind preserved in both—the history of that truth being kept safe, which divine authority hands down concerning the two first human beings, namely the man and his woman, from whom the human race was propagated—is to be heard only to this end, that it may be understood that the Apostle, by attributing the image of God to the man only, and not also to the woman, although in the different sex of two human beings, yet wished to signify something that was to be sought in one human being.[^57]

## Another interpretation: the opinion of those who held that by the man the mind is signified, by the woman the sense of the body.

**13.** 20. Nor does it escape me that certain men, who were before us distinguished defenders of the catholic faith and expositors of the divine eloquence,[^58] when in one human being, whose whole good soul they held to be a certain paradise, they sought these two, called the man the mind, but the woman the sense of the body. And according to this distribution, by which the man is set as the mind, but the sense of the body as the woman, all things seem aptly to fit, if they are considered when handled: except that it is written, in all the beasts and birds, that *there was not found for the man a helper like him*, and then the woman was made for him from his side.

For which reason I did not think the sense of the body, which we see to be common to us and the beasts, was to be set for the woman; but I wished something which the beasts did not have: and I reckoned the sense of the body rather to be understood by the serpent, which is read to be *wiser than all the beasts of the earth*. For in those natural goods which we see to be common to us and the irrational living beings, it excels by a certain liveliness of sense: not that sense of which it is written in the Epistle which is to the Hebrews, where it is read that *solid food* is for *the perfect, who by habit have their senses exercised for separating good from evil*; for those senses are of the rational nature, pertaining to understanding: but this sense which is fivefold in the body, through which not only by us, but also by the beasts, bodily appearance and motion is sensed.

**13.** 21. But whether in this way, or in that, or in some other way it is to be taken, that the Apostle called the man the *image and glory of God, but the woman the glory of the man*;[^59] yet it appears that, when we live according to God, our mind, intent on his invisible things, ought to be progressively formed from his eternity, truth, love: but something of our rational intention, that is, of the same mind, is to be directed to the use of changeable and bodily things, without which this life is not carried on; not that it be conformed to *this world*, by setting an end in such goods, and by twisting toward them the appetite of blessedness; but that whatever we reasonably do in the use of temporal things, we may do with a view to obtaining eternal things, passing through these, clinging to those.

## On wisdom and knowledge.

**14.** 21. For *knowledge* too has its own good measure, if that in it which *puffs up*, or is wont to puff up, is conquered by the love of eternal things, which does not puff up, but, as we know, *builds up*.[^60] For without knowledge not even the virtues themselves, by which one lives rightly, can be had, by which this miserable life is so governed that one may come to that which is truly blessed and eternal.

## What the difference is between wisdom and knowledge.

**14.** 22. Yet the action by which we use temporal things well differs from the contemplation of eternal things, and the latter is reckoned to wisdom, the former to knowledge. For although that which is wisdom can also be called knowledge, as the Apostle too speaks, where he says, *now I know in part, but then I shall know even as I am known*; which knowledge he surely wishes to be understood as the contemplation of God, which will be the highest reward of the saints: yet where he says, *to one indeed is given through the Spirit the word of wisdom, to another the word of knowledge according to the same Spirit*; he surely distinguishes these two without doubt, although he does not there explain what the difference is, and from where each can be recognized.

But searching the manifold abundance of the holy Scriptures, I find it written in the book of Job, the same holy man speaking: *behold, piety is wisdom; but to abstain from evils is knowledge*. In this difference it is to be understood that wisdom pertains to contemplation, knowledge to action. For he here set as *piety* the worship of God, which in Greek is called θεοσέβεια. For this sentence has this word in the Greek codices.

And what is there among eternal things more excellent than God, whose nature alone is unchangeable? And what is his worship, except his love, by which we now desire to see him, and believe and hope that we shall see him; and, as far as we make progress, *we see now through a mirror in an enigma, but then* in manifestation? For this is what the apostle Paul says, *face to face*: this too what John says, *beloved, now we are sons of God, and it has not yet appeared what we shall be*;[^61] *we know that when he appears, we shall be like him, for we shall see him as he is*.

Of these and things of this kind the discourse itself seems to me to be the *word of wisdom*.[^62] But *to abstain from evils*,[^63] which Job said is knowledge, is without doubt of temporal things. Since according to time we are among the evils from which we ought to abstain, that we may come to those eternal goods. Wherefore whatever we do prudently, bravely, temperately, and justly pertains to that knowledge or discipline by which our action is engaged in avoiding evils and seeking goods; and whatever we gather by historical cognition for the sake of examples either to be guarded against or to be imitated, and for the sake of the necessary proofs of any matters that are suited to our uses.

## Through wisdom comes the cognition of eternal things.

**14.** 23. When, therefore, discourse is made about these things, I think that discourse of knowledge is to be distinguished from the discourse of wisdom,[^64] to which belong those things which neither have been, nor will be, but are, and, on account of that eternity in which they are, are said both to have been and to be and to be about to be, without any changeableness of times. For they have not so been as to cease to be, nor are they so about to be as though they were not now: but they have always had that very being, and they will always have it. But they abide not as though fixed in spans of place, like bodies: but, in incorporeal nature, they are so present, intelligible, to the gaze of the mind, as those visible or touchable things in places are present to the senses of the body.

And not only do the intelligible and incorporeal reasons of sensible things set in places abide without spans of place; but the reasons too of motions passing in times stand, themselves likewise intelligible, not sensible, without temporal passage. To reach these by the edge of the mind is the lot of few; and when one reaches them, as far as it can be done, the one who reaches does not abide in them, but, as it were, the very edge is beaten back and repelled, and there comes about a transitory thought of a non-transitory thing.

Which transitory thought, nevertheless, passing through the disciplines by which the mind is taught, is committed to memory, so that there may be somewhere to which it can return, which is compelled to pass from there: although, if the thought did not return to memory, and find there what it had committed, it would be led, as untrained for this, just as it had been led before, and would find it where it had first found it, namely in that incorporeal truth, from which it would again be fixed, as it were copied, in the memory.

For just as the incorporeal and unchangeable reason of a square body abides, for example, the thought of a human being does not so abide in it; if, that is, it could reach it without the phantasy of a span of place. Or if the numerousness of some artful and musical sound passing through the delays of time be grasped, standing without time in a certain secret and lofty silence,[^65] it can at least be thought of as long as that song can be heard: yet what the transitory gaze of the mind has snatched from there, and, as it were swallowing it into the belly, has laid up in the memory, it will be able in a way to chew over by remembering, and to carry over into a discipline what it has thus learned. But if this be deleted by complete forgetfulness, again, with doctrine for a guide, one will come to that which had utterly slipped away, and so it will be found as it was.

## Against the reminiscence of Plato and Pythagoras. The doctrine of illumination.

**15.** 24. Hence that noble philosopher Plato tried to persuade that the souls of human beings lived here even before they bore these bodies: and that hence it is that the things which are learned are recollected, as known, rather than known as new.[^66] For he related that a certain boy, questioned about I know not what of geometry, answered as though he were most skilled in that discipline. For, questioned step by step and skillfully, he saw what was to be seen, and said what he had seen.[^67]

But if this were a recollection of things previously known, surely not all, or nearly all, could do this when questioned in that way. For not all were geometers in a former life, since they are so rare in the human race that scarcely anyone can be found: but rather it is to be believed that the nature of the intellectual mind is so constituted that, joined to intelligible things in the natural order, by the Maker’s disposing, it sees these things in a certain light of its own kind, incorporeal, just as the eye of the flesh sees the things that lie around in this bodily light, of which light it was created capable and to which it is suited. For neither does the eye itself, for this reason, distinguish white and black without a teacher, because it had already known these before it was created in this flesh.

In short, why can it come about, concerning intelligible things alone, that anyone well questioned answers what pertains to any discipline, even if he is ignorant of it? Why can no one do this concerning sensible things, except those which he has seen, set in this body, or has believed those who knew them as they indicated them, whether by anyone’s writings or words? For we must not acquiesce in those who report that Pythagoras of Samos[^68] recollected certain such things which he had experienced when he had already been here in another body: and others narrate of certain others that they suffered something of this kind in their minds:

that these were false memories, such as we very often experience in dreams, when we seem to ourselves to remember as though we had done or seen something which we neither did at all nor saw; and that the minds of those people, even waking, were affected in that way by the instigation of malignant and deceitful spirits, whose care it is to confirm or sow a false opinion about the transmigrations of souls, in order to deceive human beings—this can be conjectured from the fact that, if they truly recollected those things which they had seen here, set before in other bodies, it would happen to many, and to nearly all: since they suppose that, just as the dead come from the living, so the living come from the dead, as the sleeping from the waking, and the waking from the sleeping, without ceasing.

## The right distinction of wisdom and knowledge; a certain trinity is found in knowledge.

**15.** 25. If, then, this is the correct distinction between wisdom and knowledge—that the intellectual cognition of eternal things belongs to wisdom, while the rational cognition of temporal things belongs to knowledge—it is not difficult to judge which is to be set before the other and which after. But if some other distinction is to be applied by which these two are told apart, which the Apostle teaches beyond doubt to differ when he says, *to one is given through the Spirit the utterance of wisdom, to another the utterance of knowledge according to the same Spirit*[^69], still even of the two we have set down the difference is most evident: that the one is the intellectual cognition of eternal things and the other the rational cognition of temporal things, and no one doubts that the former is to be preferred to the latter.

And so, as we leave behind the things that belong to the outer man and desire to ascend inward from those things we have in common with the beasts, before we come to the cognition of intelligible and highest things, which are everlasting, the rational cognition of temporal things meets us. In this too, then, let us find, if we can, some trinity, just as we were finding one in the senses of the body and in those things that have entered through them, by way of images, into our soul or spirit—so that, in place of the corporeal things we touch from without by a bodily sense, we had within likenesses of bodies impressed upon the memory, from which thought was formed, with the will as a third joining the two: just as the gaze of the eyes was formed without, which the will, that seeing might come about, applied to the visible thing and joined the two, there too moving itself in as the third. But this is not to be crowded into this book, so that in the one that follows, if God will help, it may fittingly be inquired into, and what has been found may be set forth.
