# Book V: What Augustine asks of God, and what of his readers.

## Proem

**1.** 1. Beginning now from here to say the things that cannot in every way be said as they are thought, whether by some man or at least by us—although our very thought, when we think of God the Trinity, feels itself far unequal to him of whom it thinks, nor grasps him as he is, but, as it is written, even by such great ones as the apostle Paul was here, he is seen *through a glass in a riddle*—first I pray of the Lord our God himself, of whom we ought always to think, and of whom we cannot worthily think, to whom in praising him blessing is to be rendered at all times,[^1] and for declaring whom no utterance is fitting, both help to understand and explain the things I intend, and pardon if anywhere I give offense. For I am mindful not only of my will but also of my weakness. Of these too who will read these things I ask, that they pardon where they perceive that I wished rather than was able to say what they themselves either understand better, or, on account of the difficulty of my speech, do not understand; just as I pardon them, where, on account of their slowness, they cannot understand.

### God is something far better than what is better in us.

**1.** 2. But we more easily pardon one another, if we know, or at least by believing hold firm, that the things that are said of the unchangeable and invisible and supremely living and self-sufficient nature are not to be measured by the habit of visible and changeable and mortal or needy things. But although even in those things that lie near our bodily senses, or that we ourselves are in the inner man, we labor and are not equal to comprehending them by knowledge, yet not impudently does faithful piety burn toward those divine and ineffable things that are above—not the piety that the arrogance of its own powers puffs up, but the piety that the grace of the Creator and Savior himself inflames.

For by what understanding does man grasp God, who does not yet grasp his own understanding by which he wishes to grasp him? But if he now grasps this, let him diligently attend that there is nothing in his nature better than it, and let him see whether there he sees any lineaments of forms, brightnesses of colors, spacious magnitude, distance of parts, extension of mass, any motions through intervals of places, or anything of the sort. Certainly we find none of these things in that than which we find nothing better in our nature, that is, in our understanding, by which we grasp wisdom as far as we are capable.

What, then, we do not find in what is better in us, we ought not to seek in him who is far better than what is better in us: so that we may understand God, if we can, as far as we can, good without quality, great without quantity, creator without need, presiding without position, containing all things without habit, *wholly everywhere without place*,[^2] sempiternal without time, making changeable things without any change of himself, and suffering nothing. Whoever thinks of God thus, even if he cannot yet in every way find what he is, yet piously takes care, as far as he can, to think nothing of him that he is not.[^3]

### God is being itself.

**2.** 3. Yet without doubt he is substance, or, if it is better so called, essence, which the Greeks call οὐσία. For just as from "to be wise" is named "wisdom," and from "to know" is named "knowledge," so from "to be" is named "essence."[^4] And who more truly is than he who said to his servant Moses, *I am who am*; and, *you shall say to the children of Israel: 'He who is has sent me to you'*? But other things that are called essences or substances admit of accidents, by which in them there comes about a change, whether great or of whatever degree: but to God nothing of the sort can happen; and therefore he alone is the unchangeable substance or essence, who is God, to whom certainly being itself, from which "essence" is named, most of all and most truly belongs. For what is changed does not keep its very being; and what can be changed, even if it is not changed, can be such that what it had been is not: and through this that alone which not only is not changed, but also can in no way be changed, occurs without scruple as what may most truly be said to be.

### The argument of the Arians.

**3.** 4. Wherefore, that we may now begin to answer the adversaries of our faith even about those things which are neither said as they are thought, nor thought as they are: among the many things that the Arians are wont to argue against the Catholic faith, they seem to set before themselves this as their most cunning contrivance, when they say: "Whatever is said or understood of God is said not according to accident, but according to substance. Wherefore, to be unbegotten is for the Father according to substance, and to be begotten is for the Son according to substance. But to be unbegotten and to be begotten are different: therefore the substance of the Father and of the Son is different."[^5]

To whom we answer: "If whatever is said of God is said according to substance, then what was said, *I and the Father are one*, was said according to substance." There is, therefore, one substance of the Father and the Son. Or if this was not said according to substance, then something is said of God not according to substance; and therefore we are no longer compelled to understand unbegotten and begotten according to substance. Likewise it was said of the Son, *he did not think it robbery to be equal to God*: we ask, equal according to what? For if he is not said to be equal according to substance, they admit that something is said of God not according to substance; let them admit, then, that unbegotten and begotten are said not according to substance. But if they do not admit this, because they wish all things to be said of God according to substance, then according to substance the Son is equal to the Father.

### An accident always indicates some change in a thing.

**4.** 5. But "accident" is not usually said except of what can be lost by some change of the thing to which it happens. For although certain accidents are called inseparable, which in Greek are called αχώριστα—as the black color of a raven's feather—yet it loses it, not indeed while it is a feather, but because it is not always a feather. Wherefore the matter itself is changeable, and from the fact that it ceases to be that animal or that feather, and that whole body *is changed and turned* into earth,[^6] it certainly loses that color too. Although even an accident that is called separable is lost not by separation but by change; as the blackness of human hair, since, while the hair remains, it can grow white, is called a separable accident: but to those who look carefully it sufficiently appears that nothing as it were migrates from the head by separation while it grows gray, so that the blackness departs from there, whiteness succeeding, and goes somewhere, but that that quality of color is there turned and changed.

Nothing, then, is accidental in God, because nothing is changeable or able to be lost. But if it pleases anyone to call that too an accident which, although it is not lost, is yet diminished or increased—as is the life of the soul: for as long as the soul is, so long it lives, and because it is always a soul, it always lives; but because it lives more when it is wise, and less when it is foolish, here too some change comes about, not so that life is wanting, as wisdom is wanting to the foolish, but so that it is less—nothing of the sort comes about in God, because he remains altogether unchangeable.

### In God nothing is said according to accident, but according to substance or according to relation, which is not an accident, because it is not changeable.

**5.** 6. Wherefore nothing is said of him according to accident, because nothing happens to him; nor yet is everything that is said said according to substance. For in created and changeable things, what is not said according to substance, it remains, is said according to accident. For all things happen to them that can either be lost or diminished, both magnitudes and qualities; and what is said in relation to something, as friendships, kinships, services, likenesses, equalities, and any such things; and position and habit, and places and times, and acts and passions.[^7]

But in God nothing indeed is said according to accident, because nothing in him is changeable; nor yet is everything that is said said according to substance. For something is said in relation to something,[^8] as the Father in relation to the Son, and the Son in relation to the Father, which is not an accident: because both that one is always Father, and that one always Son; and not "always" as though from the time the Son was born the Father does not cease to be Father (because the Son never ceases to be Son), but because the Son was always born, and never began to be Son. For if he had at some time begun to be, or at some time ceased to be Son, it would be said according to accident.

But if what is called Father were said in relation to himself, not to the Son, and what is called Son were said in relation to himself, not to the Father, then both that one Father and that one Son would be said according to substance. But because the Father is not called Father except from the fact that he has a Son, and the Son is not so called except from the fact that he has a Father, these things are not said according to substance: because neither of them is said this in relation to himself, but in relation to one another and to each other; nor according to accident, because both what is called Father and what is called Son is eternal and unchangeable for them.

Wherefore, although to be Father and to be Son are different, yet the substance is not different: because these are said not according to substance, but according to relation; which relation, however, is not an accident, because it is not changeable.

### The argument of the Arians concerning "Unbegotten."

**6.** 7. But if they think this argument is to be resisted thus, that the Father indeed is said in relation to the Son, and the Son in relation to the Father, but unbegotten and begotten are said in relation to themselves, not to each other: for to say "unbegotten" is not the same as to say "Father"; because even if he had not begotten a Son, nothing would prevent him from being called unbegotten; and if anyone begets a son, he himself is not on that account unbegotten, because men who are begotten of other men themselves also beget others; they say, therefore, "Father is said in relation to the Son, and Son in relation to the Father, but unbegotten is said in relation to himself, and begotten in relation to himself:

and therefore, if whatever is said in relation to himself is said according to substance, but to be unbegotten and to be begotten are different, then the substance is different."

If they say this, they do not understand that about "unbegotten" they indeed say something that must be treated more carefully, because no one is a father because he is unbegotten, nor unbegotten because he is a father, and therefore "unbegotten" is thought to be said not in relation to something but in relation to himself: but "begotten," by a strange blindness, they do not notice cannot be said except in relation to something.

For it is for this reason that he is a son, that he is begotten, and because he is a son he is certainly begotten. But as "son" is referred to "father," so "begotten" to "begetter"; and as father to son, so begetter to begotten. And therefore there is one notion by which "begetter" is understood, another by which "unbegotten." For although both are said of God the Father, that one is said in relation to the begotten, that is, to the Son; which not even they deny; but this that is called "unbegotten" they maintain is said in relation to himself. They say, therefore: "if something is said of the Father in relation to himself which cannot be said of the Son in relation to himself, and whatever is said in relation to himself is said according to substance, and 'unbegotten' is said in relation to himself, which cannot be said of the Son; then 'unbegotten' is said according to substance, which, because it cannot be said of the Son, the Son is not of the same substance."[^9]

To which cunning it is answered thus, so that they themselves are compelled to say according to what the Son is equal to the Father: whether according to that which is said in relation to himself, or according to that which is said in relation to the Father. For not according to that which is said in relation to the Father, since the son is said in relation to the father, but that one is not a son, but a father. For father and son are not said in relation to themselves in the way that friends or neighbors are. For relatively a friend is said in relation to a friend; and if they love each other equally, the same friendship is in each; and relatively a neighbor is said in relation to a neighbor; and because they are equally neighbors to each other (for as much as this one is neighbor to that, so much is that one neighbor to this), the same neighborhood is in each. But because the Son is said relatively not in relation to a Son, but to the Father, it is not according to that which is said in relation to the Father that the Son is equal to the Father.

It remains that he is equal according to that which is said in relation to himself. But whatever is said in relation to himself is said according to substance: it remains, then, that he is equal according to substance. The substance of both is therefore the same. But when the Father is called unbegotten, it is said not what he is, but what he is not. And when a relative is denied, it is not denied according to substance, because the relative itself is not said according to substance.

### By the negative particle only that is denied which without it was affirmed.

**7.** 8. This must be made plain by examples. And first it must be seen that this is signified when "begotten" is said, which is signified when "son" is said. For he is a son because he is begotten; and because he is a son, he is certainly begotten. What, then, is called "unbegotten" shows this, that he is not a son: but "begotten" and "unbegotten" are conveniently said; "son," however, is said in Latin, but the custom of speech does not admit that "non-son" be said.

Yet nothing is taken from the understanding if "not son" be said, just as, if "not begotten" be said for what is called "unbegotten," nothing else is said. For thus both "neighbor" and "friend" are said relatively, yet "non-neighbor" cannot be said in the way that "enemy" is said.[^10]

Wherefore in things it is not to be considered what the usage of our speech either permits or does not permit to be said, but what understanding of the things themselves shines forth.

Let us, then, no longer say "unbegotten," although it can be said in Latin; but for it let us say "not begotten," which is equivalent. Do we then say anything other than "not son"? Now this negative particle does not bring it about that what without it is said relatively is, when it is prefixed, said substantially; but only that is denied which without it was affirmed, as in the other predicaments. For instance, when we say, "he is a man," we designate substance. He, then, who says, "he is not a man," does not enunciate another kind of predicament, but only denies that one. As, then, according to substance I affirm, "he is a man," so according to substance I deny, when I say, "he is not a man." And when it is asked how large he is, and I say, "he is four feet," that is, of four feet, I affirm according to quality; he who says, "he is not four feet," denies according to quantity. "He is white," I affirm according to quality; "he is not white," I deny according to quality. "He is a relative," I affirm according to relation; "he is not a relative," I deny according to relation. According to position I affirm, when I say, "he is lying down"; according to position I deny, when I say, "he is not lying down." According to habit I affirm, when I say, "he is armed"; according to habit I deny, when I say, "he is not armed." But it amounts to the same thing if I say, "he is unarmed." According to time I affirm, when I say, "he is of yesterday"; according to time I deny, when I say, "he is not of yesterday." And when I say, "he is at Rome," I affirm according to place; and according to place I deny, when I say, "he is not at Rome." According to that which is to act I affirm, when I say, "he strikes"; but if I say, "he does not strike," according to that which is to act I deny, so as to show that he does not do this. And when I say, "he is beaten," I affirm according to the predicament that is called to be acted upon; and according to it I deny, when I say, "he is not beaten." And there is altogether no kind of predicament according to which we wish to affirm something, but that we are convicted of denying according to that same predicament, if we wish to prefix the negative particle.[^11]

*Since these things are so*,[^12] if I affirmed substantially by saying "son," I would deny substantially by saying "not son." But because I affirm relatively when I say, "he is a son"—for I refer it to a father—I deny relatively if I say, "he is not a son"; for I refer the same negation to a parent, wishing to show that there is no parent of his. But if what is called "son" is equivalent to what is called "begotten," as we have said before, then what is called "not begotten" is equivalent to what is called "not son." But we deny relatively when we say, "not son"; relatively, therefore, we deny when we say, "not begotten." And what is unbegotten but not begotten? We do not, then, depart from the relative predicament when "unbegotten" is said. For just as "begotten" is said not in relation to itself, but that he is from a begetter, so when "unbegotten" is said, it is said not in relation to itself, but it is shown that he is not from a begetter. Yet within the same predicament, which is called relative, both significations are turned: but what is pronounced relatively does not indicate substance: thus, although begotten and unbegotten are different, it does not indicate a different substance: because, as son is referred to father and not-son to not-father, so begotten must be referred to begetter, and not-begotten to not-begetter.

### Some things are said in God according to substance, some according to relation, some by transference.

**8.** 9. Wherefore let us hold this especially, that whatever that most excellent and divine sublimity is said in relation to itself is said substantially; but what in relation to something[^13] is said not substantially but relatively; and that there is such force in "the same substance" in the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit, that whatever is said of the single ones in relation to themselves is taken not plurally in the sum, but singularly.

For just as the Father is God, and the Son is God, and the Holy Spirit is God—which no one doubts is said according to substance—yet we do not say three gods, but one God, that very most excellent Trinity.

So the Father is great, the Son great, and the Holy Spirit great: yet not three great ones, but one great one. For it is written not of the Father alone, as they perversely think, but of the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit, *you alone are God, the great one*.

And the Father is good, the Son good, and the Holy Spirit good; and not three good ones, but one good, of whom it was said, *no one is good but the one God*. For the Lord Jesus, lest, by the one who had said *good Teacher*, as though addressing him as a man, he should be understood only according to man, therefore did not say, "no one is good but the Father alone"; but, *no one is good but the one God*. For in the name of "Father" the Father is pronounced by himself alone: but in "God," both he and the Son and the Holy Spirit, because the Trinity is one God.

But position, and habit, and places, and times are said of God not properly but by transference and through likenesses. For he is said to sit *upon the cherubim*,[^14] which is said according to position; and *clothed with the deep as with a garment*, which according to habit; and *your years shall not fail*,[^15] which according to time; and *if I ascend into heaven, you are there*, which according to place.

But as for what pertains to acting, perhaps it may be said most truly of God alone: for God alone acts and is not himself made, nor is acted upon, as far as pertains to his substance by which he is God. And so the Father is almighty, the Son almighty, the Holy Spirit almighty: yet not three almighty ones, but one Almighty, *from whom are all things, through whom are all things, in whom are all things; to him be glory*.[^16]

Whatever, then, God is said in relation to himself, and is said of the single persons three times,[^17] that is, of the Father, and the Son, and the Holy Spirit, and at once of the Trinity itself, is said not plurally but singularly. For since for God to be is not one thing and to be great another, but this same thing is for him to be that it is to be great: therefore, just as we do not say three essences, so we do not say three magnitudes, but one essence and one magnitude. "Essence" I say, which in Greek is called οὐσία, which more usually we call substance.

### One essence, three persons.

**8.** 10. They indeed also say υπόστασις; but I do not know what difference they wish there to be between οὐσία and υπόστασις: so that very many of ours who treat these things in Greek speech have been accustomed to say μία οὐσία, τρεῖς ὑποστάσεις, which is in Latin *one essence, three substances*.[^18]

**9.** 10. But because our custom of speaking has now prevailed, that the same is understood when we say "essence" which is understood when we say "substance," we do not dare to say one essence, three substances; but one essence, or substance, and three persons; as many Latins, treating these things and worthy of authority, have said, when they could find no other more apt way by which to enunciate in words what without words they understood. For in truth, since the Father is not the Son, and the Son is not the Father, and the Holy Spirit, he who is also called the *gift of God*, is neither Father nor Son, they are certainly three. And therefore it was said in the plural, *I and the Father are one*. For he did not say, "is one," as the Sabellians say;[^19] but, "we are one." Yet when it is asked "what three," human speech labors in great poverty. Still it has been said, "three persons," not that it might be said, but lest it be passed over in silence.

### In God there are not three magnitudes, nor three great ones.

**10.** 11. As, therefore, we do not say three essences, so we do not say three magnitudes, nor three great ones. For in things that are great by participation in magnitude, for which to be is one thing and to be great another—as a great house, and a great mountain, and a great mind—in these things, then, magnitude is one thing, and what is great from that magnitude is another, and a great house is by no means the same as magnitude. But that is true magnitude by which not only is the house great that is great, and by which the mountain is great that is great, but also by which whatever else is called great is great: so that magnitude itself is one thing, and the things that are called great from it are another. Which magnitude is certainly primarily great, and far more excellently than the things that are great by participation in it. But God, because he is great not by that magnitude which is not what he himself is, so that God should be as it were a participant in it when he is great (otherwise that will be a greater magnitude than God, but there is nothing greater than God), is therefore great by that magnitude by which he himself is that same magnitude. And therefore, just as we do not say three essences, so neither three magnitudes: for this is for God to be, which is to be great. For the same reason we do not say three great ones, but one great one: because God is great not by participation in magnitude, but he is great by himself, who is great; because he himself is his own magnitude. Let this be said also of the goodness, and of the eternity, and of the omnipotence of God, and of absolutely all the predicaments that can be pronounced of God, that what is said in relation to himself is said not by transference and through likeness, but properly: if, however, anything can be said of him properly by the mouth of man.

### What is said relatively in the Trinity. The relation does not appear when "Holy Spirit" is said; but it appears when "Gift" is said.

**11.** 12. But that the single ones are properly said in the same Trinity, are said in no way in relation to themselves, but in relation to one another, or to the creature; and therefore it is manifest that they are said relatively, not substantially. For just as the Trinity is called one God, great, good, eternal, almighty, and the same can be called his own deity, his own magnitude, his own goodness, his own eternity, his own omnipotence: so the Trinity cannot be called Father, except perhaps by transference in relation to the creature, on account of the adoption of sons.[^20] For what is written, *hear, O Israel: the Lord your God, the Lord is one*, is certainly not to be understood with the Son excepted, or the Holy Spirit excepted, whom, our one Lord God, we rightly call also *our Father*,[^21] regenerating us through his grace.

But the Trinity can in no way be called Son.[^22] The Holy Spirit, however, according to what is written, *for God is Spirit*, can indeed be said universally, because the Father is spirit and the Son is spirit, and the Father is holy and the Son is holy. And so the Father, and the Son, and the Holy Spirit, since they are one God, and certainly God is holy, and *God is Spirit*, the Trinity can be called both Spirit and Holy.[^23] But yet that Holy Spirit who is understood not as the Trinity but as in the Trinity, in that he is properly called Holy Spirit, is said relatively, since he is referred both to the Father and to the Son, because the Holy Spirit is the Spirit both of the Father and of the Son.[^24]

But the relation itself does not appear in this name; but it appears when he is called the *gift of God*: for he is the gift of the Father and the Son, because he both *proceeds from the Father*, as the Lord says, and what the Apostle says, *he who does not have the Spirit of Christ is none of his*, he certainly says of the Holy Spirit himself. "Gift," then, "of a giver," and "giver of a gift," when we say it, we say each relatively in relation to the other.

Therefore the Holy Spirit is a certain ineffable communion of the Father and the Son; and therefore perhaps he is so called because the same appellation can fit the Father and the Son. For he himself is properly called what they are called in common: because the Father is spirit and the Son is spirit, and the Father is holy and the Son is holy. That, then, by a name which fits both, the communion of both might be signified, the gift of both is called Holy Spirit. And this Trinity is one God, alone, good, great, eternal, almighty; himself his own unity, deity, magnitude, goodness, eternity, omnipotence.

### In many relatives there is found no word by which the things referred to one another correspond to each other in turn.

**12.** 13. Nor ought it to disturb us, since we have said that the Holy Spirit is said relatively (not the Trinity itself, but he who is in the Trinity), that there does not seem to correspond to him in turn the word of the one to whom he is referred. For not as we say servant of a master and master of a servant, son of a father and father of a son, since these are said relatively, can we say so here too. For we say the Spirit of the Father,[^25] but we do not in turn say the Father of the Spirit, lest the Holy Spirit be understood as his son. Likewise we say the Holy Spirit of the Son,[^26] but we do not say the Son of the Holy Spirit, lest the Holy Spirit be understood as his father. For in many relatives this happens, that no word is found by which the things referred to one another correspond to each other in turn.

For what is so manifestly said relatively as "pledge"? For it is referred to that of which it is a pledge, and a pledge is always a pledge of some thing. When, then, we say the pledge of the Father and the Son,[^27] can we in turn say the Father of the pledge or the Son of the pledge? But when we say the gift of the Father and the Son, we cannot indeed say the Father of the gift, or the Son of the gift; but, that these may correspond to each other in turn, we say the gift of the giver, and the giver of the gift: because here a usual word could be found, there it could not.

### Principle in the Trinity is said relatively.

**13.** 14. The Father, then, is said relatively, and the same is said relatively principle, and perhaps anything else: but Father is said in relation to the Son, while principle in relation to all things that are from him. Likewise the Son is said relatively, and is said relatively both Word and Image; and in all these words he is referred to the Father: but none of these is the Father said.

And the Son is said principle: for when it was said to him, *who are you?* he answered, *the principle, who also speak to you*. But is he the principle of the Father? For he wished to show himself the creator when he said that he was the principle; just as the Father too is the principle of the creature, in that from him are all things. For "creator" too is said relatively in relation to the creature, as "master" in relation to a servant. And therefore, when we say both that the Father is principle[^28] and that the Son is principle,[^29] we do not say two principles of the creature; because both the Father and the Son together are one principle in relation to the creature, as *one Creator*,[^30] as one God.

But if whatever remains in itself and begets something or works it is a principle to the thing it begets, or to the thing it works, we cannot deny that the Holy Spirit too is rightly called principle; because we do not separate him from the appellation of Creator. And it is written of him that he works,[^31] and certainly, remaining in himself, he works; for he is not *changed and turned* into any of the things he works.[^32] And see what he works: *but to each one*, he says, *is given the manifestation of the Spirit for profit. To one indeed is given through the Spirit the utterance of wisdom; to another the utterance of knowledge according to the same Spirit; to another faith in the same Spirit; to another the gift of healings in the one Spirit; to another the working of mighty deeds; to another prophecy; to another the discerning of spirits; to another kinds of tongues. But all these one and the same Spirit works, distributing to each his own as he wills*—certainly as God. For who can work such great things but God? *But it is the same God who works all things in all*. For if we are questioned singly about the Holy Spirit, we answer most truly that he is God; and together with the Father and the Son he is one God.[^33] God, then, is said one principle in relation to the creature, not two or three principles.

### The Father and the Son the principle of the Holy Spirit.

**14.** 15. But in relation to one another in the Trinity, if the begetter is principle to that which it begets, the Father is principle to the Son, because he begot him. But whether the Father is also principle to the Holy Spirit, since it is said, *he proceeds from the Father*, is no small question. Because if it is so, he will be principle not only to the thing he begets or makes, but also to the thing he gives.

Here too there shines out, as it can, that which is wont to disturb many, why the Holy Spirit is not also a Son, since he too comes forth from the Father, as is read in the Gospel.[^34] For he comes forth, not as one born, but as one given; and therefore he is not called Son, because he neither was born as the Only-begotten, nor was made so as to be born into adoption through the grace of God, as we are.[^35] For what was born of the Father is referred to the Father alone when he is called Son, and therefore he is the Son of the Father, not also ours:[^36] but what was given is referred both to him who gave and to those to whom he gave; and so the Holy Spirit is called not only of the Father and the Son who gave, but also ours who received: as it is said, *the Lord's salvation*,[^37] who gives salvation, and the same is also *our salvation*,[^38] who have received it.

The Spirit, then, is both of God who gave, and ours who received. Not that spirit of ours by which we are, because that is the spirit of man which is in him: but in another way this one is ours, in the way that we also say, *give us our bread*. Although that spirit too which is called man's we certainly received. *For what have you*, he says, *that you did not receive?*

But it is one thing that we received in order to be, another that we received in order to be holy. Whence it is written also of John, that he would come *in the spirit and power of Elijah*: the spirit of Elijah was so called, namely the Holy Spirit whom Elijah received. This is to be understood also of Moses, when the Lord says to him, *I will take of your spirit, and will give to them*: that is, "I will give them of the Holy Spirit, whom I have already given to you."

If, then, even what is given has as its principle the one by whom it is given, because it received from nowhere else that which proceeds from him,[^39] it must be confessed that the Father and the Son are the principle of the Holy Spirit, not two principles: but as the Father and the Son are one God, and in relation to the creature one Creator and one Lord, so in relation to the Holy Spirit they are one principle; but in relation to the creature the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit are one principle, as one Creator and one Lord.

### Whether the Holy Spirit was a gift even before he was given.

**15.** 16. But a more inward question is asked,[^40] whether, just as the Son has by being born not only that he is Son, but altogether that he is; so the Holy Spirit too has by that whereby he is given not only that he is a gift, but altogether that he is: whether, then, he was before he was given, but was not yet a gift, or whether, by the very fact that God was going to give him, he was already a gift even before he was given. But if he does not proceed except when he is given, neither would he proceed before there was one to whom he might be given; how, then, was the substance itself already, if it is not except because it is given: as the Son, not only that he is Son, which is said relatively, but altogether that he is, has the substance itself by being born?

Or does the Holy Spirit always proceed, and proceed not in time, but from eternity; but because he so proceeded as to be able to be given, he was already a gift even before there was one to whom he might be given?[^41] For it is understood in one way when he is called a gift, in another when he is called a thing given. For he can be a gift even before he is given; but a thing given cannot in any way be so called unless it has been given.[^42]

### What is said of God in time is said relatively, not accidentally, because the change comes about in the creature, not in God.

**16.** 17. Nor let it disturb us that the Holy Spirit, though he is coeternal with the Father and the Son, is yet said to be something in time, as this very thing which we have called a thing given. For he is the gift of the Spirit sempiternally, but a "thing given" in time.[^43] For even "Lord" is not said except when he begins to have a servant, and this relative appellation too is for God in time: for the creature, of which he is Lord, is not sempiternal. How, then, shall we maintain that even relatives are not accidents, since nothing happens to God in time, because he is not changeable, as we treated at the beginning of this discussion?

Behold, to be Lord he does not have sempiternally, lest we be compelled to call the creature sempiternal too, because he would not rule sempiternally unless the creature also served sempiternally. But just as there cannot be a servant who has no master, so neither a master who has no servant.

And if anyone should arise who says that God indeed alone is eternal, but that times are not eternal on account of their variety and changeableness, yet that times did not begin to be in time (for there was no time before times began, and therefore it was not in time that it happened to God to be Lord, because he was Lord of the times themselves, which certainly did not begin to be in time): what will he answer about man, who was made in time, of whom certainly God was not Lord before there was one of whom he could be? At least, then, to be the Lord of man happened to God in time: and, that all controversy may seem to be removed, certainly to be your Lord, or mine, who only now began to be, happened to God in time. Or if even this seems uncertain on account of the obscure question of the soul, what about being Lord of the people of Israel? For although the nature of the soul which that people had already was—how, we do not ask—yet that people was not yet, and when it began to be is plain. Lastly, to be Lord of this tree and this crop happened in time, which only now began to be. For although the matter itself was already, yet it is one thing to be lord of the matter, another to be lord of a nature now made. For at one time a man is lord of wood, and at another time lord of a chest, though made from that very wood, which certainly did not exist when he was already lord of the wood.

How, then, shall we maintain that God is said nothing according to accident, except because nothing happens to his nature by which it is changed, so that those are relative accidents which happen with some change of the things of which they are said? As a friend is said relatively; for he does not begin to be unless he has begun to love: some change of will, then, comes about, that he be called a friend.

But a coin, when it is called a price, is said relatively, yet it is not changed when it begins to be a price: nor when it is called a pledge, and any such things. If, then, a coin can, by no change of itself, be said relatively so often, that neither when it begins to be so called, nor when it ceases, does any change come about in its nature or form by which it is a coin; how much more easily ought we to take it of that unchangeable substance of God, that something is so said relatively in relation to the creature, that, although it begins to be said in time, yet it is understood not to have happened to the substance of God itself, but to that creature in relation to which it is said? *Lord*, he says, *you have become a refuge for us*.

God, then, is called our refuge relatively, for he is referred to us; and he becomes our refuge then, when we flee to him. Does anything then come about in his nature that was not there before we fled to him? In us, then, some change comes about; for we were worse before we fled to him, and by fleeing to him we are made better: but in him none. So too he begins to be our Father when we are regenerated through his grace, since *he gave* us *power to become sons of God*.[^44] And so our substance is changed for the better when we are made his sons; at the same time he begins to be our Father, but by no change of his substance.

What, then, God begins in time to be called, which before he was not called, it is manifest is said relatively: yet not according to an accident of God, that something happened to him, but plainly according to an accident of that in relation to which God begins to be said something relatively. And that a *friend of God*[^45] begins to be righteous, he himself is changed: but far be it that God should love anyone in time, as though by a new love which was not in him before, with whom neither have past things passed away, and future things are already done.

And so he *loved* all his saints *before the foundation of the world*, just as he predestined them; but when they are converted and find him, then they are said to begin to be loved by him, that it may be said in the way in which what is said can be grasped by human feeling. So too, when he is said to be angry with the wicked, and gentle with the good, they are changed, not he; as light is harsh to weak eyes, gentle to strong, by their change, namely, not its own.
