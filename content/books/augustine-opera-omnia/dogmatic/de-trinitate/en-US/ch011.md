# How the Trinity is to be inquired into.

## Preface

**1.** 1. We are certainly seeking a trinity—not just any trinity, but that Trinity which is God, the true and highest and only God. Wait, then, whoever you are who hear this: for we are still seeking, and no one justly rebukes one who seeks such things, provided that he seeks, most firm in faith, what is most difficult either to know or to express.

But whoever either sees or teaches better quickly and justly rebukes one who makes assertions. *Seek God*, he says, *and your soul shall live*. And lest anyone rashly rejoice as though he had laid hold of him: *Seek his face always*, he says.[^1]

And the Apostle: *if anyone thinks*, he says, *that he knows something, he does not yet know as he ought to know. But whoever loves God, this one is known by him*. He did not even say, “He has known him”—which is a dangerous presumption—but, *is known by him*. So too elsewhere, when he had said, *But now, knowing God*, he at once corrects himself: *or rather*, he says, *being known by God*:

and especially in that passage, *Brethren*, he says, *I do not consider that I myself have laid hold of it: but one thing I do—forgetting what lies behind, and stretching out toward what lies ahead, I follow according to my intent toward the prize of the upward calling of God in Christ Jesus. Let as many of us, then, as are perfect be of this mind*. He calls perfection in this life nothing other than to forget what lies behind and to stretch out, according to one’s intent, toward what lies ahead.[^2] For the intent of one seeking is most safe, until that toward which we tend and toward which we stretch is laid hold of. But that is a right intent which sets out from faith. For a certain faith somehow begins knowledge: but certain knowledge will not be perfected except after this life, when we shall see *face to face*.

Let us, then, be of this mind: to know that the disposition of seeking the true is safer than that of presuming things unknown to be known. So let us seek as though we were going to find: and let us find as though we were going to seek. For *when a man has finished, then he is but beginning*.

Let us doubt with no unbelief about things to be believed, and assert with no rashness about things to be understood: in the former, authority is to be held fast; in the latter, the truth is to be searched out. As far, then, as this question is concerned, let us believe that the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit are one God, the maker[^3] and ruler of all creation: that the Father is not the Son, and the Holy Spirit is neither the Father nor the Son; but that there is a Trinity of Persons related to one another, and a unity of equal essence.[^4] And let us seek to understand this, praying for help from him whom we wish to understand, and, as far as is granted,[^5] seeking to unfold what we understand with such care and solicitude of devotion[^6] that, even if we say one thing for another, we may still say nothing unworthy.

So that if, for example, we say something of the Father which does not properly belong to the Father, it may belong to the Son, or to the Holy Spirit, or to the Trinity itself: and if we say something of the Son which is not properly fitting to the Son, it may at least be fitting to the Father, or to the Holy Spirit, or to the Trinity: likewise if we say something of the Holy Spirit which does not teach[^7] the property of the Holy Spirit, it may yet not be foreign to the Father or to the Son, or to the one God, the Trinity itself. For example, we now desire to see whether that most excellent love is properly the Holy Spirit: and if it is not, then either the Father is love, or the Son, or the Trinity itself; since we cannot resist the most certain faith and the most powerful authority of Scripture, which says, *God is love*: yet we ought not to deviate by a sacrilegious error, so as to say something of the Trinity that befits not the Creator but rather the creature,[^8] or that is fashioned by an empty thought.

### Mind and love.

**2.** 2. Since these things are so,[^9] let us consider those three things which we seem to ourselves to have found. We are not yet speaking of things above, not yet of God the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit; but of this unequal image—an image nonetheless—that is, the human being: for the weakness of our mind perhaps beholds it more familiarly and more easily.

See, I who seek this: when I love something, there are three—I, and what I love, and love itself. For I do not love love unless I love one who loves: for there is no love where nothing is loved.

There are, then, three: the one who loves, and what is loved, and love. But what if I love nothing but myself? Will there not be two—what I love, and love? For the one who loves and what is loved are one and the same, when one loves oneself: just as to love and to be loved are in the same way one and the same, when one loves oneself. For the same thing is said twice, when it is said, He loves himself, and he is loved by himself. For then to love and to be loved are not one thing and another; just as the one loving and the one loved are not one and another.

But love and what is loved are even so two. For whoever loves himself is not love unless love itself is loved. Now it is one thing to love oneself, another to love one’s love. For love is not loved unless it is already loving something; because where nothing is loved, there is no love. There are, then, two when one loves oneself: love, and what is loved. For then the one loving and what is loved are one. Hence it seems not to follow that wherever there is love, three things are thereby understood.

For let us remove from this consideration the other things, which are many, of which a human being consists;[^10] and that we may find clearly what we are now searching for, so far as we can in these matters, let us treat of the mind alone. The mind, then, when it loves itself, displays two things, the mind, and love. But what is it to love oneself, except to wish to be present to oneself for the enjoyment of oneself? And when it wishes to be just so much as it is, the will is equal to the mind, and the love equal to the one loving. And if love is some substance, it is surely not a body, but a spirit: nor is the mind a body, but a spirit. Yet love and mind are not two spirits, but one spirit; nor two essences, but one: and yet the two are one, the one loving and love; or, if you put it so, what is loved, and love. And these two are indeed said relatively to one another. For the one loving is referred to love, and love to the one loving. For the one loving loves by some love, and love is of some one loving. But mind and spirit are not said relatively, but indicate essence. For it is not because it is the mind and spirit of some person that it is a mind and spirit. For when that which is the human being is taken away—which is said by the joining of a body—when, then, the body is taken away, mind and spirit remain: but when the one loving is taken away, there is no love; and when love is taken away, there is no one loving. And therefore, insofar as they are referred to one another, they are two: but insofar as they are said of themselves, each is a spirit, and both together one spirit; each is a mind, and both together one mind. Where, then, is the trinity? Let us attend as much as we can, and let us call upon the eternal light, that it may illumine our darkness, and that we may see in ourselves, so far as we are permitted, the *image of God*.[^11]

### Mind and knowledge.

**3.** 3. For the mind cannot love itself unless it also knows itself: for how does it love what it does not know? Or if anyone says that, from a general or special knowledge, the mind believes itself to be such as it has experienced others to be, and so loves itself, he speaks most foolishly. For from where does the mind know any mind, if it does not know itself? For it is not as the eye of the body sees other eyes and does not see itself; that the mind knows other minds and is ignorant of itself.[^12] For through the eyes of the body we see bodies, because the rays that shine out through them and touch whatever we discern[^13] we cannot bend back and turn upon themselves, except when we look into a mirror.[^14]

This is discussed most subtly and most obscurely, until it is most clearly demonstrated that the matter is either so or not so.[^15] But however it stands with the power by which we discern through the eyes, that power itself—whether they be rays or something else—we certainly cannot discern with the eyes; but we seek it with the mind, and, if it can be done, we even grasp this with the mind. The mind, then, just as it gathers the knowledge of bodily things through the senses of the body, so gathers the knowledge of incorporeal things through itself. Therefore it knows itself also through itself, since it is incorporeal.[^16] For if it does not know itself, it does not love itself.

### The mind, its love, and its knowledge are a certain three, and these three are one, and when they are perfect they are equal.

**4.** 4. Now just as there are two things, the mind and its love, when it loves itself; so there are two things, the mind and its knowledge, when it knows itself. Therefore the mind itself and its love and its knowledge are a certain three, and these three are one; and when they are perfect, they are equal. For if it loves itself less than it is—as, for example, if the mind of a human being loves itself only as much as the body of a human being is to be loved, although the mind is more than the body—it sins, and its love is not perfect. Likewise, if it loves itself more than it is—as if it loved itself as much as God is to be loved, although it is incomparably less than God—even so it sins greatly, and does not have a perfect love of itself. But with greater perversity and iniquity it sins, when it loves the body as much as God is to be loved.

Likewise, knowledge, if it is less than that which is known and can be fully known, is not perfect. But if it is greater, then the nature that knows is superior to that which is known: as the knowledge of a body is greater than the body itself which is known by that knowledge. For that knowledge is a certain life in the reason of the one knowing: but a body is not life. And any life whatever is greater than any body whatever, not in bulk, but in power. But the mind, when it knows itself, does not surpass itself by its knowledge; because the same thing knows and the same thing is known. When, therefore, it knows the whole of itself, and nothing else along with itself, its knowledge is equal to it: because its knowledge is not from another nature, when it knows itself. And when it perceives the whole of itself and nothing more, it is neither less nor greater. Rightly, therefore, we said that these three, when they are perfect, are consequently equal.

### These are one substance, three relatively.

**4.** 5. At the same time we are also reminded, if we can in any way see it, that these things exist in the soul, and, as it were folded up, are unfolded that they may be perceived and counted substantially—or, so to speak, essentially—not as in a subject, like color, or shape in a body, or any other quality or quantity.[^17] For whatever is of that kind does not exceed the subject in which it is. For this color or shape of this body cannot belong also to another body. But the mind, by the love with which it loves itself, can love also something besides itself.

Likewise the mind does not know itself alone, but also many other things. Wherefore love and knowledge are not in the mind as in a subject; but these too exist substantially, just as the mind itself: because, even if they are said relatively to one another, yet each is in its own substance. It is not as color and the colored are said relatively to one another, so that the color is in the colored subject, not having its own proper substance in itself—since the colored body is a substance, but the color is in a substance: rather it is as two friends are also two human beings, which are substances; since human beings are not said relatively, but friends relatively.

### These three are inseparable.

**4.** 6. But again, although the one loving or the one knowing is a substance, knowledge a substance, love a substance, yet the one loving and love, or the one knowing and knowledge, are said relatively to one another, like friends; whereas mind or spirit are not relative terms, just as human beings are not relative; nevertheless, as friends who are human beings can exist apart from one another, the one loving and love, or the one knowing and knowledge, cannot. Although friends too seem able to be separated in body, not in soul, insofar as they are friends, yet it can happen that a friend begins even to hate his friend, and by that very fact ceases to be a friend, while the other does not know it and still loves him. But the love by which the mind loves itself, if it ceases to be, then the mind too ceases at the same time to be loving.

Likewise the knowledge by which the mind knows itself, if it ceases to be, then the mind too ceases at the same time to know itself. Just as the head of someone who has a head is surely a head, and they are said relatively to one another, although they are also substances—for the head is a body, and so is the one who has a head; and if it is not a body, neither will it have a head—but these can be separated from one another by cutting, whereas those cannot.

### These three are of one and the same substance, but confused by no commingling.

**4.** 7. But if there are some bodies that can in no way be cut and divided, yet unless they consisted of their parts they would not be bodies. A part, then, is said relatively to the whole; because every part is a part of some whole, and a whole is a whole by all its parts. But since both the part is a body, and the whole, these are not only said relatively, but also exist substantially. Perhaps, then, the mind is the whole, and as it were its parts are the love by which it loves itself, and the knowledge by which it knows itself, of which two parts that whole consists? Or are there three equal parts, by which the one whole is completed? But no part embraces the whole of which it is a part: whereas the mind, when it knows the whole of itself, that is, knows itself perfectly, its knowledge is through the whole of it; and when it loves itself perfectly, it loves the whole of itself, and its love is through the whole of it. Is it, then, as out of wine and water and honey one drink is made, and each is present throughout the whole, and yet they are three (for there is no part of the drink that does not have these three; for they are not joined, as if water and oil were, but altogether commingled; and they are all substances, and that whole liquid is a certain one substance compounded out of three)—is it some such thing that we must judge these three to be at once, mind, love, knowledge? But water, wine, and honey are not of one substance, although out of their commingling one substance of the drink is made.

But how those three are not of the same essence,[^18] I do not see; since the mind itself loves itself, and itself knows itself; and these three are so that the mind is neither loved nor known by some other thing. Of one and the same essence, therefore, these three must necessarily be: and so, if they were as it were confused by commingling, they would in no way be three, nor could they be referred to one another. Just as, if out of one and the same gold you make three similar rings, although connected to one another, they are referred to one another, because they are similar; for everything similar is similar to something; and there is a trinity of rings, and one gold: but if they are mingled with one another, and each scattered throughout the whole mass, that trinity will fall away, and will not exist at all; and not only will it be called one gold, as was said of those three rings, but there will now be no three golden things.

### These three are each in themselves, and mutually wholes in wholes.

**5.** 8. But in those three, when the mind knows itself and loves itself, there remains a trinity, mind, love, knowledge; and it is confused by no commingling: although each is in itself, and they are mutually wholes in wholes, whether each in the other two, or the two in each. And so *all are in* all.[^19]

For the mind too is surely in itself, since it is called mind with reference to itself: although it is called knowing, or known, or knowable, relatively to its knowledge; and is referred also as loving and loved or lovable to the love by which it loves itself. And knowledge, although it is referred to the mind that knows or is known, is yet also said relatively to itself as known and knowing: for the knowledge by which the mind knows itself is not unknown to itself. And love, although it is referred to the mind that loves, whose love it is, is yet also love with reference to itself, so that it is also in itself: because love too is loved, nor can it be loved by anything but love, that is, by itself. Thus these are each in themselves.

But in one another they are thus: because the mind that loves is in love, and love is in the knowledge of the one loving, and knowledge is in the mind that knows. Each is in two thus: because the mind that knows and loves itself is in its love and knowledge; and the love of the mind that loves and knows itself is in the mind and its knowledge; and the knowledge of the mind that knows and loves itself is in the mind and in its love, because it loves itself as knowing and knows itself as loving. And through this the two also are in each: because the mind that knows and loves itself is, with its knowledge, in its love, and, with its love, in its knowledge: love too and knowledge are at once in the mind, which loves and knows itself.

But how they are wholes in wholes, we have already shown above, when the mind loves the whole of itself, and knows the whole of itself, and knows the whole of its love, and loves the whole of its knowledge, when these three are perfect with reference to themselves. In a wonderful way, then, these three are inseparable from themselves, and yet each one of them is a substance, and all together are one substance or essence, though they are said relatively to one another.

### The twofold knowledge of the mind.

**6.** 9. But when the human mind knows itself and loves itself, it does not know and love something unchangeable: and each person speaks of his own mind in one way, attending to what goes on within himself; but in another way he defines the human mind by a special or general knowledge. And so, when he tells me about his own mind—whether he understands this or that, or does not understand it, and whether he wills, or does not will, this or that—I believe him: but when he says something true about the human mind in a special or general way, I recognize it and approve it.

From this it is plain that one person sees one thing in himself, which another, when he speaks of it, may believe though he does not see it; but another thing in the truth itself, which another too can behold: of which the one is changed through time, the other stands fast in unchangeable eternity. For it is not by seeing many minds with our bodily eyes that we gather, through likeness, a general or special knowledge of the human mind: but we behold the inviolable truth, from which we may perfectly define, so far as we can, not what the mind of each particular person is, but what it ought to be by the eternal reasons.

### On the eternal reasons.

**6.** 10. Hence also the images of bodily things, drawn in through the sense of the body and in a way poured into the memory—from which even things that have not been seen are thought of by a fashioned phantasm, whether otherwise than they are, or by chance just as they are—we are convinced that we either approve or disapprove within ourselves, when we rightly approve or disapprove of something, by other rules altogether, abiding unchangeably above our mind.

For both when I recall the walls of Carthage which I have seen, and when I imagine those of Alexandria which I have not seen, and, preferring some of these same imaginary forms to others, I prefer them reasonably; the judgment of truth flourishes and shines from above, and is firm by its own most incorruptible rules: and even if it is overlaid, as it were, by a kind of cloud of bodily images, it is nevertheless not enveloped and confounded.

**6.** 11. But it makes a difference whether I am shut off, beneath that mist or in it, as it were from a clear sky; or whether, as is wont to happen on the highest mountains, enjoying the free air between the two, I behold both the most serene light above, and the densest clouds below. For from where is the ardor of brotherly love kindled in me, when I hear that some man has borne sharper torments for the beauty and firmness of faith? And if that very man is pointed out to me with a finger, I am eager to join myself to him, to make myself known, to bind myself to him in friendship. And so, if the opportunity is given, I approach, I address him, I converse with him, I express my affection toward him in what words I can, and I wish that there be brought about and expressed in him, in turn, the affection he has toward me, and I strive by believing for a spiritual embrace, since I cannot so quickly track down and discern thoroughly his inner depths. And so I love a faithful and brave man with a chaste and genuine love.

But if, in the midst of our conversations, he should confess to me, or somehow incautiously reveal himself, that he believes things unfitting about God, and desires something carnal in him too, and bore those torments for such an error, whether out of desire for hoped-for money, or out of an empty greed for human praise; at once that love by which I was carried toward him, being offended and as it were struck back, and withdrawn from an unworthy man, abides in that form out of which, believing him to be such, I had loved him. Unless perhaps I now love him to this end, that he may become such, when I have found that he is not such.

But in that man nothing has been changed: yet he can be changed, so that he may become what I had already believed him to be. In my mind, however, it is the very estimation that has been changed, which stood one way concerning him before and stands another way now: and the same love has been bent, by an unchangeable justice commanding from above, from the intention of enjoying to the intention of counseling. But the very form of unshaken and stable truth—both that in which I would enjoy the man, believing him good, and that in which I counsel that he be good—floods, with imperturbable eternity, by the same light of incorruptible and most sincere reason, both the gaze of my mind, and that cloud of phantasy which I behold from above, when I think of the same man whom I had seen.

Likewise, when I turn over in my mind an arch beautifully and evenly curved, which I saw, for example, at Carthage, a certain thing, reported to the mind through the eyes and transfused into the memory, makes an imaginary spectacle. But I behold with the mind something else, according to which that work pleases me; and from which, too, if it displeased me, I would correct it. And so we judge of these things according to that, and we discern that by the gaze of the rational mind.

But those things either, when present, we touch by the sense of the body, or, when absent, we recall their images fixed in the memory, or from their likeness we fashion such things as we ourselves, if we wished and were able, would even build by our work: in one way shaping in our mind the images of bodies, or seeing bodies through the body; but in another way grasping the reasons and the ineffably beautiful art of such figures, above the edge of the mind, by simple understanding.

### In speaking, we beget a word within.

**7.** 12. In that eternal truth, then, from which all temporal things were made, we behold with the gaze of the mind the form according to which we exist, and according to which we do anything, whether in ourselves or in bodies, by true and right reason: and thence we have with us a truthful knowledge of things, conceived as it were a word, and by speaking we beget it within; nor does it depart from us in being born. But when we speak to others, we apply to the word that abides within the ministry of the voice, or of some bodily sign, so that by a kind of sensible reminder something of the same kind may come about also in the soul of the hearer as does not recede from the soul of the speaker. So we do nothing through the members of the body, in our deeds and words by which the conduct of people is approved or disapproved, that we do not anticipate by a word uttered within us. For no one willingly does anything that he has not first said in his heart.

**7.** 13. This word is conceived by love, whether of the creature or of the Creator, that is, either of changeable nature or of unchangeable truth.

### The word is conceived by love, whether of the creature or of the Creator.

**8.** 13. Therefore either by cupidity or by love: not that the creature is not to be loved; but if that love is referred to the Creator, it will no longer be cupidity, but love. For it is cupidity when the creature is loved for its own sake. Then it does not help the one who uses, but corrupts the one who enjoys.

Since, then, a creature is either equal to us or inferior, the inferior is to be used for the sake of God; but the equal is to be enjoyed, yet in God. For just as you ought to enjoy yourself, not in yourself, but in him who made you, so too the one whom you love as yourself. Let us, then, enjoy both ourselves and our brothers in the Lord, and from there let us not dare to send ourselves back, as it were, downward, and to slacken toward our lower selves. But the word is born[^20] when what has been thought out is pleasing, either for sinning or for doing rightly. Our word, then, and the mind from which it is begotten, are joined as by a love between them, and this love binds itself with them as a third, by an incorporeal embrace, without any confusion.

### In the love of spiritual things the word conceived and the word born are the same; in the love of carnal things the conceiving of the word is one thing, the bringing forth another.

**9.** 14. Now the word conceived and the word born are the same, when the will rests in the knowledge itself, which happens in the love of spiritual things. For one who, for example, perfectly knows and perfectly loves justice is already just, even if there exists no outward necessity of acting according to it through the members of the body. But in the love of carnal and temporal things, as in the very offspring of animals, the conceiving of the word is one thing, the bringing forth another. For there what is conceived by desiring is born by obtaining. Since it does not suffice avarice to know and love gold, unless it also have it; nor to know and love eating, or sexual union, unless it also do it; nor to know and love honors and offices, unless they come about.

Yet all these things, even when obtained, do not suffice: *For whoever drinks of this water*, he says, *will thirst again*. And therefore also in the Psalms: *He conceived sorrow, and brought forth iniquity*.

He says that *sorrow* or *toil* is conceived, when those things are conceived which it does not suffice to know and to will, and the soul burns and sickens in want, until it comes to them, and as it were brings them forth. Hence elegantly in the Latin tongue things obtained are called *parta*, and things discovered *reperta* and *comperta*, words that sound as though drawn from giving birth (*partus*). Because *desire, when it has conceived, brings forth sin*.

Hence the Lord cries out: *Come to me, all you who labor and are burdened*: and in another place: *Woe to those who are pregnant and nursing in those days*.[^21] And so, since he referred all things, whether deeds rightly done or sins, to the bringing forth of a word, he says, *By your mouth you will be justified, and by your mouth you will be condemned*; meaning by *mouth* not this visible one, but the inner, invisible one of thought and of the heart.

### Whether only known and loved knowledge is the word of the mind.

**10.** 15. Rightly, then, it is asked whether all knowledge is a word, or only known and loved knowledge. For we know also the things we hate; but the things that displease us are not to be called conceived or brought forth by the soul. For not all things that touch us in any way at all are conceived, so that they may only be known and yet not be called the words we are now dealing with.[^22]

For words are spoken of in one way which hold spaces of time by their syllables, whether they are uttered or thought; in another way everything that is known is called a word impressed on the soul, as long as it can be brought forth from the memory and defined, although the thing itself is displeasing; in another way when what is conceived in the mind is pleasing.

It is according to this kind of word that we must take what the Apostle says, *No one says, ‘Jesus is Lord,’ except in the Holy Spirit*: while, according to another notion of word, this is said also by those of whom the Lord himself says, *Not everyone who says to me, Lord, Lord, will enter the kingdom of heaven*.

Nevertheless, when even those things which we hate rightly displease us and are rightly disapproved, the disapproval of them is approved, and is pleasing, and is a word. Nor does the knowledge of vices displease us, but the vices themselves. For it pleases me that I know and define what intemperance is; and this is its word. Just as in an art the faults are known, and the knowledge of them is rightly approved, when one who knows discerns the form and the privation of a virtue, as to affirm and to deny, and to be and not to be; yet to be deprived of virtue and to fall away into vice is damnable.

And to define intemperance, and to speak its word, belongs to the art of morals: but to be intemperate belongs to that which is blamed by that art. Just as to know and to define what a solecism is belongs to the art of speaking: but to commit one is the fault that is reproved by the same art. A word, then, is what we now wish to discern and to convey: knowledge with love. So when the mind knows itself and loves itself, its word is joined to it by love. And since it loves knowledge and knows love, both the word is in love, and love is in the word, and both are in the one loving and speaking.

### The knowledge of the mind is its image and word.

**11.** 16. But all knowledge according to a form is like the thing it knows. For there is another knowledge according to privation, which we voice when we disapprove. And this disapproval of a privation praises the form, and therefore is approved. The soul, then, has some likeness of the known form, whether when it is pleased with it, or when its privation displeases. Wherefore, insofar as we know God, we are like him: but not like to the point of equality, since we do not know him as much as he knows himself.

And just as when we learn bodies through the sense of the body, some likeness of them comes to be in our soul, which is the phantasy of the memory—for the bodies themselves are not in the soul at all, when we think of them, but their likenesses: and so when we approve these in place of the bodies, we err; for it is an error to approve one thing in place of another—yet the imagination of a body in the soul is better than that form of the body, insofar as the former is in a better nature, that is, in a living substance, such as the soul is: so when we know God, although we are made better than we were before we knew him—and most of all when that same knowledge, being also pleasing and worthily loved, is a word, and that knowledge becomes some likeness of God—yet it is inferior, because it is in an inferior nature; for the soul is a creature, but God is the Creator.

From which it is gathered that when the mind itself knows and approves itself, this same knowledge is so its word as to be altogether on a par with it and equal, and identical: because the knowledge is neither of an inferior essence, like that of a body; nor of a superior, like that of God. And since knowledge has a likeness to the thing it knows, that is, of which it is the knowledge, this knowledge has a perfect and equal likeness, by which the mind itself, which knows, is known. And therefore it is both an image and a word, because it is expressed from that mind, since in knowing it is made equal to it, and what is begotten is equal to the one begetting.

### Why does the mind not beget its love when it loves itself?

**12.** 17. What, then, of love? Will it not be an image? not a word? not begotten? For why does the mind beget its knowledge when it knows itself, and not beget its love when it loves itself? For if it is the cause of its knowing because it is knowable, it is also the cause of its love because it is lovable. Why, then, it has not begotten both, is difficult to say. For this question is wont to trouble people even concerning the supreme Trinity itself, the most almighty creator God, *in* whose *image the human being was made*—people whom the truth of God invites to faith through human speech—why the Holy Spirit too is not believed or understood to be begotten by God the Father, so that he too may be called a son?

This we now try in some way to investigate in the human mind, so that from the inferior image, in which our very nature, as it were questioned, answers us more familiarly, we may direct the more practiced edge of the mind from the illumined creature to the unchangeable light: if, that is, the truth itself shall have persuaded us that, just as no Christian doubts that the Word of *God*[^23] is the Son, so love is the Holy Spirit. Therefore let us return to that image which is a creature, that is, to the rational mind, to question and consider it more diligently on this matter, where the knowledge of certain things, coming to exist in time, which was not there before, and the love of certain things which were not before loved, opens to us more distinctly what we are saying: because for our speech too, which must be ordered in time, a thing that is comprised in the order of times is easier to unfold.

### The solution of the question. The mind and its knowledge and love, a third, are an image of the Trinity.

**12.** 18. First, then, let it be plain that it can happen that something is knowable, that is, that can be known, and yet is not known: but that this cannot happen, that what was not knowable should be known. From which it is clearly to be held that every thing whatever we know begets in us a knowledge of itself. For knowledge is brought forth from both, from the one knowing and the one known.[^24]

And so the mind, when it knows itself, is the sole parent of its own knowledge: for it is itself both the known and the knower. But it was knowable to itself even before it knew itself: yet the knowledge of itself was not in it, when it did not know itself. In knowing itself, then, it begets a knowledge of itself equal to itself: because it knows itself no less than it is, nor is its knowledge of another essence, not only because it itself knows, but also because it knows itself, as we said above.

What, then, is to be said of love, why, even when it loves itself, it does not seem to have begotten its own love as well? For it was lovable to itself even before it loved itself, because it could love itself; just as it was knowable to itself even before it knew itself, because it could know itself. For if it were not knowable to itself, it could never have known itself: so if it were not lovable to itself, it could never have loved itself. Why, then, is it not said, by loving itself, to have begotten its love; just as, by knowing itself, it begot its knowledge? Or is this at least clearly shown thereby, that this is the principle of love, whence it proceeds: for it proceeds from the mind itself, which is lovable to itself before it loves itself; and so is the principle of the love by which it loves itself:

but is it for this reason not rightly said to be begotten by it, as is the knowledge of itself by which it knows itself, because knowledge is what is already found, which is called brought forth or discovered, which inquiry often precedes with the end of coming to rest in it? For inquiry is the appetite of finding—which is the same if you say, of discovering.

But things discovered are, as it were, brought forth: hence they are like offspring; where, if not in the knowledge itself? For there they are formed, as it were expressed. For although the things we find by seeking already existed, yet the knowledge itself did not exist, which we count as offspring being born.

Further, that appetite which is in seeking proceeds from the one seeking, and in a way hangs in suspense, nor does it come to rest in the end toward which it is intent, unless that which is sought, being found, is coupled to the one seeking. This appetite, that is, inquiry, although it does not seem to be the love by which what is known is loved—for this is still going on in order that it may be known—yet is something of the same kind. For it can already be called will, since everyone who seeks wishes to find;[^25] and if what is sought pertains to knowledge, everyone who seeks wishes to know. And if he wishes it ardently and insistently, he is said to be zealous: which is most often said in the case of attaining and acquiring various branches of learning.

The bringing forth of the mind, then, is preceded by a certain appetite, by which, in seeking and finding what we wish to know, the offspring, knowledge itself, is born: and through this, that appetite by which knowledge is conceived and brought forth cannot rightly be called the bringing forth and the offspring; and the same appetite by which one longs for the thing to be known becomes the love of the thing known, while it holds and embraces the pleasing offspring, that is, knowledge, and joins it to the one begetting.

And there is a certain image of the Trinity: the mind itself, and its knowledge, which is its offspring and its word concerning itself, and love as a third, *and these three are one*[^26] and one substance. Nor is the offspring less, since the mind knows itself just as much as it is: nor is the love less, since it loves itself just as much as it knows and just as much as it is.
