# Book VI: Some argued against the Arians from the fact that it is written that Christ is the power of God and the wisdom of God.

**1.** 1. Some think that the equality of the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit is hindered from being understood by the fact that it is written that *Christ is the power of God and the wisdom of God*: so that for this reason the equality does not seem to hold, because the Father is not himself power and wisdom, but the begetter of power and wisdom. And indeed it is wont to be asked, with no slight earnestness, how God is said to be the Father of power and wisdom. For the Apostle says, *Christ the power of God and the wisdom of God*.

And hence some of ours have reasoned against the Arians in this way—those, at least, who first raised themselves up against the Catholic faith. For Arius himself is said to have said: *if he is a son, he was born; if he was born, there was a time when the son was not*:[^1] not understanding that even to have been born is, for God, to be sempiternal, so that the Son is coeternal with the Father, just as the splendor that is begotten by fire and diffused is coeval with it, and would be coeternal if the fire were eternal. Whence indeed the later Arians cast off that opinion, and confessed that the Son of God did not begin in time.

But among the disputations which ours had against those who said, *there was a time when the Son was not*, some inserted also this reasoning: "If the Son of God is the power and wisdom of God, and God never was without power and wisdom, the Son is coeternal with God the Father: but the Apostle says, *Christ the power of God and the wisdom of God*; and to say that God at some time did not have power or wisdom is the part of a madman: therefore *there was not a time when the Son was not*."

## The difficulty that arises from this manner of arguing.

**1.** 2. This reasoning compels us to say that God the Father is not wise except by having the wisdom which he begot,[^2] not by existing through himself as the Father, wisdom itself. Then, if it is so, the Son too, as he is called God of God, light of light, it must be seen whether he can be called wisdom of wisdom, if God the Father is not wisdom itself, but only the begetter of wisdom. But if we hold this, why should he not be also the begetter of his own magnitude, and goodness, and eternity, and omnipotence, so that he himself is not his own magnitude, and his own goodness, and his own eternity, and his own omnipotence, but is great by that magnitude which he begot, and good by that goodness, and eternal by that eternity, and omnipotent by that omnipotence which was born of him, just as he is not himself his own wisdom, but is wise by that wisdom which was born of him?[^3]

For that is not to be feared, that we should be compelled to say that there are many sons of God, besides the adoption of the creature, coeternal with the Father, if he is the begetter of his own magnitude, and goodness, and eternity, and omnipotence. For this calumny is easily answered: that it is not so brought about, because many things have been named, that he is the father of many coeternal sons; just as it is not brought about that he is the father of two, when it is said, *Christ the power of God and the wisdom of God*. For the same is the power which is the wisdom, and the same the wisdom which is the power: so therefore also of the rest, that the same is the magnitude which is the power, and whatever others there are, that have either been mentioned above, or can still be mentioned.

## Whatever they are said which shows their substance, the Father and the Son are said together.

**2.** 3. But if there is said in himself nothing except what is said in relation to the Son, that is, father, or begetter, or his principle; if also, as begetting that which he begets of himself, he is consequently principle to it; but whatever else is said, is said with the Son, or rather in the Son, whether great by that magnitude which he begot, or righteous by that righteousness which he begot, or good by that goodness which he begot, or powerful by that power or strength which he begot, or wise by that wisdom which he begot: but magnitude itself is not called Father, but the generator of magnitude: while the Son, just as in himself he is called Son, which is said not with the Father but in relation to the Father, so not in himself is he great, but with the Father whose magnitude he is; so too he is called wise with the Father, whose wisdom he is; just as the Father is wise with the Son, because he is wise by that wisdom which he begot;[^4] whatever, then, they are said in relation to themselves, is not said of the one without the other, that is, whatever they are said which shows their substance, both are said together.

If these things are so, then neither is the Father God without the Son, nor the Son God without the Father, but both together God. And what was said, *in the beginning was the Word*, is understood: in the Father was the Word. Or if *in the beginning* is said as if it were said "before all things"; what follows, *and the Word was with God*—the Word indeed is taken as the Son alone, not the Father and the Son together, as though both were one Word (for thus *Word* is as *Image*,[^5] but the Father and the Son are not both together an image, but the Son alone is the Image of the Father, just as he is also the Son: for both are not together the Son): but what is added, *and the Word was with God*, it is much that it be so understood: *the Word*, which is the Son alone, *was with God*, which is not the Father alone, but the Father and the Son together God.

But what wonder, if in two things far different from each other this can be said? For what is so different as mind and body? Yet it can be said, "the mind was with man," that is, in man: although the mind is not the body, while man is mind and body together.[^6] So that even what is consequently written, *and the Word was God*, may be so understood: "the Word, which is not the Father, was God together with the Father."

Do we then say it thus, that the Father is the generator of magnitude, that is, the generator of his power, or the generator of his wisdom: but the Son is magnitude, *power, and wisdom*: but God great, almighty, wise, both together? How, then, is it "God of God, light of light"? For not both together are God of God, but the Son alone is of God, namely the Father: nor are both together light of light, but the Son alone is of the light, the Father.

Unless perhaps, to intimate and most briefly to impress that the Son is coeternal with the Father, it was so said, "God of God, and light of light," and anything said in this manner, as if it were said, "this which is not the Son without the Father, of this which is not the Father without the Son, that is, this light which is not light without the Father, of this light the Father which is not light without the Son": so that when it is said, "God," which is not the Son without the Father, and "of God," which is not the Father without the Son, it may be perfectly understood that the begetter did not precede that which he begot.

But if it is so, this alone cannot be said of them, "this of that," in which both together are not. As "Word of Word" cannot be said, because both are not together Word, but the Son alone; nor "Image of Image," because both are not together image; nor "Son of Son," because both are not together Son, according to what is said, *I and the Father are one*. For *we are one* was said; "what he is, that am I too according to essence, not according to relation."

## The Father and the Son are one according to the unity of substance.

**3.** 4. And I do not know whether it is found said in the Scriptures, "they are one," of things whose nature is different. But if several things of the same nature also think differently, they are not one insofar as they think differently. For if they were already one from the fact that they were men, he would not say, *that they may be one, as we too are one*, when he commended his disciples to the Father. But truly Paul and Apollos, because both were men, and thought the same: *he who plants*, he says, *and he who waters, are one*. When, therefore, *one* is so said that "what one" is not added, and several are called one, the same nature and essence, not disagreeing nor dissenting, is signified.

But when "what one" is added, something can be signified that is made one out of several, though different in nature. As soul and body are certainly not one (for what is so different?), unless there be added or understood what one, that is, one man, or one animal. Hence the Apostle: *he who cleaves to a harlot*, he says, *is one body*: he did not say, "they are one," or, "it is one"; but he added, "body," as one body composed by union out of two different things, male and female. And, *he who cleaves to the Lord*, he says, *is one spirit*: he did not say, "*he who cleaves to the Lord* is one," or, "they are one"; but he added, *spirit*. For the spirit of man and the Spirit of God are different in nature; but by cleaving there comes about one spirit out of two different ones, so that without the human spirit the Spirit of God is blessed and perfect, but the spirit of man is blessed only with God.[^7]

And not in vain, as I think, when the Lord said so much and so often in the Gospel according to John about that unity—whether his own with the Father, or ours with one another—he nowhere said, "that we and they may be one": but, *that they may be one*, *as we too are one*. The Father, then, and the Son are one, certainly according to the unity of substance, and there is one God, and one great one, and one wise one, as has been treated.

## The Son equal to the Father in all things that are said of his substance.

**3.** 5. Whence, then, is the *Father greater*?[^8] For if greater, greater by magnitude: but since the Son is his magnitude, neither is that one certainly greater than him whom he begot, nor is that one greater than the magnitude by which he is great: therefore equal. For whence is he equal, if not by that whereby he is, for whom to be is not one thing, and to be great another? Or if the *Father is greater* by eternity, the Son is not equal in any matter whatever. For whence is he equal? If you say by magnitude, that magnitude is not equal which is less eternal, and so with the rest. Or is he perhaps equal in power, but in wisdom not equal? But how is that power equal which is less wise? Or is he equal in wisdom, but in power not equal? But how is that wisdom equal which is less powerful? It remains, then, that if he is not equal in any one matter, he is not equal. But Scripture cries out, *he did not think it robbery to be equal to God*. Every adversary of the truth, then, who is at all held by apostolic authority, is compelled to confess the Son equal to God in some one matter whatever. Let him choose which he will: from this it will be shown to him that he is equal in all things that are said of his substance.

## An example from human virtue.

**4.** 6. For if the virtues that are in the human mind, although each is understood in one way and another, are yet in no way separated from one another, so that whoever are equal, for example, in fortitude, are equal also in prudence, and temperance, and justice. For if you say that these are equal in fortitude, but that one excels in prudence; it follows that this one's fortitude is less prudent, and through this they are not even equal in fortitude, since that one's fortitude is more prudent. And so you will find with the other virtues, if you run through them all with the same consideration. For it is not a question of the strength of the body, but of the fortitude of the mind.

How much more, then, in that unchangeable and eternal substance, incomparably simpler than the human mind, do these things so hold? For to the human mind to be is not the same as to be brave, or prudent, or just, or temperate; for the mind can be, and have none of these virtues. But to God to be is the same as to be brave, or to be just, or to be wise, and whatever you may say of that simple manifoldness, or manifold simplicity, by which his substance is signified.

Wherefore, whether it be so said "God of God," that this name fits the single ones, yet not so that both together are two gods, but that there is one God. For they so cohere together, as the Apostle witnesses comes about even in distant and different substances. For both the *Lord alone is spirit*, and the *spirit of man*[^9] alone is certainly spirit, yet *if it cleaves* to the Lord *it is one spirit*: how much more there, where there is an altogether inseparable and eternal connection, lest it seem absurdly to be said as it were the Son of both, when he is called the Son of God,[^10] if that which is said "God" is said only of both together: or whether whatever is said of God that indicates his substance is said only of both together, indeed of the Trinity itself together? Whether, then, it be this or that, which is to be discussed more carefully, now, as far as the matter goes, it is enough to see that in no way is the Son equal to the Father, if, namely, in anything that pertains to signifying his substance he is found unequal, as we have already shown.

But the Apostle said he was equal. In all things, then, the Son is equal to the Father,[^11] and is of one and the same substance.

## The Spirit the "Love" of the Father and the Son, and equal to them in all things.

**5.** 7. Wherefore the Holy Spirit too consists in the same unity and equality of substance. For whether he be the unity of both, or the holiness, or the love, or the unity because he is love, and the love because he is holiness, it is manifest that he is not one of the two by whom each is joined, by whom the begotten is loved by the begetter and loves his own generator, and that they are not by participation, but by their own essence, nor by the gift of any superior, but by their own proper gift, *keeping the unity of the Spirit in the bond of peace*.

And this, by grace, we are commanded to imitate, both toward God and toward ourselves,[^12] *on which two commandments hang all the Law and the Prophets*. So those three are God, one, alone, great, wise, holy, blessed. But we are blessed *from him, and through him, and in him*; because by his gift we are one among ourselves, but with him *one spirit*, because our soul is cleaved fast behind him. And for us *it is good to cleave to God*, because *he will destroy everyone who fornicates away from him*.

The Holy Spirit, then, is something common to the Father and the Son, whatever it is. Or it is the very communion, consubstantial and coeternal; which, if it can fittingly be called friendship, let it be so called; but it is more aptly called love. And this too is substance, because God is substance, and *God is love*, as it is written. But just as he is substance together with the Father and the Son, so he is great together, and good together, and holy together, and whatever else is said in relation to himself: since for God to be is not one thing, and to be great or to be good another, and the rest, as we showed above. For if love is there less great than wisdom, wisdom is loved less than it is: it is, therefore, equal, so that as great as wisdom is, so much is it loved: but Wisdom is *equal to the Father*, as we argued above: equal, therefore, is also the Holy Spirit; and if equal, equal in all things, on account of the supreme simplicity that is in that substance. And therefore there are not more than three: one loving him who is from him, and one loving him from whom he is, and the love itself. Which, if it is nothing, how is *God love*? If it is not substance, how is God substance?

## How the substance of God is both simple and manifold.

**6.** 8. But if it is asked how that substance is both simple and manifold, the creature must first be considered, why it is manifold, but in no way truly simple. And first the universal body certainly consists of parts; so that there is there one part greater, another lesser, and the whole is greater than any part however large. For both heaven and earth are parts of the universal mass of the world: and the earth alone, and heaven alone, consists of innumerable parts, and in a third of itself is less than in the rest, and in half less than in the whole; and the whole body of the world, which is generally wont to be called by two parts, that is, heaven and earth, is certainly greater than heaven alone or earth alone. And in each body magnitude is one thing, color another, figure another. For with magnitude diminished the same color and the same figure can remain, and with color changed the same figure and the same magnitude can remain, and with the same figure not remaining a thing can be just as large and colored in the same way: and whatever other things are said at once of a body can be changed both at once and severally without the rest. And through this the nature of body is convicted of being manifold, but in no way simple.

The spiritual creature too, such as the soul, is indeed in comparison with body simpler: but without comparison with body it is manifold, itself too not simple. For it is simpler than body for this reason, that it is not diffused by mass through space of place, but in each body it is whole both in the whole and whole in any part of it; and therefore, when something comes about in however small a particle of body which the soul perceives, although it does not come about in the whole body, yet the whole soul perceives it, because it is not hidden from the whole: but yet even in the soul, since to be skilled is one thing, to be unskilled another, to be sharp one, to be retentive another, desire one, fear another, gladness one, sadness another; and since some of these can be without others, and some more, some less, innumerable things, and in innumerable ways, can be found in the nature of the soul; it is manifest that its nature is not simple but manifold. For nothing simple is changeable; but every creature is changeable.

## Nor, because he is a Trinity, is God therefore to be thought triple.

**7.** 8. But God is indeed said in manifold ways to be great, good, wise, blessed, true, and whatever else does not seem unworthily said; but the same is his magnitude which is his wisdom (for he is great not by mass, but by power), and the same goodness which is wisdom and magnitude, and the same truth which is all those things; and there to be blessed is not one thing and to be great, or wise, or true, or good, or altogether to be itself, another.

**7.** 9. Nor, because he is a Trinity, is he therefore to be thought triple; otherwise the Father alone, or the Son alone, will be less than the Father and the Son together. Although it is not found how it can be said either the Father alone or the Son alone; since always and inseparably both that one is with the Son, and that one with the Father: not that both are the Father, or both the Son; but because they are always in one another, neither alone. But because we say that the Trinity itself is also God alone, although he is always with the spirits and holy souls; but we say "alone" what God is, because they are not also God with him: so we say that the Father alone is Father, not because he is separated from the Son, but because both together are not the Father.

## No increase comes to the nature of God from number.

**8.** 9. Since, therefore, the Father alone, or the Son alone, or the Holy Spirit alone is as great as the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit together, he is in no way to be called triple. For bodies grow by their conjunction. For although *he who cleaves to his wife is one body*,[^13] yet a greater body comes about than if it were of the man alone, or of the wife alone.

But in spiritual things, when a lesser cleaves to a greater, as the creature to the Creator, that one becomes greater than it was, not this one. For in those things that are not great by mass, to be greater is to be better. But the spirit of any creature becomes better when it cleaves to the Creator than if it does not cleave: and therefore also greater, because better. *He*, then, *who cleaves to the Lord is one spirit*, but yet the Lord does not on that account become greater, although that one becomes so *who cleaves to the Lord*.

In God himself, therefore, when the equal Son cleaves to the equal Father, or the Holy Spirit equal to the Father and the Son, God does not become greater than each of them; because there is nothing by which that perfection may grow. But perfect is the Father, or the Son, or the Holy Spirit, and perfect God is the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit: and therefore a Trinity rather than triple.

## The Trinity itself is the one true God.

**9.** 10. And since we have shown how it can be said "the Father alone," because there is no Father there but he; that sentence must be considered by which it is said that the *one true God* is not the Father alone, but the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit. For if anyone asks whether the Father alone is God: how will it be answered that he is not, unless perhaps we say thus, that the Father is indeed God, but that he is not God alone; but that God alone is the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit? But what shall we do with that testimony of the Lord? For he was speaking to the Father, and had named the Father to whom he spoke, when he said: *but this is eternal life, that they may know you the one true God*.[^14]

Which indeed the Arians are wont to take thus, as if the Son were not the true God.[^15] These being excluded, it must be seen whether we are compelled to understand, when it was said to the Father, *that they may know you the one true God*, as though he wished to intimate this, that the Father alone too is the true God, lest we should understand God to be nothing but those three together, the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit. Do we then, from the Lord's testimony, call both the Father one true God, and the Son one true God, and the Holy Spirit one true God, and at once the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit, that is, at once the Trinity itself, not three true gods, but one true God?[^16] Or, since he added, *and Jesus Christ whom you have sent*, must "one true God" be understood, and is the order of the words, "that they may know you and Jesus Christ whom you have sent as one true God"?

Why, then, was he silent about the Holy Spirit? Is it because it is consequent that, wherever there is named one cleaving to another in such great peace that through it both are one, from this the very peace too is now understood, although it be not mentioned? For in that place too the Apostle seems as it were to pass over the Holy Spirit, and yet there he is understood, where he says, *all things are yours; but you are Christ's; and Christ is God's*; and again, *the head of the woman is the man; the head of the man is Christ; but the head of Christ is God*.

But again, if God is nothing but all three together, how is *the head of Christ God*, that is, the head of Christ the Trinity, since in the Trinity is Christ, so as to be the Trinity? Or is that which is the Father with the Son the head to that which is the Son alone? For the Father is God with the Son, but the Son alone is Christ: especially because the *Word now made flesh* is speaking, according to whose humility the Father is even greater, as he says, *because the Father is greater than I*; so that this very being God, which to him is one with the Father, may be the head of the man the Mediator, which he alone is. For if we rightly call the mind the principal part of man, that is, as it were the head of the human substance, although man himself is man with the mind; why is not the Word with the Father, which together is God, much more fittingly and much more the head of Christ, although *Christ as man*[^17] cannot be understood except with the Word which *was made flesh*?

But this, as we have already said, we shall consider somewhat more carefully later. But now the equality of the Trinity and the one and the same substance have been demonstrated, as briefly as we could, so that, however that question may stand, which we have deferred to discuss with keener attention, nothing may hinder us from confessing the supreme equality of the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit.

## The properties of the persons according to Hilary.

**10.** 11. A certain man, wishing most briefly to intimate the properties of the single persons in the Trinity, said, *eternity in the Father, form in the Image, use in the Gift*.[^18] And because he was a man of no slight authority in the treatment of the Scriptures and the assertion of the faith—for Hilary put this in his books—having scrutinized as far as I can the hidden meaning of these words, that is, of *Father*, and *Image*, and *Gift*, of *eternity*, and *form*, and *use*, I think that in the word *eternity* he followed nothing except that the Father has no Father from whom he is, but the Son is from the Father so as to be, and so as to be coeternal with him.

For an image, if it perfectly fulfills that of which it is the image, is itself made equal to it, not it to its image. In which image he named form, I believe, on account of beauty, where there is already such great congruence, and first equality, and first likeness, disagreeing in nothing, and in no way unequal, and in no part unlike, but answering exactly to him whose image it is. Where there is first and highest life, for which to live is not one thing and to be another, but the same to be and to live: and first and highest understanding, for which to live is not one thing and to understand another, but that which is to understand, this is to live, this is to be,[^19] one all things: as a perfect Word, to which nothing is lacking, and a certain art of the almighty and wise God, full of all the living unchangeable reasons; and all are one in it, as it is itself one from the One, with whom it is one.

There God knows all the things he made through it, and therefore, though times depart and succeed, nothing departs from or succeeds to the knowledge of God. For these things that were created are not known by God because they were made: but rather were made, or are changeable, because they are unchangeably known by him.

That ineffable embrace, then, of the Father and the Image is not without enjoyment, without love, without joy. That love, therefore, that delight, that felicity or blessedness—if indeed it can be worthily said by any human voice—he briefly called *use*, and it is in the Trinity the Holy Spirit, not begotten, but the sweetness of the begetter and the begotten, with vast bounty and abundance flooding all creatures according to their capacity, that they may keep their order and rest in their places.

## A trace of the Trinity appears in the creature.

**10.** 12. All these things, then, which were made by divine art, show in themselves both a certain unity, and form, and order. For whatever of these there is, is both some one thing, as are the natures of bodies and the dispositions of souls; and is formed by some form, as are the figures or qualities of bodies, and the teachings or arts of souls; and seeks or keeps some order, as are the weights or placings of bodies, and the loves or delights of souls.

It is fitting, therefore, that, *seeing the things that are made, understood through the Creator*, we understand the Trinity, of whom in the creature there appears, as is fitting, a *trace*.[^20] For in that Trinity is the highest origin of all things, and the most perfect beauty, and the most blessed delight. And so those three both *seem to be determined by one another*[^21] *and are infinite in themselves*.

But here in corporeal things, one thing is not as much as three together, and two things are something more than one: but in that highest Trinity one is as much as three together, nor are two something more than one. And they are infinite in themselves. So both the single ones are in the single ones, and all in the single ones, and the single ones in all, and all in all, and all one. Whoever sees this, whether *in part*, or *through a glass in a riddle*, let him rejoice in knowing God, and *let him honor him as God, and give thanks*: but whoever does not see, let him strive through piety toward seeing; not through blindness toward calumniating. For God is one, yet a Trinity.

Nor is it to be taken confusedly, *from whom are all things, through whom are all things, in whom are all things*; nor of many gods, but *to him be glory forever and ever. Amen*.[^22]
