# Book XI: A trace of the Trinity even in the outer man.

## Preface

**1.** 1. No one doubts that, just as the inner man is endowed with understanding, so the outer man is endowed with the sense of the body. Let us strive, then, if we can, to track down in this outer man too some trace of the Trinity, not because he too is in the same way the *image of God*.[^1] For the Apostolic sentence is plain, which declares that the inner man is renewed *unto the knowledge of God*[^2] *according to the image of him who created him*: while in another place he says, *and though our outer man is corrupted, yet the inner is renewed from day to day*.

In this one, then, which is corrupted, let us seek, as we can, a certain image of the Trinity—and if not a more expressive one, yet perhaps one easier to discern. For not in vain is this one too called a man, except that there is in him some likeness of the inner man. And by that very order of our condition by which we have been made mortal and carnal, we handle visible things more easily and, as it were, more familiarly than intelligible ones: since the former are outside, the latter within, and we sense the former by the sense of the body, the latter we understand by the mind; and we ourselves, as souls, are not sensible, that is, bodies, but intelligible, since we are life;

yet, as I said, so great has the custom with bodies become, and so does our attention, slipping back into these in a wondrous way, cast itself outward, that when it has been carried off from the uncertainty of bodies, that it might be fixed in the spirit by a far more certain and stable knowledge, it flees back to these, and there seeks rest where it drew its weakness. To which sickness we must accommodate ourselves: so that, if ever we try to distinguish inner spiritual things more fittingly and to convey them more easily, we may take proofs of likeness from outer bodily things.

The outer man, then, endowed with the sense of the body, senses bodies: and this sense, as is easily noticed, is fivefold; in seeing, hearing, smelling, tasting, touching. But it is both a great deal, and not necessary, that we question all these five senses about what we seek. For what one of them reports to us holds good in the rest as well. And so let us use chiefly the testimony of the eyes. For this sense of the body most excels, and is, in proportion to the difference of its kind, nearest to the vision of the mind.

### A certain trinity in seeing.

**2.** 2. When, therefore, we see some body, these three things—which is most easy—are to be considered and distinguished. First, the thing itself which we see, whether a stone, or some flame, or anything else that can be seen with the eyes; which surely could exist even before it was seen: next, the seeing, which was not there before we sensed the thing set before the sense: third, that which holds the sense of the eyes upon the thing that is seen, as long as it is seen, that is, the attention of the mind. In these three, then, there is not only a manifest distinction, but also a distinct nature.

For first, that visible body is of a far other nature than the sense of the eyes, by whose meeting with it the seeing comes about. And the seeing itself, what else does it appear to be but the sense informed by the thing that is sensed? Although, the visible thing being taken away, there is no such seeing, nor could any at all exist, if there were no body that could be seen: yet the body by which the sense of the eyes is informed, when that same body is seen, is in no way of the same substance as the very form which is impressed on the sense by it, which is called seeing. For the body is separable in its nature from the seeing:[^3] but the sense which was already in the living being, even before it saw what it could see, when it ran upon something visible, or the seeing which comes to be in the sense from the visible body, when it is now joined and seen; the sense, then, or the seeing—that is, the sense not formed from without, or the sense[^4] formed from without—belongs to the nature of the living being, altogether other than that body which we sense by seeing, by which the sense is formed not so as to be a sense, but so as to be a seeing. For if the sense were not in us even before the object of a sensible thing, we would not differ from the blind, while we see nothing, whether in the dark or with closed eyes. But in this we differ, that there is in us, even when we are not seeing, that by which we are able to see, which is called sense: but in them it is not; nor for any other reason than that they lack it are they called blind.

Likewise that attention of the mind, which holds the sense upon the thing we see, and joins the two together, differs in nature not only from that visible thing—since this is mind, that is body—but also from the sense itself and the seeing: since this attention belongs to the mind alone: but the sense of the eyes is called the sense of the body for no other reason than that the eyes too are members of the body: and although a lifeless body does not sense, yet the soul, mingled with the body, senses through a bodily instrument, and that same instrument is called sense. And this, intercepted also by a bodily affliction, when someone is blinded, is extinguished, although the same mind remains, and its attention, the eyes having been lost, does not indeed have the sense of the body which by seeing it might join to a body outside and fix its gaze on that seen body, yet by the very effort it shows that, the sense of the body having been taken away, it could neither perish nor be diminished. For a certain appetite of seeing remains intact, whether this can come about or cannot. These three things, then, the body that is seen, and the seeing itself, and the attention that joins both, are manifest for distinguishing, not only on account of the properties of each, but also on account of the difference of their natures.

### How seeing is begotten from the visible thing.

**2.** 3. And in these, although the sense does not proceed from that body which is seen, but from the body of the sensing living being, to which the soul is tempered in its own wondrous way: yet from the body that is seen the seeing is begotten, that is, the sense itself is formed; so that now there is not only the sense, which can be intact even in the dark, as long as the soundness of the eyes lasts, but also the sense formed, which is called seeing. The seeing, then, is begotten from the visible thing, but not from it alone, unless there be present also one who sees.

Wherefore the seeing is begotten from the visible and the one seeing, so indeed that from the one seeing comes the sense of the eyes, and the attention of the one looking and gazing: yet that informing of the sense, which is called seeing, is impressed by the body alone that is seen, that is, by some visible thing: which being taken away, no form remains that was in the sense, while that which was seen was present: yet the sense itself remains which was there even before anything was sensed; as in water there is an imprint as long as the body itself that is impressed is present; which being taken away, there will be none, while the water remains, which was there even before it took on that form of the body. And therefore we cannot indeed say that the visible thing begets the sense: yet it begets a form, as it were its own likeness, which comes to be in the sense, when by seeing we sense something.

But the form of the body which we see, and the form which comes to be from it in the sense of the one seeing, we do not distinguish by that same sense; since the conjunction is so close that there lies open no place for distinguishing. But by reason we gather that we could in no way have sensed, unless there came to be in our sense some likeness of the body beheld. For not because, when a ring is impressed on wax, no image is therefore made because it is not distinguished except when it has been separated. But since, after the wax is separated, there remains what was made so that it can be seen, therefore it is easily persuaded that the form impressed from the ring was already in the wax even before it was separated from it. But if the ring were applied to a liquid fluid, when it was withdrawn no image would appear:

nor, for that reason, ought reason not to distinguish that there was in that fluid, before it was withdrawn, the form of the ring made from the ring, which is to be distinguished from that form which is in the ring, whence this was made which will not be when the ring is withdrawn, although that remains in the ring whence this was made. So the sense of the eyes does not, for the reason that it does not remain when the body is withdrawn, therefore not have the image of the body that is seen as long as it is seen. And through this, to slower minds it can with the greatest difficulty be persuaded that an image of the visible thing is formed in our sense when we see it, and that this same form is the seeing.

### The matter is shown more clearly by an example.

**2.** 4. But those who have perhaps noticed what I shall mention will not labor so in this inquiry. Often, when we have looked rather long at some lights, and then have closed our eyes, there turn before our gaze, as it were, certain bright colors variously changing among themselves, and shining less and less, until they cease altogether: which we must understand to be the remnants of that form which had been made in the sense when the bright body was seen, varied by failing little by little and, as it were, by degrees. For even the lattices of inserted windows, if we happened to be looking at them, have often appeared among those colors: so that it is plain that this affection was impressed on our sense from the thing that was seen. It was there, then, even when we were seeing, and it was clearer and more expressed; but so closely joined with the appearance of the thing that was being discerned that it could not be distinguished at all; and it itself was the seeing.

Indeed, when the little flame of a lamp is, as it were, doubled by a kind of divergence of the rays of the eyes, two seeings come about,[^5] though the thing that is seen is one. For those same rays, each shining out from its own eye, are affected separately, while they are not allowed to run together upon the body to be beheld equally and conjointly, so that one beholding may come to be out of both. And therefore, if we close one eye, we shall see not a doubled fire, but, as it is, one. But why, the left being closed, that appearance ceases to be seen which was toward the right, and in turn, the right being closed, that one dies away which was toward the left, is both long and not necessary to the present matter to inquire and discuss now.[^6] For what suffices for the question undertaken: unless there came to be in our sense a certain image most like the thing we discern, the appearance of the flame would not be doubled according to the number of the eyes, when a certain manner of discerning has been employed which can separate the meeting of the rays. For from one eye, drawn aside, or pressed, or twisted in any way, if the other is closed, something that is one can in no way be seen doubly.

### The three things that are in seeing differ in nature, yet come together into one.

**2.** 5. Since these things are so,[^7] let us remember how these three, though diverse in nature, are tempered into a certain unity; that is, the form of the body which is seen, and its image impressed on the sense, which is the seeing or the sense formed, and the will of the mind which applies the sense to the sensible thing, and holds the seeing itself upon it.

The first of these, that is, the visible thing itself, does not belong to the nature of the living being, except when we discern our own body. But the second so belongs that it comes to be both in the body, and through the body in the soul: for it comes to be in the sense, which is neither without body nor without soul. But the third belongs to the soul alone, because it is will.

Since, then, the substances of these three are so diverse, yet they come together into so great a unity that the first two can scarcely be distinguished, even with reason intervening as judge, namely the form of the body which is seen, and its image which comes to be in the sense, that is, the seeing. But the will has so great a power of coupling these two that it both applies the sense to be formed to the thing that is discerned, and holds it, once formed, upon it. And if it is so violent that it can be called love, or cupidity, or lust, it vehemently affects also the rest of the body of the living being: and where a more sluggish and harder matter does not resist, it changes it into a like appearance and color.

One may see the little body of the chameleon varied, by a most easy turning, to the colors which it sees. But because the bodily bulk of other animals is not easy for turning, their offspring most often betray the lusts of the mothers, what they have beheld with great delight. For the more tender and, so to speak, more formable the first beginnings of the seeds are, so much the more effectively and capaciously do they follow the intention of the mother’s soul, and the phantasy that came to be in it through the body which it eagerly beheld. There are examples that could be abundantly recalled: but one suffices, from the most faithful Books, of what Jacob did, so that the sheep and goats brought forth offspring varied in colors, by setting before them varied rods in the channels of water, which they gazed upon as they drank at the time when they had conceived.[^8]

### The three in the thinking of memory.

**3.** 6. But the rational soul lives in a deformed way when it lives according to the trinity of the outer man; that is, when to those things which form the sense of the body from without it applies, not a praiseworthy will, by which it may refer these to something useful, but a base cupidity by which it clings to them. Because, even when the appearance of the body which was bodily sensed is taken away, there remains in the memory a likeness of it, to which the will may turn back its gaze, so as to be formed within from it, just as the sense was formed from without by the sensible body set before it. And so that trinity comes about out of memory, and inner seeing, and the will that couples both. And when these three are forced together into one, from that very forcing-together it is called thought[^9] (*cogitatio*).

Nor now is the substance diverse in these three. For neither is that sensible body there, which is altogether distinct from the nature of the living being, nor is the sense of the body there formed so as to become seeing, nor does the will itself act so as to apply the sense to be formed to a sensible body and hold it, once formed, upon it: but in place of that appearance of the body which was sensed from without, there succeeds the memory retaining that appearance which the soul drank in through the sense of the body; and in place of that seeing which was outside when the sense was formed from the sensible body, there succeeds within a like seeing, when from that which the memory holds the gaze of the mind is formed, and absent bodies are thought of: and the will itself, just as outside it applied the sense to be formed to the body set before it, and joined it once formed, so it turns the gaze of the remembering mind to the memory, so that from what that has retained this may be formed, and there may be in thought a like seeing.

But just as by reason the visible appearance by which the sense of the body was formed was distinguished from its likeness which came to be in the formed sense so as to be seeing (otherwise they were so joined as to be supposed altogether one and the same):

so that phantasy, when the mind thinks of the appearance of a body it has seen, although it consists of the likeness of the body which the memory holds, and of that which is formed from it in the gaze of the remembering mind; yet it so appears one and single that two things are not found to be there except by the judgment of reason, by which we understand that it is one thing that remains in the memory, even when we are thinking of something else, and another that comes to be when we remember, that is, return to the memory, and there find the same appearance.

And if it were no longer there, we would say that we had so forgotten as to be unable to recall it at all. But if the gaze of the one remembering were not formed from that thing which was in the memory, the seeing of the one thinking would in no way come about; but the conjunction of both, that is, of that which the memory holds, and of that which is expressed from it so that the gaze of the one remembering may be formed, because they are most alike, makes it appear, as it were, one. But when the gaze of the one thinking has been turned away from there, and has ceased to behold that which was discerned in the memory, nothing of the form which had been impressed will remain in that gaze: and it will be formed from that to which it has again been turned, so that another thought may come about. Yet that remains which it left in the memory, to which it may again be turned when we remember it, and, once turned, be formed, and become one with that from which it is formed.

### How the unity comes about.

**4.** 7. But that will which carries the gaze hither and thither to be formed, and joins it once formed, if it has flowed together wholly into the inner phantasy, and has turned the gaze of the mind altogether away from the presence of the bodies that lie around the senses, and from the senses of the body themselves, and has turned it entirely to that image which is discerned within; so great a likeness of the bodily appearance, expressed from the memory, is poured over it[^10] that not even reason itself is allowed to distinguish whether the body itself is seen outside, or something of the kind is thought of within.

For sometimes people, either allured or terrified by excessive thinking of visible things, have suddenly uttered cries of such a kind, as though they were really in the midst of such actions or sufferings. And I remember hearing from someone that he used to discern in thinking so expressed and, as it were, solid an appearance of a female body, that, feeling himself as it were joined to it, he even had an emission in his genitals.

So great is the power the soul has over its own body, and so much is it able to turn and change the quality of its garment,[^11] how the one clothed is affected, who clings to his garment. Of the same kind of affection is also that by which in sleep we are deluded through images. But it makes a very great difference whether, the senses of the body being lulled, as they are in those who sleep, or disturbed from the inner framework, as they are in the raving, or alienated in some other way, as they are in those who divine or prophesy, the attention of the mind runs by a certain necessity upon the images that present themselves, whether from the memory or by some other hidden power, through certain spiritual minglings of a likewise spiritual substance; or whether, as sometimes happens to the sound and waking, the will, occupied by thought, turns itself away from the senses, and so forms the gaze of the mind with the various images of sensible things, as though the sensible things themselves were being sensed.

But these impressions of images come about not only when the will is intent on such things by desiring; but also when, for the sake of avoiding and guarding against them, the mind is hurried into beholding the things it would flee. Whence not only by desiring, but also by fearing, either the sense is brought upon the sensible things themselves, or the gaze of the mind is brought to be formed by the images of sensible things. And so the more vehement either fear or cupidity has been, the more expressly is the gaze formed, whether of the one sensing from the body that lies near in place, or of the one thinking from the image of the body that is contained in the memory.

What, then, some body in a place is to the sense of the body, this the likeness of the body in the memory is to the gaze of the mind: and what the seeing of the one looking is to that appearance of the body from which the sense is formed, this the seeing of the one thinking is to the image of the body established in the memory from which the gaze of the mind is formed: and what the intention of the will is for coupling the body seen and the seeing, so that there may come to be there a certain unity of three, although their nature is diverse; this the same intention of the will is for coupling the image of the body which is in the memory and the seeing of the one thinking, that is, the form which the gaze of the mind has taken on in returning to the memory: so that here too there may come to be a certain unity out of three, no longer distinct by diversity of nature, but of one and the same substance; because this whole is within, and the whole is one mind.

### The trinity of the outer man is not the image of God.

**5.** 8. But just as, when the form and appearance of a body has perished, the will cannot recall the sense of the one discerning to it: so, when the image which the memory carries has been deleted by forgetfulness, there will be nothing to which the will, by remembering, may turn back the gaze of the mind to be formed.

But because the mind prevails to fashion not only things forgotten, but even things not sensed nor experienced, by increasing, diminishing, changing, and composing at its pleasure the things that have not slipped away, it often imagines something to be so which it either knows not to be so, or does not know to be so.

In which kind we must take care lest it either lie so as to deceive, or hold an opinion so as to be deceived. These two evils being avoided, the imagined phantasms do it no harm: just as the experienced sensible things, retained in memory, do no harm, if they are neither eagerly desired when they please, nor basely fled when they offend. But when the will, leaving better things, wallows greedily in these, it becomes unclean: and so they are thought of both perniciously when present, and more perniciously when absent.

Badly, then, and deformedly does one live according to the trinity of the outer man: because that trinity too, although it imagines inwardly, yet imagines outer things, and brought them forth for the sake of sensible and bodily things to be used. For no one could use these even well, unless the images of sensed things were held in the memory: and unless the greatest part of the will dwells in higher and inner things, and unless those very things that are applied, whether bodies outside or their images within, unless whatever it takes in them it refers to a better and truer life, and rests in that end for the sake of which it judges these things are to be done—what else do we do but what the Apostle forbids us to do, saying, *do not be conformed to this world*?

Wherefore this trinity is not the image of God:[^12] for it comes about, from the lowest, that is, the bodily creature, than which the soul is higher, in the soul itself through the sense of the body. Nor yet is it altogether unlike: for what does not, according to its own kind and its own little measure, have a likeness of God, since God made *all things very good*,[^13] for no other reason than that he himself is supremely good? Insofar, then, as anything is good, to that extent, namely, although far distant, it nonetheless has some likeness of the highest good; and if a natural likeness, surely a right and ordered one; but if a vicious one, surely a base and perverse one. For souls even in their very sins pursue nothing but a certain likeness of God, by a proud and preposterous and, so to speak, slavish liberty.[^14] So neither could sin have been persuaded upon our first parents, unless it had been said, *you shall be as gods*. Not indeed everything that in creatures is in some way like God is also to be called his image: but that alone above which he himself alone is. For that is expressed wholly from him, between which and him no nature is interposed.

### The relations among the three in the former trinity.

**5.** 9. Of that seeing, then—that is, of the form which comes to be in the sense of the one discerning—the parent, as it were, is the form of the body from which it comes to be. But that parent is not a true one; whence neither is this a true offspring: for it is not begotten wholly from it, since something else is applied to the body, that it may be formed from it, namely the sense of the one seeing. Wherefore to love this is to be estranged.[^15]

And so the will which joins both, as it were the parent and the offspring, is more spiritual than either of them. For that body which is discerned is not spiritual at all. But the seeing which comes to be in the sense has something spiritual mixed in, because it cannot come about without a soul. But it is not wholly so: since that which is formed is the sense of the body.

The will, then, which joins both, is recognized, as I said, as more spiritual, and therefore begins to suggest, as it were, the person of the Spirit in that trinity. But it belongs more to the formed sense than to that body from which the sense is formed. For the sense and the will belong to the living being’s soul, not to a stone or any body that is seen. It does not, then, proceed from that, as it were a parent; but neither from this, as it were an offspring, that is, from the seeing and the form which is in the sense. For before the seeing came to be, the will was already there, which by discerning applied the sense to be formed to the body: but there was not yet anything pleasing.

For how could that be pleasing which had not yet been seen? But a thing pleasing is the will at rest. And therefore we can call the will neither the offspring, as it were, of the seeing, because it was there before the seeing; nor its parent, as it were, because it was formed and expressed not from the will but from the body that was seen.

### The end of the will.

**6.** 10. We can perhaps rightly call the seeing the end and rest of the will, with regard to this one thing at least. For it will not, on that account, will nothing else, because it sees something it was willing. Not, then, the will of a human being altogether, whose end is nothing but blessedness;[^16] but with regard to this one thing, for the time being, the will of seeing has no end but the seeing, whether it refers this to something else or does not refer it. For if it does not refer the seeing to something else, but only willed in order to see; there is no need to argue how it may be shown that the end of the will is the seeing: for it is plain. But if it refers it to something else, it surely wills something else, and it will no longer be the will of seeing: or if of seeing, not of seeing this.

As if someone wishes to see a scar, in order thereby to teach that there had been a wound; or wishes to see a window, in order through the window to see those passing by: all these and other such wills have their own proper ends, which are referred to the end of that will by which we wish to *live blessedly*,[^17] and to come to that life which is not referred to anything else, but suffices, through itself, the one who loves it. The will of seeing, then, has the seeing as its end: and the will of seeing this thing has the seeing of this thing as its end. So the will of seeing the scar seeks its own end, that is, the seeing of the scar, and does not reach beyond it: for the will of proving that there had been a wound is another will, although bound to that one, whose end likewise is the proof of the wound. And the will of seeing the window has the seeing of the window as its end: for there is another will linked to this one, of seeing through the window those passing by, whose end likewise is the seeing of those passing by.

But all wills are right, and all bound to one another, if that one to which they are all referred is good: but if it is depraved, they are all depraved. And therefore the connection of right wills is a certain road of those ascending to blessedness, traveled by sure steps as it were: but the entanglement of depraved and distorted wills is a chain by which one who acts thus will be bound, so that he may be cast *into the outer darkness*.[^18] Blessed, then, are those who by their deeds and conduct sing the *song of ascents*:[^19] and *woe to those who draw sins along, as a long rope*.

But the rest of the will which we call the end is such, if it is still referred to something else, as we may call the rest of the foot in walking, when it is set down so that the other may press on from it as one proceeds with steps. But if something so pleases that the will rests in it with some delight; yet that is not yet that toward which it tends, but this too is referred to something else, so that it is reckoned not as the homeland to a citizen, but as a refreshment, or even a lodging, to a traveler.

### The relations among the three in the other trinity.

**7.** 11. But now, in the other trinity—an inner one, indeed, compared with this one in sensible things and the senses, but yet one conceived from it—when now the gaze of the mind is formed not from the body by the sense of the body, but from the memory, when the appearance of the body which we sensed from without has clung in the memory itself, we call that appearance which is in the memory the parent, as it were, of that which comes to be in the phantasy of the one thinking.

For it was in the memory even before it was thought of by us, just as the body was in its place even before it was sensed, so that the seeing might come to be. But when it is thought of, from that which the memory holds it is expressed in the gaze of the one thinking, and by remembering that appearance is formed which is, as it were, the offspring of that which the memory holds.

But neither is that a true parent, nor is this a true offspring. For the gaze of the mind which is formed from the memory when by remembering we think of something does not proceed from that appearance which we remember having seen; since we could not remember them unless we had seen them: but the gaze of the mind which is formed by remembering was there even before we saw the body which we remember; how much more before we committed it to memory? Although, then, the form which comes to be in the gaze of the one recalling comes to be from that which is in the memory; yet the gaze itself does not arise from it, but was there before this. It follows, however, that if that is not a true parent, neither is this a true offspring. But both that, as it were a parent, and this, as it were an offspring, suggest something, whence inner and truer things may be seen more practicedly and more certainly.

**7.** 12. It is now plainly more difficult to discern whether the will which couples the seeing to the memory is the parent or the offspring of either of them: and this difficulty of distinguishing is made by the parity and equality of one and the same nature and substance. For it cannot be done here as outside, where the formed sense was easily distinguished from the sensible body, and the will from both, on account of the diversity of nature which is in all three with respect to one another, of which we discoursed enough above. For although this trinity, of which we now inquire, was brought in from without to the mind; yet it is enacted within, and there is nothing of it besides the nature of the mind itself. In what way, then, can it be demonstrated that the will is neither the parent, as it were, nor the offspring, as it were, either of the bodily likeness which is contained in the memory, or of that which is expressed from it when we remember, since it so couples both in thinking that they appear singularly as one, and cannot be distinguished except by reason?

And this first must be seen, that there can be no will of remembering, unless we hold in the inmost parts of the memory either the whole, or something of the thing which we wish to remember. For what we have forgotten in every way and in every part, no will of remembering arises for: since whatever we wish to recall, we have already recalled to be or to have been in our memory. For example, if I wish to recall what I dined on yesterday, either I have already recalled that I dined, or, if not even this yet, surely I have recalled something about that very time, if nothing else, at least yesterday itself, and that part of it in which one is wont to dine, and what it is to dine. For if I had recalled nothing of the kind, I could not wish to recall what I dined on yesterday.

From which it can be understood that the will of remembering proceeds indeed from those things which are contained in the memory, with the addition at the same time of those which are expressed from there by discerning through recollection; that is, from the coupling of a certain thing which we have recalled, and of the seeing which came to be from it in the gaze of the one thinking when we recalled it. But the will itself, which couples both, requires also something else which is, as it were, near and contiguous to the one remembering. There are, then, as many trinities of this kind as there are recollections, because there is none of them in which these three are not present: that which is stored in the memory even before it is thought of, and that which comes to be in thought when it is discerned, and the will joining both, and out of both and itself as a third completing some one thing.

Or rather is a certain single trinity recognized in this kind, so that we may call generally one thing whatever of bodily appearances lies hidden in the memory, and again one thing the general seeing of the mind recalling and thinking of such things, to the coupling of which two a third, the coupling will, is joined, so that this whole may be some one thing out of a certain three?

### The true account of thinking.

**8.** 12. But since the gaze of the mind cannot behold all the things that the memory holds at one glance together, the trinities of thoughts alternate by turns, yielding and succeeding, and so this trinity becomes innumerably most numerous: yet not infinite, if the number of things stored in the memory is not exceeded. For from the moment each one began to sense bodies by any bodily sense, even if one could add the things he has forgotten, the number would surely be sure and determinate, although innumerable. For we call innumerable not only the infinite, but also those things which are so finite as to exceed the capacity of one counting.

**8.** 13. But from this it can be noticed somewhat more plainly that it is one thing which the memory holds stored, and another which is expressed from it in the thought of the one remembering, although, when the coupling of both comes about, it appears one and the same: because we cannot remember the appearances of bodies except as many as we sensed, and as great as we sensed, and just as we sensed: for the mind drinks them into the memory from the sense of the body: yet those seeings of those thinking, although from those things which are in the memory, are nonetheless multiplied and varied innumerably and altogether infinitely.

For I remember one sun, because I saw one, as it is: but if I wish, I think of two, or three, or as many as I will; but the gaze of the one thinking of many is formed from the same memory by which I remember one. And I remember just so much as I saw. For if I remember it greater or smaller than I saw, I no longer remember what I saw, and therefore do not remember at all. But because I do remember, I remember just so much as I saw, yet I think it greater or smaller at will. And I remember it as I saw it; but I think of it running as I will, and standing where I will, coming from where I will and to where I will. It is ready to hand for me to think of it square too, when I remember it round; and of any color, although I have never seen a green sun, and therefore do not remember one: and as the sun, so the rest.

But these forms of things, since they are bodily and sensible, the mind indeed errs when it supposes them to be outside in the same way it thinks them within, either when they have already perished outside and are still retained in the memory, or when even what we remember is formed otherwise, not by the faithfulness of recalling, but by the variety of thinking.

**8.** 14. Although very often we believe also those who truthfully narrate things which they themselves perceived by the senses. And when we think of these in the very hearing, when they are narrated, the gaze does not seem to be turned back to the memory so that seeings of those thinking may come to be: for we think of these things not when we ourselves remember, but when another narrates:

and that trinity does not seem to be filled out here, which comes about when an appearance lying hidden in the memory and the seeing of the one remembering are coupled by the will as a third. For it is not what was hidden in my memory, but what I hear, that I think of, when something is narrated to me.

I do not mean the very utterances of the speaker, lest anyone suppose that I have gone out into that trinity which is enacted outside in sensible things and the senses: but I think of those appearances of bodies which the narrator signifies by words and sounds; which I surely think of not by remembering, but by hearing.

But if we consider more diligently, not even then is the measure of the memory exceeded. For I could not even understand the narrator, if, although I now heard for the first time the things he says, even strung together, I did not nonetheless remember the single things generally. For one who narrates to me, for example, some mountain stripped of forest and clothed with olive trees, narrates to one who remembers the appearances both of mountains and of forests and of olive trees; which, had I forgotten, I would not know at all what he was saying, and therefore could not think of that narration.

So it comes about that everyone who thinks of bodily things, whether he himself fashions something, or hears, or reads either one narrating past things or one foretelling future things,[^20] runs back to his own memory, and there finds the manner and measure of all the forms which, thinking, he beholds. For no one can at all think either of a color he has never seen, or a shape of body, or a sound he has never heard, or a taste he has never tasted, or a smell he has never smelled, or any handling of body he has never sensed. But if no one thinks of anything bodily except what he has sensed, because no one remembers anything bodily except what he has sensed, then as the manner of sensing is in bodies, so the manner of thinking is in the memory. For the sense receives the appearance from the body we sense, and the memory from the sense, and the gaze of the one thinking from the memory.

### The office of the will.

**8.** 15. Now the will, just as it joins the sense to the body, so it joins the memory to the sense, so the gaze of the one thinking to the memory. But that which conciliates and joins these things, the same also disjoins and separates them, that is, the will. But it separates the sense of the body from sensible bodies by a motion of the body, lest we sense something, or that we may cease to sense: as when we turn our eyes away from what we do not wish to see, or close them: so the ears from sounds, so the nostrils from smells. So too, either by closing the mouth, or by spitting something out of the mouth, we turn away from tastes. In touch also we either withdraw the body lest we touch what we do not wish, or, if we were already touching, we throw it off or repel it. So by a motion of the body the will acts, lest the sense of the body be coupled to sensible things. And it does this as much as it can: for when in this action, on account of the condition of servile mortality, it suffers difficulty, torment follows, so that nothing is left to the will but endurance.

But the will turns the memory away from the sense, when, intent on something else, it does not allow it to cling to present things. This is easy to notice, when often, someone speaking in our presence, by thinking of something else we seem to ourselves not to have heard him. But it is false: for we did hear, but do not remember, the utterances slipping away through the sense of the ears moment by moment, the nod of the will being turned aside, by which they are wont to be fixed in the memory. So we would say more truly, when something of the kind happens, We do not remember, than, We did not hear.

For it happens to readers too, and to me very often, that having read through a page or a letter, I do not know what I have read, and read it again. For, the nod of the will being intent on something else, the memory was not applied to the sense of the body in the way the sense itself was applied to the letters.

So too those walking, with the will intent on something else, do not know by what way they passed: which, if they had not seen it, they would not have walked, or would have walked with greater attention by groping, especially if they were going through unknown places: but because they walked easily, they surely saw: yet because the memory was not joined to that same sense in the way the sense of the eyes was joined to the places wherever they were going, they could in no way remember, even most recently, what they saw.

Now further, to wish to turn the gaze of the mind away from that which is in the memory is nothing else than not to think of it from there.

### Appearance is begotten from appearance in turn.

**9.** 16. In this arrangement, then, when we begin from the appearance of the body, and arrive at the appearance which comes to be in the beholding of the one thinking, four appearances are found born, as it were, by degrees, one from another: the second from the first; the third from the second; the fourth from the third. For from the appearance of the body which is discerned arises that which comes to be in the sense of the one discerning; and from this, that which comes to be in the memory; and from this, that which comes to be in the gaze of the one thinking.

Wherefore the will couples, as it were, the parent with the offspring three times: first the appearance of the body with that which it begets in the sense of the body; and this again with that which comes to be from it in the memory; and this also, thirdly, with that which is brought forth from it in the beholding of the one thinking. But the middle coupling, which is the second, although it is nearer, is not so like the first as the third is. For there are two seeings; one of the one sensing, the other of the one thinking: but that there may be a seeing of the one thinking, there comes to be in the memory, from the seeing of the one sensing, something like it, to which the gaze of the mind may so turn itself in thinking, as the gaze of the eyes turns itself to the body in discerning.

For that reason I wished to commend two trinities in this kind: one, when the seeing of the one sensing is formed from the body; the other, when the seeing of the one thinking is formed from the memory. But the middle one I did not wish to, because it is not usually called a seeing there, when the form which comes to be in the sense of the one discerning is committed to the memory. Everywhere, however, the will does not appear except as the coupler, as it were, of parent and offspring. And therefore, from wherever it proceeds, it can be called neither parent nor offspring.

### On imagination.

**10.** 17. But if we remember nothing except what we have sensed, nor think of anything except what we remember; why do we very often think of false things, when we surely do not falsely remember the things we have sensed: except because that will, which I have already taken care to show, as far as I could, to be the joiner and separator of things of this kind, leads the gaze of the one thinking, to be formed, through the hidden places of the memory as it pleases, and, for thinking of things we do not remember, impels it to take from those things we do remember, one thing from here, another from there;

which, coming together into one seeing, make something that is therefore called false, because it either is not outside in the nature of bodily things, or does not seem to be expressed from the memory, since we remember having sensed nothing of the kind?

For who has seen a black swan? and therefore no one remembers one: yet who cannot think of one? For it is easy to flood with black color that shape which by seeing we have come to know, a color which we have nonetheless seen in other bodies: and because we have sensed both, we remember both. Nor do I remember a four-footed bird, because I have not seen one:[^21] but I behold such a phantasy most easily, when to some winged shape such as I have seen I add two other feet such as I have likewise seen.

Wherefore, while we think of things joined together which we remember having sensed singly, we seem to think of what we do not remember; though we do this with the memory governing, from which we take all the things that we compose in manifold and various ways according to our will.

For not even the very magnitudes of bodies, which we have never seen, do we think of without the aid of the memory. For as great a span as our gaze is wont to occupy over the magnitude of the world, just so far do we extend any masses of bodies whatever, when we think of them as greatest. And reason indeed goes on to greater things, but phantasy does not follow. For reason reports the infinity of number too, which no seeing of one thinking of bodily things grasps. The same reason teaches that the most minute little bodies too are divided infinitely; yet when we have come to those thinnesses or minutenesses which we remember having seen, we can no longer behold slighter and more minute phantasies, although reason does not cease to pursue and divide. So we think of no bodily things except either those we remember, or those formed from what we remember.

### Number, weight, measure.

**11.** 18. But because the things impressed singly on the memory can be thought of in manifold number, measure seems to pertain to the memory, but number to the seeing. For although the multiplicity of such seeings is innumerable, yet to each single thing in the memory there is prescribed an untransgressible measure. Measure, then, appears in the memory, number in the seeings: just as in the visible bodies themselves there is a certain measure, to which the sense of those seeing is most numerously fitted, and from one visible thing the gaze of many discerning is formed: so that even one person, on account of the number of his two eyes, very often sees one thing with a doubled appearance, as we taught above. In these things, then, from which seeings are expressed, there is a certain measure: but in the seeings themselves, number. But the will which joins and orders these, and couples them by a certain unity, and places the appetite of sensing or of thinking, resting, only in those things from which seeings are formed, is like a *weight*. Wherefore I would have given a foretaste that these three, *measure, number, weight*, are to be noticed in all other things as well.

Now for the time being I have demonstrated, as I could and to those to whom I could, that the will, the coupler of the visible thing and the seeing, as it were of parent and offspring, whether in sensing or in thinking, can be called neither parent nor offspring. Whence time admonishes us to seek this same trinity in the inner man, and from this animal and carnal one of which I have so long spoken, who is called the outer, to tend inward.[^22] There we hope to be able to find, according to a trinity, the *image of God*,[^23] with the help of him himself who, as the things themselves indicate, so holy Scripture too bears witness, has arranged all things *in measure and number and weight*.
