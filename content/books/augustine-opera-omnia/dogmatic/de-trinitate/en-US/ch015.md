# Book XIII: The Aim of This Book

**1.** 1. In the twelfth book of this work, above, we did enough to distinguish the office of the rational mind in temporal things, where not only our cognition but also our action is engaged, from the more excellent office of the same mind, which is devoted to the contemplation of eternal things and is bounded by cognition alone. But I think it more convenient to insert something from the Holy Scriptures, by which the two may more easily be told apart.

## In the Opening of John's Gospel Some Things Pertain to Knowledge, Some to Wisdom

**1.** 2. The Evangelist John began his Gospel thus: *in the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God; the same was in the beginning with God. All things were made through him, and without him was made nothing that was made; in him was life, and the life was the light of men, and the light shines in the darkness, and the darkness did not comprehend it. There was a man sent from God, whose name was John; he came for a testimony, to bear witness to the light, that all might believe through him. He was not the light, but came to bear witness to the light. It was the true light that enlightens every man coming into this world. He was in the world, and the world was made through him, and the world did not know him. He came unto his own, and his own received him not. But to as many as received him he gave power to become sons of God, to those who believe in his name, who were born not of blood, nor of the will of the flesh, nor of the will of man, but of God. And the Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us. And we saw his glory, glory as of the only-begotten of the Father, full of grace and truth*[^1].

This whole passage that I have set down from the Gospel has, in its earlier parts, that which is unchangeable and everlasting, whose contemplation makes us blessed; but in what follows, eternal things are recalled mixed with temporal things. And through this some things there pertain to knowledge, some to wisdom, as our distinction laid out earlier in the twelfth book. For the words *in the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God; the same was in the beginning with God. All things were made through him, and without him was made nothing; that which was made in him was life, and the life was the light of men, and the light shines in the darkness, and the darkness did not comprehend it*[^2] call for the contemplative life, and must be discerned by the intellectual mind. And the more anyone advances in this matter, the wiser, without doubt, will he become.

But because of his saying, *the light shines in the darkness, and the darkness did not comprehend it*[^3], there was indeed need of faith, by which one might believe what one did not see.[^4] For by "darkness" he wished to be understood the hearts of mortals, turned away from such a light and less fit to behold it; on which account he adds and says, *there was a man sent from God, whose name was John; he came for a testimony, to bear witness to the light, that all might believe through him*[^5].

This was now done in time, and pertains to knowledge, which is contained in historical cognition. But we think of the man John in an image that has been impressed upon our memory from our acquaintance with human nature. And those who do not believe these things think of him in the very same way as those who do. For to both alike it is known what a man is, whose outer part, that is, the body, they have learned through the body's eyes, but whose inner part, that is, the soul, they hold known in themselves, since they too are men, and through human society—so that they can think of what is said, *there was a man whose name was John*[^6]; for they know names too by speaking and hearing.

But as for what is there, *sent from God*[^7], those who hold it hold it by faith; and those who do not hold it by faith either waver in doubt or mock in unbelief. Yet both, if they are not of the number of those too foolish, who say in their heart, *there is no God*[^8], when they hear these words think of both—both what God is and what it is to be sent by God; and if not as the things really are, at least as far as they are able.

## How We See the Faith That Is in Us

**1.** 3. Now faith itself, which each one sees to be in his heart if he believes, or not to be there if he does not believe, we know in another way: not as the bodies we see with the bodily eyes and, through their images that we hold in memory, think of even when they are absent; nor as those things that we have not seen and somehow form by thought out of the things we have seen, and commit to memory, so that we may return to them when we wish and there discern them—or rather, discern such images of them as we have fixed there—likewise by recollection; nor as a living man, whose soul, even though we do not see it, we infer from our own, and, from the movements of his body, behold the living man even in thought, as we have learned by seeing. Not thus is faith seen in the heart in which it is, by the one whose it is; rather, a most certain knowledge holds it, and the conscience cries it aloud.

Therefore, although we are bidden to believe for this reason, that we cannot see what we are bidden to believe, yet faith itself, when it is in us, we see in ourselves: for faith is present with respect to absent things, and faith is within with respect to things that are without, and faith is seen with respect to things that are not seen, and yet it itself comes to be in the hearts of men in time;[^9] and if out of faithful men they become unbelieving, it perishes from them.

But sometimes faith is also accommodated to false things: for we speak in such a way as to say, "faith was placed in him, and he deceived." Such faith—if indeed it too is to be called faith—perishes from hearts without blame when the truth, once found, drives it out. But desirably the faith concerning true things passes over into those very things. For it is not to be said "it perishes" when the things that were believed are seen. For is it still to be called faith, since faith has been defined in the Letter to the Hebrews, and it has been said that it is *the conviction of things that are not seen*[^10]?

## In the Same Narrative of John Some Things Are Known by the Body's Sense, Some by the Mind's Reason

**1.** 4. Then what follows, *he came for a testimony, to bear witness to the light, that all might believe through him*[^11], is, as we said, a temporal action. For witness is borne in time even concerning an everlasting thing, which is the intelligible light. To bear witness of this John came, who *was not the light, but came to bear witness to the light*[^12]. For he adds: *it was the true light that enlightens every man coming into this world. He was in the world, and the world was made through him, and the world did not know him. He came unto his own, and his own received him not*[^13]. All these words those who know the Latin tongue understand from the things they have come to know.

Of these things some have become known to us through the body's senses, such as a man, such as the world itself, whose magnitude we discern so plainly, such as the sounds of these very words—for hearing too is a sense of the body; but some through the mind's reason, such as what was said, *and his own received him not*[^14]; for it is understood "they did not believe in him," and what this is we have come to know by no sense of the body, but by the mind's reason.

Of the words themselves we have learned, not the sounds, but the significations, partly through the body's sense, partly through the mind's reason. Nor have we now heard these words for the first time; rather we had heard them already, and not only the words themselves but also what they signify we held known in memory, and here we recognized them. For when this two-syllable word is uttered, *mundus* ("world"), since it is a sound, it has become known as a corporeal thing through the body, that is, through the ear; but what it signifies has also become known through the body, that is, through the eyes of the flesh.

For the world, insofar as it is known, is known to those who see. But this word of four syllables, which is "crediderunt" ("they believed"), glides through the ear of the flesh by its sound, since it is a body; but what it signifies is known by no sense of the body, but by the mind's reason. For unless we knew through the mind what "they believed" is, we would not understand what those did not do of whom it was said, *and his own received him not*[^15]. The sound of the word, then, rings without upon the ears of the body, and reaches the sense that is called hearing. The form of a man, too, is known to us in our own selves, and is present without in others to the body's senses—to the eyes, when he is seen; to the ears, when he is heard; to the touch, when he is held and touched; it has also in our memory its own image, incorporeal indeed, but like a body.

The wonderful beauty of the world itself, finally, is at hand without, both to our gaze and to that sense which is called touch, if we touch any part of it; it too has within, in our memory, its own image, to which we return when we think of it whether shut in by walls or even in the dark. But of these images of corporeal things—incorporeal indeed, yet bearing the likenesses of bodies, and pertaining to the life of the outer man—we have already spoken enough in the eleventh book. But now we are dealing with the inner man and his knowledge, the knowledge that is of temporal and changeable things; and when, into the scope of this knowledge, something is taken up even from things pertaining to the outer man, it is to be taken up for this purpose, that something may thereby be taught which aids rational knowledge. And through this, the rational use of the things we have in common with the irrational animals pertains to the inner man; nor can it rightly be said that in this we have something in common with the irrational animals.

## Faith Is a Thing of the Heart; How the Faith of Believers Is One

**2.** 5. But faith—about which, by a fixed plan of our arrangement, we are compelled to discuss somewhat at greater length in this book, faith which those who have it are called faithful, and those who do not have it unbelievers, like those who *received not* the Son of God *coming unto his own*[^16]—although it has *come to be in us from hearing*[^17], yet does not pertain to that sense of the body which is called hearing, since it is not a sound; nor to the eyes of this flesh, since it is not a color or bodily shape; nor to that which is called touch, since it has nothing of bulk; nor to any sense of the body at all, since this is a thing of the heart, not of the body; nor is it outside us, but in our innermost selves; nor does any man see it in another, but each one in himself.

Finally, it can even be feigned by pretense, and thought to be in one in whom it is not. Each one, therefore, sees his own faith within himself; but in another he believes it to be, he does not see it; and he believes the more firmly the more he comes to know its fruits, which *faith working through love*[^18] is wont to produce.

Wherefore to all of whom the Evangelist subjoins and says, *but to as many as received him he gave power to become sons of God, to those who believe in his name, who were born not of blood, nor of the will of the flesh, nor of the will of man, but of God*[^19], this faith is common: not as some bodily shape is common to the sight of all eyes to which it is present—for from that one shape the gaze of all who behold it is in a certain way informed—but as one may say that the human face is common to all men; for this is said in such a way that nevertheless each one has his own.

We say most truly that the faith impressed from one teaching upon the hearts of the several believers who believe this same thing is one; but the things that are believed are one thing, and the faith by which they are believed another. For the former are in things that are said either to be, or to have been, or to be going to be; but the latter is in the mind of the believer, visible only to the one whose it is—though it is in others too, not the same faith, but a like one. For it is not one in number, but in kind; yet on account of the likeness and the absence of any diversity, we call it one rather than many. For when we see two men most alike, we speak of one face and marvel at it in both.

It is therefore easier to say that there were many souls—each one, of course, belonging to each of those of whom we read in the Acts of the Apostles that there was in them one soul[^20]—than that, where the Apostle said *one faith*[^21], anyone should dare to say there are as many faiths as there are faithful. And yet he who says, *O woman, great is your faith*[^22], and to another, *O you of little faith, why did you doubt*[^23], signifies that each one has his own.

But the faith of believers is said to be one in the same way that the will of those who will is one: since even in those who will this same thing, the will of each is visible to himself, but hidden from another, although he wills the same;[^24] and if it shows itself by some signs, it is believed rather than seen.[^25] But each one, conscious of his own mind, does not merely believe this to be his own will, but plainly perceives it.

## Some Acts of Will Are the Same in All

**3.** 6. There is indeed a certain great agreement of the same nature that lives and uses reason, such that, although it is hidden from the one what the other wills, there are nevertheless some acts of will of all that are known even to the individuals; and although each man does not know what one other man wills, in certain matters he can know what all men will.

Hence is celebrated that most witty pleasantry of a certain mime, who, when he had promised in the theater that he would tell at other shows what they all had in mind and what they all willed, and when on the appointed day, with great expectation, a larger crowd had flowed together, with all in suspense and silent, is reported to have said: *you wish to buy cheap and to sell dear*. In this saying of a most trifling stage-performer, all nevertheless found their own consciences, and applauded with wonderful favor him who set before the eyes of all things true, and yet unforeseen in the saying.

But why was so great an expectation aroused at his promising that he would tell the will of all, except that the wills of other men are hidden from a man? But surely this was not hidden from him? Surely it is hidden from no one? And for what reason, finally, except that there are certain things that each one may not unfittingly conjecture about himself in others, by a sharing or an agreement of vice or of nature? But it is one thing to see one's own will, another to conjecture another's, however certain the conjecture. For I hold the founding of Rome to be as certain among human affairs as the founding of Constantinople, although I have seen Rome with my own eyes, while of the other I know nothing except what I have believed on the testimony of others.

And that mime, indeed, whether by looking at himself or by also having experience of others, believed that to wish *to buy cheap and to sell dear* was common to all. But since it is in truth a vice, anyone can attain a righteousness of this kind, or fall in with the plague of some other vice contrary to this, by which he resists this one and overcomes it. For I myself know a man who, when a book for sale had been offered to him, and he perceived that the seller was ignorant of its value and was therefore asking some small sum, gave him the just price, which was much greater, when he was not expecting it.

What if there is also someone possessed by so great a worthlessness that he sells cheap what his parents left and buys dear what his lusts may consume? This extravagance is not, I think, incredible; and if such men are sought, they may be found, or perhaps, even unsought, may turn up—men who, by a worthlessness greater than the stage's, would mock the stage's proposition or pronouncement, buying debaucheries at a great price but selling their estates at a small one. We have also known some who, for the sake of largess, bought grain dearer and sold it cheaper to their fellow citizens.

That too which the old poet Ennius said, *all mortals wish themselves to be praised*[^26], he surely conjectured in others both from himself and from those of whom he had experience, and he seems to have pronounced the wills of all men.

Finally, if that mime too had said, "you all wish to be praised, none of you wishes to be blamed,"[^27] he would likewise have seemed to say something that was the will of all. Yet there are those who hate their own vices, and in the things in which they are displeased with themselves do not wish to be praised by others, and give thanks for the goodwill of those who rebuke them, since they are blamed in order that they may be corrected.

But if he had said, "you all wish to be blessed, you do not wish to be wretched,"[^28] he would have said something that no one would fail to recognize in his own will. For whatever else anyone may secretly will, he does not depart from this will, which is well enough known to all and in all men.

## The Will to Attain and Keep Blessedness Is One in All, but About Blessedness Itself There Is Great Variety of Wills

**4.** 7. But it is a wonder that, although the will to attain and keep blessedness is one in all, there should arise again so great a variety and diversity of wills about blessedness itself[^29]—not that anyone does not want it, but that not all know it. For if all knew it, it would not be thought by some to lie in virtue of the mind; by others, in pleasure of the body; by others, in both; and by others and others, in this place and that.[^30] For as each thing especially delighted them, so in it they set the blessed life.

How, then, do all men so ardently love what not all men know? Who can love what he does not know? as I have already discussed this matter in the books above.[^31] Why, then, is blessedness loved by all, and yet not known by all? Or perhaps all know what it is, but not all know where it is, and from this comes the dispute? As though it were a matter of some place in this world, where each one who wishes to live blessedly ought to wish to live—and not rather that we should ask where blessedness is, just as we ask what it is.

For surely, if it is in pleasure of the body, he is blessed who enjoys pleasure of the body; if in virtue of the mind, he who enjoys this; if in both, he who enjoys both. And so, when one says, "to live blessedly is to enjoy pleasure of the body," but another, "to live blessedly is to enjoy virtue of the mind," do not either both not know what the blessed life is, or not both know it? How, then, do both love it, if no one can love what he does not know?

Or perhaps it is false, what we have set down as most true and most certain, that *all men wish to live blessedly*[^32]? For if to live blessedly is, for example, to live according to virtue of the mind, how does he wish to live blessedly who does not wish this? Should we not say more truly: "this man does not wish to live blessedly, because he does not wish to live according to virtue, which alone is to live blessedly"? Not all, then, wish to live blessedly; rather, few wish this, if there is no living blessedly except living according to virtue of the mind, which many do not wish.[^33]

Will it then be false—a thing of which not even that Academic Cicero himself doubted (though to the Academics all things are doubtful), who, when he wished in the dialogue *Hortensius* to take the beginning of his discussion from some certain thing about which no one would dispute, said, *we certainly all wish to be blessed*[^34]? Far be it that we should call this false. What then? Must it be said that, even if to live blessedly is nothing other than to live according to virtue of the mind, yet he too who does not wish this wishes to live blessedly? This indeed seems too absurd. For it is as if we should say: "even he who does not wish to live blessedly wishes to live blessedly." Who would listen to this contradiction, who would bear it? And yet to this necessity drives us, if it is both true that all wish to live blessedly, and that not all wish to live in the way in which alone one lives blessedly.

## No One Is Blessed Except He Who Both Has All That He Wills and Wills Nothing Wrongly

**5.** 8. Or perhaps this is what can rescue us from these straits: that, since we said that each one there set the blessed life in what most delighted him—as pleasure delighted Epicurus,[^35] virtue Zeno; and so one man one thing, another another—we should say that to live blessedly is nothing other than to live according to one's own delight, and that therefore it is not false that *all wish to live blessedly*[^36], since all so wish as each is delighted? For this too, if it had been pronounced to the people in the theater, all would find it in their own wills.

But this too Cicero, when he had set it before himself from the opposite side, so refuted that those who hold this view should blush. For he says: *but lo, not philosophers indeed, yet men ready to dispute, say that all are blessed who live as they themselves wish*[^37]: this is what we said, "as each is delighted." But presently he added: *that indeed is false. For to will what is not fitting is itself the most wretched thing; nor is it so wretched not to attain what you will, as to will to attain what you ought not*[^38].

Most excellently and most truly said. For who would be so blind in mind, so alien from all light of seemliness and so wrapped in the darkness of disgrace, as to call blessed a man who lives wickedly and basely, with none forbidding, none avenging, and none even daring to rebuke—and on top of this with very many praising him, since, as the divine Scripture says, *the sinner is praised in the desires of his soul, and he who does iniquity is blessed*[^39]—who fulfills all his most criminal and most shameful wishes, blessed, then, because he lives as he wishes? When in truth, though even thus he would be wretched, he would yet be less so if he could have had none of the things he had wickedly willed. For by an evil will, even alone, a man is made wretched; but more wretched by the power through which the desire of the evil will is fulfilled.

Wherefore, since it is true that all men wish to be blessed, and seek this one thing with the most ardent love, and on account of it seek whatever else they seek; and since no one can love what he is wholly ignorant what or of what kind it is, nor can he be ignorant what that is which he knows that he wills—it follows that all men know the blessed life. But all who are blessed have what they will, although not all who have what they will are forthwith blessed; yet forthwith wretched are those who either do not have what they will, or have what they do not rightly will. No one, therefore, is blessed except he who both has all that he wills and wills nothing wrongly.

## The First Condition of Blessedness: That a Man Live Rightly, or Will Nothing Evil

**6.** 9. Since, then, the blessed life consists of these two things, and is known to all and dear to all, what do we suppose to be the cause why, of these two, when they cannot have both, men rather choose to have all that they will than to will all things well even if they do not have them?

Or is it the very perversity of the human race that, although it is not hidden from them that neither he is blessed who does not have what he wills, nor he who has what he wills wickedly, but rather he who both has whatever good things he wills and wills no evil ones—yet, of these two by which the blessed life is made complete, when both are not given, that is rather chosen by which one departs further from the blessed life (for whoever attains what he has wickedly desired is further from it than he who does not attain what he has desired); when rather a good will ought to be chosen and set first, even when it has not attained what it seeks? For he draws near to the blessed man who wills well whatever he wills, and who, when he has attained it, will be blessed.

And surely it is not evil things, but good ones, that make a man blessed, when they do make him so; and of these good things he already has something, and that not to be lightly esteemed—namely, that very good will, who desires to rejoice in the good things of which human nature is capable, and not in the perpetration or attainment of any evil; and who, with a prudent, temperate, brave, and just mind, pursues good things such as can exist even in this wretched life, and attains them as far as is granted, so that even amid evils he is good, and, when all evils are ended and all good things fulfilled, he will be blessed.

## The Second Condition: That a Man Have What He Wills

**7.** 10. And through this, in this mortal life so full of errors and hardships, faith is especially necessary, by which one believes in God. For no good things—and above all those by which anyone becomes good, and those by which he will become blessed—can be found to come into a man or to accrue to him from any source but God. But when, out of this life, the man who in these miseries is faithful and good has come to the blessed life, then there will truly be what now can in no way be, that a man should live just as he wills. For he will not will to live wickedly in that happiness, nor will he will anything that will be lacking, nor will anything be lacking that he has willed. Whatever is loved will be present, nor will what is not present be desired. Everything that will be there will be good, and the most high God will be *the highest Good*[^40], and will be at hand for the enjoyment of those who love him; and—what is altogether most blessed—it will be certain that it will be so forever.

But as it is, the philosophers have made for themselves, as it pleased each of them, their own blessed lives, that by their own virtue, as it were, they might be able to do what they could not do by the common condition of mortals—namely, to live as they willed.[^41] For they perceived that no one could be blessed otherwise than by having what he willed and suffering nothing that he did not will.

But who would not wish that whatever life he is delighted by, and therefore calls blessed, should be so in his own power that he could have it forever? And yet who is in such a state? Who wishes to suffer troubles, even though he wishes and is able to endure them bravely if he does suffer them? Who would wish to live amid torments, even one who is able to live amid them praiseworthily by holding to justice through patience? Those who endured these evils thought them transitory, whether by desiring to have, or by fearing to lose, what they loved, whether wickedly or praiseworthily. For many have through transitory evils striven bravely toward abiding goods. And these indeed are blessed in hope, even when they are amid transitory evils, through which they come to goods that are not transitory.

But he who is blessed in hope is not yet blessed: for he awaits through patience a blessedness that he does not yet hold. But he who, without any such hope, without any such reward, is tortured, however great a tolerance he may bring to bear, is not truly blessed, but bravely wretched. For he is not for that reason not wretched, that he would be more wretched if he also bore his wretchedness impatiently.

Moreover, if he does not suffer those things which he would not wish to suffer in his body, not even then is he to be held blessed, since he does not live as he wills. For—to pass over other things which, with the body unharmed, pertain to the mind's offenses, without which we would wish to live, and which are innumerable—he would surely wish, if he could, so to have his body safe and sound, and to suffer no troubles from it, as to have this in his power, or in the very incorruption of the body: and because he does not have this, but hangs in uncertainty, surely he does not live as he wills.

For although through fortitude he is prepared to receive, and to bear with an even mind, whatever adversity may befall, yet he prefers that it should not befall, and if he can, brings it about that it does not; and so he is prepared for either outcome, in such a way that, as far as in him lies, he chooses the one and avoids the other, and if he runs into what he avoids, he therefore bears it willingly, because what he willed could not come about. So that he may not be overwhelmed, then, he endures; but he would not wish to be pressed.

How, then, does he live as he wills? Is it because he is willingly brave to bear the things he would not wish to have inflicted? Therefore he wills what he can, precisely because he cannot do what he wills. This is the whole of that blessedness of proud mortals—whether to be laughed at or rather to be pitied—who boast that they live as they will, because they willingly and patiently bear the things they do not wish to befall them. For this, they say, is what Terence wisely said: *since that which you wish cannot come about, wish for that which you can*[^42].

Who denies that this was aptly said? But the counsel was given to a wretched man, lest he should be more wretched. But to the blessed man, such as all wish to be, it is neither rightly nor truly said, *that which you wish cannot come about*. For if he is blessed, whatever he wishes can come about; for he does not wish what cannot come about. But this life is not the life of this mortality, nor will it be except when there is also immortality. And if this could in no way be given to man, then blessedness too would be sought in vain; for without immortality it cannot exist.

## Blessedness Cannot Exist Without Immortality

**8.** 11. Since, then, *all men wish to be blessed*[^43], if they wish it truly,[^44] they surely wish also to be immortal: for otherwise they cannot be blessed. And indeed, asked about immortality too, as about blessedness, all answer that they wish for it. But a blessedness of some sort—one rather so called than really such—is sought in this life, or rather is feigned, while immortality is despaired of, without which true blessedness cannot exist. For he lives blessedly, as we said above[^45] and established firmly enough by argument, who lives as he wills and wills nothing evil.

But no one wills immortality wickedly, if human nature is capable of it by God's gift; and if it is not capable of this, neither is it capable of blessedness. For that a man may live blessedly, it is needful that he live. But the man whom, as he dies, life itself deserts—who can have the blessed life remain with him? And when it deserts him, it deserts him either unwilling, beyond doubt, or willing, or neither. If unwilling, how is it a blessed life, which is so far within the will that it is not within one's power? And since no one is blessed by willing or having anything, how much less is he blessed who is deserted, unwilling, not by honor, not by possession, not by any other thing, but by the very blessed life, when he will have no life at all?

Whence, even if no feeling is left by which he might be wretched (for the blessed life departs precisely because his whole life departs), he is nevertheless wretched as long as he feels, because he knows that, against his will, that is being consumed for the sake of which he loves the rest, and which he loves above the rest. The life, therefore, cannot both be blessed and desert one who is unwilling: because no one is made blessed unwillingly; and through this, how much more wretched does it make one by deserting him unwilling, if, were it present to one unwilling, it would make him wretched? But if it deserts one willing, even so, how was it a blessed life, which the one who had it wished to perish?

It remains for them to say that neither is in the mind of the blessed man—that is, that when he is deserted by the blessed life, as through death his whole life deserts him, he neither wills it nor refuses it, but stands toward both with a prepared and even heart. But neither is that a blessed life which is of such a sort as to be unworthy of the love of him whom it makes blessed. For how is it a blessed life, which the blessed man does not love? Or how is that loved, whose flourishing or perishing is received with indifference? Unless perhaps the virtues, which we love thus for the sake of blessedness alone, dare to persuade us not to love blessedness itself. And if they do this, then surely we cease to love even them, when we do not love that for whose sake alone we loved them.

Then how will that opinion be true—so well examined, so tested, so refined, so certain—that *all men wish to be blessed*, if those who are already blessed neither refuse nor wish to be blessed? Or if they do wish it, as truth cries out, as nature compels—nature into which the supremely good and immutably blessed Creator has implanted this—if, I say, those who are blessed wish to be blessed, then surely they do not wish not to be blessed. But if they do not wish not to be blessed, then beyond doubt they do not wish that to be consumed and to perish whereby they are blessed. And they cannot be blessed except while living: they do not wish, therefore, that to perish whereby they live. They wish, therefore, to be immortal, whoever truly either are blessed or desire to be. But he does not live blessedly to whom what he wills is not present: in no way, therefore, will the life be able to be truly blessed, unless it be everlasting.

## But Faith Promises That the Whole Man Will Be Immortal, Not by Human Argument, but by Divine Authority

**9.** 12. Whether human nature is capable of this—which nevertheless it confesses to be desirable—is no small question. But if faith be present, which is in those to whom Jesus *gave power to become sons of God*[^46], there is no question at all.

For of those trying to find these things out by human reasonings, scarcely a few, endowed with great talent, abounding in leisure, and learned in the most subtle doctrines, have been able to arrive at investigating the immortality of the soul alone.[^47] Yet even for this soul they did not find a stable, that is, a true, blessed life: for they said that it returns to the miseries of this life even after blessedness. And those of them who were ashamed of this opinion, and supposed that the purified soul was to be placed in everlasting blessedness without a body, hold such views about the backward eternity of the world that they themselves refute this opinion of theirs about the soul:[^48] which it would be long to demonstrate here, but in the twelfth book of *The City of God* it has been set forth by us, I think, sufficiently.[^49]

But this faith promises that the whole man will be immortal—the man who surely consists of soul and body[^50], and on this account truly blessed—not by human argument, but by divine authority. And therefore, when it had been said in the Gospel that Jesus gave *power to become sons of God to those who received him*[^51], and what it is to have received him had been briefly set forth by saying *to those who believe in his name*[^52], and in what way they became sons of God had been added, namely that[^53] they *were born not of blood, nor of the will of the flesh, nor of the will of man, but of God*[^54]: lest this weakness of men, which we see and bear about, should despair of so great an excellence, there was at once annexed, *and the Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us*[^55], so that by the contrary there might be persuaded what seemed incredible. For if the Son of God by nature became, for the sake of the sons of men, by mercy the son of man—for this is what *the Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us*[^56] means—how much more credible is it that the sons of man by nature should become, by the grace of God, sons of God, and dwell in God, in whom alone and from whom alone they can be blessed, made partakers of his immortality—for the sake of persuading which the Son of God became a partaker of our mortality?[^57]

## The Incarnation of the Word Frees the Minds of Mortals from Despair of Immortality

**10.** 13. Those, therefore, who say, "Did God then lack some other way by which to free men from the misery of this mortality, that he should will his only-begotten Son, God coeternal with himself, to become man, by putting on a human soul and flesh, and, made mortal, to suffer death?"—it is too little so to refute them as to assert that this way, by which God deigns to free us through *the Mediator of God and men, the man Christ Jesus*[^58], is good and befitting the divine dignity: rather we must also show that no other way possible to God was lacking, to whose power all things equally are subject; but that there was not, and ought not to have been, any other way more fitting for healing our misery. For what was so necessary for raising up our hope, and for freeing the minds of mortals, cast down by the very condition of their mortality, from the despair of immortality, as that it should be shown us at what price God valued us and how greatly he loved us?[^59]

And what is more manifest and more splendid a sign of this thing than that the Son of God, immutably good, remaining in himself what he was, and receiving from us for us what he was not—without detriment to his own nature deigning to enter into fellowship with ours—should first, without any evil desert of his own, bear our evils; and so, to those now believing how greatly God loves us, and to those now hoping for what they had despaired of, confer his gifts upon us without any good deserts of ours—nay rather, with even our evil deserts going before—by an undue bounty?

## The Deserts Which Are Called Ours Are Gifts of God

**10.** 14. For even those things which are called our deserts are his gifts. For that faith may work through love,[^60] *the love of God is poured out in our hearts through the Holy Spirit who has been given to us*[^61]. But he was given then, when Jesus was glorified by the resurrection. For then he promised that he would send him, and sent him:[^62] because then, as was written of him, and foretold beforehand, *he ascended on high, he led captivity captive, he gave gifts to men*[^63].

These gifts are our deserts, by which we come to the highest good of immortal blessedness.[^64] *But God*, says the Apostle, *commends his love toward us, in that, while we were yet sinners, Christ died for us. Much more, then, being now justified in his blood, shall we be saved from wrath through him*[^65]. He adds further, and says, *for if, when we were enemies, we were reconciled to God through the death of his Son, much more, being reconciled, shall we be saved in his life*[^66].

Those whom he called first *sinners*, these he afterward called *enemies of God*; and those whom he first called *justified in the blood of Jesus Christ*, these he afterward called *reconciled through the death of the Son of God*; and those whom he first called *saved from wrath through him*, these afterward *saved in his life*. Not, therefore, before this grace were we sinners in just any way, but we were in such sins that we were enemies of God.

But above, the same Apostle called us, sinners and enemies of God, by two names again and again: by one as it were most gentle, by the other plainly most harsh, saying, *for if Christ, while we were yet weak, in due time died for the ungodly*[^67]. Those whom he called *weak*, the same he named *ungodly*. Weakness seems something slight; but sometimes it is such that it is named ungodliness. Yet unless it were weakness, it would not need a physician: who is, in Hebrew, Jesus,[^68] in Greek Soter, but in our speech *savior*. This word the Latin tongue did not have before, but could have, just as it could have it when it wished.

But this preceding statement of the Apostle, where he says, *while we were yet weak, in due time he died for the ungodly*[^69], coheres with these two following ones, in one of which he said *sinners*, in the other *enemies of God*, as though he had assigned to each of those single things—referring *sinners* to *weak*, and *enemies of God* to *ungodly*.

## A Difficulty Concerning Justification

**11.** 15. But what is it, *justified in his blood*[^70]? What power, I beg, is there in this blood, that believers should be justified in it? And what is it, *reconciled through the death of his Son*[^71]? Was it really so, that when God the Father was angry with us, he saw the death of his Son for us, and was appeased toward us? Was his Son then already so far appeased toward us that he even deigned to die for us, while the Father was still so far angry that, unless the Son died for us, he would not be appeased?

And what is it that, in another place, this same teacher of the Gentiles says: *what then shall we say to these things? If God is for us, who is against us? He who did not spare his own Son, but gave him up for us all, how has he not with him also given us all things*[^72]? Surely, unless the Father were already appeased, he would not, not sparing his own Son, give him up for us? Does not this opinion seem, as it were, contrary to that one? In the one the Son dies for us, and the Father is reconciled to us through his death;[^73] but in this one, as though the Father *first loved us*[^74], he himself, for our sake, does not spare the Son, he himself gives him up for us to death.[^75]

But I see that the Father loved us even before—not only before the Son died for us, but before he founded the world,[^76] with the Apostle himself as witness, who says: *as he chose us in him before the foundation of the world*[^77]. Nor was the Son, while the Father did not spare him, given up for us as it were unwilling, since of him too it was said, *who loved me, and gave himself up for me*[^78]. All things, therefore, the Father and the Son and the Spirit of both work together, equally and in concord: yet we were *justified in the blood of Christ, and reconciled to God through the death of his Son*[^79]; and how this was done, I will explain even here, as I can, as far as shall seem sufficient.

## On Account of Adam's Sin, by the Just Judgment of God, the Human Race Was Handed Over into the Power of the Devil

**12.** 16. By a certain justice of God the human race was handed over into the power of the devil, the sin of the first man passing over originally into all who are born by the mingling of both sexes, and binding all posterity by the debt of the first parents. This handing-over was first signified in Genesis, where, when it had been said to the serpent, *you shall eat earth*[^80], it was said to the man, *earth you are, and into the earth you shall go*[^81]. By the words *into the earth you shall go*, the death of the body was foretold, since he would not have experienced even that, had he remained as he was made, upright; but by saying to him while living, *earth you are*, he showed that the whole man was changed for the worse. For *earth you are* is of the same sort as that other, *my Spirit shall not remain in these men, since they are flesh*[^82]. Then, therefore, he showed that he had been handed over to him to whom it had been said, *you shall eat earth*[^83].

But the Apostle proclaims this more openly, where he says: *and you, when you were dead in your trespasses and sins, in which you once walked according to the age of this world, according to the prince of the power of the air, the spirit who now works in the sons of unbelief, among whom we all also once conducted ourselves in the desires of our flesh, doing the wishes of the flesh and of the affections; and we were by nature children of wrath, like the rest*[^84]. The sons of unbelief are the unbelievers; and who is not this before he becomes faithful? Wherefore all men, from their origin, are under the prince of the power of the air, *who works in the sons of unbelief*[^85]. And what I said, "from their origin," is what the Apostle says, that he too had been *by nature* like the rest: by nature, that is, as it is depraved by sin, not as it was created upright in the beginning.

But this manner by which man was handed over into the power of the devil ought not to be understood as though God did this, or commanded it to be done: but that he only permitted it, yet justly. For when he deserted the sinner, the author of sin at once invaded. Nor indeed did God so desert his creature that he did not show himself to it as God creating and giving life, and, amid penal evils, also bestowing many good things upon the evil. For he did not *shut up his mercies in his anger*[^86].

Nor did he let man go from the law of his power, when he permitted him to be in the power of the devil: since the devil himself is not alien from the power of the Almighty, just as neither from his goodness. For whence would even the malign angels have any sort of subsisting life, except through him *who gives life to all things*[^87]? If, then, the committing of sins, through the just anger of God, subjected man to the devil, surely the remission of sins, through the kindly reconciliation of God, rescues man from the devil.

## The Devil Was to Be Overcome Not by God's Power, but by Justice

**13.** 17. But the devil was to be overcome not by the power of God, but by justice. For what is more powerful than the Almighty? Or what creature's power can be compared to the power of the Creator? But since the devil, by the vice of his own perversity, became a lover of power and a deserter and assailant of justice—for so men too imitate him the more, the more they, with justice neglected or even hated, are zealous for power, and either rejoice in attaining it or are inflamed with desire of it—it pleased God that, for the sake of rescuing man from the power of the devil, the devil should be conquered not by power, but by justice; and that so men too, imitating Christ, should seek to conquer the devil by justice, not by power. Not that power is to be shunned as though it were something evil; but the order is to be kept, by which justice is first.

For how great can the power of mortals be? Let mortals, then, hold to justice; power will be given to the immortals. Compared to which, however great the power of those men who are called powerful on earth, it is found to be a laughable weakness, and there *a pit is dug for the sinner* where the wicked seem to have very great power. But the just man sings and says: *blessed is the man whom you instruct, O Lord, and teach out of your law: that you may give him rest from days of evil, until a pit be dug for the sinner. For the Lord will not cast off his people, and he will not forsake his inheritance, until justice be turned into judgment, and all who have it are upright in heart*[^88]. In this time, then, in which the power of the people of God is deferred, *the Lord will not cast off his people, and he will not forsake his inheritance*, however bitter and unworthy the things which it, humble and weak, may suffer, *until justice*—which now the weakness of the pious has—*be turned into judgment*, that is, receive the power of judging: which is kept for the just in the end, when, in its own order, power shall have followed the justice that goes before. For power joined to justice, or justice approaching power, makes judiciary power. But justice belongs to the good will: whence it was said by the angels at Christ's birth, *glory to God in the highest, and on earth peace to men of good will*[^89]. But power ought to follow justice, not to go before:

and therefore it is also placed among "second things," that is, prosperous things; for "second things" (*secundae*) are so called from "following" (*sequendo*). For since two things make a man blessed, as we discussed above[^90]—to will well, and to be able to do what you will—there ought not to be that perversity which was noted in that same discussion, that, of the two things which make a man blessed, he should choose to be able to do what he wills and neglect to will what he ought; whereas he ought first to have a good will, but great power afterward.

Moreover, a good will is to be purified from vices, by which, if a man is overcome, he is overcome to this end, that he will evilly; and how then will his will still be good? It is therefore to be wished that power be given now, but against the vices, for the conquering of which men do not wish to be powerful, but wish to be powerful for the conquering of men; and to what end this, except that, truly conquered themselves, they may falsely conquer, and be victors not in truth, but in opinion?

Let a man wish to be prudent, wish to be brave, wish to be temperate, wish to be just; and that he may truly be able to be these, let him plainly wish for power, and seek to be powerful in himself, and, in a wonderful way, against himself for himself. But the other things which he wills well, and yet cannot have, such as immortality and true and full happiness, let him not cease to desire, and let him patiently await them.

## The Death of Christ Was Undue

**14.** 18. What, then, is the justice by which the devil was overcome? What, if not the justice of Jesus Christ? And how was he overcome? Because, although he found in him nothing worthy of death, he yet killed him. And surely it is just that the debtors whom he held should be let go free, believing in him whom, without any debt, he killed. This is what we are said to be justified by, *in the blood of Christ*.[^91] For so that innocent blood was poured out *unto the remission* of our *sins*.[^92] Whence he says of himself in the Psalms that he is *free among the dead*[^93]. For he alone among the dead is free from the debt of death. Hence too, in another Psalm, he says, *what I did not seize, then I was paying back*[^94]: wishing "seizure" to be understood as sin, since it is a usurping of what is unlawful. Whence, through the mouth of his own flesh as well, as is read in the Gospel, he says, *behold, the prince of this world comes, and in me he finds nothing*[^95], that is, no sin; *but that all may know*, he says, *that I do the will of my Father, arise, let us go hence*[^96]. And from there he goes on to his Passion, that for us debtors he might pay back what he himself did not owe.

Would the devil have been overcome by this most just law, if Christ had wished to deal with him by power, not by justice? But he set aside what he could do, that he might first do what he ought. And for this reason it was needful that he be both man and God. For unless he were man, he could not be killed; unless he were God, we would not believe that he was unwilling to do what he could, but unable to do what he willed; nor that justice had been preferred by him to power, but rather we would suppose that power was lacking to him.

But as it is, he suffered human things for us, because he was man; but had he been unwilling, he could even have not suffered this, because he was also God. Therefore justice was made more pleasing in humility, because so great a power in his divinity could, had he been unwilling, have not undergone the humility; and so, by one dying who was so powerful, to us mortals who are powerless there was both commended justice and promised power. For of these two he did the one by dying, the other by rising again. For what is more just than to come *even to the death of the cross*[^97] for justice? And what is more powerful than to rise from the dead, and to ascend into heaven with the very flesh in which he was killed?

And so justice first, and power afterward, overcame the devil: justice, namely, because he *had no sin*[^98] and was most unjustly killed by him; but power, because *having been dead he lived again, never afterward to die*[^99]. But he would have overcome the devil by power, even if he could not have been killed by him: although it is of greater power to overcome death itself by rising again than to avoid it by living. But there is another reason why we are justified *in the blood of Christ*, when through the remission of sins we are rescued from the power of the devil: this belongs to the fact that the devil is conquered by Christ by justice, not by power. For Christ was crucified out of the weakness which he took on in mortal flesh, not out of immortal power:[^100] of which weakness nevertheless the Apostle says, *that which is weak of God is stronger than men*[^101].

**15.** 19. It is therefore not difficult to see the devil overcome, when he who was killed by him rose again. That is the greater thing, and deeper for understanding: to see the devil overcome when he seemed to himself to have conquered, that is, when Christ was killed. For then that blood, since it was the blood of him who *had no sin* at all,[^102] was poured out *unto the remission* of our *sins*,[^103] so that, because the devil deservedly held those whom he had bound, as guilty of sin, by the condition of death, he should deservedly let them go through him whom, guilty of no sin, he undeservedly afflicted with the penalty of death. Overcome by this justice, the strong one *was bound with this bond*, that *his vessels might be snatched away*—vessels which had been with him and his angels *vessels of wrath*, and were turned into *vessels of mercy*.[^104]

For these very words of our Lord Jesus Christ, spoken from heaven to him when he was first called, the apostle Paul relates.[^105] For among the other things he heard, he speaks thus of what was said to him: *for to this end I have appeared to you, to appoint you a minister and a witness of the things you see from me, and of those for which I will also appear to you, delivering you from the people and from the nations, to whom I send you to open the eyes of the blind, that they may be turned from darkness, and from the power of Satan to God, that they may receive remission of sins, and a lot among those who are sanctified, and the faith that is in me*[^106].

Whence the same Apostle too, exhorting believers to give thanks to God the Father, says: *who has rescued us from the power of darkness, and has transferred us into the kingdom of the Son of his love, in whom we have redemption unto the remission of sins*[^107]. In this redemption the blood of Christ was given as it were a price for us, on receiving which the devil was not enriched but bound: that we might be loosed from his bonds, and that he might not drag any one of those whom Christ, free from all debt, redeemed by undeservedly pouring out his blood—entangled in the nets of sins—to the doom of the second and everlasting death;[^108] but that they should die only thus far, belonging to the grace of Christ, *foreknown*[^109] and predestined[^110] and chosen *before the foundation of the world*[^111], insofar as Christ himself died for them, in the death of the flesh only, not of the spirit.[^112]

## The Evils of This Age Profit the Elect

**16.** 20. For although even the death of the flesh itself came originally from the sin of the first man,[^113] yet its good use has made the most glorious martyrs. And therefore not only it, but all the evils of this age—the pains and labors of men—although they come from the deserts of sins, and especially of original sin, whence this very life too was made bound by the bond of death, yet, even when sins are remitted, they had to remain, that with them man might contend for the truth, and from them the virtue of the faithful might be exercised: so that the new man, through the new covenant, might be prepared, amid the evils of this age, for a new age, wisely tolerating the misery that this condemned life has deserved, and prudently rejoicing because it will be ended; but faithfully and patiently awaiting the blessedness which the liberated future life will have without end.

For the devil, cast out from his dominion and from the hearts of the faithful—in whose condemnation and unbelief, although himself condemned, he yet reigned—is allowed, for the condition of this mortality, to oppose only as far as he knows it to be expedient for them; of which the Sacred Writings sound forth through the apostolic mouth: *God is faithful, who will not permit you to be tempted beyond what you are able; but with the temptation will also make a way out, that you may be able to bear it*[^114].

But these evils, which the faithful piously endure, profit them either for amending sins, or for exercising and proving justice, or for demonstrating the misery of this life, so that that life where there will be true and perpetual blessedness may both be desired more ardently and sought more earnestly. But these things are kept around those of whom the Apostle says: *we know that for those who love God all things work together unto good, for those who are called according to his purpose*[^115].[^116] *For those whom he foreknew, he also predestined to be conformed to the image of his Son, that he himself might be the firstborn among many brothers. And those whom he predestined, them he also called; and those whom he called, them he also justified; but those whom he justified, them he also glorified*[^117]. Of these predestined ones none perishes with the devil; none will remain under the power of the devil unto death. Then follows what I have already mentioned above: *what then shall we say to these things? If God is for us, who is against us? He who did not spare his own Son, but gave him up for us all, how has he not with him also given us all things*[^118]?

## How Fittingly the Death of Christ Was Chosen, That We Might Be Justified in His Blood

**16.** 21. Why, then, should not the death of Christ come about? Nay rather, why, with countless other ways set aside by which the Almighty could use to free us, should this one above all be chosen to come about—where nothing of his divinity was either diminished or changed,[^119] and from the humanity he took on so great a benefit was conferred upon men, that by the everlasting Son of God, who is likewise the son of man, an undue temporal death should be paid back, by which he might free them from the due everlasting death?

The devil held our sins, and through them deservedly fixed us in death. He who had no sins of his own dismissed them, and was undeservedly led to death by the devil. So great was the worth of that blood, that he who killed Christ by an undue death, even for a time, ought to detain no one clothed with Christ in the due everlasting death. *Therefore God commends his love toward us, in that, while we were yet sinners, Christ died for us. Much more, then, being now justified in his blood, shall we be saved from wrath through him*[^120]. *Justified*, he says, *in his blood*: justified plainly in this, that we were freed from all sins; but freed from all sins, since for us the Son of God, *who had none*,[^121] was killed. *We shall* therefore *be saved from wrath through him*:[^122] from the wrath, namely, of God, which is nothing other than *just vengeance*.[^123] For the wrath of God is not, as that of man, a disturbance of the mind: but it is the wrath of him to whom the Holy Scripture says in another place, *but you, O Lord of hosts, judge with tranquillity*[^124]. If, then, the just divine vengeance has received such a name, what is rightly understood as the reconciliation of God, except when such wrath is ended?[^125]

Nor were we enemies of God except as sins are enemies of justice, which sins being remitted, such enmities are ended, and those whom he himself justifies are reconciled to the just one.[^126] Yet even as enemies he surely loved them: since *he did not spare his own Son, but for us all*, while we were yet enemies, *gave him up*. Rightly, then, the Apostle, continuing, added, *for if, when we were enemies, we were reconciled to God through the death of his Son*, by which that remission of sins was made, *much more, being reconciled, shall we be saved in his life*[^127]. Saved in his life, who through his death were reconciled. For who would doubt that he will give his life for friends, for whom, while they were enemies, he gave his death? *And not only so*, he says, *but we also glory in God through our Lord Jesus Christ, through whom we have now received the reconciliation*[^128]. Not only, he says, shall we be saved, *but we also glory*: and not in ourselves, but *in God*; and not through ourselves, but *through our Lord Jesus Christ, through whom we have now received the reconciliation*, according to the things discussed above.

Then the Apostle subjoins: *for this reason, as through one man sin entered into this world, and through sin death, and so death passed into all men, in whom all sinned*[^129]: and the rest, in which he discusses at greater length two men—the one and same first Adam, by whose sin and death, as by hereditary evils, we his posterity are bound; but the other the second Adam, who is not only man but also God, by whose paying for us what he did not owe we are freed from our debts, both ancestral and our own.[^130] Accordingly, since on account of that one the devil held all who were begotten through his vitiated carnal concupiscence, it is just that on account of this one he should let go all who are spiritually regenerated through his own immaculate grace.

## Other Advantages of the Incarnation

**17.** 22. There are also many other things which, in the incarnation of Christ—which displeases the proud—are to be wholesomely looked upon and considered. Of these one is that it was shown to man what place he had among the things God founded: since human nature could be so joined to God that out of two substances one person should be made, and through this now out of three: God, soul, and flesh; so that those proud malign spirits, who interpose themselves as mediators for deceiving, as though for helping, should not dare to set themselves before man because they have no flesh; and especially because he deigned to die even in this same flesh,[^131] lest they should persuade men to worship them as gods because they seem to be immortal.

Then, that the grace of God might be commended to us, without any preceding deserts, in the man Christ:[^132] since not even he himself, that he might, joined to the true God in so great a unity, become the Son of God, one person together with him, attained this by any preceding deserts; but from the time when he began to be man, from that time he is also God: whence it was said, *the Word was made flesh*[^133].

There is also this, that the pride of man, which is the greatest impediment to his cleaving to God, could, through so great a humility of God, be refuted and healed. Man learns too how[^134] far he has withdrawn from God, and what avails him toward a medicinal pain, when he returns through such a Mediator, who comes to the aid of men as God by his divinity, and meets them as man by his weakness.[^135] And what greater example of obedience could be offered to us, who had perished through disobedience, than that the God the Son should be to God the Father *obedient even unto the death of the cross*[^136]? And where could the reward of that obedience be better shown than in the flesh of so great a Mediator, which rose again to eternal life? It belonged also to the justice and goodness of the Creator, that the devil should be overcome by the same rational creature which he rejoiced to have overcome, and by one coming from that very race—the race which, with its origin vitiated, he held whole and entire through one man.

## Why the Son of God Took On a Man from the Race of Adam and from a Virgin

**18.** 23. For surely God could have taken on a man from elsewhere, in whom he might be the *Mediator of God and men*[^137], not from the race of that Adam who by his sin bound the human race;[^138] just as he created that very first man whom he created, not from the race of anyone. He could, then, either thus, or in any other way he wished, create one other by whom the conqueror of the former might be overcome: but he judged it better both to take on a man from the very race that had been overcome, through whom he might overcome the enemy of the human race; and yet from a virgin, whose conceiving the spirit, not the flesh, faith, not lust, went before.[^139] Nor was there present the concupiscence of the flesh, by which the rest are sown and conceived who draw original sin: but, with this utterly removed, holy virginity was made fruitful by believing, not by lying together; so that what was born from the offspring of the first man should draw only the origin of the race, not also of the crime. For there was born a nature not vitiated by the contagion of the transgression, but the sole medicine of all such vices. There was born, I say, a man having no sin, and going to have no sin at all, through whom should be reborn, to be freed from sin, those who could not be born without sin.

For although conjugal chastity makes good use of the carnal concupiscence which is in the genital members, yet it has involuntary motions, by which it shows either that it could have been none at all in paradise before sin, or that, if it was, it was not such as sometimes to resist the will. But now we perceive it to be such that, fighting against the law of the mind, even if there is no cause of begetting, it brings on the goads of coupling: where, if it is yielded to, it is sated by sinning; if it is not yielded to, it is bridled by dissenting—which two things, who could doubt, were foreign to paradise before sin? For neither did that honorableness do anything unseemly, nor did that happiness suffer anything unquiet.

It was fitting, therefore, that this carnal concupiscence should be there none at all, when the offspring of the Virgin was conceived, in whom the author of death was going to find nothing worthy of death, and yet was going to kill him—to be overcome by the death of *the author of life*[^140]: the conqueror of the first Adam and holder of the human race, conquered by the second Adam and losing the Christian race, freed out of the human race from the human crime, through him who was not in the crime, although he was of the race; so that that deceiver should be overcome by him from that race which he had overcome by the crime.

And this was so done that man might not be exalted; but *he who glories, let him glory in the Lord*[^141]. For he who was overcome was only man; and therefore he was overcome, because he proudly desired to be a god: but he who overcame was both man and God; and therefore, born of a virgin, he overcame in this way, because God humbly ruled that man—not as he rules the other saints, but bore him.

These so great gifts of God, and whatever others there are which it would be long now to seek out and discuss concerning this matter, would be none at all, unless *the Word* became *flesh*[^142].

## Our Knowledge Is Christ, and Our Wisdom Too Is the Same Christ

**19.** 24. But all these things which, for us, *the Word made flesh* did and endured in time and in place, according to the distinction we have undertaken to demonstrate, pertain to knowledge, not to wisdom.[^143] But inasmuch as he is the Word without time and *without place*, he is coeternal with the Father and *wholly everywhere*;[^144] of whom, if anyone can, so far as he can, bring forth a truthful discourse, that discourse will be the *utterance of wisdom*:[^145] and through this *the Word made flesh, which is Christ Jesus, has both the treasures of wisdom and of knowledge*.[^146]

For the Apostle, writing to the Colossians, says: *for I want you to know what a struggle I have for you, and for those who are at Laodicea, and for whoever have not seen my face in the flesh; that their hearts may be comforted, being knit together in love and in all the riches of the fullness of understanding, unto the knowledge of the mystery of God, which is Christ Jesus, in whom are hidden all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge*[^147]. To what extent the Apostle knew these treasures, how much of them he had penetrated, and to what great things in them he had attained, who can know?

I, however, according to what is written, *but to each one of us is given the manifestation of the Spirit for profit: to one indeed is given through the Spirit the utterance of wisdom, to another the utterance of knowledge according to the same Spirit*[^148], if these two are so far apart that wisdom is attributed to divine things and knowledge to human, recognize both in Christ, and so does, with me, every faithful one of his. And when I read, *the Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us*[^149], in the Word I understand the true Son of God,[^150] in the flesh I recognize the true *son of man*,[^151] and both together joined into one person of God and man by the ineffable bounty of grace. On account of which it follows and says, *and we saw his glory, glory as of the only-begotten of the Father, full of grace and truth*[^152]. If we refer grace to knowledge, truth to wisdom,[^153] I think we do not depart from that distinction of these two things which we have commended.

For among things that arose in time, this is the highest grace, that man was joined to God in unity of person: but among eternal things[^154] the highest truth is rightly attributed to the Word of God. But that the same one is the *only-begotten of the Father, full of grace and truth*[^155]—this was brought about so that the same one should be, in the things done for us in time, he by whom, through that same faith, we are cleansed, that we may steadfastly contemplate him in eternal things. But those foremost philosophers of the nations, who *were able to behold the invisible things of God, understood through the things that were made*[^156], yet, because they philosophized without the Mediator, that is, without the man Christ—whom they neither believed would come, with the prophets, nor had come, with the apostles—*held the truth*, as was said of them, *in iniquity*[^157].

For, set among these lowest things, they could not but seek certain means by which they might arrive at those sublime things which they had understood: and so they fell in with deceiving demons, through whom it came about *that they changed the glory of the incorruptible God into the likeness of an image of corruptible man and of birds and of four-footed beasts and of serpents*[^158]. For in such forms they both set up and worshiped idols.[^159]

Our knowledge, therefore, is Christ, and our wisdom too is the same Christ.[^160] He himself implants in us faith concerning temporal things, he himself exhibits the truth concerning everlasting things. Through him we go on to him, we tend through knowledge to wisdom: yet from the one and same Christ we do not depart, *in whom are hidden all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge*[^161]. But now we speak of knowledge, and afterward we shall speak of wisdom, as far as he himself shall grant. Nor should we so take these two as though it were not allowed to call either that wisdom which is in human things, or that knowledge which is in divine things. For by a broader usage of speaking, both can be called wisdom, both knowledge. Yet in no way would it have been written in the Apostle, *to one is given the utterance of wisdom, to another the utterance of knowledge*[^162], unless these single things were also properly called by their single names, of whose distinction we now treat.

## What Was Done in This Book

**20.** 25. Let us now, then, see what this lengthy discourse has accomplished, what it has gathered, where it has arrived. *To wish to be blessed*[^163] belongs to all men: yet faith—by which, with the heart cleansed, blessedness is reached—does not belong to all. So it comes about that through that faith which not all will, one must tend to that blessedness which there can be no one who does not will. *To wish to be blessed*, all see in their own heart; and so great is the agreement of human nature in this matter, that the man is not deceived who, from his own mind, conjectures this of another's mind; in short, we know that all will it.

But many despair of being able to be immortal, although that which all will—that is, to be blessed—none can be otherwise; yet they would will to be immortal too, if they could: but by not believing that they can, they do not so live that they can. Faith, therefore, is necessary that we may attain blessedness in all the goods of human nature, that is, both of the mind and of the body. But that this faith is fixed in Christ, who in the flesh *rose from the dead*, *to die no more*[^164]; and that no one is freed from the dominion of the devil except through him, through the remission of sins; and that in the devil's party the life must be wretched, and that same life perpetual—which is rather to be called death than life—this same faith holds. Of which, in this book too, I have discussed, as I could, according to the span of time, since I have already said much about this matter in the fourth book of this work; but there for one purpose, here for another: there, namely, to show why and how Christ was sent by the Father in the fullness of time,[^165] for the sake of those who say that he who sent and he who was sent cannot be equal in nature; but here, to distinguish active knowledge from contemplative wisdom.[^166]

## Certain Three Things in Faith

**20.** 26. For it seemed good, as we ascend by steps as it were in both, to seek out in the inner man a certain trinity, each of its own kind, just as we first sought one in the outer man; that to that Trinity which is God we may, according to our small measure—if indeed we can do even this—come, to behold it at least *in an enigma* and *through a mirror*[^167], with a mind more practiced in these lower things.

Whoever, therefore, commits the words of this faith to memory in their sounds alone, not knowing what they signify—as those who do not know Greek are wont to hold Greek words by heart, or Latin words likewise, or those of any other language of which they are ignorant—do they not have[^168] a certain trinity in their mind, since the sounds of the words are in the memory even when one is not thinking of them; and from them the gaze of his recollection is formed, when he thinks of them; and the will of the one recollecting and thinking joins the two?

Yet in no way would we say that this man, when he does this, acts according to the trinity of the inner man, but rather of the outer: because he remembers only that, and, when he wills, beholds as much as he wills, which pertains to the sense of the body that is called hearing, and in such thinking he turns over nothing other than the images of corporeal things, that is, of sounds.

But if he holds and recalls what those words signify, he now indeed does something of the inner man: but he is not yet to be said or thought to live according to the trinity of the inner man, if he does not love those things which are there proclaimed, commanded, promised. For he can hold and think of them even to this end, that, esteeming them to be false, he may try even to refute them. That will, therefore, which there joins the things that were held in the memory and the things which from them are impressed on the gaze of thought, does indeed make up a certain trinity, since it is itself the third: but one does not live according to it, when the things that are thought of, as it were false, do not please.

But when they are believed to be true, and the things that are there to be loved are loved, one now lives according to the trinity of the inner man: for each one lives according to what he loves. But how are things loved which are not known, but only believed? This question has already been treated in the earlier books,[^169] and it was found that no one loves what he is utterly ignorant of; but that things are loved out of those which are known, when unknown things are said to be loved.

Now we so close this book as to admonish that *the just man lives by faith*[^170]; which *faith works through love*[^171], in such a way that the very virtues too, by which one lives prudently, bravely, temperately, and justly, are all referred to that same faith: for otherwise they will not be able to be true virtues. Which virtues nevertheless avail not so much in this life that there is not sometimes need here for the remission of sins of whatever kind; which is not done except through him who by his blood overcame the prince of sins. From this faith and such a life, whatever notions are in the mind of a faithful man, when they are contained in the memory, and inspected by recollection, and please the will, render a certain trinity of their own kind. But the image of God, of which we shall speak afterward with his help, is not yet in it: which will then appear better, when it shall have been shown where it is; for which let the reader wait in a future volume.
