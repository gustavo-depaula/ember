# Book XV: Augustine Wishes to Exercise the Reader in the Things That Have Been Made, That He May, If He Can, Understand the Trinity by Reason

## Preface

**1.** 1. Wishing to exercise the reader *in the things that have been made*, toward knowing him by whom they were made, we have now come to his image, which is man, in that by which he excels the other animals, that is, reason or understanding, and whatever else can be said of the rational or intellectual soul, which pertains to that thing which is called the mind or *animus*.[^1] By which name some authors of the Latin tongue distinguish, by a certain manner of speaking of their own, that which excels in man, and is not in a beast, from the soul (*anima*) which is also in a beast.[^2] Above this nature, then, if we seek something, and seek the truth, it is God: a nature, namely, not created, but creating. Whether this is a Trinity, we ought now to demonstrate, not only to those who believe, by the authority of Divine Scripture, but also to those who understand, by some reason, if we can. But why I have said "if we can," the matter itself will better indicate when it has begun to be sought by discussion.

### God, Incomprehensible, Is Always to Be Sought: for He Is Sought That He May Be Found More Sweetly, and Found That He May Be Sought More Eagerly

**2.** 2. For God himself, whom we seek, will help, as I hope, that our labor be not fruitless, and that we may understand how it was said in the sacred *Psalm*: *let the heart of those who seek the Lord rejoice: seek the Lord, and be strengthened; seek his face always*[^3]. For it seems that what is always sought is never found: and how then will the heart of those who seek rejoice, and not rather be saddened, if they have not been able to find what they seek? For it does not say: "let the heart of those who find rejoice"; but, *of those who seek the Lord*.[^4] And yet the prophet Isaiah testifies that the Lord God can be found while he is sought, when he says, *seek the Lord, and as soon as you have found him, call upon him: and when he has drawn near to you, let the impious one forsake his ways, and the unjust man his thoughts*[^5].

If, then, he can be found when sought, why was it said, *seek his face always*[^6]? Or is he perhaps to be sought even when found? For incomprehensible things are to be sought in such a way that he who has been able to find how incomprehensible was what he was seeking may not think he has found nothing.

Why, then, does he so seek, if he comprehends that what he seeks is incomprehensible, except that he must not cease as long as he advances in the very inquiry into incomprehensible things, and the seeker becomes better and better, seeking so great a good, which is both sought to be found and found to be sought? For it is both sought that it may be found more sweetly, and found that it may be sought more eagerly.

According to this can be received what was said in the book of *Ecclesiasticus*, that wisdom says: *those who eat me shall still hunger, and those who drink me shall still thirst*[^7]. For they eat and drink, because they find; and because they hunger and thirst, they still seek. Faith seeks, understanding finds: on account of which the prophet says, *unless you believe, you will not understand*[^8]. And again, understanding still seeks him whom it has found: for *God looked down upon the sons of men*, as is sung in the sacred Psalm, *to see if there is one who understands, or who seeks God*[^9]. To this end, then, ought man to be understanding, that he may seek God.

### It Is Not in Vain That Signs of the Trinity Are Sought in Creation

**2.** 3. We shall, then, have lingered long enough in the things that God made,[^10] that through them he himself who made them might be known: for *his invisible things, from the creation of the world, are beheld, understood through the things that have been made*[^11]. Whence in the book of *Wisdom* are reproved those who *from the good things that are seen could not know him who is, nor, attending to the works, recognize the craftsman; but thought either fire, or the wind, or the swift air, or the circle of the stars, or the violence of the waters, or the lights of heaven, to be the gods that govern the world: by whose beauty if, delighted, they thought these to be gods, let them know how much better is the Lord of them. For the begetter of beauty created these things. Or if they marveled at their power and working, let them understand from these how much stronger is he who established them. For from the greatness of the beauty and of the creature the Creator of these things can*[^12] *knowably be seen*[^13]. I have set down these things from the book of *Wisdom* for this reason, lest any of the faithful should think that I have in vain and emptily sought, in creation, first through certain trinities of its own kind, in a certain way step by step, until I came to the mind of man, for signs of that highest Trinity which we seek when we seek God.[^14]

### The Things That, in the Earlier Books, Were Brought by Discussion to Knowledge Are Briefly Gathered Together

**3.** 4. But since the necessity of discoursing and reasoning has compelled us through fourteen books to say many things which we cannot all behold at once, so that we may, by a swift thought, refer them to that which we wish to apprehend, I will do, as far as I can with the Lord's help, this: that whatever in the several volumes I brought by discussion to knowledge, I will, the discussion set aside, briefly gather together, and set down, as it were under one gaze of the mind, not how each thing was made convincing, but the very things that were made convincing: lest the things that follow be so far from those that precede that the inspection of what follows should cause forgetfulness of what precedes; or at least, if it should, what has slipped away may be quickly recovered by rereading.

**3.** 5. In the first book, according to the sacred Scriptures, the unity and equality of that highest Trinity is shown.[^15]

In the second, third, and fourth, the same: but the question, diligently treated, of the mission of the Son and of the Holy Spirit made three books; and it was demonstrated that he who was sent is not for that reason less than he who sent, because the one sent and the other was sent, since the Trinity, which is in all things equal, likewise also, in its nature immutable and invisible and everywhere present, works inseparably.[^16]

In the fifth, on account of those to whom it therefore seems that the substance of the Father and of the Son is not the same, because they think that everything said of God is said according to substance, and therefore, since to beget and to be begotten, or the begotten and the unbegotten, are different, contend that the substances are different,[^17]

it is demonstrated that not everything said of God is said according to substance, as he is called good and great according to substance, and whatever else is said with reference to himself; but that some things are said also relatively, that is, not with reference to himself, but to something[^18] that he himself is not; as the Father is said with reference to the Son, or the Lord with reference to the creature serving him;[^19]

where, if anything is said relatively, that is, with reference to something that he himself is not, even from time, as is, *Lord, you have become a refuge for us*[^20], nothing happens to him by which he is changed, but he himself remains altogether immutable in his nature or essence.[^21]

In the sixth, how Christ was called by the apostolic mouth *the power of God and the wisdom of God*[^22] is so discussed that the same question is deferred to be more diligently treated again: whether he by whom Christ was begotten is not himself wisdom, but only the Father of his Wisdom, or whether wisdom begot Wisdom.[^23]

But whichever of these it might be, even in this book the equality of the Trinity appeared, and that God is not threefold, but a Trinity: nor that the Father and the Son are as it were something double in relation to the single Holy Spirit; where neither are the three anything more than one of them.[^24] It was discussed also how can be understood what Bishop Hilary says: *eternity in the Father, beauty in the Image, use in the Gift*[^25].

In the seventh, the question which had been deferred is set forth, in such a way that God who begot the Son is not only the Father of his power and wisdom, but also is himself power and wisdom; and so too the Holy Spirit: nor yet are there together three powers or three wisdoms, but one power and one wisdom, as one God and one essence.[^26] Then it was asked how they are said to be one essence, three persons, or, by some Greeks, one essence, three substances:[^27] and it was found that this is said by the necessity of expression, so that they might be uttered by some one name, when it is asked what the three are, whom we truly confess to be three—namely, the Father, and the Son, and the Holy Spirit.[^28]

In the eighth, with reason rendered also to those who understand, it is clear that in the substance of truth not only is the Father not greater than the Son, but also the two together are not anything greater than the Holy Spirit alone, nor are any two in the same Trinity anything greater than one, nor are all three together anything greater than each one. Then, through the truth which is beheld when understood, and through the highest good from which is every good, and through the justice on account of which a just mind is loved by a mind not yet just, I admonished that the nature not only incorporeal but also incommutable, which is God, might, as far as can be, be understood: and through love, which in the Holy Scriptures is called God, through which the Trinity too began somehow to appear to those who understand, as there are the lover, and what is loved, and love.[^29]

In the ninth, the discussion came to the image of God, which is man according to the mind: and in it a certain trinity is found, that is, the mind, and the acquaintance by which it knows itself, and the love by which it loves itself and its acquaintance; and these three are shown to be equal among themselves, and of one essence.[^30]

In the tenth, this same thing was treated more diligently and more subtly, and brought to this point, that in the mind was found a more evident trinity of it, namely, in memory and understanding and will.[^31] But since this too was found, that the mind could never have been such as not to remember itself, not to understand and love itself, although it did not always think of itself, and when it did think of itself, did not distinguish itself by that same thought from corporeal things; the discussion of the Trinity, of which this is the image, was deferred, so that even in those corporeal sights a trinity might be found, and in it the reader's attention might be more distinctly exercised.[^32]

In the eleventh, then, the sense of the eyes was chosen, in which what was found might be recognized, even though not stated, in the other four senses of the body as well: and so the trinity of the outer man appeared, first in those things which are discerned without—namely, from the body which is seen, and the form which is thence impressed on the gaze of the one discerning, and the intention of the will joining the two.[^33] But these three were made clear to be not equal among themselves, nor of one substance. Then in the mind itself, from those things which were sensed without, as it were brought in, another trinity was found, where the same three appeared to be of one substance: the imagination of the body which is in the memory, and the informing thence when the gaze of the one thinking is turned to it, and the intention of the will joining the two. But this trinity was found to pertain to the outer man, because it was brought in from the bodies which are sensed without.

In the twelfth, it seemed good that wisdom should be distinguished from knowledge,[^34] and in that which is properly called knowledge, because it is lower, a certain trinity of its own kind was first to be sought: which, although it already pertains to the inner man, is nevertheless not yet to be called or thought the image of God.[^35]

And this is done in the thirteenth book through the commendation of the Christian faith.[^36]

But in the fourteenth there is discussed the true wisdom of man, that is, the wisdom given as a gift of God by a participation in that same God, which is distinct from knowledge:[^37] and the discussion came to this point, that a trinity appears in the image of God, which is man according to the mind, which *is renewed in the knowledge of God according to the image of him who created man to his image*[^38], and so receives wisdom, where there is contemplation of eternal things.

### The Whole Nature of Things Proclaims That It Has a Most Excellent Creator

**4.** 6. Now, then, in the very eternal things, incorporeal and incommutable, in the perfect contemplation of which the blessed life, which is none but eternal,[^39] is promised us, let us seek the Trinity which is God. For not only the authority of the Divine Books proclaims that God is, but the whole nature of things itself, which surrounds us, to which we ourselves also belong, proclaims that it has a most excellent Founder,[^40] who gave us a mind and a natural reason by which we see that living things are to be preferred to non-living, things endowed with sense to those without sense, things with understanding to those without understanding, immortal things to mortal, powerful to powerless, just to unjust, beautiful to deformed, good to evil, incorruptible to corruptible, immutable to mutable, invisible to visible, incorporeal to corporeal, blessed to wretched. And through this, since without doubt we set the Creator before created things, it behooves us to confess that he both supremely lives, and perceives and understands all things; and cannot die, be corrupted, or be changed; and is not a body, but a spirit most powerful of all, most just, most beautiful, best, most blessed.

### Many Perfections Are Reduced to a Certain Fewness

**5.** 7. But all these things which I have said, and whatever else, in a like manner of human speech, seems worthily to be said of God, befit both the whole Trinity, which is one God, and the single Persons in that same Trinity. For who would dare to say[^41] that either the one God, which is the Trinity itself, or the Father, or the Son, or the Holy Spirit, is either not living, or perceiving or understanding nothing; or that, in that nature according to which they are predicated equal among themselves,[^42] any one of them is mortal or corruptible, or mutable, or corporeal; or who would deny that there is anyone there most powerful, most just, most beautiful, best, most blessed?[^43] If, then, these and all such things can be said both of the Trinity itself and of the single Persons in it, where or how will the Trinity appear?

Let us, then, first reduce these many things to a certain fewness. For what is called life in God is his very essence and nature. God, then, does not live except by a life which he himself is to himself. But this life is not such as is in a tree, where there is no understanding, no sense. Nor such as is in a beast: for the life of a beast has a fivefold sense, but has no understanding: but that life which is God perceives and understands all things; and perceives by the mind, not by the body, because God is a spirit.[^44] But not as the animals which have bodies, does God perceive through the body; for he does not consist of soul and body:[^45] and through this that simple nature, as it understands so it perceives, as it perceives so it understands; and sense is the same to it as understanding.

Nor in such a way that at some time it ceases to be or began to be: for it is immortal. Nor was it said in vain of him that he alone has immortality:[^46] for his immortality is truly immortality, in whose nature there is no change. The same is also the true eternity by which God is immutable, without beginning, without end; consequently also incorruptible. One and the same thing, then, is said, whether God be called eternal, or immortal, or incorruptible, or immutable: likewise when he is called living, and understanding—which is surely wise—this same thing is said. For he did not acquire a wisdom by which to be wise, but is himself Wisdom. And this is life, and the same is virtue or power, and the same is beauty, by which he is called powerful and beautiful. For what is more powerful and more beautiful than Wisdom, which *reaches mightily from end to end, and orders all things sweetly*[^47]? Goodness, too, and justice, are they distant from each other in the nature of God, as they are in his works, as though they were two diverse qualities of God, one goodness, the other justice? Surely not: but what is justice, that is goodness; and what is goodness, that is blessedness. But God is called incorporeal or bodiless for this reason, that he may be believed or understood to be a spirit,[^48] not a body.

### Whatever Seems to Be Said of God According to Qualities Is to Be Understood According to Essence

**5.** 8. Accordingly, if we say: "eternal, immortal, incorruptible, immutable, living, wise, powerful, beautiful, just, good, blessed, spirit"; of all these, the last that I set down seems to signify, as it were, only the substance, but the rest the qualities of this substance: but it is not so in that ineffable and simple nature. For whatever seems to be said there according to qualities is to be understood according to substance or essence. Far be it that God should be called spirit according to substance, and good according to quality: but both according to substance. So all the rest that we have mentioned, of which we have already said much in the earlier books.[^49] Of the four first, then, which were just now enumerated and arranged by us, that is, eternal, immortal, incorruptible, immutable, let us choose some one; because these four signify one thing, as I have already discussed; lest the attention be distended through many; and let it rather be that which was set down first, that is, "eternal." Let us do this also with the four second, which are, living, wise, powerful, beautiful. And since a life of some kind is in a beast too, in which there is no wisdom; but these two, namely, wisdom and power, are so compared with each other in man that the Holy Scripture said: *the wise man is better than the strong*[^50]; and beautiful, moreover, even bodies are wont to be called: of these four which we choose, let "wise" be chosen: although these four in God are not to be called unequal; for there are four names, but the thing is one. But of the third and last four, although in God to be just is the same as to be good, as to be blessed, and to be a spirit is the same as to be just and good and blessed; yet because in men a spirit can be not blessed, and one can be just and good yet not blessed; but he who is blessed is surely also just and good and a spirit: let us rather choose that which in men too cannot be without those three, which is "blessed."

### How Can We Not Only Believe, but Also Understand, That There Is a Trinity in the Simplicity of God?

**6.** 9. When, then, we say, "eternal, wise, blessed," are these three the Trinity which is called God? We reduce, indeed, those twelve to this fewness of three: but in that way perhaps we can also reduce these three to some one of them. For if one and the same thing in the nature of God can be wisdom and power, or life and wisdom; why can not one and the same thing in the nature of God be eternity and wisdom, or blessedness and wisdom? And through this, just as it made no difference whether we said those twelve or these three, when we reduced those many to this fewness; so it makes no difference whether we say these three, or that one in whose singularity we have shown the two others can likewise be reduced.

What manner of discussing, then, what force at last of understanding and power, what vivacity of reason, what edge of thought will show—to be silent now of the others—how this one thing which is called "wisdom," which is God, is a Trinity? For not as we acquire wisdom from him, so does God from someone: but he is himself his own Wisdom; because his Wisdom is not one thing and his essence another, to whom this is to be that is to be wise. It is said indeed in the Holy Scriptures that Christ is the power of God and the Wisdom of God:[^51] but how this is to be understood, lest the Son should seem to make the Father wise, was discussed in the seventh book;[^52] and reason came to this, that the Son is so Wisdom of Wisdom, as Light of Light, God of God. Nor could we find the Holy Spirit to be anything else than that he too is Wisdom, and all together one Wisdom, as one God, one essence.[^53] This Wisdom, then, which is God, how do we understand to be a Trinity? I did not say, "how do we believe?" for this ought not to be a question among the faithful: but if in some way, through understanding, we can see what we believe, what will that way be?

### A Shadowing-Forth of the Trinity in Man

**6.** 10. For if we recall where, in these books, the Trinity began to appear to our understanding, the eighth meets us. For there, as we could, by discussing we tried to raise the attention of the mind to understanding that most excellent and immutable nature, which our mind is not. Which nevertheless we so beheld as that it was neither far from us, and was above us, not by place, but by its own venerable and wonderful excellence, so that it seemed to be with us by its present light. In which, nevertheless, no Trinity yet appeared to us, because we were not holding a firm gaze of the mind, in that brightness, to seek it: only, because there was no mass where it behooved to believe that the magnitude of two or three is more than that of one, we discerned this somehow.

But when we came to love, which in the Holy Scripture is called God,[^54] the Trinity shone forth a little, that is, the lover, and what is loved, and love.[^55] But because that ineffable light beat back our gaze, and the weakness of our mind was in a certain way convicted that it could not yet be conformed to it, for the sake of restoring the laboring attention we turned aside, between the thing begun and the thing arranged, to the as it were more familiar consideration of our own mind, according to which man was made *to the image of God*[^56]: and thence, in the creature which we are, that we might be able to behold the invisible things of God, understood through the things that have been made,[^57] we lingered from the ninth book to the fourteenth.

And behold, now—our understanding exercised in lower things as much as was necessary, or perhaps more than was necessary—we wish to raise ourselves to behold the highest Trinity which is God, and we cannot. For do we, just as we see most certain trinities, whether those which are made without from corporeal things, or when those very things which were sensed without are thought of; or when those things which arise in the mind, and do not pertain to the senses of the body, such as faith, such as the virtues which are the arts of the life to be lived, are discerned by manifest reason and contained in knowledge; or when the mind itself, by which we know whatever we truly say we know, is known to itself, or thinks of itself, or when it beholds something which it itself is not, eternal and incommutable—do we, then, just as in all these we see most certain trinities, because they are made in us or are in us, when we remember, look upon, will these things, so see also the Trinity God, because there too, by understanding, we behold as it were the one speaking, and his Word, that is, the Father and the Son, and the love proceeding thence, common to both, namely, the Holy Spirit?

Or do we rather see those trinities pertaining to our senses or to our mind than believe them, but believe that God is a Trinity rather than see it? But if this is so, surely either we behold none of his invisible things, understood through the things that have been made;[^58] or, if we behold any, we do not behold the Trinity in them, and there is something there which we behold, there is something which, even though not beheld, we ought to believe.

But that we behold an immutable good, which we ourselves are not, the eighth book showed;[^59] and the fourteenth, when we spoke of the wisdom which man has from God, admonished.[^60] Why, then, do we not recognize the Trinity there? Does this wisdom which is called God not understand itself, not love itself? Who would say this? Or who is there who does not see that, where there is no knowledge, there is in no way wisdom? Or is it to be thought that the Wisdom which is God knows other things and does not know itself, or loves other things and does not love itself? Which, if it is foolish and impious to say or believe, behold then a Trinity: namely, Wisdom, and the acquaintance with itself, and the love of itself. For so too in man we find a trinity, that is, the mind, and the acquaintance by which it knows itself, and the love by which it loves itself.[^61]

### But This Shadowing-Forth Is Deficient and Unapproachable

**7.** 11. But these three are so in man that they themselves are not man. For *man is*, as the ancients defined, *a rational mortal animal*.[^62] Those things, then, excel in man, are not themselves man.[^63] And one person, that is, each single man, has those three in the mind, or has a mind.[^64] But if we so define man as to say: "man is a rational substance consisting of soul and body";[^65] there is no doubt that man has a soul which is not body, has a body which is not soul. And through this those three are not man, but are man's, or are in man. The body too being removed, if the soul alone is thought of, the mind is something of it, as it were its head, or eye, or face: but these are not to be thought of as bodies. Not the soul, then, but that which excels in the soul, is called the mind.[^66] But can we say that the Trinity is so in God as to be something of God, and not itself be God? Wherefore each single man, who is called the image of God not according to all things that pertain to his nature, but according to the mind alone, is one person, and is the image of the Trinity in the mind. But that Trinity of which it is the image is nothing else, whole, than God, is nothing else, whole, than the Trinity. Nor does anything pertain to the nature of God which does not pertain to that Trinity: and the three Persons are of one essence,[^67] not as each single man is one person.

**7.** 12. Likewise, in this too there is a great difference, that whether we say in man the mind, and its acquaintance with itself, and its love; or memory, understanding, will,[^68] we remember nothing of the mind except through memory, nor understand except through understanding, nor love except through will.[^69]

But in that Trinity who would dare to say that the Father understands neither himself, nor the Son, nor the Holy Spirit, except through the Son, or loves them except through the Holy Spirit, but through himself only remembers either himself or the Son or the Holy Spirit; and in the same way that the Son remembers neither himself nor the Father except through the Father, nor loves except through the Holy Spirit, but through himself only understands both the Father and himself and the Holy Spirit; similarly that the Holy Spirit through the Father remembers both the Father and the Son and himself, and through the Son understands both the Father and the Son and himself, but through himself only loves both himself and the Father and the Son: as though the Father were the memory both of himself and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit, but the Son the understanding both of himself and of the Father and of the Holy Spirit, but the Holy Spirit the love both of itself and of the Father and of the Son?

Who would presume to think or affirm these things in that Trinity? For if there the Son alone understands, both for himself and for the Father and for the Holy Spirit, we return to that absurdity, that the Father is not wise of himself, but of the Son; nor did Wisdom beget Wisdom, but the Father is said to be wise by that wisdom which he begot. For where there is no understanding, neither can there be wisdom: and through this, if the Father does not himself understand for himself, but the Son understands for the Father, surely the Son makes the Father wise. And if for God to be is the same as to be wise, and his essence is the same as his wisdom, then not the Son from the Father, which is true; but rather the Father has essence from the Son, which is most absurd and most false. That we discussed, convicted, and rejected this absurdity in the seventh book is most certain.[^70]

God the Father is, then, wise by that Wisdom which he himself is to himself, and the Son is the Wisdom of the Father from the Wisdom which is the Father, of whom the Son was begotten. Wherefore consequently the Father is also understanding by that understanding which he himself is to himself; for he would not be wise who was not understanding: but the Son is the understanding of the Father, begotten of the understanding which is the Father.

This too can not unfittingly be said of memory. For how is he wise who remembers nothing, or does not remember himself? Accordingly, since the Father is Wisdom, the Son is Wisdom,[^71] just as the Father is the Wisdom of himself, so too is the Son: and just as the Father remembers both himself and the Son, by a memory not of the Son, but his own; so the Son remembers both himself and the Father, by a memory not of the Father, but his own. And where there is no love, who would say there is any wisdom? From which it is gathered that the Father is his own love, just as he is his own understanding and memory.

Behold, then, those three—that is, memory, understanding, love or will—in that highest and immutable essence which is God, are not the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit, but the Father alone. And because the Son too is Wisdom begotten of Wisdom,[^72] just as neither the Father understands for him, nor the Holy Spirit for him, but he himself for himself; so neither does the Father remember for him, nor the Holy Spirit love for him, but he himself for himself: for he is himself his own memory, his own understanding, his own love; but that he is so, he has from the Father, of whom he was born. The Holy Spirit too, because it is Wisdom proceeding from Wisdom,[^73] does not have the Father as memory, and the Son as understanding, and itself as love; for it would not be Wisdom if another remembered for it, and another understood for it, and it itself only loved for itself: but it itself has these three, and so has them that these very things it itself is. Yet that it is so, it has thence whence it proceeds.

**7.** 13. What man, then, can comprehend that Wisdom by which God knows all things, in such a way that the things that are called past do not there pass away, nor the things that are called future are awaited as though absent, to come, but both past and future, with present things, are all present; nor are things thought of singly, nor is there a passing by thought from one to another, but in one gaze all are at once at hand: what man, I say, comprehends this Wisdom, which is likewise prudence, likewise knowledge; since by us not even our own is comprehended? For the things that are present to our senses or to our understanding we can somehow behold: but the things that are absent, and yet were present, we know through memory, which we have not forgotten. Nor do we conjecture past things from future, but future from past, yet not with firm cognition. For when we, as it were more plainly and certainly, foresee certain of our coming thoughts, as it were nearest, we do this by memory's working, when we are able to do it as much as we are able—which seems to pertain not to things that are future, but to past things.

This can be tested in those sayings or songs whose sequence we render from memory. For unless we foresaw by thought what follows, surely we would not say it. And yet, that we may foresee, it is not foresight that instructs us, but memory. For until all that we say or sing is finished, there is nothing that is not brought forth foreseen and looked ahead to. And yet when we do this, we are not said to sing or say with foresight, but from memory; and those who in bringing forth many such things have very great power are wont to have, not their foresight, but their memory praised. That these things come to be in our mind, or from our mind, we know, and are most certain: but how they come to be, the more attentively we wish to attend, the more both our speech fails, and the attention itself does not endure, so that our understanding, if not our tongue, may arrive at something clear.

And do we suppose that we can, with so great a weakness of mind, comprehend whether God's foresight is the same as his memory and understanding—he who beholds not by thinking of things singly, but embraces in one, eternal and immutable and ineffable vision all the things he knows? In this difficulty and these straits, then, it is good to cry out to the living God: "*your knowledge has become wonderful from me; it has grown strong, and I shall not be able to reach it*[^74]. For from myself I understand how wonderful and incomprehensible is your knowledge,[^75] by which you made me; since I cannot even comprehend myself whom you made: and yet *in my meditation a fire blazes up*[^76], so that I may seek your face always."[^77]

### The Knowledge of God "Through a Mirror in an Enigma"

**8.** 14. I know that wisdom is an incorporeal substance, and is a light in which are seen the things that are not seen by carnal eyes: and yet so great and so spiritual a man says, *we now see through a mirror in an enigma, but then face to face*[^78]. If we ask what sort of thing this mirror is, and what it is, surely this occurs, that in a mirror nothing but an image is discerned. This, then, we have tried to do: that through this image which we are, we might somehow see, as through a mirror, him by whom we were made. This is signified too by what the same Apostle says, *but we, beholding with unveiled face the glory of the Lord, are transformed into the same image from glory to glory, as by the Spirit of the Lord*[^79].

He said *speculantes*, seeing through a *mirror* (*speculum*), not looking out from a *watchtower* (*specula*). Which in the Greek tongue, from which the Apostolic Writings were translated into Latin, is not ambiguous. For there *speculum*, where the images of things appear, differs altogether, even in the sound of the word, from *specula*, from whose height we behold something farther off; and it appears well enough that the Apostle said *beholding the glory of the Lord* from *speculum*, not from *specula*.

But in that he says, *we are transformed into the same image*:[^80] surely he wishes the image of God to be understood, saying "the same," namely this very one, that is, the one we behold; because the same is both the image and the glory of God, as he says elsewhere, *a man indeed ought not to veil his head, since he is the image and glory of God*[^81]: of which words we have already discoursed in the twelfth book.[^82]

He says *we are transformed*, then, that is, we are changed from form to form, and pass from an obscure form into a luminous form; because even that obscure one is the image of God; and if image, surely also glory, in which we men were created, excelling the other animals.[^83]

For of human nature itself it was said: *a man indeed ought not to veil his head, since he is the image and glory of God*.[^84] Which nature, most excellent among created things, when it is justified from impiety by its Creator, is transformed[^85] from a deformed form into a beautiful form. For even in its very impiety, the more damnable the vice, the more certainly is the nature praiseworthy. And on account of this he added, *from glory to glory*: from the glory of creation to the glory of justification. Although this can be understood in other ways too, what was said, *from glory to glory*: from the glory of faith to the glory of sight; from the glory by which we are sons of God, to the glory by which *we shall be like him, for we shall see him as he is*.[^86]

But in that he added, *as by the Spirit of the Lord*; he shows that the good of so desirable a transformation is conferred upon us by the grace of God.

### An Enigma Is an Obscure Allegory

**9.** 15. These things were said on account of what the Apostle says, that *now* we see *through a mirror*. But because he added, *in an enigma*, this is unknown to many who do not know those writings in which there is a certain doctrine about the modes of expression, which the Greeks call "tropes," and we too use that Greek word for the Latin. For just as we say "schemata" more usually than "figures," so we say "tropes" more usually than "modes." But to express the names of the single modes or tropes in Latin, so that single ones may correspond to single ones, is most difficult and most unusual. Whence some of our translators, where the Apostle says, *which are in allegory*, not wishing to set down the Greek word, rendered it by a circumlocution, saying, *which signify one thing by another*.[^87]

But of this trope, that is, allegory, there are several species, among which is also that which is called an enigma. But the definition of the general name must of necessity embrace all the species too. And through this, just as every horse is an animal, but not every animal is a horse: so every enigma is an allegory, but not every allegory is an enigma. What, then, is allegory, except a trope where one thing is understood from another, such as that to the *Thessalonians*: *therefore let us not sleep as do the rest: but let us watch, and be sober. For those who sleep, sleep by night; and those who are drunk, are drunk by night: but let us who are of the day be sober*[^88]? But this allegory is not an enigma. For except to the very slow, this sense is ready at hand. But an enigma is, to explain it briefly, an obscure allegory,[^89] such as is, *the leech had three daughters*[^90]; and whatever is similar. But where the Apostle named allegory, he found it not in words, but in a fact, when, of the two sons of Abraham, one of the slave-woman, the other of the free woman, which was not said but also done, he showed that two Testaments are to be understood;[^91] which, before he expounded it, was obscure: accordingly such an allegory, which is the general name, could specifically be named an enigma.

### By the Name of "Mirror" the Apostle Wished the Image to Be Understood; by the Name of "Enigma," a Likeness, but an Obscure One

**9.** 16. But because not only those who do not know those writings by which the tropes are learned ask what the Apostle meant, that we now see in an enigma; but even those who know desire to know what that enigma is in which we now see: from both, one meaning is to be found; both from that which he says, *we now see through a mirror*; and from that which he added, *in an enigma*. For it is one, when the whole is said thus, *we now see through a mirror in an enigma*. Accordingly, as it seems to me, just as by the name of *mirror* he wished the image to be understood; so by the name of *enigma* a likeness, but an obscure one, and difficult to see through. Since, then, by the name of *mirror* and *enigma* any likenesses signified by the Apostle can be understood, which are suited to understanding God, in the way one can; yet nothing is more suited than that which is not in vain called his image.

Let no one, then, marvel that even in this manner of seeing which has been granted to this life, namely through a mirror in an enigma, we labor that we may somehow see. For the name of *enigma* would not sound here, if there were ease of vision. And this is a greater enigma, that we do not see what we cannot but see. For who does not see his own thought? and who sees his own thought—I do not mean with the carnal eyes, but with that inner gaze itself? Who does not see it, and who sees it?

Since thought is a certain vision of the mind, whether the things that are also seen by the corporeal eyes, or perceived by the other senses, are present, or are not present, and their likenesses are discerned by thought; or whether none of these, but those things are thought of which are neither corporeal nor likenesses of corporeal things, such as the virtues and the vices, such as, finally, thought itself is thought of; or those things which are handed down through the disciplines and the liberal doctrines; or whether the higher causes and reasons of all these things are thought of in the immutable nature; or whether we even think of evil and vain and false things, either with the sense not consenting, or with the consent erring.

### On the Word of the Mind, in Which, as in a Mirror and an Enigma, We See the Word of God

**10.** 17. But now let us speak of those things which we think of as known, and have in our acquaintance even if we do not think of them, whether they pertain to contemplative knowledge, which I have discussed is properly to be called wisdom, or to active knowledge, which is properly to be called knowledge. For both at once are of one mind, and the image of God is one. But when the lower is treated more distinctly and separately, then it is not to be called the image of God, although even then there is found in it some likeness of that Trinity; which we showed in the thirteenth volume.[^92]

Now, then, we speak at once of the whole knowledge of man, in which whatever things are known to us are known: which surely are true, otherwise they would not be known. For no one knows false things, except when he knows them to be false: and if he knows this, he knows something true; for it is true that those things are false. Of these things, then, we now discourse, which we think of as known, and which are known to us even if they are not thought of by us. But surely, if we wish to say them, we cannot except thought. For even if words do not sound, he who thinks surely says in his own heart.

Whence comes that passage in the book of *Wisdom*: *they said within themselves, thinking not rightly*[^93]. For he expounded what *they said within themselves* is, when he added, *thinking*. Like to this is, in the Gospel, that certain Scribes, when they had heard the Lord say to the paralytic, *be of good cheer, son, your sins are forgiven you; said within themselves, "this man blasphemes."* For what is *they said within themselves*, except by thinking? Then it follows, *and when Jesus had seen their thoughts, he said: "why do you think evil in your hearts?"*[^94] So Matthew. But Luke narrates this same thing thus: *the Scribes and Pharisees began to think, saying: "who is this who speaks blasphemies? Who can forgive sins, but God alone? But when Jesus knew their thoughts, answering he said to them: Why do you think in your hearts?"*[^95] Such as in the book of Wisdom is, *they said, thinking*; such is here, *they thought, saying*. And both there and here it is shown that to say within oneself and in one's heart is this,[^96] to say by thinking. For they said within themselves, and it was said to them: *why do you think?* And of that rich man whose field brought forth abundant fruits, the Lord himself says, *and he thought within himself, saying*[^97].

**10.** 18. Certain thoughts, then, are speakings of the heart, where the Lord showed that there is also a mouth, when he says: *not what enters into the mouth defiles a man; but what proceeds out of the mouth, this defiles a man*[^98]. In one sentence he has embraced two certain mouths of man, one of the body, the other of the heart. For surely that from which they had thought a man is defiled enters the mouth of the body; but that from which the Lord said a man is defiled proceeds from the mouth of the heart.[^99] For so he himself expounded what he had said. For a little after, of this matter he says to his disciples: *are you still without understanding too? Do you not understand that everything which enters into the mouth goes into the belly, and is cast out into the privy?* Here certainly he most plainly showed the mouth of the body. But in what follows, showing the mouth of the heart: *but the things which proceed from the mouth come forth from the heart, and these defile a man. For from the heart come forth evil thoughts*, and the rest. What is clearer than this exposition?

And yet, because we say that thoughts are speakings of the heart, they are not therefore not also visions arisen from the visions of acquaintance, when they are true. For without, when these come about through the body, speech is one thing, vision another: but within, when we think, both are one. Just as hearing and seeing are two certain things distant from each other in the senses of the body, but in the mind to see and to hear are not one thing and another: and through this, although speech without is not seen but rather heard, yet the holy Gospel said that interior speakings, that is, thoughts, were seen by the Lord, not heard: *they said*, it says, *within themselves, "this man blasphemes"*; then it subjoined, *and when Jesus had seen their thoughts*. He saw, then, what they said. For he saw by his own thought their thoughts, which they thought they alone saw.

### The Word Which We Say in the Heart, as Long as We Think of What Is True, Belongs to No Language

**10.** 19. Whoever, then, can understand the word, not only before it sounds, but even before the images of its sounds are turned over in thought—for this is what belongs to no language, namely those which are called the languages of the nations, of which ours is Latin: whoever, I say, can understand this, can already see, *through* this *mirror* and *in* this *enigma*[^100], some likeness of that Word of which it was said, *in the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God*[^101].

For it must needs be that, when we speak what is true, that is, speak what we know, from the very knowledge which we hold in the memory there is born a word that is altogether such as is that knowledge from which it is born. For thought formed from the thing which we know is the word which we say in the heart: which is neither Greek, nor Latin, nor of any other language; but when there is need to convey it to the acquaintance of those to whom we speak, some sign is taken up by which it may be signified. And most often a sound, sometimes also a nod—the one is presented to the ears, the other to the eyes—so that through corporeal signs the word which we carry in the mind may become known even to the senses of the body. For what is it to nod, except in a certain way to speak visibly?

There is in the Holy Scriptures a testimony of this opinion; for in the Gospel according to John it is read thus: *amen, amen, I say to you, that one of you will betray me. The disciples therefore looked at one another, doubting of whom he spoke. Now there was one of his disciples reclining on the bosom of Jesus, whom Jesus loved: to him therefore Simon Peter nodded, and says to him, "who is it of whom he speaks?"*[^102] Behold, by nodding he said what by sounding he did not dare to say. But these and such corporeal signs we present either to the ears or to the eyes of those present to whom we speak; letters were also invented, by which we might converse even with those absent: but these are signs of voices, while the voices themselves, in our speech, are signs of the things which we think.[^103]

### The Likeness of the Word of God Is to Be Sought in Our Inner Word, but in This Likeness There Is the Greatest Unlikeness

**11.** 20. Accordingly the word which sounds without is a sign of the word which shines within, to which the name "word" more fittingly belongs.

For that which is brought forth by the mouth of the flesh is the voice of the "word": and it too is called "word," on account of that from which it was taken up that it might appear without. For so our word in a certain way becomes a voice of the body, by taking up that in which it may be manifested to the senses of men; as the *Word* of God *was made flesh*,[^104] by taking up that in which it too might be manifested to the senses of men.[^105] And just as our word becomes a voice, and is not changed into a voice; so the *Word of God* was indeed *made flesh*, but far be it that it should be changed into flesh. For by taking up that flesh, not by consuming itself into it, both this our word becomes a voice, and that Word was made flesh.

Wherefore he who desires to come to some likeness of the Word of God, however unlike in many ways, let him not look at our word which sounds in the ears, neither when it is brought forth by the voice, nor when it is thought in silence. For the words of all sounding languages are thought even in silence, and songs are run through in the mind, while the mouth of the body is silent: nor only the numbers of syllables, but even the modes of the melodies, although they are corporeal and pertain to that sense of the body which is called hearing, are at hand, through certain incorporeal images of their own, to those who think and silently turn over all these things.

But these things are to be passed over, that we may come to that word of man, through whose likeness of whatever kind, as *in an enigma*[^106], the Word of God may somehow be seen: not that which was made to this or that prophet, and of which it was said, *but the Word of God grew and was multiplied*[^107]; and of which again it was said, *therefore faith is from hearing, but hearing through the word of Christ*[^108]; and again, *when you had received from us the word of the hearing of God, you received it not as the word of men, but as it truly is, the word of God*[^109].

And countless things are likewise said in the Scriptures about the word of God, which is disseminated through human hearts and mouths in the sounds of many and diverse languages. But it is called the word of God for this reason, that divine doctrine, not human, is handed down. But that Word of God we seek somehow now to see through this likeness, of which it was said: *God was the Word*[^110]; of which it was said: *all things were made through him*[^111]; of which it was said: *the Word was made flesh*[^112]; of which it was said: *the fountain of wisdom is the Word of God in the highest*[^113].

We must come, then, to that word of man,[^114] to the word of the rational living being,[^115] to the word of the image of God, not born of God, but made by God,[^116] which is neither uttered in sound, nor thought in the likeness of a sound, such as must belong to some language, but which precedes all the signs by which it is signified, and is begotten of the knowledge which abides in the mind, when that same knowledge is spoken within, just as it is. For the vision of thought is most like the vision of knowledge. For when it is spoken through a sound, or through some corporeal sign, it is not spoken just as it is, but as it can be seen or heard through the body. When, then, that which is in the acquaintance is in the word, then there is a true word, and truth, such as is expected of a man, so that what is in the former is also in the latter; what is not in the former is also not in the latter; here is recognized: *yes, yes; no, no*[^117]. So this likeness of the made image approaches, as far as it can, to that likeness of the born image, by which God the Son is proclaimed substantially like the Father in all things.

There is to be noted, in this *enigma*, also this likeness of the Word of God, that just as it was said of that Word, *all things were made through him*[^118], where God is proclaimed to have made all things through his only-begotten Word; so there are no works of man which are not first spoken in the heart: whence it is written, *the word is the beginning of every work*[^119].

But here too, when the word is true, then it is the beginning of a good work. And the word is true, when it is begotten of the knowledge of working well, so that there too may be kept, *yes, yes; no, no*:[^120] so that if *yes* is in that knowledge by which one is to live, it may be also in the word by which one is to work; if *no*, no: otherwise such a word will be a lie, not truth; and thence sin, not right work.

There is also in this likeness of our word a likeness of the Word of God, that our word can be such that no work follows it; but there can be no work unless a word precede: just as the Word of God could be with no creature existing;[^121] but no creature could be except through him through whom all things were made.[^122] And therefore not God the Father, not the Holy Spirit, not the Trinity itself, but the Son alone, which is the *Word* of God, *was made flesh*;[^123] although with the Trinity acting: that, with our word following and imitating his example, we might live rightly, that is, having no lie either in the contemplation or in the working of our word. But this is the perfection of this image which will be at some time. To attain which, the good Master[^124] instructs us by the Christian faith and the doctrine of piety, that, *with face unveiled* from the veil of the Law, which is a shadow of things to come,[^125] *beholding the glory of the Lord*, that is, gazing through a mirror,[^126] we may be transformed *into the same image from glory to glory, as by the Spirit of the Lord*[^127], according to the earlier discussion of these words.[^128]

**11.** 21. When, then, by this transformation this image shall have been renewed to perfection,[^129] *we shall be like* God, *for we shall see him*, not through a mirror, but *as he is*: which the Apostle Paul calls *face to face*. But *now*, *in this mirror, in this enigma*, in this likeness of whatever kind, how great also the unlikeness is, who can set forth? Yet I will touch on some things, as I am able, by which it can be noted.

### Academic Philosophy. He Who Is Deceived Lives. The Three Books Against the Skeptics

**12.** 21. First, that knowledge itself, from which our thought is truly formed when we speak what we know, of what kind or how great can it come to a man, however most skilled and most learned? For, excepting the things that come into the mind from the senses of the body—in which so many things are otherwise than they seem, that one who is insane seems to himself sound, too crammed with their verisimilitude; whence the Academic philosophy so prevailed that, doubting of all things, it was insane far more wretchedly[^130]—

these things, then, being excepted, which come into the mind from the senses of the body, how much of things remains which we so know as we know that we live? in which we are by no means afraid lest we be perchance deceived by some verisimilitude,[^131] since it is certain that even he who is deceived lives; nor is this counted among those sights which are presented from without, so that the eye is deceived in it, as it is deceived when an oar is seen broken in the water,[^132] and to those sailing the towers seem to move,[^133] and six hundred other things[^134] which are otherwise than they seem; because this is not discerned through the eye of the flesh.

It is an inmost knowledge by which we know that we live, where not even the Academic can say: "perhaps you are asleep, and do not know it, and see in dreams." For who is ignorant that the sights of those dreaming are most like the sights of those awake?[^135] But he who is certain of the knowledge of his own life does not say in it, "I know I am awake"; but, "I know I am alive": whether, then, he sleeps or is awake, he lives. Nor in that knowledge can he be deceived through dreams; because both to sleep and to see in dreams belongs to one who lives.

Nor can the Academic say this against that knowledge, "perhaps you are raving and do not know it"; because the sights of those raving are most like the sights of the sound: but he who raves lives.[^136] Nor does he say against the Academics, "I know I am not raving"; but, "I know I am alive." Never, then, can he be deceived nor lie who has said that he knows he is alive. Let a thousand kinds of deceptive sights, then, be presented to him who says, "I know I am alive"; he will fear none of these, since even he who is deceived lives.

But if such things alone pertain to human knowledge, they are very few; except that in each kind they are so multiplied that they are not only not few, but are even found to extend through an infinite number. For he who says, "I know I am alive," says that he knows some one thing;[^137] accordingly, if he says: "I know that I know that I am alive"; there are now two; but that he knows these two is a third thing to know: so he can add a fourth, and a fifth, and innumerable ones, if he has the capacity. But because he cannot, by adding single ones, comprehend the innumerable number, or say it innumerable times, this very thing he most certainly comprehends and says: that this is both true, and so innumerable that he[^138] cannot comprehend and say its infinite number.

This can similarly be noted in a certain act of will. For who is there to whom it is not impudently answered, "perhaps you are deceived," when he says, "I want to be blessed"? And if he says, "I know that I want this, and I know that I know this"; now to these two he can add a third, that he knows these two; and a fourth, that he knows that he knows these two; and likewise go on into an infinite number.

Likewise, if someone says, "I do not want to err"; will it not be true that, whether he errs or does not err, he nevertheless does not want to err? Who is there who would not most impudently say to him, "perhaps you are deceived"? when surely, wherever he is deceived, he is yet not deceived that he does not want to be deceived. And if he says that he knows this, he adds as great a number of known things as he wishes, and perceives the number to be infinite.

For he who says: "I do not want to be deceived, and I know that I do not want this, and I know that I know this"; can thence, even if not with convenient expression, show an infinite number: and other things are found which avail against the Academics, who contend that nothing can be known by man.

But a measure is to be applied, especially because in this work we did not undertake this. There are three books of ours on the subject,[^139] written at the first time of our conversion, which whoever shall have been able and willing to read, and, having read them, shall have understood, will surely not be moved by any of the many arguments which were found by them against the perception of truth.

For since there are two kinds of things that are known, one of those which the mind perceives through the senses of the body, the other of those which it perceives through itself: those philosophers babbled much against the senses of the body; but certain most firm perceptions, through itself, of true things which the mind has—such as is that which I said, "I know I am alive"—they could in no way call into doubt.

But far be it from us that we should doubt the things which we have learned through the senses of the body to be true: for through them we have learned the heaven and the earth, and the things in them which are known to us, as far as he who founded both us and them willed them to become known to us.

Far be it also that we should deny that we know the things we have learned by the testimony of others: otherwise we do not know that there is an Ocean;[^140] we do not know that there are lands and cities which most celebrated report commends; we do not know that there were men and their works, which we have learned by historical reading; we do not know the things which are daily reported from everywhere, and are confirmed by consonant and constant[^141] evidences; finally, we do not know in what places, or from what men, we arose; because all these things we have believed on the testimony of others.

And if it is most absurd to say this, it must be confessed that the senses, not only of our own bodies, but also of the bodies of others, have added very much to our knowledge.

### As Our Knowledge Is Unlike the Knowledge of God, So Our Word Is Unlike the Word of God

**12.** 22. All these things, then—both those which it has perceived through itself, and those through the senses of its own body, and those by the testimonies of others—the human mind knows and holds stored in the treasury of memory; from which is begotten a true word, when we speak what we know, but a word before all sound, before all thought of sound. For then the word is most like the known thing from which it is begotten, and its image, since from the vision of knowledge the vision of thought arises—which is a word of no language, a true word from a true thing, having nothing of its own, but all from that knowledge from which it is born. Nor does it matter when he learned it who speaks what he knows; for sometimes, as soon as he learns, he says this; provided only that the word be true, that is, arisen from known things.

**13.** 22. But does God the Father, of whom was born the Word, God of God; does God the Father, then, in that wisdom which he himself is to himself, learn some things through the sense of his body, and others through himself? Who would say this, who thinks of God, not as a rational living being,[^142] but above the rational soul; as far as he can be thought of by those who set him before all living beings and all souls, although they see, conjecturing *through a mirror* and *in an enigma*[^143], not yet *face to face as he is*?[^144] Does God the Father learn from elsewhere, from someone, the very things which he knows, not through a body, which he has none, but through himself, or did he need messengers or witnesses to know them? Surely not: for to know all the things he knows, that perfection suffices for him. He has indeed messengers, that is, angels, yet not such as announce to him things he does not know; for there are none which he does not know: but their good is to consult his truth about their own works; and this is what is meant when they are said to announce some things to him, not that he learns from them, but that they learn from him through his Word without bodily sound. They also announce what he has willed, sent by him to whom he willed, hearing all from him through that Word of his; that is, finding in his truth what they are to do, what, to whom, and when it is to be announced.

For we too pray to him, and yet do not teach him our necessities. *For your Father knows*, says his Word, *what is necessary for you, before you ask him*[^145]. Nor did he come to know these things from some time, that he might know them: but all future temporal things, and among them even what and when we were going to ask of him, and whom and concerning what things he was either going to hear or not to hear, he foreknew without beginning beforehand. But all his creatures, both spiritual and corporeal, he knows not because they are; rather they are because he knows them. For he was not ignorant of what he was going to create. Because, then, he knew, he created; not because he created, he knew. Nor did he know them otherwise as created than as to be created: for nothing was added to his wisdom from them; but, those things existing as was fitting, and when it was fitting, that wisdom remained as it was. So it is written too in the book of *Ecclesiasticus*: *before they were created, all things were known to him; so also after they were finished*[^146]. *So*, he says, not otherwise, both *before they were created, and after they were finished, so are they known to him*.

Far, then, is our knowledge unlike this knowledge. But what is the Knowledge of God, that same is also Wisdom; and what is Wisdom, that same is essence or substance. Because in the wonderful simplicity of that nature, to be wise is not one thing and to be another; but what it is to be wise, this it is also to be, as we have already often said in the earlier books too.[^147] But our knowledge, in very many things, is both losable and recoverable, because for us to be is not the same as to know or to be wise: since we can be, even if we do not know, nor are wise about, the things we have learned from elsewhere. On account of this, just as our knowledge is unlike that knowledge of God, so too our word, which is born of our knowledge, is unlike that Word of God which was born of the essence of the Father. But it is as if I said: "of the knowledge of the Father, of the wisdom of the Father"; or, what is more expressive: "Knowledge of the Father, Wisdom of the Father."

### The Word of God Is Equal to the Father in All Things

**14.** 23. The Word, then, of God the Father, the only-begotten Son, like and equal to the Father in all things, God of God, Light of Light, Wisdom of Wisdom, essence of essence; is altogether that which the Father is, yet not the Father; because this one is the Son, that one the Father.

And through this he knows all that the Father knows: but to him, to know is from the Father, as to be. For to know and to be are there one. And therefore, just as to be is not to the Son from himself, so neither is to know. Accordingly, as it were speaking himself, the Father begot a Word equal to himself in all things. For he would not have spoken himself wholly and perfectly, if there were anything less or more in his Word than in himself. There that is supremely recognized, *yes, yes; no, no*[^148].

And therefore this Word is truly truth: since whatever is in that knowledge from which it was begotten, is also in it; but what is not in it, is not in it either. And this Word can never have anything false: because it is immutably so disposed as is that from which it is. *For the Son cannot do anything of himself, except what he sees the Father doing*[^149]. He cannot do this powerfully, nor is this a weakness, but a firmness, by which truth cannot be false. So God the Father knows all things in himself, knows them in the Son: but in himself as himself, in the Son as his Word, which is of all these things that are in himself. The Son too knows all things likewise, in himself, namely, as those things which were born of those which the Father knows in himself: but in the Father, as those things of which were born the things which the Son himself knows in himself.

The Father and the Son, then, know each other: but the one by begetting, the other by being born. And all the things that are in their knowledge, in their wisdom, in their essence, each one of them sees at once; not piecemeal or singly, as though with an alternating gaze from here to there, and from there to here, and again from there or thence to one thing and another, so that he could not see some things except by not seeing others: but, as I said, he sees all things at once, of which there is none that he does not always see.

### How Great Is the Unlikeness of Our Word and the Word of God; Our Word Is Not Always Truthful nor Everlasting

**14.** 24. But that word of ours which has no sound nor thought of sound, but is of that thing which by seeing within we say, and therefore belongs to no language; and thence is somehow like, in this enigma,[^150] that Word of God which is also God, since this too is so born of our knowledge as that too was born of the knowledge of the Father: our word, then, which we have found to be somehow like it, let it not be irksome to behold how unlike it also is, as far as could be said by us.

**15.** 24. Is our word born of our knowledge alone? Do we not say many things even which we do not know? And we say them not doubting, but supposing them to be true: which, if perchance they are true, are true in the very things of which we speak, not in our word; because a word is not true, except that which is begotten of a thing that is known. Our word, then, is false in this way, not when we lie, but when we are deceived. But when we doubt, there is not yet a word about the thing of which we doubt, but there is a word about the doubt itself.

For although we do not know whether what we doubt about is true, yet we know that we doubt: and through this, when we say this, the word is true; since we say what we know. What of this, that we can even lie? When we do this, surely, willing and knowing, we have a false word: where the true word is that we lie; for this we know. And when we confess that we have lied, we speak truly: for we say what we know;[^151] for we know that we have lied. But that Word which is God, and is more powerful than we, cannot do this. *For he cannot do anything, except what he sees the Father doing*[^152]: and he does not speak of himself, but from the Father is all that he speaks, since the Father uniquely speaks him:[^153] and great is the power of that Word, that it cannot lie; because there cannot be there *yes and no*, but *yes, yes; no, no*.[^154]

But indeed "that is not to be called a word which is not true." If so, I gladly assent. What then, when our word is true, and therefore is rightly called a word: can it, as it can be called either vision of vision, or knowledge of knowledge, so be called essence of essence, as that Word of God is supremely called[^155] and supremely is to be called? Why so? Because for us to be is not the same as to know. For we know many things which in a certain way live through memory, and in a certain way die through forgetfulness:[^156] and therefore, when those things are no longer in our acquaintance, we nevertheless are; and when our knowledge, having slipped from the mind, has perished from us, we nevertheless live.

**15.** 25. Even those things which are so known that they can never slip away, since they are present, and pertain to the nature of the mind itself—such as is that which we know, that we live (for this remains as long as the mind remains, and because the mind always remains, this too always remains): this, then, and whatever like things are found, in which the image of God is rather to be beheld, although they are always known, yet because they are not always also thought of, how an everlasting word can be said of them, since our word is said by our thought, is difficult to find. For it is everlasting for the mind to live, everlasting to know that it lives: and yet it is not everlasting to think of its life, or to think of the knowledge of its life; since, when it begins to think of one thing and another, it will cease to think of this, although it does not cease to know it. From which it comes about that, if there can be in the mind some everlasting knowledge, and the thought of that same knowledge cannot be everlasting, and our true inmost word is not said except by our thought, God alone is understood to have a Word everlasting and coeternal with himself.

Unless perhaps it is to be said that the very possibility of thought—since that which is known, even when it is not thought of, can yet be truly thought of—is a word as perpetual as the knowledge itself is perpetual. But how is it a word, which is not yet formed in the vision of thought? How will it be like the knowledge from which it is born, if it does not have its form, and yet is already called a word because it can have one? For it is as if it were said that it is already to be called a word because it can be a word.

But what is that which can be a word, and therefore is already worthy of the name of word? What, I say, is this formable but not yet formed thing, except something of our mind, which we toss this way and that by a certain rolling motion, when now this, now that, is thought by us, as it has been found or occurred? And then a true word comes to be, when that which I said we toss by a rolling motion arrives at that which we know, and is formed from it, taking on its likeness in every way; so that, as each thing is known, so it is also thought of, that is, without voice, without thought of voice, which surely belongs to some language, so it is said in the heart. And through this, even if we concede—lest we seem to labor over a controversy of a word—that something of our mind which can be formed from our knowledge is already to be called a word, even before it is formed, because it is already, so to speak, formable; who does not see how great is the unlikeness here from that Word of God, which is so in the form of God[^157] that it was not first formable and afterward formed, nor can at any time be formless, but is a simple form, and simply equal to him of whom it is, and to whom it is wonderfully coeternal?

**16.** 25. Wherefore that Word of God is so spoken of that the thought of God is not spoken of, lest something as it were rolling be believed to be in God, which now receives, now recovers a form, that it may be a word, and can lose it, and roll about in a certain formless way. For he had well known words, and had examined the force of thought, the excellent speaker who said in his poem: *and he turns over within himself the varied outcomes of war*[^158]; that is, he thinks.

That Son of God, then, is not called the thought of God, but the Word of God. For our thought, arriving at that which we know, and formed from it, is our true word. And therefore the Word of God ought to be understood without the thought of God, that the form itself may be understood as simple, not having anything formable which could also be formless. There are indeed in the Holy Scriptures said to be *thoughts of God*;[^159] but in that manner of speaking by which there is said there to be also a forgetfulness of God,[^160] which surely, in its proper sense, is none in God.

### Our Word, Even When We Shall Be Like God, Is Never to Be Made Equal to the Word of God

**16.** 26. Wherefore, since so great is now, in this enigma, the unlikeness of God and the Word of God, in which nevertheless some likeness has been found; this too must be confessed, that even when *we shall be like him*, when *we shall see him as he is*[^161] (which surely he who said it, beyond doubt attended to this unlikeness which now is), not even then shall we be equal to him in nature. For always the nature which is made is less than the one making it.[^162] And then indeed our word will not be false, because we shall neither lie nor be deceived: perhaps too our thoughts will not be rolling, going from one thing to another and returning,[^163] but we shall see all our knowledge at once with one gaze: yet when this too shall have come about, if it shall have come about, the creature which was formable will be formed, so that nothing now is lacking to that form to which it ought to come: but yet it will not be to be made equal to that simplicity, where it is not something formable that has been formed or reformed, but form; and there, neither formless nor formed, the substance itself is eternal and immutable.

### Love Is Common to the Three

**17.** 27. We have spoken enough of the Father and the Son, as far as *through* this *mirror* and *in* this *enigma*[^164] we have been able to see. Now of the Holy Spirit we must discourse, as far as it is granted, by God's gift, to see. Which Holy Spirit, according to the Holy Scriptures, is neither of the Father alone, nor of the Son alone, but of both:[^165] and therefore conveys to us the common love by which the Father and the Son love each other.

But that the divine word might exercise us, it made not things set ready at hand, but things to be searched out in secret and drawn out from secret, to be sought with greater zeal. So Scripture did not say, "the Holy Spirit is love"; which, if it had said, it would have taken away no small part of this question: but it said, *God is love*[^166]; so that it is uncertain, and therefore to be sought, whether God the Father is love, or God the Son, or God the Holy Spirit, or God the Trinity itself. For we are not going to say that God was called love for the reason that love itself is some substance worthy of the name of God; but that it is a gift of God, as it was said to God, *since you are my patience*[^167]: for it was not said for the reason that our patience is God's substance; but that it is to us from him, as is read elsewhere, *since from him is my patience*[^168]. For this sense the very speech of the Scriptures easily refutes. For *you are my patience* is of the same sort as *you are, O Lord, my hope*[^169]; and *my God, my mercy*[^170], and many like things. But it was not said, "O Lord my love"; or, "you are my love"; or, "God my love": but it was said thus, *God is love*; as it was said, *God is a Spirit*[^171]. He who does not discern this, let him seek understanding from the Lord, not exposition from us: for we cannot say anything more openly.

**17.** 28. *God*, then, *is love*: but whether the Father, or the Son, or the Holy Spirit, or the Trinity itself, since it too is not three gods, but one God, this is asked. But I have already discussed above in this book[^172] that the Trinity which is God is not so to be received, from those three things which we showed in the trinity of our mind, as that the Father were, as it were, the memory of all three, and the Son the understanding of all three, and the Holy Spirit the love of all three,

as though the Father neither understood for himself nor loved, but the Son understood for him, and the Holy Spirit loved for him, while he himself only remembered for himself and for them;

and that the Son neither remembered nor loved for himself, but the Father remembered for him, and the Holy Spirit loved for him, while he himself only understood both for himself and for them;

and that the Holy Spirit neither remembered nor understood for himself, but the Father remembered for him, and the Son understood for him, while he himself only loved both for himself and for them: but rather thus, that all three, both all and each, have all three, each in his own nature.

Nor are these distant in them, as in us memory is one thing, understanding another, love or charity another: but let there be some one thing which avails for all, like wisdom itself; and it is so held in the nature of each one, that he who has it, is what he has, as an immutable and simple substance.

If, then, these things have been understood, and, as far as it has been granted us to see or conjecture in such great matters, have become clear to be true; I do not know why, just as wisdom is said to be both Father and Son and Holy Spirit, and all together not three, but one Wisdom; so love too should not be said to be both Father and Son and Holy Spirit, and all together one love. For so too the Father is God, and the Son is God, and the Holy Spirit is God, and all together one God.

### Yet the Holy Spirit Is Properly Called Love, as the Word Is Properly Called the Wisdom of God

**17.** 29. And yet not in vain, in this Trinity, is none called the Word of God but the Son, nor the Gift of God but the Holy Spirit,[^173] nor he of whom the Word is begotten and from whom the Holy Spirit principally proceeds but God the Father.[^174] But I added "principally," because the Holy Spirit is found to proceed from the Son too.[^175] But this too the Father gave him—not to one already existing and not yet having: but whatever he gave to the only-begotten Word, he gave by begetting. So, then, he begot him that the common Gift should proceed also from him, and that the Holy Spirit should be the spirit of both.

This distinction of the inseparable Trinity, then, is not to be received in passing, but to be diligently beheld. For hence it came about that the Word of God was also properly called the Wisdom of God,[^176] although both the Father and the Holy Spirit are Wisdom. If, then, any one of these three is properly to be named love, what is more fitting than that this should be the Holy Spirit? namely, that in that simple and highest nature substance be not one thing and love another; but the substance itself be love, and love itself be substance, whether in the Father, or in the Son, or in the Holy Spirit; and yet that the Holy Spirit be properly named Love.

**17.** 30. Just as by the name of the Law sometimes all the eloquences of the Holy Scriptures of the Old Instrument together are signified. For the Apostle, setting down a testimony from the prophet Isaiah, where he says, *in other tongues and with other lips I will speak to this people*, nevertheless premised, *it is written in the Law*[^177]. And the Lord himself says, *it is written in their Law, that they hated me without cause*[^178]; though this is read in a Psalm.[^179]

But sometimes it is properly called the Law, which was given through Moses,[^180] according to what was said, *the Law and the Prophets were until John*[^181]; and, *on these two precepts hangs the whole Law and the Prophets*[^182]. Here surely it was properly called the Law, which was from Mount Sinai. But by the name of the Prophets the Psalms too were signified: and yet in another place the Savior himself says, *it behooved that all things should be fulfilled which were written in the Law, and the Prophets, and the Psalms concerning me*[^183]. Here again he wished the name of the Prophets to be understood, the Psalms being excepted.

The Law, then, is said universally, together with the Prophets and the Psalms, and is also said properly of that which was given through Moses.[^184] Likewise the Prophets are said commonly, together with the Psalms, and are said properly apart from the Psalms.

And by many other examples it can be shown that many names of things are both set down universally, and applied properly to certain things, unless, in a matter that is plain, length of speech is to be avoided. I have said this for this reason, lest anyone should think that we unfittingly call the Holy Spirit Love, because both God the Father and God the Son can be named Love.

### Because Through the Spirit the Love of God Is Poured Out in Our Hearts

**17.** 31. Just as, then, we properly call the only Word of God by the name of Wisdom, although universally both the Holy Spirit[^185] and the Father himself are Wisdom;[^186] so the Holy Spirit is properly named by the word Love, although universally both the Father and the Son are Love.

But the Word of God, that is, the only-begotten Son of God, was openly called the wisdom of God by the apostolic mouth, where he says, *Christ the power of God and the Wisdom of God*[^187]:

but where the Holy Spirit is called love, we find, if we diligently search out the eloquence of the apostle John; who, when he had said, *most beloved, let us love one another, because love is from God*; following, added, *and everyone who loves is born of God: he who does not love has not known God, because God is love*[^188]. Here he made manifest that he had called that love God, which he said was from God. God, then, from God is love.

But because both the Son is born of God the Father, and the Holy Spirit proceeds from God the Father,[^189] it is rightly asked which of them we ought rather to understand here to have been called love, God. For the Father alone is God in such a way as not to be from God: and through this the love which is God in such a way as to be from God is either the Son or the Holy Spirit.

But in what follows, when he had mentioned the love of God, not by which we love him, but by which *he himself loved us, and sent his Son as a propitiation for our sins*; and had thence exhorted that we too should love one another, and that so God should remain in us, since he had surely called love God, immediately, wishing to say something more openly about this matter, *in this*, he says, *we know that we remain in him, and he in us, because he has given us of his Spirit*[^190]. The Holy Spirit, then, of whom he has given us, makes us remain in God, and him in us: but this love does. He is therefore God, love.

Finally, a little after, when he had repeated this very thing and said, *God is love*; immediately he subjoined, *and he who remains in love remains in God, and God remains in him*[^191]: whence above he had said, *in this we know that we remain in him, and he in us, because he has given us of his Spirit*. He himself, then, is signified where it is read, *God is love*. God the Holy Spirit, then, who proceeds from God, when he has been given to a man, kindles him to the love of God and of neighbor, and is himself love.[^192] For a man has nothing from which to love God, except from God.

On account of which a little after he says: *let us love him, because he first loved us*[^193]. The apostle Paul too says, *the love of God is poured out in our hearts, through the Holy Spirit who has been given to us*[^194].

### Because the Spirit Is the Gift of God

**18.** 32. There is nothing more excellent than this gift of God.[^195] It alone is what divides between the sons of the eternal kingdom[^196] and the sons of eternal perdition. Other gifts too are given through the Spirit,[^197][^198] but without love they profit nothing.[^199] Unless, then, the Holy Spirit be so imparted to each one as to make him a lover of God and of neighbor, he is not transferred from the left to the right.[^200] Nor is the Spirit properly called the Gift, except on account of love; which he who does not have, if he speaks with the tongues of men and of angels, is sounding brass and a tinkling cymbal: and if he has prophecy, and knows all mysteries, and all knowledge, and if he has all faith, so as to remove mountains, he is nothing: and if he distributes all his substance, and if he gives up his body to be burned, it profits him nothing.[^201] How great a good, then, is that without which goods so great lead no one to eternal life?

But love itself, or charity (for both are the name of one thing), if he has it who does not speak with tongues, nor has prophecy, nor knows all mysteries and all knowledge, nor distributes all his goods to the poor, either by not having what to distribute or being prevented by some necessity, nor gives up his body to be burned, if there is no temptation to such suffering, leads to the kingdom, so that faith itself is not made useful except by love. For without love faith can indeed exist, but cannot also profit. On account of which the apostle Paul too says, *in Christ Jesus neither circumcision nor uncircumcision avails anything, but faith which works through love*[^202]: thus distinguishing it from that faith by which even the demons believe and tremble.[^203]

Love, then, which is from God and is God, is properly the Holy Spirit, through whom the Love of God is poured out in our hearts, through which the whole Trinity dwells in us.[^204] Wherefore most rightly the Holy Spirit, since he is God, is also called the Gift of God.[^205] And what is this Gift properly to be understood as, except Love, which leads to God, and without which any other gift of God whatever does not lead to God?

### Which Is Proved from the Scriptures

**19.** 33. Or is this too to be proved, that the Holy Spirit was called the Gift of God in the Sacred Writings? If this too is awaited, we have in the *Gospel according to John* the words of the Lord[^206] saying, *if anyone thirsts, let him come to me, and drink. He who believes in me, as the Scripture says, rivers of living water shall flow from his belly*.[^207] Then the Evangelist, following, added: *but this he said of the Spirit, which those believing in him were going to receive*[^208]. Whence the apostle Paul too says: *and we have all drunk of one Spirit*[^209]. But whether this water, which is the Holy Spirit, was called the gift of God, this is asked. But as here we have found this water to be the Holy Spirit, so we find elsewhere, in the same Gospel, this water called the Gift of God.[^210]

For the same Lord, when he was speaking with the Samaritan woman at the well, to whom he had said, *give me to drink*; when she had answered that the Jews did not associate with Samaritans; Jesus answered and said to her: *if you knew the Gift of God, and who it is that says to you, "give me to drink," you would perhaps have asked of him, and he would have given you living water. The woman says to him: "Lord, you have nothing to draw with, and the well is deep; whence then have you living water?"* and the rest. *Jesus answered and said to her: "everyone who drinks of this water will thirst again; but he who drinks of the water which I will give him will not thirst forever: but the water which I will give him will become in him a fountain of water springing up into eternal life."*[^211] Since, then, this living water, as the Evangelist expounded,[^212] is the Holy Spirit, beyond doubt the Spirit is the Gift of God,[^213] of which here the Lord says: *if you knew the Gift of God, and who it is that says to you, "give me to drink"; you would perhaps have asked of him, and he would have given you living water*. For what he says there, *rivers of living water shall flow from his belly*; he says in this place, *it will become in him a fountain of water springing up into eternal life*.

**19.** 34. The apostle Paul too says, *to each one of us is given grace according to the measure of the gift of Christ*; and that he might show that the gift of Christ is the Holy Spirit, following, he added, *on account of which it says: "he ascended on high, he led captivity captive, he gave gifts to men."*[^214] But it is most well-known that the Lord Jesus, when after the resurrection from the dead he had ascended into heaven, gave the Holy Spirit, filled with whom those who had believed spoke in the tongues of all the nations.[^215]

Nor let it disturb you that he says *gifts*, not "Gift": for he set down that testimony from a Psalm. But this in the *Psalm* is read thus: *you ascended on high, you led captivity captive, you received gifts among men*. For so many manuscripts have, and especially the Greek ones, and from the Hebrew we have it so rendered. The Apostle, then, said *gifts*, as the Prophet did, not "gift." But although the Prophet said, *you received gifts among men*; the Apostle preferred to say, *he gave gifts to men*; so that from both words—the one prophetic, the other apostolic, since in both is the authority of divine speech—the fullest sense might be rendered.

For both are true, both that he gave to men, and that he received among men. He gave to men, as the head to its members: he received among men—the same one, surely—in his members,[^216] on account of which members of his he cried from heaven, *Saul, Saul, why do you persecute me*? and of which members of his he says, *when you did it to one of the least of mine, you did it to me*. He himself, then, Christ, both gave from heaven, and received on earth.

But for this reason both the Prophet and the Apostle said *gifts*, because through the gift, which is the Holy Spirit, many *gifts*, which are proper to each, are distributed in common to all the members of Christ. For not each one severally has all, but these have these, others others: although all have that very Gift from which the proper gifts are distributed to each, that is, the Holy Spirit.[^217]

For elsewhere too, when he had mentioned many gifts, *all these*, he says, *works one and the same Spirit, distributing his own to each as he wills*[^218]. Which word is found also in the *Letter which is to the Hebrews*, where it is written, *God bearing witness with signs and wonders, and various powers, and distributions of the Holy Spirit*[^219]. And here, when he had said: *he ascended on high, he led captivity captive, he gave gifts to men; but that he ascended*, he says, *what is it, except that he also descended into the lower parts of the earth? He who descended is the same who also ascended above all the heavens, that he might fill all things. And he himself gave some indeed to be apostles, but some prophets, but some evangelists, but some pastors and teachers*[^220].

Behold why they were called gifts: because, as he says elsewhere: *are all apostles? are all prophets?*[^221] and the rest. But here he added, *for the perfecting of the saints, unto the work of ministry, unto the building up of the body of Christ*[^222]. This is the house which, as the Psalm sings, is built after the captivity:[^223] since of those who are rescued from the devil, by whom they were held captive, of these is built the house of Christ, which body[^224] is called the Church.[^225]

But this *captivity* he himself *led captive*,[^226] who overcame the devil. And lest he should drag with him into eternal punishment those who were going to be members of the holy head, he bound him first with the bonds of justice, then of power. The devil himself, then, is the captivity which was led captive by him who *ascended on high*, and *gave gifts to men*[^227], or received among men.

**19.** 35. But the apostle Peter, as is read in that canonical book where the *Acts of the Apostles* are written, speaking of Christ, when the Jews were moved in heart and said, *what then shall we do, brothers? show us*; said to them: *do penance, and let each one of you be baptized in the name of*[^228] *Jesus Christ, unto the remission of sins; and you shall receive the gift of the Holy Spirit*[^229].

Likewise in the same book it is read that Simon Magus wished to give money to the apostles, that he might receive from them the power by which, through the imposition of his hand, the Holy Spirit might be given. To whom the same Peter says: *your money be with you unto perdition, because you have esteemed that you could possess the gift of God through money*[^230].

And in another place of the same book, when Peter was speaking to Cornelius and those who were with him, announcing and preaching Christ, the Scripture says: *while Peter was still speaking these words, the Holy Spirit fell upon all who were hearing the word, and those of the circumcision who had come with Peter were astonished, because the gift of the Holy Spirit was poured out upon the nations too. For they heard them speaking with tongues, and magnifying God*[^231].

Of this deed of his, that he had baptized the uncircumcised, because before they were baptized—that he might remove the knot of this question—the Holy Spirit had come upon them, when Peter afterward rendered an account to the brothers who were at Jerusalem, and they, hearing this, were disturbed, he says, after the rest, *but when I had begun to speak to them, the Holy Spirit fell upon them, as also upon us in the beginning. And I remembered the word of the Lord, as he said: John indeed baptized with water, but you shall be baptized with the Holy Spirit. If, then, God gave them an equal gift, as also to us who believed in the Lord Jesus Christ; who was I, that I could forbid God to give them the Holy Spirit*[^232]?

And there are many other testimonies of the Scriptures which concordantly attest that the Gift of God[^233] is the Holy Spirit, insofar as he is given to those who through him love God. But it is too long to gather all. And what is enough for those to whom the things we have said are not enough?

**19.** 36. They are surely to be admonished, since they now see that the Holy Spirit is called the Gift of God, that when they hear, *the gift of the Holy Spirit*,[^234] they should recognize that manner of speaking which was said, *in the stripping off of the body of the flesh*.[^235] For just as the body of the flesh is nothing other than flesh; so the Gift of the Holy Spirit is nothing other than the Holy Spirit. He is, then, the Gift of God insofar as he is given to those to whom he is given. But in himself he is God, even if he is given to no one, because God was coeternal with the Father and the Son before he was given to anyone. Nor, because they give him, is he given, therefore less than they. For he is so given as the Gift of God, that he also gives himself as God. For it cannot be said that he who is not within his own power, of whom it was said, *the Spirit breathes where he wills*[^236]; and, in the Apostle, what I have already mentioned above,[^237] *all these works one and the same Spirit, distributing his own to each as he wills*. There is there no condition of the given and dominion of the givers, but a concord of the given and of the givers.

### Because the Communion of the Father and the Son Is Ineffable. But He Is Not Love Alone in the Trinity, Because Love Is the Divine Substance

**19.** 37. Wherefore, if the Holy Scripture proclaims, *God is Love*;[^238] and that Love is from God, and works in us that we may remain in God, and he in us, and we know this thence, because he has given us of his Spirit: he himself, the Spirit, is God, love.[^239] Then, if among the gifts of God nothing is greater than love, and there is no greater gift of God than the Holy Spirit, what is more consistent than that he himself be the Love who is called both God and from God? And if the Love by which the Father loves the Son, and the Son loves the Father, ineffably demonstrates the communion of both; what is more fitting than that he should be called Love properly, who is the Spirit common to both? For this is more soundly believed or understood, that not the Holy Spirit alone is Love in that Trinity, but that he is not in vain properly named love, on account of the things that have been said. Just as he is not alone in that Trinity either spirit or holy, because both the Father is spirit, and the Son is spirit, and the Father is holy, and the Son is holy, which piety does not doubt:[^240] and yet he himself is not in vain properly called the Holy Spirit. For because he is common to both, he is himself properly called that which both are in common.

Otherwise, if in that Trinity the Holy Spirit alone is love, surely the Son too is found to be the Son not of the Father alone, but also of the Holy Spirit. For he is so called and read in countless places the only-begotten Son of God the Father, that yet that too is true which the Apostle says of God the Father: *who rescued us from the power of darkness, and transferred us into the kingdom of the Son of his love*[^241]. He did not say, "of his Son"; which if he said, he would say most truly, just as, because he often said it, he said most truly: but he says, *of the Son of his love*. The Son, then, is also of the Holy Spirit, if there is not in that Trinity the love of God except the Holy Spirit.

And if this is most absurd, it remains that the Holy Spirit is not alone there love, but, on account of the things of which I have discussed enough, is properly so called: but that which was said, *of the Son of his love*, is to be understood as nothing other than "of his beloved Son," or, finally, of the Son of his substance. For the love of the Father, which is in his nature ineffably simple, is nothing other than his very nature and substance, as we have already often said, and it is not irksome often to repeat. And through this "the Son of his love" is none other than he who is begotten of his substance.

### Against the Opinion of Eunomius

**20.** 38. Wherefore the dialectic of Eunomius, from whom the Eunomian heretics arose, is to be laughed at: who, since he could not understand, nor was willing to believe,[^242] that the only-begotten Word of God, through whom all things were made, is the Son of God by nature, that is, begotten of the substance of the Father; said that he is the Son not of his nature or substance or essence, but the son of the will of God, wishing, namely, to assert that there is accidental to God the will by which he begot the Son:[^243] namely for this reason, that we at some time will something which before we did not will; as though our nature were not for this reason understood to be mutable, which far be it that we should believe to be in God. For it was not written for any other reason, *many thoughts are in the heart of a man; but the counsel of the Lord remains forever*[^244]; than that we may understand or believe[^245] that, just as God is eternal, so his counsel is forever, and through this immutable, as he himself is. But what is true of thoughts, this can most truly be said also of acts of will: "many acts of will are in the heart of a man; but the will of the Lord remains forever." Some, lest they should call the only-begotten Word the son of the counsel or will of God, said that the same Word is the very counsel or will of the Father. But better, as I judge, is it said counsel of counsel, and will of will, as substance of substance, wisdom of wisdom: lest, by that absurdity which we have already refuted, the Son should be said to make the Father wise or willing, if the Father does not have in his substance counsel or will.

Acutely indeed did someone reply to a heretic most craftily asking whether God begot the Son willing or unwilling: so that if he said, "unwilling," a most absurd misery of God would follow; but if, "willing," he would at once conclude, as it were by an invincible reason, what he intended, that the Son is not of nature, but of will. But he, most vigilantly, asked of him in turn whether God the Father is God willing or unwilling: so that if he answered, "unwilling," that misery would follow which it is great insanity to believe of God; but if he said, "willing," it would be answered to him, "therefore he too is God by his will, not by his nature."[^246] What, then, remained, except that he should fall silent, and see himself bound by his own question with an insoluble bond? But the will of God, if any person in the Trinity is also properly to be called it, this name befits the Holy Spirit rather, as does love. For what else is love than will?

### Epilogue of What Has Been Said: Man, the Image of the Trinity, Ought to Refer All That He Lives to Remembering, Seeing, and Loving the Trinity

**20.** 39. I see that I have discussed the Holy Spirit in this book,[^247] according to the Holy Scriptures, this much, which is enough for the faithful, who already know that the Holy Spirit is God,[^248] of no other substance, nor less than the Father and the Son, which in the earlier books we taught to be true according to those same Scriptures.[^249] Of the creature too which God made, as far as we were able, we admonished those who demand a reason about such things, that they might behold his invisible things, through the things that have been made,[^250] as they could, understood, and especially through the rational or intellectual creature, which was made *to the image of God*;[^251] through which, as through a mirror, they might, as far as they could, if they could, discern the Trinity God, in our memory, understanding, will.[^252] Which three, divinely established by nature in his own mind, whoever vividly perceives, and how great it is in it, whence even the everlasting and immutable nature can be recalled, beheld, desired—he remembers through memory, beholds through understanding, embraces through love—surely finds the image of that highest Trinity. To remembering, seeing, loving which highest Trinity, that he may recall it, contemplate it, be delighted in it, he ought to refer all that he lives. But lest he should so compare this image, made by that same Trinity and by its own fault changed for the worse, to that same Trinity, as to think it like in every way; but rather discern in this likeness of whatever kind also a great unlikeness, I have admonished as far as seemed sufficient.

### On the Likeness of the Father and the Son in Our Memory and Understanding

**21.** 40. I have taken care to signify, as I could—not that it might now be seen *face to face*, but through this likeness *in an enigma*, by conjecturing however little—God the Father and God the Son, that is, God the begetter, who in his Word coeternal with himself in a certain way spoke all things which he has substantially, and that same Word of his, God, who has neither more nor less anything, even he himself, substantially, than what is in him who begot the Word not falsely but truly: I have taken care to signify these, as I could, in the memory and understanding of our mind: attributing to memory all that we know, even if we do not think of it; but to understanding the informing of thought in a certain proper manner.

For by thinking, what we find to be true, this we are especially said to understand, and this indeed we again leave in the memory. But that is the more hidden depth of our memory, where we first found this too when we thought of it, and where the inmost word is begotten, which is of no language, as it were knowledge of knowledge, and vision of vision, and understanding which appears in thought, of understanding which was already in the memory, but lay hidden: although thought itself too, unless it had a certain memory of its own, would not return to those things which it had left in the memory, when it thought of other things.

### In the Will the Image of the Holy Spirit

**21.** 41. But of the Holy Spirit I have shown nothing *in* this *enigma* that would seem like him, except our will, or love or affection, which is a more powerful will: since our will, which is naturally in us, has various affections, according as the things to which it is adjacent or which it meets, by which we are allured or offended, present themselves to it. What, then, is it? Are we going to say that our will, when it is right, does not know what it seeks, what it avoids? Moreover, if it knows, surely there is in it a certain knowledge of its own, which cannot be without memory and understanding. Or is anyone to be listened to who says that love, which *does not act perversely*[^253], does not know what it does?

As, then, there is understanding, there is love in that principal memory, in which we find ready and stored up that to which we can come by thinking; because we find both these things there, when by thinking we find that we both understand something and love it, which were there even when we were not thinking of them: and as there is memory, there is love in this understanding which is formed by thought; which true word, in the language of no nation, we say within, when we say what we know; for it does not return to anything except by recollecting, and the gaze of our thought does not care to return except by loving: so love, which joins the vision set in the memory, and the vision of thought formed from it, as it were parent and offspring, unless it had a knowledge of seeking, which cannot be without memory and understanding, would not know what it rightly loves.

### But How Great Is the Disparity of the Trinity Which Is in Man from the Trinity Which Is God

**22.** 42. But these things, when they are in one person, as is man, someone can say to us: "these three, memory, understanding, and love, are mine, not their own; nor do they do what they do for themselves, but for me—nay, I do it through them. For I remember through memory, understand through understanding, love through love: and when I turn the gaze of thought to my memory, and so say in my heart what I know, and a true word is begotten of my knowledge, both are mine, both the knowledge surely and the word. For I know, I say in my heart what I know. And when in my memory by thinking I find that I already understand, already love something—which understanding and love were there even before I thought of them—I find my understanding and my love in my memory, by which I understand, I love, not they themselves. Likewise, when thought remembers, and wishes to return to those things which it had left in the memory, and to behold them, understood,[^254] and to say them within, it is my memory that remembers, and by my will, not its own, that it wills. My love itself too, when it remembers and understands what it ought to seek, what to avoid, remembers through my memory, not through its own; and through my understanding, not through its own, it understands whatever it understandingly loves."

Which can be said briefly: "I, through all those three, remember, I understand, I love, who am neither memory, nor understanding, nor love, but have these." These things, then, can be said by one person, who has these three, is not itself these three. But in the simplicity of that highest nature which is God, although there is one God, yet there are three persons, the Father, and the Son, and the Holy Spirit.

### The Disparity Is Drawn Out

**23.** 43. The Trinity itself, the thing, is one thing, then; the image of the Trinity in another thing is another; on account of which image both that in which these three are is at once called an image; as an image is at once called both the panel, and what is painted on it; but on account of the painting which is on it, the panel too is called by the name of image.

But in that highest Trinity, which incomparably excels all things, so great is the inseparability, that, whereas a trinity of men cannot be called one man, in that one there is both called and is one God; nor is that Trinity in one God, but it is one God.[^255] Nor again, just as this image which is man, having those three, is one person, is that Trinity so: but there are three Persons, the Father of the Son, and the Son of the Father, and the Spirit of the Father and the Son. For although the memory of man, and especially that which the beasts do not have, that is, by which intelligible things are so contained that they did not come into it through the senses of the body, has in its small measure, in this image of the Trinity, an incomparably unequal, but yet some sort of likeness of the Father; and likewise the understanding of man, which is formed thence by the intention of thought, when what is known is said, and is a word of the heart of no language, has in its great disparity some likeness of the Son; and the love of man, proceeding from knowledge, and joining memory and understanding, as it were common to parent and offspring, whence it is understood to be neither parent nor offspring, has in this image some, though far unequal, likeness of the Holy Spirit: yet not, just as in this image of the Trinity these three are not one man, but are of one man, so in that highest Trinity, of which this is the image, are those three of one God, but it is one God, and they are three, not one person.

Which surely is wonderfully ineffable, or ineffably wonderful, that, although this image of the Trinity is one person, but the highest Trinity itself is three Persons, that Trinity of three Persons is more inseparable than this of one. For that, in the nature of divinity—or, what is better said, of deity—is what it is, and is among themselves immutably and always equal: nor was it ever not, or otherwise; nor will it ever not be, or otherwise. But those three which are in the unequal image, even if not by places, since they are not bodies, yet among themselves now in this life are separated by "magnitudes."

For not because there are no masses there, do we therefore not see in one person memory to be greater than understanding, in another the contrary: in another these two to be surpassed by the magnitude of love, whether the two themselves are equal among themselves, or are not.[^256] And so by single ones two, and by two single ones, and by single ones single ones, the lesser are overcome by the greater. And when, healed of all languor, they shall be equal among themselves, not even then will that thing which through grace is not changed be made equal to the nature immutable: because the creature is not made equal to the Creator, and when it shall be healed of all languor, it will be changed.

### The image is to be understood as a mirror.

**23.** 44. But this Trinity, which is not only incorporeal but also supremely inseparable and truly unchangeable, we shall see far more clearly and certainly when the vision promised to us *face to face* has come[^257] than we now see its image, which we ourselves are. Yet those who, in this image, see *through* the *mirror* and *in* the *riddle*[^258] insofar as it is granted us to see in this life, are not those who behold in their own mind the things we have set out and commended; they are rather those who see the mind as an image, so that they can in some way refer what they see to him whose image it is, and through the image they see by beholding it, can also see him by conjecture, since they cannot yet see him *face to face*. For the Apostle does not say, we now see a mirror, but, *we see* *through a mirror*.

**24.** 44. Those, then, who see their mind in the way it can be seen, and in it this trinity about which I have argued in many ways as best I could, yet do not believe or understand[^259] it to be the image of God, do indeed see the mirror, but they so fail to see through the mirror the one who is now to be seen *through the mirror* that they do not even know that the very mirror they see is a mirror, that is, an image.

But if they did know this, perhaps they would also perceive that the one whose mirror this is must be sought through it and in some way seen through it in the meantime, when the heart has been cleansed by *faith unfeigned*,[^260] so that he who is now seen *through a mirror* may be able to be seen *face to face*.[^261]

When the faith that cleanses the heart is despised, what do they accomplish by understanding the things that are argued so subtly about the nature of the human mind, except that they are condemned by the witness of their own understanding? In this understanding they surely would not labor, and would scarcely reach anything certain, were they not wrapped in penal darkness and weighed down by the *corruptible body that burdens the soul*.[^262] And by what merit is this evil inflicted, except that of sin?

Admonished, therefore, by the greatness of so great an evil, they ought to follow the Lamb *who takes away the sin of the world*.

**25.** 44. For over those who belong to him, even those much slower in talent than these people, the envious powers have no right to retain them when at the end of this life they are released from the body. The Lamb conquered those powers, slain by them though he owed them no debt of sin, not by the might of power but by the justice of his blood.[^263]

Thus, freed from the power of the devil, they are received by the holy angels, delivered from all evils through *the man Christ Jesus, the mediator of God and men*; for since the divine Scriptures, both old and new, are in agreement—those through which Christ was foretold and those through which he was proclaimed—*there is no other name under heaven by which men must be saved*. And once cleansed from every contagion of corruption, they are established in peaceful dwellings until they receive back their bodies, but now incorruptible, bodies that will adorn them, not burden them. For it has pleased the best and wisest Creator that the spirit of man, devoutly subject to God, should have its body happily subject to it, and that this happiness should remain without end.

### In the vision we shall see without any difficulty why the Holy Spirit does not proceed as one begotten by the Father and the Son.

**25.** 45. There we shall see the truth without any difficulty, and we shall enjoy it in its utmost clarity and certainty. Nor shall we seek anything by a mind that reasons, but by a mind that contemplates we shall discern why the Holy Spirit is not the Son, although he proceeds from the Father.

In that light there will be no question; but here this very experience has appeared so difficult to me—and without doubt it will appear similarly difficult to those who read these things carefully and intelligently—that, although I had promised in another place in the second book of this work that I would speak of it,[^264] whenever I wished to show something in the creation that we are which resembles that reality, my expression, such as it was, did not keep up with my understanding; though even in the understanding itself I felt that I had achieved more of an attempt than an effect.

And in the one person that man is, I did indeed wish to have found an image of that supreme Trinity, and to show, especially in the ninth book, those three things in a changeable thing so that they might be more easily understood, even through intervals of time.[^265] But the three things of one person could not, as human reflection requires, be made to correspond to those three persons, as we have demonstrated in this fifteenth book.

**26.** 45. Then, in that supreme Trinity which is God, there are no intervals of time through which it could be shown, or even asked, whether the Son was first born of the Father and the Holy Spirit afterward proceeded from them both. For Holy Scripture calls him the Spirit of both.[^266] For he is the one of whom the Apostle says, *but because you are sons, God has sent the Spirit of his Son into our hearts*;[^267] and he is the one of whom the same Son says, *for it is not you who speak, but the Spirit of your Father who speaks in you*.

And by many other testimonies of the divine words it is proved that the Spirit who is properly called in the Trinity the Holy Spirit is the Spirit of the Father and of the Son. Of him the Son himself again says, *whom I will send to you from the Father*; and in another place, *whom the Father will send in my name*. That he proceeds from both is taught in this way: that the Son himself says, *he proceeds from the Father*. And when he had risen from the dead and appeared to his disciples, *he breathed on them and said,* *receive the Holy Spirit*, so as to show that the Spirit proceeds from him as well. And it is the *power* that *went out from him*, as we read in the Gospel, *and healed all*.[^268]

### The Lord Jesus gave the Holy Spirit as God and received him as man.

**26.** 46. As to what the reason was that after his resurrection he first gave the Holy Spirit on earth[^269] and afterward sent him from heaven,[^270] I think it is this: that through this gift *love is poured out in our hearts*,[^271] by which we love God and neighbor, according to those two commandments *on* which *the whole Law and the Prophets depend*.[^272] Signifying this, the Lord Jesus gave the Holy Spirit twice: once on earth on account of the love of neighbor, and again from heaven on account of the love of God. And if perhaps some other reason is given for the Holy Spirit being given twice, we ought not to doubt that it was the same Spirit[^273] that was given when Jesus breathed on them, of whom he soon afterward said, *go, baptize the*[^274] *nations in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit*, where this Trinity is especially commended.[^275] He, then, is the one who was also given from heaven on the day of Pentecost,[^276] that is, ten days after the Lord ascended into heaven.

How, then, is he not God who gives the Holy Spirit? Indeed, how great is the God who gives God? For none of his disciples gave the Holy Spirit. They prayed, of course, that he might come upon those on whom they laid hands; they did not themselves give him. This custom the Church preserves even now in its prelates. And so Simon Magus too, when he offered the apostles money, did not say, *give me also this power, that* "I may give the Holy Spirit," but, he said, *that on whomever I lay hands, he may receive the Holy Spirit*. For Scripture had not said above, "Simon, seeing that the apostles gave the Holy Spirit," but had said, *Simon, seeing that through the laying on of the apostles' hands the Holy Spirit was given*.

For this reason the Lord Jesus himself not only gave the Holy Spirit as God, but also received him as man; and therefore he was said to be full of grace.[^277] And it is written of him still more plainly in the *Acts of the Apostles*, *that God anointed him with the Holy Spirit*. Not, of course, with visible oil, but with the gift of grace, which is signified by the visible ointment with which the Church anoints the baptized. Nor indeed was Christ then anointed with the Holy Spirit when, after his baptism, the Spirit descended upon him like a dove;[^278] for then he deigned to prefigure his body, that is, his Church, in which especially the baptized receive the Holy Spirit.[^279]

But he is to be understood as anointed with that mystical and invisible anointing when the *Word* of God *was made flesh*;[^280] that is, when human nature, without any preceding merits of good works, was joined to God the Word in the virgin's womb, so that with him it became one person. For this reason we confess him to have been born of the Holy Spirit and the Virgin Mary. For it is utterly absurd that we should believe that he received the Holy Spirit when he was already thirty years old—for it was at that age that he was baptized by John[^281]—but rather that he came to that baptism[^282] as one altogether without sin, and likewise not without the Holy Spirit.

For if it is written of John his servant and forerunner, *he will be filled with the Holy Spirit even from his mother's womb*—because, although sown by his father, he nevertheless received the Holy Spirit while being formed in the womb—what is to be understood or believed of the man Christ, the very conception of whose flesh was not carnal but spiritual?[^283] In that which is also written of him, that he received from the Father the promise of the Holy Spirit and poured it out,[^284] both natures are shown, namely the human and the divine: for he received as man and poured out as God. We, however, can indeed receive this gift according to our small measure, but we certainly cannot pour it out upon others; rather, that this may be done, we call upon God over them, by whom it is brought about.

### The Spirit proceeds from the Father and the Son, but principally from the Father.

**26.** 47. Can we, then, ask whether the Holy Spirit had already proceeded from the Father when the Son was born,[^285] or whether he had not yet proceeded, and once the Son was born proceeded from both—where there are no times, just as we were able to ask, when we found in time[^286] that the will proceeds first from the human mind so that what is found may be called offspring, and once this is brought forth or begotten, that will is perfected, coming to rest in this end, that what had been the seeking one's appetite becomes the enjoying one's love, a love that now proceeds from both, that is, from the begetting mind and the begotten notion, as from a parent and offspring? Such things cannot be asked at all there, where nothing is begun in time so as to be perfected by something following in time.

Therefore, whoever can understand the generation of the Son from the Father without time, let him understand the procession of the Holy Spirit from both without time.

And whoever can understand, in what the Son says, *as the Father has life in himself, so he has granted the Son also to have life in himself*, that the Father did not give life to the Son as one already existing without life, but begot him without time in such a way that the life which the Father gave to the Son by begetting him is coeternal with the life of the Father who gave it—let him understand that, just as the Father has it in himself that the Holy Spirit proceeds from him, so he has granted to the Son that the same Holy Spirit proceeds from him, and both without time;

and that the Holy Spirit is thus said to proceed from the Father in such a way that it is understood that what he also proceeds from the Son, he has from the Father. For if the Son has whatever he has from the Father, then he certainly has from the Father that the Holy Spirit also proceeds from him.[^287]

But let no times be imagined there, times that have a before and an after; for there are absolutely none there.

How, then, would he not most absurdly be called the son of both, since, just as generation from the Father gives the Son his essence without any beginning of time and without any changeableness of nature, so procession from both gives the Holy Spirit his essence without any beginning of time and without any changeableness of nature? For this reason, although we do not call the Holy Spirit begotten, we nevertheless do not dare to call him unbegotten, lest by this word anyone should suspect either two fathers in that Trinity, or two who are not from another.

For the Father alone is not from another, and therefore he alone is called unbegotten—not indeed in the Scriptures, but in the customary usage of those who dispute and who bring forth such speech as they are able about so great a matter.[^288] The Son, however, is born of the Father; and the Holy Spirit proceeds principally from the Father, and, since the Father gives this without any interval of time, proceeds in common from both.[^289] Now he would be called the son of the Father and the Son if—what is abhorrent to the sense of all sound persons—both had begotten him. He is therefore not begotten by both, but the Spirit of both proceeds from both.

### It is extremely difficult to distinguish generation from procession.

**27.** 48. But because in that coeternal, equal, incorporeal, ineffably unchangeable, and inseparable Trinity it is extremely difficult to distinguish generation from procession, let it suffice for the time being, for those who are not able to be stretched any further, to set before them what we said about this matter in a certain sermon delivered to the ears of the Christian people, and what, having said it, we wrote down. For among other things, when through the testimonies of the Holy Scriptures I had taught that the Holy Spirit proceeds from both:

*If, then,* I said, *the Holy Spirit proceeds both from the Father and from the Son, why did the Son say, 'he proceeds from the Father'? Why, do you think, except in the way he is accustomed to refer to him even what is his own, namely the one from whom he himself also is? Hence too that saying of his, 'my teaching is not mine, but his who sent me.'*

*If, therefore, what is here understood is his teaching, which he nonetheless said was not his own but the Father's, how much more is it to be understood there that the Holy Spirit proceeds from him as well, where he speaks thus, 'he proceeds from the Father,' in such a way that he did not say, 'he does not proceed from me'? Now from him from whom the Son has it that he is God—for he is God from God—from him he certainly has it that the Holy Spirit also proceeds from him; and through this the Holy Spirit has it from the Father himself that he proceeds also from the Son, just as he proceeds from the Father.*

*Here too, in some fashion—as far as it can be understood by such as we are—that also is understood: why the Holy Spirit is said not to be born, but rather to proceed; because if he too were called the Son, he would surely be called the son of both, which is utterly absurd. For no one is the son of two persons unless of a father and a mother. But far be it from us to suspect anything of the sort between God the Father and God the Son. For not even a son of men proceeds at the same time both from his father and from his mother; but when he proceeds from the father into the mother, he does not then proceed from the mother; and when he proceeds from the mother into this light, he does not then proceed from the father.*

*The Holy Spirit, however, does not proceed from the Father into the Son, and from the Son proceed to sanctify the creation; rather, he proceeds from both at once, although the Father has granted the Son that, just as he proceeds from himself, so he should proceed from him also. For we cannot say that the Holy Spirit is not life, since the Father is life and the Son is life; and through this, just as the Father, having life in himself, granted the Son also to have life in himself, so he granted him that life should proceed from him, just as it also proceeds from the Father himself.*[^290]

These things from that sermon I have transferred into this book, but speaking to the faithful, not to unbelievers.[^291]

### But the rule of faith must be firmly held; in the meantime we must pray and seek and live well, so that we may understand.

**27.** 49. But if some are less suited to contemplate this image, and to see how true these things are that are in their own mind—where there are not three things in such a way that they are three persons, but all three belong to the man, who is one person—why do they not rather believe, concerning that supreme Trinity which is God, what is found in the Sacred Writings, instead of demanding that a perfectly transparent account be rendered to them, an account that is not grasped by the human mind, slow and weak as it is? And surely, once they have unshakably believed the Holy Scriptures as most truthful witnesses, let them act by praying and seeking and living well, so that they may understand—that is, so that, as far as it can be seen, what is held by faith may be seen by the mind. Who would forbid this? Indeed, who would not rather urge it?

But if they think these things must be denied because they cannot discern them with blind minds, then those who are blind from birth ought also to deny the sun. Therefore the light shines in the darkness; and if the darkness does not comprehend it,[^292] let them first be enlightened by the gift of God so that they may be believers, and let them begin to be light in comparison with unbelievers; and once this foundation has been laid, let them be built up to see the things they believe, so that one day they may be able to see. For there are things that are believed in such a way that they can no longer be seen at all. For Christ is not to be seen again on the cross; but unless this is believed, which was so done and so seen that it is no longer hoped to come and to be seen, one does not arrive at the Christ as he is to be seen without end.

But as far as concerns that supreme, ineffable, incorporeal, and unchangeable nature, to be discerned in some fashion through the understanding, nowhere does the keen edge of the human mind exercise itself better—provided the rule of faith governs it—than in that which man himself has in his nature better than the other animals, and better even than the other parts of his own soul, namely the mind itself: to which a certain sight of invisible things has been granted, and to which—presiding honorably as in a higher and inner place—even the senses of the body report all things to be judged; and than which there is nothing higher to which it is subject for governance, except God.

### The solution to a most difficult question is suggested: love proceeds from thought, yet not as the image of thought.

**27.** 50. But amid these many things I have now said, I dare to profess that I have said nothing worthy of the ineffability of that supreme Trinity; rather, I confess that *the knowledge of it has grown too wonderful for me, and I cannot attain to it*;[^293] O you, my soul, where do you feel yourself to be, where do you lie, where do you stand, until all your weaknesses are healed by him *who has become merciful to all your iniquities*?[^294]

You surely recognize that you are in that inn to which the Samaritan brought the one whom he found half dead, with many wounds inflicted on him by robbers.[^295] And yet you have seen many true things, not with these eyes by which colored bodies are seen, but with those for which he was praying who said, *let my eyes see equity*.

Surely, then, you have seen many true things, and you have distinguished them from that light by whose shining upon you you saw them: lift up your eyes to the light itself, and fix them upon it, if you can. For thus you will see how the birth of the Word of God differs from the procession of the Gift of God, on account of which the only-begotten Son said that the Holy Spirit is not begotten of the Father—otherwise he would be his brother—but proceeds.[^296] Hence, since there is a certain consubstantial communion of the Father and the Son, the Spirit of both is called the Spirit of both, not—far be it—the son of both.

But to discern this clearly and plainly, you cannot fix your gaze there; I know, you cannot. I speak the truth, I speak to myself, I know what I cannot do: yet that light itself shows you in yourself those three things in which you may recognize the image of that supreme Trinity which you are not yet able to contemplate with fixed eyes.

It shows you that a true word exists in you when it is begotten from your knowledge, that is, when we say what we know; even though we neither utter nor think a sound that means something in the language of any nation, but our cognition is formed from that which we know, and in the keen edge of cognition there is an image most like the cognition[^297] that the memory contained—these two, namely, being joined by a third, the will or love, as a parent and offspring.

This will, indeed, proceeds from cognition—for no one wills what he does not know at all, whether it exists or of what kind it is—yet it is not the image of thought; and therefore a certain distinction between birth and procession is suggested in this intelligible matter, since to behold by thought is not the same as to desire, or even to enjoy by the will, as anyone who can sees and distinguishes.

You too have been able—though you have not been and are not able to set it forth in adequate speech—to see, with difficulty, amid the clouds of corporeal likenesses that do not cease to meet human thoughts, that which you barely saw.

But that light, which is not what you are, showed you this also: that those incorporeal likenesses of bodies are one thing, and that the truth, which we behold by understanding once those likenesses are rejected, is another. This and other things equally certain that light showed to your inner eyes. What, then, is the reason why you cannot see that light itself with a fixed gaze, except weakness? And what has made you weak, except iniquity? Who, then, heals all your weaknesses, except *he who becomes merciful to all your iniquities*?[^298] And so I shall now at last conclude this book better by prayer than by argument.

### A prayer. The conclusion of the book, with a petition and an apology for much speaking.

**28.** 51. Lord our God, we believe in you, Father and Son and Holy Spirit. For Truth would not say, *go, baptize the*[^299] *nations in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit*, unless you were Trinity. Nor would you command us to be baptized, Lord God, in the name of one who is not Lord God. Nor would it be said by the divine voice, *hear, O Israel; the Lord your God is*[^300] *one God*, unless you were Trinity in such a way that you were one Lord God.

And if you, God the Father, were yourself also the Son, your Word, Jesus Christ, and were yourself also your gift, the Holy Spirit, we would not read in the Writings of Truth, *God sent his Son*;[^301] nor would you, O Only-begotten, say of the Holy Spirit, *whom the Father will send in my name*, and, *whom I will send to you from the Father*. Directing my intention to this rule of faith, as much as I could, as much as you made me able, I have sought you, and I have desired to see with my understanding what I have believed, and I have argued much, and labored.

Lord my God, my one hope, hear me, lest out of weariness I should be unwilling to seek you, but may I *seek your face* always and ardently.[^302] Give the strength to seek, you who have made yourself to be found, and have given an ever greater hope of finding you. Before you is my strength and my weakness: preserve the one, heal the other. Before you is my knowledge and my ignorance: where you have opened to me, receive me as I enter; where you have closed, open to me as I knock.[^303]

May I remember you, understand you, love you. Increase these things in me, until you reform me to wholeness.

I know it is written, *in much speaking you will not escape sin*. But would that I spoke only by preaching your word and praising you! Then I would not only escape sin, but would acquire good merit, however much I spoke in this way. For a man made blessed by you would not have enjoined sin upon his true son in the faith, to whom he wrote, saying, *preach the Word, be urgent in season, out of season*. Is it to be said that he did not speak much, who not only in season but even out of season did not keep silent about your word, Lord? But it was not much, because it was only as much as was necessary. Free me, my God, from the much speaking that I suffer within, in my soul, which is wretched in your sight and takes refuge in your mercy. For I am not silent in my thoughts, even when I am silent in words. And if indeed I thought only what would please you, I certainly would not ask that you free me from this much speaking. But my thoughts are many, such as you know, *the thoughts of men, for they are vain*. Grant me not to consent to them, and, even if at times they delight me, nonetheless to reject them, and not to linger in them as if dozing. Nor let them so prevail with me that anything should pass from them into my deeds; but at least let my judgment be safe from them, my conscience safe, with you protecting me. A certain wise man, when he spoke of you in his book, which is now called by the proper name Ecclesiasticus, said, *we say many things, and we do not attain, and the sum of our words is: he is all*.

When, therefore, we have arrived at you, these *many* things that *we say, and do not attain,* will cease; and you will remain, the one who is *all in all*;[^304] and without end we shall say one thing, praising you in unity, having ourselves also been made one in you.

O Lord, one God, God the Trinity, whatever I have said in these books that is from you, may those who are yours acknowledge it; if anything is from myself, may you and those who are yours pardon it.

Amen.
