# Book III

## Preface

### The reason for Augustine's labor.

**1. 1**. Let those who wish believe that I would rather labor by reading than by dictating things for others to read. But those who do not wish to believe this, and yet both can and will test it, let them furnish things by which my inquiries may be answered in reading, or the questions of others, which, for the sake of the person I bear in the service of Christ, and for the zeal with which I burn to have our faith fortified against the error of carnal and animal men, it is necessary for me to endure;[^1] and let them see how easily I would refrain from this labor, and with how great joy I could keep my pen at rest.

But if the things we read about these matters either have not been sufficiently published in the Latin tongue, or are not found, or at least can with difficulty be found by us; and if our command of the Greek language is not so great that we are found in any way fit for reading and understanding books on such matters—of which kind of literature I do not doubt that all the things we can usefully seek are contained in those few that have been translated for us;

but if I am unable to resist the brothers demanding, by the right by which I have been made their servant, that I serve their praiseworthy zeal in Christ especially with my tongue and pen—the team of two which love drives in me—and if I myself confess that I have learned by writing many things I did not know; this labor of mine ought not to seem superfluous to anyone lazy or very learned, since it is, in no small part, necessary to many who are diligent and many who are unlearned, among whom I too am.

From the things, therefore, that we have already read written by others about this matter, very much supported and helped, I have undertaken to seek—with him himself exhorting—the things that I think can be piously sought and discussed concerning the Trinity, the one supreme and supremely good God, and to discuss them with his help.[^2]

So that, if there are no other writings of this kind, there may be something for those to have and read who wish and are able; but if there already are, the more such things there can be, the more easily may some be found.

### He desires not only a pious reader, but also a free corrector.

**1. 2**. Indeed, since in all my writings I desire not only a pious reader, but also a free corrector, much most of all in these, where the very greatness of the question—would that it could have as many discoverers as it has gainsayers! Nevertheless, just as I do not want my reader to be devoted to me, so I do not want my corrector to be devoted to himself. Let the former not love me more than the Catholic faith; let the latter not love himself more than the Catholic truth.

As I say to the former: "Do not be a slave to my writings as though to canonical Scriptures; but in the latter, when you have found even what you did not believe, believe it without hesitation; in the former, however, what you did not hold as certain, unless you have understood it as certain, do not firmly retain."

So I say to the latter: "Do not correct my writings from your own opinion or contention, but from divine reading or unshaken reason. If you grasp anything true in them, by existing it is not mine, but by understanding and loving it, let it be both yours and mine; but if you convict anything false, by erring it was mine, but now by guarding against it, let it be neither yours nor mine."

### What was said in the previous book.

**1. 3**. Hence, then, let this third book take its beginning from where the second had arrived. For when it had come to this, that we wished to show that the Son is not less than the Father because the one sent and the other was sent, nor that the Holy Spirit is less than both because in the Gospel he is read to be sent both by the one and by the other;[^3] we undertook to inquire this: since the Son was sent there where he was, because he *came into this world*, and *was in this world*; since the Holy Spirit too was sent there where he himself also was, *for the Spirit of the Lord has filled the world, and that which holds all things has knowledge of the voice*; whether the Lord was sent for this reason, that he was born of the hidden in the flesh, and from the bosom of the Father to the eyes of men appeared, as it were going forth, in the *form of a servant*;[^4] the Holy Spirit too for this reason, that he himself also was seen *in a bodily appearance as a dove*,[^5] and *in divided tongues as of fire*;[^6] so that for them to be sent was to proceed, from spiritual secrecy, to the sight of mortals in some bodily form; which, because the Father did not do it, he is said only to have sent, and not also to have been sent.

Then it was inquired why the Father too was not at some time said to be sent, if through those bodily appearances which appeared to the eyes of the ancients he himself was demonstrated.

But if the Son was then demonstrated, why was he said to be sent so long afterward, *when the fullness of time came*, that he should be born of a woman; since he was sent even before, when he appeared bodily in those forms.

Or if he could not rightly be said to be sent except when *the Word was made flesh*; why is the Holy Spirit read to be sent, whose incarnation of this kind was not made.

But if through those ancient demonstrations neither the Father nor the Son, but the Holy Spirit was shown; why was even he now said to be sent, since he was sent by those modes even before.

Then we subdivided, that these things might be treated most diligently, and made the question threefold, of which one part was explained in the second book; two remain, of which I shall set about discussing in turn.[^7]

For it has already been inquired and treated that in those ancient bodily forms and sights, not the Father only, nor the Son only, nor the Holy Spirit only appeared, but either, indifferently, the Lord God who is understood as the Trinity itself, or any one person of the Trinity, which the text of the reading would signify by the surrounding indications.

### The second inquiry: whether a new creature was formed, in which God was shown, or angels were sent?

**1. 4**. Now, then, let us first inquire into what follows. For in the second place in that distribution it was set down, whether for this work alone a creature was formed, in which God, as he then judged it behooved, might be shown to human sight; or whether angels, who already existed, were so sent that they spoke in the person of God, assuming a bodily appearance from corporeal creation for the use of their ministry; or whether they changed and turned their own body, to which they are not subjected but which they rule as subjected,[^8] into the appearances they wished, accommodated and fitted to their actions, according to the power attributed to them by the Creator. This part of the question having been treated through, as far as the Lord shall grant, it will finally have to be seen—that which we had set out to inquire—whether the Son and the Holy Spirit were sent even before; and if it is so, what difference there is between that sending and the one we read of in the Gospel;[^9] or whether none of them was sent except when either the Son was made of the Virgin Mary, or the Holy Spirit appeared in a visible form, whether in a dove or in tongues of fire.[^10]

### Augustine confesses that it exceeds the powers of his attention whether the angels assume something from the lower elements or transform their own bodies.

**1. 5**. But I confess that it exceeds the powers of my attention, whether the angels—the spiritual quality of their own body remaining, while they work more secretly through it—assume from the lower, more corpulent elements something which, fitted to themselves, they change and turn, as it were some garment, into whatever bodily appearances, even those true ones, just as true *water was turned into* true *wine*[^11] by the Lord; or whether they transform their own proper bodies into what they will, accommodated to what they do.

But whichever of these it be, it does not pertain to the present question. And although these things, since I am a man, I can grasp by no experiment, as can the angels who do these things, and know them better than I know—how far my body is changed at the disposition of my will, whether what I have experienced in myself, or what from others—yet which of these I should believe from the authorities of the divine Scriptures, there is now no need to say, lest I be compelled to prove it, and the discourse become longer about a matter which the present question does not need.

### Now it must be seen whether the angels were producing those appearances of bodies and those voices.

**1. 6**. This now must be seen: whether the angels were then producing both those appearances of bodies appearing to the eyes of men, and those voices sounding to their ears, when the sensible creature itself, serving the Creator at his nod, was turned for the time into the work that was needful, as it is written in the book of Wisdom: *for the creature serving you its maker is stretched out for torment against the unjust, and becomes gentler for doing good to those who trust in you. On account of this, then too, transfiguring itself into all things, it served your all-nourishing grace, according to the will of those who desired you*.

For the power of the will of God reaches, through the spiritual creature, even to the visible and sensible effects of the corporeal creature. For *where* does the *Wisdom* of *God* the omnipotent *not* work what it *wills*,[^12] which *reaches from end to end mightily, and orders all things sweetly*?

### The will of God is the cause superior to all others.

**2. 7**. But there is another natural order in the conversion and changeableness of bodies, which, although it too serves at the nod of God,[^13] has nevertheless, by the persistence of custom, lost its wonder; such as are the things that are changed, at very short or at least not long intervals of time, in sky, earth, and sea, whether by things being born, or perishing, or appearing at various times in various ways; but others, although coming from the same order, are nevertheless less usual on account of the longer intervals of time. Which, although many are astonished at them, have been grasped by the investigators of this age, and, by the progress of generations in which they are more often repeated and known by more people, are the less wondered at. Such are the eclipses of luminaries, and certain rarely occurring appearances of stars, and earthquakes, and monstrous births of living things, and whatever similar things, of which nothing happens except by the will of God, but to most people this does not appear.

And so it was permitted to the vanity of the philosophers to attribute these things even to other causes, either true but proximate, since they could not see at all the cause superior to all others, that is, the will of God; or false, and brought forth not even from the very investigation of corporeal things and motions, but from their own suspicion and error.

### Examples.

**2. 8**. I will say something, if I can, by way of example, by which these things may be more open. There is certainly in the human body a certain mass of flesh, and an appearance of form, and an order and distinction of members, and a tempering of health. This body the inbreathed soul rules, and the same soul is rational; and therefore, although changeable, it is yet such as can be a partaker of that unchangeable wisdom, so that there may be a *partaking of it in the selfsame*, as it is written in the Psalm of all the saints, of whom, as of living stones, that Jerusalem *our mother* eternal *in heaven* is built.[^14] For it is sung thus: *Jerusalem, which is built as a city, whose partaking is in the selfsame*. For the *selfsame* in this place is understood to be that supreme and unchangeable Good which is God, and his wisdom and will. To whom it is sung in another place: *you will change them, and they will be changed; but you are the same*.

### The will of God, the supreme cause, acts through the just soul.

**3. 8**. Let us, then, set up in our mind such a wise man, whose rational soul is already a partaker of the unchangeable and eternal truth, which it consults about all its actions,[^15] and does nothing at all that it has not recognized in it to be done, so that, subject to it and obeying it, it may act rightly.

If this man, having consulted the supreme reason of divine justice, which he would hear in secret with the ear of his heart, and at its bidding, in some office of mercy wearied his body with labor and contracted an illness; and the physicians being consulted, by one it should be said that the cause of the disease was the dryness of the body, but by another the immoderation of humor; one of them would say the true cause, the other would err, yet both would pronounce about proximate causes, that is, bodily ones. But if the cause of that dryness were sought, and the voluntary labor were found; we would now have come to a higher cause, which would proceed from the soul to affect the body which it rules; but not even this would be the first.

For that, without doubt, was higher, in the unchangeable Wisdom itself, which the soul of the wise man, serving in love and ineffably obeying its bidding, had undertaken the voluntary labor. And so nothing but the will of God would most truly be found to be the first cause of that illness.

But now if, in that dutiful and pious labor, that wise man had employed servants laboring with him in the good work, yet not serving God with the same will, but desiring to attain the reward of their carnal desires, or avoiding carnal discomforts; and had employed also beasts of burden, if the management of accomplishing that work required this—which beasts would certainly be irrational living things, nor would they on that account move their members under the burdens because they thought anything of that good work, but by the natural appetite of their own pleasure and the avoidance of trouble; and finally, had employed also those very bodies lacking all sense, which were necessary to that work, namely grain, and wine, and oil, clothing, money, a book, and any such things. In all these bodies, certainly, engaged in that work, whether animate or inanimate, whatever should be moved, worn down, repaired, destroyed, reformed, changed, affected in one way and another by places and times; would there be any other cause of all these visible and changeable deeds, except that invisible and unchangeable will of God, using, through the just soul as the seat of Wisdom, all things—both evil and irrational souls, and finally bodies, whether those breathed into and animated, or those lacking all sense—when it first used that very good and holy soul itself, which it had subjected to itself for pious and religious obedience?

### The will of God uses all things at the unchangeable disposition of his wisdom.

**4. 9**. What, then, we have set down by way of example concerning one wise man—though still wearing a mortal body, though seeing *in part*[^16]—this it is permitted to think concerning some household, where there is a society of some such men, this concerning a city, or even concerning the whole world, if the headship and the rule of *human affairs* should be in the hands of the wise, who are holily and perfectly subject to God.[^17] But because this is not yet (for it behooves us in this pilgrimage first to be exercised mortally,[^18] and to be instructed through the strength of meekness and patience under scourges), let us think of that very "supernal" and heavenly country, from which we are on pilgrimage.

For there the will of God, *who makes his angels spirits, and his ministers a flaming fire*, presiding over the spirits joined in the highest peace and friendship, and fused into one will by a certain spiritual fire of love, as in a lofty and holy and secret seat, as in his own house and in his own temple, from there diffuses itself through all things by certain most ordered motions of the creature, first spiritual, then corporeal, and uses all things at the unchangeable disposition of its decree, whether incorporeal or corporeal things, whether rational or irrational spirits, whether good through his grace or evil through their own will.

But just as the grosser and lower bodies are ruled, in a certain order, through the subtler and more powerful; so all bodies are ruled through the spirit of life, and the spirit of irrational life through the spirit of rational life, and the spirit of rational life that is a deserter and a sinner through the spirit of rational life that is pious and just, and that through God himself, and so the whole creation through its Creator, from whom and through whom and in whom it was also founded and established; and on this account the will of God is the first and supreme cause of all bodily appearances and motions.

For nothing happens visibly and sensibly that is not either commanded or permitted from the inner, invisible, and intelligible court of the supreme Emperor, according to the ineffable justice of rewards and punishments, of favors and retributions, in that most ample and immense commonwealth, as it were, of the whole creation.

### The working of God in the sacrament of the Body of Christ.

**4. 10**. If, then, the apostle Paul, although he still bore the burden of the body, *which is corrupted and weighs down the soul*, although he still saw *in part* and *in an enigma*, *desiring to be dissolved and to be with Christ*, and *groaning within himself, awaiting the adoption, the redemption of his body*, could nevertheless by signifying preach the Lord Jesus Christ[^19]—in one way through his tongue, in another through a letter, in another through the sacrament of his body and blood;

for we call neither his tongue, nor the parchments, nor the ink, nor the signifying sounds uttered by the tongue, nor the signs of letters written on little skins, the body and blood of Christ; but only that which, taken from the fruits of the earth and consecrated by the mystical prayer, we duly receive for spiritual salvation in memory of the Lord's passion for us;[^20] which, although it is brought by the hands of men to that visible appearance, is not sanctified so as to be so great a sacrament except by the Spirit of God working invisibly, since God works all these things that are done through bodily motions in that work, first moving the invisible things of the ministers, whether the souls of men, or the services of hidden spirits subjected to him;

what wonder is it if, even in the creation of heaven and earth, of sea and air, God does the sensible and visible things he wills, to signify and demonstrate himself in them, as he himself knows it behooves, not with his own substance appearing—by which he is, which is altogether unchangeable, and more sublime, inwardly and more secretly, than all the spirits he created?

### God himself works unusual mighty deeds.

**5. 11**. For by the divine power administering the whole spiritual and corporeal creation, on certain days of every year the waters of the sea are summoned and poured out over the face of the earth. But when this was done at the prayer of holy Elijah—because there had preceded so continuous and so long a clear sky that men were failing from hunger, nor had the air itself, at the hour at which that servant of God prayed, shown by any moist appearance the signs of rain soon to come—the divine power appeared in the so great and so swiftly following rains, by which that miracle was given and dispensed.[^21]

So God produces the customary lightnings and thunders. But because on Mount Sinai they were happening in an unusual mode, and those voices were uttered not with a confused noise, but it appeared by the most certain indications that certain signs were being given by them, they were miracles.[^22]

Who draws the moisture through the root of the vine to the cluster, and makes wine, except God, who, with man planting and watering, *gives the increase*? But when, at the nod of the Lord, *water was turned into wine* with unusual swiftness,[^23] the divine power was declared even with the foolish confessing it.

Who clothes the shrubs with leaf and flower in the customary season, except God? But when the rod of the priest Aaron flowered, the divinity conversed in a certain way with doubting humanity.[^24] And certainly for begetting and forming all woods and the flesh of all animals, the earthly matter is common; and who makes these, except him who said that this earth should bring forth,[^25] and in that same word of his rules and drives the things he created?

But when he turned that same matter from the rod of Moses into the flesh of a serpent, closely and swiftly, it was a miracle[^26]—of a changeable thing indeed, but yet an unusual change. But who animates each living thing being born, except him who animated that serpent for the hour, as was needful?

**5. 11**. And who restored their souls to the corpses, when the dead rose,[^27] except him who animates the flesh in the wombs of mothers, so that they are born to die? But when those things happen in a kind of continuous flow, as of things slipping and flowing on, and passing from the hidden into the open, and from the open into the hidden by a customary route, they are called natural; but when, for the admonishing of men, they are pressed upon us by an unusual changeableness, they are named *mighty deeds*.

### The magic arts can do nothing except by power given from above.

**7. 12**. Here I see what may occur to weak thought, namely why these miracles are done even by magic arts. For the magi of Pharaoh likewise made serpents, and other similar things.[^28] But this is the more to be wondered at: how that power of the magi, which could make serpents, when it came to the tiniest flies, failed altogether. For gnats are very tiny little flies, by which, as the third plague, the proud people of the Egyptians was struck.[^29] There certainly the magi, failing, said: *this is the finger of God*. Whence it is given to understand that not even those transgressor angels and aerial powers—thrust down into this lowest darkness as into a prison of their own kind, from the habitation of that sublime ethereal purity, through whom the magic arts can do whatever they can—are able to do anything *except by power given from above*.

And it is given either to deceive the deceivers, as upon the Egyptians, and as it was given even upon the magi themselves, so that in the seduction of those spirits they might seem worthy of admiration to those by whom these things were done, but worthy of condemnation by the truth of God; or to admonish the faithful, lest they desire to do any such thing as a great matter, for which reason these things are also handed down to us by the authority of Scripture; or to exercise, prove, and manifest the patience of the just. For by no small power of visible miracles, Job lost all that he had, both his sons and the very health of his body.

### God is the creator of all things; the evil angels are not creators in the magic arts.

**8. 13**. Nor on this account must it be thought that this matter of visible things serves these transgressor angels at their nod, but rather God, by whom this power is given, as far as, in his sublime and spiritual and unchangeable seat, he judges. For even to the condemned wicked in the mine, water and fire and earth serve, that they may make from them what they will, but as far as it is allowed.

Nor indeed are those evil angels to be called creators, because through them the magi, resisting the servant of God, made frogs and serpents; for they themselves did not create them. For of all things that are born corporeally and visibly, certain hidden seeds lie concealed in these corporeal elements of this world. For these are one thing—the things now conspicuous to our eyes from fruits and living things; but another thing those hidden seeds of these seeds, from which, at the Creator's command, the first water brought forth swimming and flying things, but the first earth the first germinations of its kind, and the first animals of its kind.[^30] For they were not then so brought forth into offspring of this kind that in those that were brought forth that power was consumed; but for the most part there are lacking the fitting occasions of the temperings, by which they may burst forth and accomplish their kinds.

For behold, the tiniest shoot is a seed; for, fittingly committed to the earth, it makes a tree. But the subtler seed of this shoot is some grain of the same kind, and this far visible to us. But now the seed even of this grain, although we cannot see it with our eyes, we can yet conjecture by reason; because, unless there were some such power in these elements, there would not for the most part be born from the earth things that had not been sown there; nor so many animals, with no commixture of males and females preceding, whether in earth or in water, which nevertheless grow and by coupling bring forth others, although they themselves arose with no parents coupling.

And certainly bees conceive the seeds of their young not by coupling, but gather them as it were scattered through the lands with their mouths.[^31] For the *Creator of the invisible* seeds is himself the *Creator of all things*, since whatever things, by being born, come forth to our eyes, take the first beginnings of their advance from hidden seeds, and take their increases of due magnitude and the distinctions of their forms from the original, as it were, rules.

As, therefore, we call neither parents the creators of men, nor farmers the creators of crops, although the power of God works inwardly to create these things, with their motions applied from outside; so it is not lawful to think not only the evil, but even the good angels creators, if, by the subtlety of their sense and body, they know the seeds of these things, more hidden to us, and scatter them latently through fitting temperings of the elements, and so furnish the occasions both of things to be generated and of increases to be accelerated.

But neither do the good do these things except as far as God commands, nor do the evil do them unjustly except as far as he justly permits. For the wicked have their unjust will by malice; but they receive power only justly, whether for their own punishment, or for that of others, either the punishment of the evil or the praise of the good.

### Even our mind, none but God can form by justifying it.

**8. 14**. And so the apostle Paul, distinguishing God creating and forming inwardly from the works of the creature which are applied from outside, and taking a likeness from agriculture, says: *I planted, Apollos watered; but God gave the increase*. As, therefore, in our very life, none but God can form the mind by justifying it, but men too can preach the Gospel outwardly, not only *the good through truth*, but also *the evil through opportunity*; so God works inwardly the creation of visible things; but the outward operations, whether of the good or of the evil, whether of angels or of men, or even of whatever animals, according to his command and the distributions of powers and the appetites of advantages imparted by him, he applies to the nature of things in which he creates all things, just as agriculture to the earth. Wherefore I can no more say that the evil angels, invoked by magic arts, were the creators of frogs and serpents,[^32] than I can say that evil men are the creators of the crop which I see to have sprung up through their work.[^33]

### Nor was Jacob the creator of the colors in the flocks.

**8. 15**. Just as neither was Jacob the creator of the colors in the flocks, because he set before the mothers, as they drank at conception, variegated rods for them to gaze upon. But neither were the flocks themselves the creators of the variegation of their offspring, because there had clung to their soul a particolored image, impressed through the eyes from the gazing on the variegated rods, which could only affect, from the sympathy of the commixture, the body that was animated by the spirit so affected, whence the tender beginnings of the offspring were tinged, so far as color goes.[^34]

For that they should be thus affected from themselves—either the soul from the body, or the body from the soul—the congruences of reason[^35] bring about, which live unchangeably in that supreme *Wisdom of God*,[^36] which no spaces of places contain; and since she herself is unchangeable, she deserts none of the things that exist even changeably, because *nothing* of them was *created except through her*.

For that from the flocks not rods but flocks should be born,[^37] this the unchangeable and invisible reason of the Wisdom of God did, *through whom all things were created*; but that from the variegation of the rods the color of the conceived flocks should draw something, this the soul of the pregnant beast did, affected outwardly through the eyes, and inwardly drawing along with itself, according to its small measure, a "rule" of forming, which it received from the inmost power of its Creator.

But[^38] how great is the power of the soul to affect and change corporeal matter (though it cannot be called the creator of the body, since every cause of changeable and sensible substance, and every measure and number and weight of it, whence it comes about that it both exists and that its nature is so or so, exists from the intelligible and unchangeable life which is above all things, and reaches even to the lowest and earthly things), is a long discourse, and not now necessary. But I judged that Jacob's deed concerning the flocks should be recalled for this reason: that it might be understood that, if the man who set out those rods so cannot be called the creator of the colors in the lambs and kids; nor the souls of the mothers themselves, which, so far as nature allowed, sprinkled upon the seeds conceived in the flesh the image of variegation conceived through the eyes of the body; much less can the evil angels, through whom the magi of Pharaoh then did those things, be called the creators of frogs and serpents.[^39]

### God alone founds and administers the creature; creatures can only apply some operation from outside.

**9. 16**. For it is one thing to found and administer the creature from the inmost and supreme hinge of causes—which he who does, *is God the only Creator*; but another to apply, according to the powers and faculties distributed by him, some operation from outside, so that at this time or that, in this way or that, what is created may come forth. For these things have, originally and primordially, all been already created in a certain texture of the elements, but they come forth as opportunities are received.

For just as mothers are pregnant with offspring, so the world itself is pregnant with the causes of things that are born; which are not created in it except by that supreme essence, where nothing arises or dies, nor begins to be, nor ceases. But to apply causes coming from outside—which, although they are not natural, are yet applied according to nature, so that the things that are contained, hidden in the secret bosom of nature, may burst forth and be created outwardly, in a certain way unfolding the measures and numbers and weights they received in secret from him who *disposed all things in measure and number and weight*—not only evil angels, but even evil men can, as I taught above by the example of agriculture.[^40]

### Men marvel at certain swiftnesses of increases.

**9. 17**. But lest, as it were, a different reasoning move anyone concerning animals, because they have a spirit of life with the sense of seeking what is according to nature and avoiding the contrary; this too may be seen, how many men know from what herbs, or flesh, or whatever juices or humors of any things—whether so placed, or so buried, or so crushed, or so mixed—what animals are wont to be born. Of which who would be so insane as to call himself the creator?

What wonder, then, if, just as any most worthless man can know from what this or that worms and flies are born,[^41] so the evil angels, by the subtlety of their sense, know in the more hidden seeds of the elements from what frogs and serpents are born, and, by applying these through fixed and known opportunities of temperings with hidden motions, cause them to be created—they do not create them?

But men do not marvel at the things that are wont to be done by men. But if anyone perhaps marvels at the swiftnesses of increases, that those living things were made so quickly; let him attend to how these too are procured by men, according to the small measure of human faculty. For whence does it come that the same bodies grow worms more quickly in summer than in winter, more quickly in warmer than in colder places? But these things are applied by men with so much the more difficulty, as there are lacking the subtleties of the senses and the mobilities of bodies in their earthly and sluggish members. Whence for angels of whatever kind, by how much it is easier to draw the proximate causes from the elements, by so much the more wonderful do their swiftnesses in works of this kind appear.

### God is the one creator.

**9. 18**. But there is no creator except him who principally forms these things. Nor can anyone do this, except him with whom are, in the first place, the measures, numbers, and weights of all things that are; and he *is the one creator God*, from whose ineffable might it comes about even that what these angels could do if they were permitted, they therefore cannot, because they are not permitted.

For no other reason occurs why those who made frogs and serpents could not make the tiniest flies, except that a greater dominion of God forbidding it was present through the Holy Spirit, which the magi themselves also confessed, saying: *this is the finger of God*.

But what they can do by nature, and cannot do because of prohibition, and what they are not permitted to do by the very condition of their nature, it is difficult for man to explore—nay, rather impossible, except through that *gift of God*[^42] which the Apostle recalls, saying, *to another the discerning of spirits*.

For we know that a man can walk, and cannot do even this if he is not permitted, but cannot fly even if he is permitted. So too those angels can do certain things if they are permitted by more powerful angels at God's command; but certain things they cannot do, not even if they are permitted by them; because he does not permit, from whom they have such a measure of their nature—who, even through his angels, for the most part does not permit those things which he granted them the power to do.

### Not in all the things that are announced to us by God is the person of God himself taken up.

**9. 19**. Excepting, then, those things that happen corporeally in the order of the nature of things by the most customary running on of times—such as are the risings and settings of the stars, the births and deaths of animals, the innumerable diversities of seeds and germinations, mists and clouds, snows and rains, lightnings and thunders, thunderbolts and hails, winds and fires, cold and heat, and all such things; excepting also those that are rare in the same order, such as eclipses of the lights, and unusual appearances of the stars, and monsters, and earthquakes, and the like; excepting, then, all these, of which indeed the first and supreme cause is nothing but the will of God—whence in the Psalm too, when certain things of this kind had been recalled, *fire, hail, snow, ice, the spirit of the tempest*, lest anyone should believe them to be driven either by chance, or only by corporeal causes, or even by spiritual ones yet existing apart from the will of God, he immediately added, *which do his word*.

**10. 19**. But these things, as I had begun to say, being excepted, there are others which, although from the same corporeal matter, are nevertheless applied to our senses to announce something divinely, which are properly called miracles and signs; nor in all the things that are announced to us by the Lord God is the person of God himself taken up. But when it is taken up, it is sometimes demonstrated in an angel, sometimes in that appearance which is not what an angel is, although it is administered, disposed through an angel; again, when it is taken up in that appearance which is not what an angel is, sometimes the body already existed, and is assumed into some change to demonstrate this; sometimes it arises for this, and, the thing being accomplished, is again taken away.

Just as also, when men announce, sometimes they speak the words of God in their own person, as when it is premised, *the Lord said*;[^43] or, *thus says the Lord*;[^44] or some such thing; but sometimes, premising nothing of the kind, they take the very person of God upon themselves, as it is, *I will give you understanding, and will establish you in the way by which you shall go*. So not only in words, but also in deeds, the person of God to be signified is imposed upon the prophet, that he may bear it in the ministry of prophecy; as he bore his person who divided his garment into twelve parts, and of them gave ten to the servant of King Solomon, the future king of Israel;[^45] sometimes also a thing which was not what the prophet was, and already existed among earthly things, was assumed into a signification of this kind; as Jacob, waking from the dream he had seen, made of the stone which, sleeping, he had at his head;[^46] sometimes the same appearance is made for this, either to remain for a while, as could that bronze serpent lifted up in the desert, as can letters too;[^47] or, the ministry being accomplished, to pass away, as the bread made for this is consumed in the receiving of the sacrament.

### Not all the things that are announced to us by God can have astonishment as at something marvelous.

**10.** 20. But because these things are known to men, since they are done through men, they can be held in honor as religious acts, yet they cannot cause astonishment as marvels. And so the things that are done through angels, the more difficult and unfamiliar they are, the more wonderful they are to us; whereas to the angels themselves they are familiar and easy, like their own actions.

An angel speaks to a man in the person of God, saying, *I am the God of Abraham, and the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob*, though Scripture had said beforehand, *an angel of the Lord appeared to him*. A man too speaks in the person of God,[^48] saying, *Hear, O my people, and I will speak; O Israel, and I will testify to you; I am God, your God*. A *rod* was taken up for a signification, and *changed into a serpent* by angelic power;[^49] and although a man lacks that power, nonetheless a stone too was taken up by a man for some such signification.[^50] Between the deed of an angel and the deed of a man there is the greatest difference. The one is both to be wondered at and to be understood, but the other is only to be understood. What is understood from each is perhaps one and the same, but the things from which it is understood are different—as if the name of the Lord were written both in gold and in ink. The one is more precious, the other cheaper; yet what is signified by both is one and the same.

And although the serpent from Moses's rod signified the same thing as Jacob's stone, nonetheless Jacob's stone signified something better than the serpents of the magicians.[^51]

For just as the anointing of the stone signified Christ in the flesh, in which he was anointed *with the oil of gladness above his fellows*, so Moses's *rod turned into a serpent*[^52] signified that same Christ *made obedient unto death, even the death of the cross*. Hence he says, *as Moses lifted up the serpent in the wilderness, so must the Son of man be lifted up, that whoever believes in him may not perish but have eternal life*; just as those who looked upon that serpent lifted up in the wilderness did not perish from the serpents' bites. For *our old man was nailed to the cross with him, that the body of sin might be done away with*.

For by the serpent is understood death, which was brought about by the serpent in paradise, by that mode of expression which indicates the thing effected by means of what effects it.[^53] Therefore the *rod into a serpent* means Christ into death, and the serpent again into a rod means Christ into resurrection, the whole of him together with *his body, which is the Church*—which will come about at the end of time, signified by the serpent's tail, which Moses held so that it might return into a rod.

But the serpents of the magicians, like the dead of this world, will not be able to rise again in Christ unless, believing in Christ, they enter as though devoured into his body.[^54] So Jacob's stone, as I have said, signified something better than the serpents of the magicians;[^55] and yet the deed of the magicians was far more wonderful. But these considerations do not prejudge the things to be understood, any more than if a man's name were written in gold and God's in ink.

### How the angels do these things Augustine is not able to comprehend.

**10.** 21. As for those clouds and fires, how did the angels make or take them up to signify what they were announcing—even if it was the Lord or the Holy Spirit who was shown in those corporeal forms—what man knows? Just as the "infants"[^56] do not know what is placed upon the altar and consumed when the celebration of the rite of piety is completed, nor from what or in what way it is prepared, nor from what it is taken up for the use of religion.

And if they should never learn by experience, whether their own or others', and never see that form of things except amid the celebrations of the sacraments when it is offered and given, and should be told on the gravest authority whose body and blood it is, they will believe nothing other than that the Lord appeared in that very form to the eyes of mortals and that from his side, struck in such a way, that liquid actually flowed.[^57]

But it is altogether useful for me to remember my own limits and to warn my brothers that they too should remember theirs, lest human weakness advance further than is safe. For how the angels do these things, or rather how God does these things through his angels,[^58] and how much he wills to be done even through evil angels—whether by permitting, or commanding, or compelling, from the hidden seat of his most exalted rule—I am able neither to penetrate with the keenness of my eyes, nor to lay bare by confidence of reason, nor to grasp by any advance of the mind, so as to speak with such certainty about all that may be asked concerning these matters as I would if I were an angel, or a prophet, or an apostle.

*For the thoughts of mortals are timid, and our foresight uncertain. For the body that is corruptible weighs down the soul, and the earthly dwelling presses down the mind that thinks on many things. And with difficulty do we estimate the things that are on earth, and the things that are within our sight we find with toil. But the things that are in the heavens, who has searched them out?*[^59] But because it goes on to say, *and who shall know your mind, unless you have given wisdom and sent your Holy Spirit from on high?* The things that are in the heavens we do not indeed search out—the kind of thing that includes angelic bodies according to their proper dignity, and a certain corporeal activity belonging to them; yet according to the Spirit of God sent to us from on high and his grace imparted to our minds, I dare to say with confidence that neither God the Father, nor his Word, nor his Spirit—since *God is one*[^60]—in respect of that which he is, and which is one and the same, is in any way changeable, and therefore much less is visible. For there are some things which, though changeable, are nonetheless not visible—such as our thoughts, memories, and acts of will, and every incorporeal creation; but nothing is visible that is not changeable.

### The essence of God can in no way be visible in itself through itself.

**11.** 21. Therefore the substance, or, if it is better so called, the essence of God, in which according to our small measure we understand from however small a portion the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit, since it is in no way changeable, can in no way be visible in itself through itself.

### All the things that appeared to the fathers were done through angels.

**11.** 22. Accordingly, it is clear that all those things which appeared to the fathers, when God was presented to them according to his dispensation suited to the times, were done through a creation.

And even if it is hidden from us how he did these things through his angelic ministers, nonetheless that they were done through angels we say not on our own judgment, lest we should seem to anyone *to be wise beyond what we ought to be wise*;[^61] *but* we are wise *unto soberness, as God has apportioned to* us *the measure of faith*, *and we believe, and therefore we speak*.

For the authority of the divine Scriptures stands forth, from which our mind must not deviate, nor, abandoning the firm ground of the divine utterance, be cast headlong over the precipices of its own conjectures, where neither bodily sense governs nor the clear reason of truth shines out.

For it is written most plainly in the Letter to the Hebrews, when the dispensation of the New Testament was being distinguished from the dispensation of the Old Testament according to the fitness of the ages and times, that not only those visible things, but the very utterance itself, was made through angels. For it says thus: *But to which of the angels did he ever say, 'Sit at my right hand, until I make your enemies a footstool for your feet'? Are they not all ministering spirits, sent forth to minister for the sake of those who are to obtain salvation as an inheritance?*

Hence he shows that all those things were done not only *through angels* but also for our sake—that is, for the sake of the people of God, to whom is promised the inheritance *of eternal life*.[^62] So too it is written to the Corinthians: *all these things happened to them in figure; but they were written for our admonition, upon whom the end of the ages has come*.

Then, because the *utterance was made* at that time *through angels*, but now through the Son, he goes on to demonstrate it openly and plainly: *therefore*, he says, *we ought to give more abundant heed to the things we have heard, lest perhaps we drift away. For if the utterance spoken through angels was made firm, and every transgression and disobedience received a just recompense of reward, how shall we escape if we neglect so great a salvation?*

And as though you were asking which *salvation*, in order to show that he is now speaking of the New Testament—that is, of the utterance made not *through angels* but *through the Lord*—he says, *which, having taken its beginning to be declared through the Lord, was confirmed unto us by those who heard him, while God bore witness with signs and wonders and various mighty works and distributions of the Holy Spirit according to his will*.

### God speaks through angels.

**11.** 23. "But," someone says, "why then is it written, *the Lord said to Moses*, and not rather, the angel *said to Moses*?" Because when a herald announces the words of a judge, it is not recorded in the proceedings, "that herald said," but "that judge said." So too, when a holy prophet speaks, even if we say, "the prophet said," we wish nothing else to be understood than that "the Lord said it." And if we say, *the Lord said*, we do not remove the prophet, but we point out who spoke through him. And indeed that Scripture often makes plain that the angel is the Lord, when, as he speaks, it is repeatedly said, *the Lord said*, as we have already shown.[^63]

But on account of those who, when Scripture there names an angel, wish the Son of God himself in his own person to be understood—because on account of the announcement of the Father's will and his own he was called an angel by the prophet—on that account I wished to give clearer testimony from this letter, where it is not said "through an angel," but *through angels*.

### The Lord appeared to Moses through an angel.

**11.** 24. For Stephen too, in the Acts of the Apostles, recounts these things in the same manner in which they were written in the ancient books:[^64] *Brethren and fathers, listen*, he says: *the God of glory appeared to our father Abraham when he was in Mesopotamia*. But lest anyone should suppose that the God of glory then appeared, in respect of that which he is in himself, to the eyes of any mortal, in what follows he says that an angel appeared to Moses. *Moses fled*, he says, *at this saying, and became a sojourner in the land of Midian, where he begot two sons. And when forty years were completed there, an angel of the Lord appeared to him in the desert of Mount Sinai in a flame of fire in a bush. And Moses, seeing it, marveled at the sight. And as he drew near to consider it, the voice of the Lord came, saying, 'I am the God of your fathers, the God of Abraham, and the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob.' And Moses, trembling, did not dare to look. And the Lord said to him, 'Loose the sandal from your feet,'*[^65] and so forth. Here certainly he says both "an angel" and "the Lord," and the same one "the God of Abraham, and the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob," as it is written in Genesis.[^66]

### He also appeared to Abraham through an angel.

**11.** 25. But perhaps someone will say that the Lord appeared to Moses through an angel, but to Abraham through himself? Yet let us not seek this from Stephen; let us question the very book from which Stephen recounted these things. For is it the case that, because it is written, *and the Lord God said to Abraham*, and a little later, *and the Lord God appeared to Abraham*, therefore these things were not done through angels? Since in another place it likewise says, *and God appeared to him at the oak of Mamre, as he sat at the door of his tent at midday*, and yet consistently adds, *and lifting up his eyes he looked, and behold, three men stood near him*—of whom we have already spoken.[^67] For how will those people be able—who either are unwilling to rise from the words to the understanding, or easily cast themselves headlong from the understanding back into the words—how will they be able to explain that God was seen in three men, unless they confess that these men, as the sequel also teaches, were angels?[^68] Or will they dare to say, because it is not said "an angel spoke to him" or "appeared," that in Moses's case that vision and voice were made through an angel, since it is so written, but in Abraham's case, because no mention of an angel was made, God appeared and sounded forth through his own substance?

What of the fact that even in Abraham's case there was no silence about an angel? For it is read thus, when his son was commanded to be sacrificed: *and it came to pass after these words that God tested Abraham, and said to him, 'Abraham, Abraham.' And he said, 'Here I am.' And he said to him, 'Take your beloved son, whom you love, Isaac, and go into the high land, and offer him there as a holocaust upon one of the mountains that I will tell you.'* Here certainly God, not an angel, is mentioned. But a little later Scripture has it thus: *and Abraham, stretching out his hand, took the sword to kill his son. And an angel of the Lord called him from heaven, and said to him, 'Abraham, Abraham.' And he said, 'Here I am.' And he said, 'Do not lay your hand upon the boy, nor do anything to him.'*

What is to be answered to this? Will they say that God commanded Isaac to be killed, and an angel forbade it,[^69] and further that the father himself, against the precept of God who had commanded him to kill, obeyed the angel so as to spare him? This sense is to be laughed at and cast aside. But Scripture does not allow even this so gross and abject a sense any place, immediately adding: *for now I know that you fear God, and have not spared your beloved son for my sake*. What is, *for my sake*, but for the sake of him who had commanded that the boy be killed? Therefore the same one is *God* to Abraham who is the *angel*—or rather, is it God through an angel? Take what follows; here certainly the *angel* is now most plainly expressed. Yet notice what is woven in: *Abraham, lifting up his eyes, looked, and behold, a ram was caught by its horns in a thicket; and Abraham went and took the ram and offered it as a holocaust in place of Isaac his son. And Abraham gave that place the name, 'The Lord saw,' so that they say today, 'On the mountain the Lord was seen.'*

Just as a little before, what God said through an angel, *for now I know that you fear God*—not that God is then to be understood as having come to know, but as having brought it about that through God Abraham himself might come to know how great were the powers of his heart for obeying God even to the immolation of his only son; by that mode of expression in which the thing effected is signified by means of what effects it, as the cold is called sluggish because it makes men sluggish—so that he was said to have come to know because he had made Abraham himself come to know, from whom the firmness of his own faith might have been hidden, had it not been proved by such a trial. So here too *Abraham gave that place the name, 'The Lord saw'*; that is, that he caused himself to be seen. For he immediately goes on to say, *so that they say today, 'On the mountain the Lord was seen.'*

Behold, the same *angel* is called *Lord*; why, unless because the Lord was present through an angel? And now in what follows, the *angel* speaks altogether prophetically, and plainly discloses that God speaks through an angel. *And*, he says, *the angel of the Lord called Abraham again from heaven, saying, 'By myself I have sworn, says the Lord, because you have done this thing, and have not spared your beloved son for my sake,'* and so forth.

These words certainly—so that the one through whom the Lord speaks may say, *thus says the Lord*—the prophets too are accustomed to have. Or does the *Son of God* say of the Father, *says the Lord*, and is he himself that *angel* of the Father?

What then of those three men—do they not see how they are pressed, those men who appeared to Abraham when it had been foretold, *the Lord appeared to him*? Or, because they were called *men*, were they not angels? Let them read Daniel saying, *behold, the man Gabriel*.

### The Law was given in the edicts of angels.

**11.** 26. But why do we delay any longer to stop their mouths with another most evident and most weighty document, where neither *angel* in the singular, nor *men* in the plural, but plainly angels are spoken of, through whom not some utterance or other was *made*, but the Law itself is most plainly shown to have been given[^70]—the Law which certainly no believer doubts that God gave to Moses to subjugate the people of Israel, yet which was nonetheless given *through angels*?

Thus Stephen speaks: *you stiff-necked people*, he says, *uncircumcised in heart and ears, you always resist the Holy Spirit, as your fathers did. Which of the prophets did your fathers not persecute? And they killed those who foretold the coming of the Just One, of whom you have now been the betrayers and murderers—you who received the Law by the edicts of angels, and have not kept it*.

What is more evident than this? What is stronger by so great an authority? The *Law* was indeed *given* to that people *by the edicts of angels*; but through it the *coming of the Lord Jesus Christ* was being arranged and foretold; and he himself, as the *Word of God*, was in a wonderful and ineffable way present in the *angels* by whose *edicts* the Law was being given.[^71] Hence he says in the Gospel, *if you believed Moses, you would believe me also; for he wrote of me*.

*Through angels*, therefore, the Lord then spoke; *through angels* the Son of God, the *Mediator of God and* men,[^72] who would come from the seed of Abraham, was arranging his own coming, so that he might find those by whom he would be received, confessing themselves guilty—those whom the unfulfilled Law had made *transgressors*.

Hence the Apostle also says to the Galatians, *what then is the Law? It was set forth for the sake of transgression, until the seed should come to whom the promise was made, ordained through angels in the hand of a Mediator*; that is, *ordained through angels in* his own *hand*. For he was *born* not by a created condition, but by power.

But that he calls not some one of the angels the *Mediator*, but the *Lord Jesus Christ* himself, insofar as he deigned to become *man*, you have in another place: *one*, he says, *God, and one Mediator of God and men, the man Christ Jesus*.

Hence that Passover in the slaying of the lamb;[^73] hence all those things which are prefigured in the Law concerning Christ to come in the flesh[^74] and to suffer, but also to rise again—the Law which was *given by the edicts of angels*,[^75] among which angels were certainly the Father, and the Son, and the Holy Spirit; and sometimes the Father, sometimes the Son, sometimes the Holy Spirit, and sometimes, without any distinction of person, God was prefigured through them, though appearing in visible and sensible forms—yet through his creation, not through his substance—to behold whom hearts are cleansed by all these things that are seen with the eyes and heard with the ears.[^76]

### When God was said to appear to the fathers, the voices and corporeal forms were made through angels. The third question, to be treated in the following book: concerning the missions of the Son and of the Holy Spirit.

**11.** 27. But now enough, as I think, has been argued and demonstrated for our capacity of what we had undertaken to show in this book;[^77] and it has been established, both by the probability of reason, as far as man, or rather as far as I, was able, and by the firmness of authority, as far as the divine utterances lay open from the holy Scriptures, that for our ancient fathers, before the Incarnation of the Savior, when God was said to appear, those voices and corporeal forms were made through angels—whether the angels themselves spoke or did something in the person of God, as we have shown the prophets too are wont to do, or took up from a creation what they themselves were not, where God was figuratively shown to men; and Scripture teaches by many examples that the prophets did not omit this kind of signification either.

It remains, then, that we should see—now that the Lord has been born *of the Virgin*, and the Holy Spirit has descended *in bodily form like a dove*,[^78] and the tongues of fire have been seen with a sound made from heaven *on the day of Pentecost after the Lord's ascension*[^79]—that it was not the Word of God himself, in respect of his substance by which he is equal and coeternal with the Father, nor the Spirit of the Father and the Son, in respect of his substance by which he too is equal and coeternal with both, but rather a creation, which could be formed and exist in those modes, that appeared to corporeal and mortal senses; and we should see what difference there is between those manifestations and these properties of the Son of God and of the Holy Spirit, though made through a visible creation—which we shall more suitably begin in another volume.
