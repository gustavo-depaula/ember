# Book VIII: Proem. In the Trinity those things are said distinctly which are said relatively in relation to one another, but there is no diversity of essence.

## Proem

**1.** 1. We have said elsewhere[^1] that those things are properly said in that Trinity, pertaining distinctly to the single persons, which are said relatively in relation to one another, as the Father and the Son and the *Gift* of both,[^2] the Holy Spirit; for the Father is not the Trinity, nor the Son the Trinity, nor the Gift the Trinity. But that which the single ones are said in relation to themselves, is said not plurally as three, but as one, the Trinity itself: as God the Father, God the Son, God the Holy Spirit; and good the Father, good the Son, good the Holy Spirit; and almighty the Father, almighty the Son, almighty the Holy Spirit; yet not three gods, or three good ones, or three almighty ones; but one God, good, almighty, the Trinity itself;[^3] and whatever else is said of the single ones not relatively in relation to one another, but in relation to themselves.

For these things are said according to essence, because this is there to be which is to be great, which is to be good, which is to be wise, and whatever else each single person there, or the Trinity itself, is said in relation to itself. And therefore three persons or three substances are said,[^4] not that any diversity of essence be understood, but that some one word may be answered when it is said "what three" or "what three things"; and that there is such great equality in that Trinity, that not only is the Father not greater than the Son, as far as pertains to divinity, but neither are the Father and the Son together anything greater than the Holy Spirit, nor is any single one of the three persons whatever anything less than the Trinity itself.

These things have been said, and if they be repeated by turning them over more often, they become known indeed more familiarly: but some measure too is to be applied, and God is to be entreated with most devout piety, that he may open the understanding, and take away the zeal of contention, by which the essence of truth may be discerned by the mind, without any mass, without any changeableness.[^5]

Now, therefore, insofar as the Creator, wonderfully merciful, himself helps, let us attend to these things, which we shall treat in a more inward manner than the foregoing, although they are the same: keeping that rule, that what has not yet shone out to our understanding may not be let go from the firmness of faith.

### In the Trinity two or three persons are not anything greater than one of them.

**1.** 2. For we say that in this Trinity two or three persons are not anything greater than one of them: which the carnal habit does not grasp,[^6] for no other reason than that it perceives the true things that were created as it can, but the very truth by which they were created it cannot behold: for if it could, in no way would this corporeal light be more manifest than what we have said. For in the substance of truth, since it alone truly is, nothing is greater except what more truly is. But whatever is intelligible and unchangeable is not one thing truer than another, because it is equally unchangeably eternal: nor is what is there called great, great from anything other than that whereby it truly is. Wherefore, where magnitude itself is truth, whatever has more of magnitude must needs have more of truth; whatever, then, does not have more of truth, does not have more of magnitude either. Further, whatever has more of truth is assuredly truer, as that is greater which has more of magnitude: that, then, is there greater which is truer. But the Father and the Son together are not truer than the Father singly, or the Son singly. Not, therefore, are both together anything greater than each one of them. And since the Holy Spirit too equally truly is, the Father and the Son together are not anything greater than he, because they are not truer. The Father too and the Holy Spirit together, since they do not surpass the Son in truth (for they are not truer), do not surpass him in magnitude. And so the Son and the Holy Spirit together are as great a thing as the Father alone, because they as truly are. So too the Trinity itself is as great as each single person there. For there that is not greater which is not truer, where truth itself is magnitude. Because in the essence of truth, this is to be true which is to be; and this is to be which is to be great: this, then, to be great which is to be true. What, therefore, is there equally true, must needs be also equally great.

### God is Truth.

**2.** 3. In bodies, however, it can happen that this gold is just as truly gold as that, and yet this piece is larger than that, since the magnitude there is not the same thing as the truth; and it is one thing for it to be gold, another for it to be large. So too in the nature of the mind: a mind is not called true by the same standard by which it is called great. For even one who is not great-souled has a true mind, since the essence of body and of mind is not the essence of Truth itself, as is the Trinity, the one, sole, great, true, truthful God, Truth. If we try to think of him, so far as he allows and grants, let no contact or embrace through stretches of space be thought of, as of three bodies; no fitting together of joints, as the fables tell of three-bodied Geryon;[^7] rather, whatever of such a kind presents itself to the mind, so that it would be greater in three than in one, and less in one than in two, let it be rejected without any hesitation: for in this way everything bodily is rejected.

In spiritual things, on the other hand, let nothing changeable that presents itself be thought to be God. For it is no small part of knowledge, when we breathe up out of this depth toward that height, if before we can know what God is we can already know what he is not. For he is certainly neither earth nor heaven, nor anything like earth and heaven, nor anything such as we see in the sky, nor anything of that kind which we do not see and which is perhaps in the sky. Nor, if you increase in the imagination of your thought the light of the sun as much as you can, whether to make it greater or to make it brighter, a thousand times or beyond counting, is this God either. Nor is God anything like the way the angels are conceived—pure spirits “breathing into” the heavenly bodies, and changing and turning them at the will by which they serve God—[^8] nor, even if all of them, since they are *thousands upon thousands*, were gathered into one and became one, is God anything of that sort. Nor even if you think of those same spirits without bodies—which is indeed most difficult for a carnal way of thinking.

Look, see if you can, *O soul weighed down by the body that is corruptible*, and burdened with earthly thoughts many and various; look, see if you can: God is truth. For this is written, *that God is light*; not as these eyes see, but as the heart sees, when it hears,[^9] “It is truth.” Do not ask what truth is; for at once the mists of bodily images and the clouds of phantasms will set themselves against you, and will disturb the serenity that shone upon you at the first stroke, when I said, “Truth.” See, in that very first stroke by which you are dazzled as by a flash, when it is said, “Truth,” remain there if you can: but you cannot; you slip back into these accustomed and earthly things. By what weight, I ask, do you slip back, if not by the birdlime of the desires you have contracted and by the wanderings of your pilgrimage?[^10]

### God is the highest Good.

**3.** 4. See again, if you can. You certainly love nothing but the good; for the earth is good with its lofty mountains and its temperate hills and its level plains, and a pleasant and fertile estate is good, and a house is good when its parts are well proportioned and it is spacious and full of light, and animals, living bodies, are good, and air is good when mild and healthful, and food is good when pleasant and suited to health, and health is good when free from pain and weariness, and a person’s face is good when evenly proportioned and cheerful in expression and bright in color, and the mind of a friend is good in the sweetness of agreement and the loyalty of love, and a just man is good, and riches are good because they readily serve our purposes, and the sky is good with its sun and moon and stars, and the angels are good in their holy obedience, and speech is good when it teaches pleasantly and moves[^11] the hearer fittingly, and a song is good when melodious in its rhythms and weighty in its thoughts.

Why go on and on? This good and that good. Take away this and that, and see the good itself, if you can; so you will see God, good not by some other good, but the Good of every good. For among all these goods, whether those I have mentioned or others that are perceived or thought of, we would not, when we judge truly, call one better than another unless the notion of the good itself were impressed upon us, by which we both approve something and prefer one thing to another.[^12] Thus God is to be loved, not as this good or that good, but as the Good itself. For the good to be sought for the soul is not one over which it should fly in judging, but one to which it should cling in loving; and what is this but God? Not a good mind, or a good angel, or a good heaven, but the good Good.

For perhaps in this way what I mean is more easily grasped. When I hear, for example, the words “a good mind,” just as there are two words, so from those words I understand two things: one by which it is a mind, another by which it is good. And indeed, that it might be a mind, it did nothing itself; for there was not yet anything that could act[^13] so that it might exist: but that it might be a good mind, I see that something must be done by the will; not because the very thing by which it is a mind is nothing good—for from what else is it already called, and most truly called, better than body?—but because it is not yet called a good mind for this reason, that there still remains for it the act of will by which it may become more excellent.

And if it neglects this, it is rightly blamed, and rightly said not to be a good mind. For it differs from one that does this; and because that one is praiseworthy, surely this one that does not do it is blameworthy. But when it does this with zeal and becomes a good mind, it cannot attain this unless it turns toward something that it is not itself. And toward what does it turn in order to become a good mind, if not toward the good, since it loves and seeks and obtains this? And if it turns away again, and becomes not good, then by this very fact, that it turns away from the good, unless that good from which it turns away remained in itself, there would be nothing to which it could turn again, should it wish to amend itself.

### The unchangeable Good and changeable goods.

**3.** 5. Therefore there would be no changeable goods unless there were the unchangeable Good. So when you hear of this good and that good, which can in other respects be called also not good, if you are able, setting aside those things that are good by participation in the Good, to behold the Good itself by participation in which they are good—for you understand the Good itself at the same time as you hear of this or that good—if then, with those things set aside, you are able to behold the good through itself, you will have beheld God. And if you cling to him in love, you will straightway be made blessed. But let it be a shame, when other things are loved only because they are good, by clinging to them not to love the Good itself from which they are good.

This too: that the mind, merely because it is a mind—even when it is not yet good in the way it becomes good by turning to the unchangeable Good, but, as I said, is merely a mind—is pleasing to us, when we understand rightly, so that we prefer it even to every bodily light; yet it is pleasing to us not in itself, but in that art by which it was made. For it is thereby approved as made, where it is seen that it ought to have been made. This is truth, and the simple good; for it is nothing other than the Good itself, and through this, the highest Good as well.[^14] For the good cannot be diminished or increased except that which is good from another good.

The mind, then, turns to become good toward that from which it has its being as a mind. The will agrees with nature so that the mind is perfected in good, when that good is loved by a turning of the will—the good from which comes also that which is not lost even by a turning away of the will. For by turning itself away from the highest Good, the mind loses its being a good mind; yet it does not lose its being a mind, since this too is already a good better than body: this, then, the will loses, which the will obtains. For there already existed a mind that could will to turn toward that from which it was; but one that might will to exist before it existed did not yet exist. And this is our good, where we see whether anything ought to have existed or ought to exist, when we grasp that it ought to have existed or ought to exist, and where we see that it could not have existed unless it ought to have existed, even when we do not grasp how it ought to have existed. This good, then, *is not far from each one of us: for in him we live and move and have our being*.

### God is to be loved through faith, that the heart may be purified.

**4.** 6. But we must stand fast by love toward that good and cling to it, so that we may fully enjoy his presence from whom we are, and without whom we could not even exist. For since *we still walk by faith and not by sight*, we certainly *do not yet see* God, as the same Apostle says, *face to face*. Yet unless we now already love him, we shall never see him. But who loves what he does not know? Something can indeed be known and not loved: but whether what is not known can be loved, this I ask; for if it cannot, no one loves God before he knows him. And what is it to know God, but to behold him with the mind and firmly to perceive him? For he is not a body, that he should be sought with the eyes of flesh.

But even before we are able to behold and perceive God, as he can be beheld and perceived, which is granted to the pure in heart—for *blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God*—unless he is loved *through faith*,[^15] the heart cannot be purified so as to be fit and suited for seeing him. For where are those three things, for the sake of building which up in the soul all the structures of the divine Books rise, *faith, hope, love*,[^16] if not in a soul that believes what it does not yet see, and hopes for and loves what it believes? So even what is unknown is loved;[^17] yet it is believed. But assuredly we must take care that the soul, believing what it does not see, does not fashion for itself something that is not, and hope for and love what is false. For if this happens, there will not be *love from a pure heart and a good conscience and faith unfeigned*, which *is the end of the commandment*, as the same Apostle says.

### Faith involves a prior knowledge.

**4.** 7. Now it is inevitable that, when we believe some bodily things that we have read or heard of but have not seen, the mind fashions for itself something in the lineaments and shapes of bodies, as it occurs to the one thinking—something that is either not true, or, even if it is true (which can happen very rarely), it is nevertheless of no profit to us to hold it by faith, but profitable for something else that is conveyed through it.

For who, among those who read or hear what the apostle Paul wrote, or what was written about him, does not fashion in his mind a face both for the Apostle himself and for all whose names are mentioned there? And since, among so great a multitude of people to whom those Letters are known, one thinks of the lineaments and shape of those bodies in one way and another in another, which one thinks more nearly and more like the truth is certainly uncertain.

Nor is our faith occupied there with what bodily face those people had; but only that, by the grace of God, they lived in such a way and did the things to which that Scripture bears witness: this it is both profitable to believe, and not to be despaired of, and to be sought after. For even the face of the Lord’s own flesh is varied and fashioned by the diversity of countless thoughts, though it was one, whatever it was.

Nor, in the faith we have concerning the Lord Jesus Christ, is what is salutary what the mind fashions for itself—perhaps far otherwise than the reality is—but what we think of him in accordance with the form of a human being: for we have, fixed in us as by a rule, the knowledge of human nature, in accordance with which, whenever we look upon anything of that kind, we at once recognize it to be a human being, or the form of a human being.

### How the Trinity, though unknown, is loved.

**5.** 7. In accordance with this knowledge our thought is shaped, when we believe that for our sake God became a human being, for an example of humility, and to show forth the love of God toward us.[^18] For it profits us to believe this, and to hold it in our heart firm and unshaken: that the humility by which God was born of a woman[^19] and led by mortals through so many insults to death is the supreme remedy by which the swelling of our pride might be healed, and a deep mystery by which the bond of sin might be loosed.[^20]

So too the power of his miracles and of his resurrection itself: since we know what omnipotence is, we believe it of the omnipotent God, and we think of deeds of this kind in accordance with the forms and kinds of things, whether implanted in us by nature or gathered from experience, so that our faith may not be feigned.

For we do not know the face of the Virgin Mary, from whom, untouched by a man and uncorrupted even in the very birth, he was wondrously born. Nor have we seen with what lineaments of limbs Lazarus was; nor Bethany; nor the tomb and that stone which he ordered to be rolled away when he raised him;[^21] nor the new tomb hewn in the rock from which he himself rose again;[^22] nor the Mount of Olives from which he ascended into heaven:[^23] nor do we at all know, any of us who have not seen these things, whether they are as we think them; rather, we judge it more probable that they are not so.

For when the same face of some place or person or any body whatever has met our eyes as met our mind when, before we saw it, we were thinking of it, we are moved by no small wonder; so rarely, and almost never, does this happen:

and yet we believe these things most firmly, because we think in accordance with a special and general knowledge that is certain to us. For we believe that the Lord Jesus Christ was born of a virgin who was called Mary. But what a virgin is, and what it is to be born, and what a proper name is, we do not believe but simply know. Whether, however, that was the face of Mary which met our mind when we say or recall these things, we neither know at all nor believe. And so here, with our faith safe, we may say, “Perhaps she had such a face, perhaps not such a face”; but “Perhaps Christ was born of a virgin,” no one with the Christian faith safe would say.

### On the knowledge of justice.

**5.** 8. Therefore, since we desire to understand, so far as is granted us, the eternity and equality and unity of the Trinity, and since we must believe before we understand,[^24] and must be watchful lest our faith be feigned[^25]—for it is that same Trinity that we must enjoy in order to live blessedly;[^26] but if we believe something false about it, our hope will be empty and our love not chaste[^27]—how, then, do we, by believing, love that Trinity which we do not know? Is it in accordance with the special or general knowledge by which we love the Apostle Paul? Even if he did not have the face that comes to us when we think of him—and this we wholly do not know—yet we know what a human being is. For, not to go far afield, this is what we are, and it is plain that he was this, and that his soul, joined to a body, lived a mortal life. This, then, we believe of him, which we find in ourselves, according to the form or kind by which all human nature alike is comprised.

What, then, do we know about that excellence of the Trinity, whether in a special or a general way—as if there were many such trinities, some of which we have experienced, so that by a rule of likeness, by an impressed knowledge either special or general, we might believe that one also to be of such a kind, and so might love a thing that we believe and do not yet know, from its parity with a thing we do know? This is certainly not so. Or is it that, just as we love in the Lord Jesus Christ the fact that he rose from the dead, although we have never seen anyone rise from there, so we can, by believing, love the Trinity which we do not see and the like of which we have never seen? But what it is to die, and what it is to live, we surely know: for we both live, and have at some time seen and experienced the dead and the dying. And what is it to rise again, but to come back to life, that is, *to return from death to life*?[^28] When, then, we say and believe that there is a Trinity, we know what a trinity is, because we know what three are; but this we do not love. For we easily have that whenever we wish—to pass over other examples—merely by flashing three fingers. Or rather do we love, not that it is any trinity, but that it is the Trinity, God? This, then, we love in the Trinity, that it is God: but we have seen or known no other God, *for God is one*,[^29] he alone whom we have not yet seen and yet, by believing, love. But from what likeness or comparison of known things we are to believe, so that we may love the as yet unknown God,[^30] this is the question.

### On the true love through which one comes to the knowledge of the Trinity.

**6.** 9. Come back with me, then, and let us consider why we love the Apostle. Is it on account of the “human” form, which we know very well, because we believe him to have been a man? Surely not; otherwise there is now no one for us to love, since that man no longer exists; for his soul has been separated from his body. But what we love in him we believe to be alive even now: for we love a just mind. By what general or special rule, then, except that we know both what a mind is and what a just person is?

And as for what a mind is, we say, not unfittingly, that we know it for this reason: that we ourselves also have a mind. For we have never seen it with our eyes, nor gathered a general or special notion from the likeness of many things seen; but rather, as I said, because we ourselves also have one. For what is known so intimately, and feels its own existence, as that by which all other things too are felt, that is, the mind itself? For the movements of bodies, by which we perceive that others besides ourselves are alive, we recognize from our own likeness: because we too move our body by living, just as we observe those bodies to be moved.

For when a living body is moved, no way is opened for our eyes to see the mind, a thing that cannot be seen by the eyes: but we perceive that there is something in that mass such as is in us for the moving of our own mass in like manner, namely, life and soul. Nor is this, as it were, proper to human prudence and reason. For the beasts too perceive that not only they themselves are alive, but also one another, each the other, and ourselves as well. And they do not see our souls, but perceive them from the movements of the body, and that at once and most easily, by a kind of natural agreement. We know, then, the mind of anyone at all from our own, and from our own we believe in one we do not know.[^31] For we do not only perceive a mind, but can even know what a mind is by considering our own: for we have a mind.

But from where do we know what “just” is? For we had said that we love the Apostle for no other reason than that his mind is “just.” We know, then, also what just is, as we know what a mind is. But what a mind is, as has been said, we know from ourselves: for a mind is within us. From where, however, do we know what “just” is, if we are not just? But if no one knows what “just” is unless he is just, no one loves the just unless he is just: for he cannot love the one whom he believes to be just, for this very reason, that he believes him to be just, if he is ignorant of what “just” is; in accordance with what we showed above, that no one loves what he believes and does not see, except from some rule of general or special knowledge. And by this, if no one but the just loves the just, how will anyone who is not yet just wish to be just? For no one wishes to be what he does not love. But that he who is not yet just may become just, he will surely wish to be just: and that he may wish it, he loves the just. He loves the just, then, even though he is not yet just. But he cannot love the just if he is ignorant of what “just” is. He knows, therefore, what “just” is, even though he is not yet just.

Where, then, does he know it? Has he seen it with his eyes? Or any body that is just, as it is white, or black, or square, or round? Who would say this? But with his eyes he has seen nothing but bodies. The just, however, is not in a person except the mind: and when a person is called just, it is said of the mind, not of the body. For justice is a certain beauty of the mind, by which people are beautiful, many even who are misshapen and deformed in body.

But as the mind is not seen with the eyes, so neither is its beauty. Where, then, does he who is not yet just know what “just” is, and love the just so that he may become just? Are there perhaps certain signs that flash out through the movement of the body, by which this or that person appears to be just? But from where does he know that those are the signs of a just mind, not knowing at all what “just” is? He knows it, therefore.

But where do we know what “just” is, even when we are not yet just? If it is outside ourselves that we know it, we know it in some body. But this is not a thing of the body. In ourselves, then, we know what “just” is. For I do not find this anywhere else, when I seek to express it, but with my own self: and if I ask another what “just” is, he seeks within himself what to answer; and whoever has been able to answer the truth on this matter has found within himself what he should answer.

And when I wish to speak of Carthage, I seek within myself in order to speak, and within myself I find the image of Carthage: but I received it through the body, that is, through the sense of the body, since I was bodily present in it, and saw and perceived it, and held it in memory, so that I might find within myself the word for it, when I wished to speak of it. For the image of it in my memory is its word—not this sound of three syllables when “Carthage” is named, nor even the name itself thought of silently through stretches of time; but that which I discern in my mind when I utter this trisyllable with my voice, or even before I utter it.

So too when I wish to speak of Alexandria, which I have never seen, an image of it is ready at hand within me. For since I had heard from many, and believed, that it is a great city, in the way it could be described to me, I fashioned in my mind such an image of it as I could: and this is its word within me, when I wish to speak of it, before I utter with my voice the five syllables of its name, which is known to almost everyone. Yet if I could bring forth this image from my mind to the eyes of people who know Alexandria, surely either they would all say, “That is not it”; or, if they said, “That is it,” I would be greatly astonished, and gazing in my mind on that very image, that is, on its likeness as it were a picture, I would still not know that it was the city itself, but would believe those who held it from having seen it.

But it is not in this way that I seek what “just” is, nor in this way that I find it, nor in this way that I behold it when I express it; nor in this way am I approved when I am heard; nor in this way do I approve when I hear; as if I had seen some such thing with my eyes, or learned it through some sense of the body, or heard it from those who had so learned it.

For when I say, and say knowingly, “A mind is just which by knowledge and reason, in life and conduct, *renders to each his own*,”[^32] I am not thinking of some absent thing, like Carthage, nor fashioning it as I can, like Alexandria, whether it be so or not; but I discern something present, and I discern it within myself, even though I am not myself what I discern, and many, if they hear, will approve.

And whoever hears me and knowingly approves discerns this same thing within himself, even though he too is not himself what he discerns. But the just person, when he says it, discerns and says what he himself is. And where does he too discern it, except within himself?

But this is no wonder: for where would he discern himself, except within himself? What is wonderful is that the mind should see within itself what it has nowhere else seen, and should see the truth, and should see the very true just mind, and yet be itself a mind and not be the just mind which it sees within itself. Is there another just mind in a mind not yet just? Or if there is not, what does it see there, when it sees and says what a just mind is, and sees it nowhere but in itself, when it is not itself a just mind? Or is that which it sees the inner truth, present to the mind that has the power to behold it? Not all have the power: and those who do have the power to behold it are not all also that which they behold, that is, they are not themselves just minds, even as they can see and say what a just mind is. And from where will they be able to be just, except by clinging to that very form which they behold, that from it they may be shaped and be just minds; not only discerning and saying that a mind is just which “by knowledge and reason, in life and conduct, renders to each his own,” but so that they themselves also may live justly and be just in their conduct, by rendering to each his own, *owing no one anything except to love one another*?[^33] And from where does one cling to that form, except by loving? Why, then, do we love another whom we believe to be just, and not love that very form where we see what a just mind is, so that we too may be able to be just? Or rather, unless we also loved that form, we would in no way love the one whom we love on account of it; but so long as we are not just, we love it less than is needed for us to be able to be just.

A person, then, who is believed to be just is loved out of that form and truth which he who loves discerns and understands within himself; but that form and truth itself there is no way to love from any other source.

For we find nothing else of the kind besides it, so that we might, when it is unknown, by believing love it, on the ground that we already know some such thing. For whatever of the kind you behold, it is the form itself: and there is nothing of the kind, since it alone is such as it itself is.

Whoever, then, loves people ought to love them either because they are just, or that they may be just. For so too ought he to love himself, either because he is just, or that he may be just; for in this way he *loves his neighbor as himself*, without any danger.

For one who loves himself otherwise loves himself unjustly, since he loves himself to this end, that he may be unjust; therefore to this end, that he may be evil, and by this he no longer loves himself; for *he who loves iniquity hates his own soul*.

### God is to be sought within.

**7.** 10. Therefore, in this question of ours about the Trinity and about knowing God, there is nothing chiefly to be considered but what true love is, or rather what love is. For that is to be called love which is true; otherwise it is cupidity: and so the covetous are improperly said to love, just as those who love are improperly said to covet. But this is true love: that, clinging to the truth, *we may live justly*;[^34] and therefore that we may despise all mortal things for the love of human beings, by which we wish them to live justly. For so we shall be able to be profitably ready even to die for the brethren, which the Lord Jesus Christ taught us by his example. For since there are two commandments on which *the whole Law hangs, and the Prophets*, love of God and love of neighbor, not without reason does Scripture very often put one for both: whether of God alone, as in that passage, *we know that all things work together for good for those who love God*; and again, *but whoever loves God, this one is known by him*; and that, *that the love of God is poured forth in our hearts through the Holy Spirit who is given to us*; and many others, because one who loves God consequently does what God commanded, and loves only insofar as he does it; it follows, then, that he loves his neighbor also, because God commanded this.[^35] Or Scripture mentions only the love of neighbor, as in that passage, *bear one another’s burdens, and so you will fulfill the law of Christ*; and that, *for the whole Law is fulfilled in one word, in this that is written: you shall love your neighbor as yourself*; and in the Gospel, *all that you wish people to do for you that is good, this*[^36] *do you also to them; for this is the Law and the Prophets*. And we find many other places in the Holy Writings in which love of neighbor alone seems to be enjoined unto perfection, and the love of God to be passed over in silence; though on both commandments hang the Law and the Prophets.[^37] But this too is for this reason, that one who loves his neighbor consequently loves love itself above all. And *God is love, and whoever abides in love abides in God*. It follows, then, that he chiefly loves God.

**7.** 11. Therefore those who seek God[^38] through those powers that rule the world or parts of the world are carried away from him and flung far off; not by intervals of space, but by a diversity of desires:[^39] for they try to go outward, and forsake their own inner depths, in which God is more inward than they.

And so even if they have heard of, or somehow conceived of, some holy heavenly power, they desire its deeds rather, which human weakness marvels at; they do not imitate the piety by which divine rest is gained. For they would rather be able proudly to do what an angel does than devoutly to be what an angel is. For no holy being rejoices in its own power, but in his from whom it has the power to do whatever it fittingly can: and it knows that it is more powerful to be joined to the Almighty by a pious will than to be able by one’s own will to do what those who cannot do such things tremble at.

And so the Lord Jesus Christ himself, doing such things, in order that by them he might teach those who marveled greater things, and might turn those who were intent and held in suspense by unusual temporal things toward the eternal and inward things, said, *Come to me, all you who labor and are burdened, and I will refresh you; take my yoke upon you*. And he did not say, “Learn from me that I raise up the four-days-dead”;[^40] but he said, *Learn from me, for I am meek and humble of heart*.

For most solid humility is more powerful and more secure than the most windy loftiness. And therefore he goes on to say, *and you will find rest for your souls*. For *love is not puffed up*; and *God is love*; and *the faithful in love rest in him*, called back from the noise that is outside to the silent joys. See, *God is love*: why do we go and run to the heights of the heavens and the depths of the earth, seeking him who is with us, if we would but be with him?[^41]

### Whoever loves his brother is born of God and knows God.

**8.** 12. Let no one say, “I do not know what I should love.” Let him love[^42] his brother, and let him love that same love. For he knows the love by which he loves better than the brother whom he loves. See, he can now have God better known than his brother: plainly better known, because more present; better known, because more inward; better known, because more certain. Embrace love, who is God, and embrace God with love. This is the love that binds together all the good angels and all the servants of God in the bond of holiness, and joins us and them mutually to one another, and subjects us to himself. The more healed, then, we are of the swelling of pride, the more full we are of love: and with what is one full who is full of love, if not with God?

“But,” you say, “I see love, and as far as I can I behold it with my mind, and I believe the Scripture that says that *God is love, and whoever abides in love abides in God*: but when I see love, I do not see the Trinity in it.” On the contrary, you do see the Trinity if you see love. But I will remind you, if I can, so that you may see that you see: only let love herself be present, that we may be moved by love to some good. For when we love love, we love something that loves, on this very account that it loves something. Therefore what does love love, that love herself may also be loved? For it is not love that loves nothing. But if it loves itself, it must love something, in order to love itself as love. For just as a word indicates something, and also indicates itself, but does not indicate itself to be a word unless it indicates that it indicates something: so too love indeed loves itself, but unless it loves itself as loving something, it does not love itself as love. What, then, does love love, but what we love with love? But that, to begin from one nearest us, is our brother.

Let us attend to how greatly the apostle John commends brotherly love: *whoever loves his brother*, he says, *abides in the light, and there is no stumbling-block in him*. It is plain that he has placed the perfection of justice in the love of one’s brother: for one in whom there is no stumbling-block is surely perfect. And yet he seems to have passed over the love of God in silence: which he would never do, except that in that very brotherly love he wishes God to be understood. For most plainly, a little later in the same Epistle, he says thus: *beloved, let us love one another, for love is from God; and everyone who loves is born of God and has known God. He who does not love has not known God; for God is love*. This linking sufficiently and plainly declares that this same brotherly love—for brotherly love is that by which we love one another—is proclaimed by such great authority to be not only *from God* but also *God*. When, then, we love our brother out of love, we love our brother out of God: nor can it be that we do not chiefly love that same love by which we love our brother.

From this it is gathered that those two commandments cannot be without each other. For since *God is love*, he certainly loves God who loves love; but it is necessary that he love love who loves his brother. And therefore, what he says a little later, *he cannot love God whom he does not see, who does not love his brother whom he sees*; because this is the reason for his not seeing God, that he does not love his brother. For one who does not love his brother is not in love; and one who is not in love is not in God, because *God is love*. Further, one who is not in God is not in the light: because *God is light, and in him there is no darkness at all*. He, then, who is not in the light, what wonder if he does not see the light, that is, does not see God, because he *is in darkness*? But he sees his brother with human sight, by which God cannot be seen. But if he loved with spiritual love the one whom he sees with human sight, he would see God, who is love itself, with that inner sight by which he can be seen. And so *he who does not love his brother whom he sees, how can he love God*, whom for this reason he does not see, because *God is love*, which he lacks who does not love his brother? And let not that question now move us, how much love we owe to spend upon our brother, how much upon God: upon God incomparably more than upon ourselves; upon our brother[^43] as much as upon ourselves: but we love ourselves the more, the more we love God. From one and the same love, therefore, we love God and neighbor; but God for God’s sake, and ourselves *and our neighbor for God’s sake*.[^44]

### From the form of justice we love the just.

**9.** 13. For what is it, I ask, that makes us burn when we hear and read: *behold, now is the acceptable time, behold, now is the day of salvation: giving no offense in anything, that our ministry be not blamed; but in everything commending ourselves as ministers of God, in much patience, in tribulations, in necessities, in distresses, in beatings, in imprisonments, in tumults, in labors, in watchings, in fastings, in chastity, in knowledge, in long-suffering, in kindness, in the Holy Spirit, in love unfeigned, in the word of truth, in the power of God; through the weapons of justice on the right hand and on the left, through glory and dishonor, through ill repute and good repute; as deceivers, and yet truthful; as unknown, and yet recognized; as dying, and behold we live; as chastened, and not put to death; as sorrowful, yet always rejoicing; as poor, yet enriching many; as having nothing, and possessing all things*? What is it that kindles us with love for the apostle Paul, when we read these things, but that we believe he lived in this way? Yet that the ministers of God ought so to live, we do not believe from having heard it of certain men, but we behold it within ourselves, or rather above ourselves, in the truth itself.[^45] Him, then, whom we believe to have lived in this way, we love from what we see. And unless we chiefly loved this form, which we discern as ever stable and unchangeable, we would not for that reason love him, that we hold by faith that his life, when he lived in the flesh, was fitted and conformed to this form.

But somehow we are stirred more, and to a love of that very form, through the faith by which we believe that someone lived in this way; and through the hope by which we who are human beings, from the fact that some human beings have lived in this way, in no way despair that we too can so live, so that we both desire this more ardently and pray for it more confidently. Thus the love of that form, according to which they are believed to have lived, makes their life loved by us; and their life, when believed, kindles a more blazing love toward that same form; so that the more blazingly we love God, the more certainly and serenely we see him; because in God we behold the unchangeable form of justice, according to which we judge that a person ought to live. Faith, then, avails for the knowledge and for the love of God, not as of one wholly unknown or wholly unloved; but so that he may be known more clearly, and may be loved more firmly.

### A certain three things in Love, as it were a trace of the Trinity.

**10.** 14. But what is love, or charity, which the Divine Scripture so greatly praises and proclaims, but the love of the good? Now love is of someone loving, and by love something is loved. See, there are three: the one who loves, and what is loved, and love. What, then, is love, but a certain life coupling, or seeking to couple, two things, namely the one who loves and what is loved? And this is so even in the lowest and carnal loves: but that we may draw something purer and clearer, let us tread the flesh underfoot and ascend to the mind. What does the mind love in a friend, but the mind? There too, then, there are three: the one who loves, and what is loved, and love.

It remains to ascend from here also, and to seek these things higher up, so far as is granted to a human being.[^46] But here let our attention rest a little—not that it should now suppose it has found what it seeks, but as a place is usually found where something is to be sought;[^47] that thing is not yet found, but it has now been found where it is to be sought:[^48] so let it suffice to have said this, that, as from the joint of some beginning, we may weave the rest.
