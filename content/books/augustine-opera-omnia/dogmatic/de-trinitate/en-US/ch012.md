# Book X: The love of a mind eager to learn is not love of a thing unknown.

**1.** 1. Now let a more refined attention approach the unfolding of these very things, consequently and more clearly. And first, since no one can in any way love a thing wholly unknown, we must look carefully at the kind of love that learners have, that is, of those who do not yet know, but still desire to know, some branch of learning. For even in those matters in which the word “zeal” is not customarily used, there are wont to arise loves from hearsay, when the soul is kindled, by the fame of some beauty, to see it and enjoy it; because it generally knows the beauties of bodies, from the fact that it has seen very many, and there is within it something by which what is longed for outwardly is approved. And when this happens, it is not a love of a wholly unknown thing that is stirred, whose kind is thus known. But when we love a good man whose face we have not seen, we love him from the knowledge of the virtues which we know in the truth itself.

But to come to know branches of learning, it is most often the authority of those who praise and commend them that kindles us: and yet, unless we had a briefly impressed notion of each branch of learning in our soul, we would burn with no zeal to learn it. For who, for example, would expend any care and labor on learning rhetoric, unless he first knew that it is the science of speaking? Sometimes too we marvel at the ends of these branches of learning, when we have heard of them or experienced them, and from this we burn to acquire by learning the ability by which we may be able to reach them.

As if to one who does not know letters it were said that there is a certain skill by which anyone is able, however far away, to send words made by the hand in silence, which in turn the one to whom they are sent gathers not with the ears, but with the eyes, and he sees that this is done; would he not, while he longs to know by what means he could do this, be moved with all zeal toward that end which he already holds as known? So are the zealous efforts of learners kindled: for what anyone wholly does not know, he can in no way love.

## A sign.

**1.** 2. So too, if anyone hears an unknown sign, such as the sound of some word, by which he does not know what is signified, he desires to know what it is, that is, what thing that sound was established to call to mind: as if he hears it said “metheglin,”[^1] and, not knowing what it is, asks. Now then it is necessary that he already know it to be a sign, that is, that that utterance is not empty, but that something is signified by it: otherwise this trisyllable is already known, and has impressed its articulated form on the soul through the sense of the ears: what more would be sought in it, by which it might become better known, all of whose letters and all of whose spans of sound are known; unless because at the same time it became known to be a sign, and stirred the desire to know of what thing it is the sign?

The more, then, it is known, but not fully known, the more the soul desires to know about it what remains. For if it knew only that this utterance exists, and did not know it to be the sign of some thing, it would now seek nothing further, having perceived the sensible thing, as far as it could, by sensing it. But because it now knows not only that it is an utterance, but also that it is a sign, it wishes to know it perfectly. And no sign is perfectly known, unless it is known of what thing it is the sign.

One, then, who seeks with burning care to know this, and, kindled with zeal, presses on, can he be said to be without love? What, then, does he love? For surely nothing can be loved unless it is known. Nor does he love these three syllables, which he already holds as known. But if he already loves this in them, that he knows they signify something; that is not what is now in question, for he does not seek to know this: but in that which he is eager to know, we ask what he loves, which he surely does not yet know: and therefore we marvel why he loves, since we know most firmly that nothing can be loved unless it is known.

What, then, does he love, except that he knows and beholds, in the reasons of things, what is the beauty of that branch of learning by which the knowledge of all signs is contained; and what is the usefulness in that skill by which human society shares its thoughts among itself, lest the gatherings of people be worse for them than any solitude, if they do not mingle their thoughts by conversing. The soul, then, discerns this becoming and useful form, and knows it, and loves it; and whoever inquires into any significant utterances he does not know is eager that it be perfected in himself, as far as he can. For it is one thing that he beholds it in the light of truth, another that he longs for it in his own ability.

For he beholds in the light of truth how great and how good it is to understand and speak the tongues of all nations, and to hear none as a foreigner, and to be heard by none as such. The grace of this knowledge is already discerned by thought, and the known thing is loved; which is so beheld, and so inflames the zeal of learners, that they are moved about it, and long for it in all the labor they expend on attaining such an ability, so that they may even embrace by use what they foreknow by reason: and so each one, as he draws near in hope to that ability, burns the more fervently toward it with love. For those branches of learning are pursued the more eagerly which are not despaired of as capable of being grasped. For of a thing for the attaining of which one bears no hope, he either loves it tepidly, or does not love it at all, although he sees how beautiful it is.

Wherefore, since the knowledge of all tongues is despaired of by almost everyone, each one is most eager to know that of his own nation. And if he feels that he does not suffice even for that to the point of perfection, yet no one is so slothful in this knowledge that, when he has heard an unknown word, he does not wish to know what it is, and, if he can, seek it out and learn it.

And while he seeks this, he is surely in the zeal of learning, and seems to love an unknown thing; which is not so. For that form touches his soul, which it knows and thinks of, in which shines the grace of joining souls together in the hearing and rendering of known utterances: and this kindles with zeal one who indeed seeks what he does not know, but beholds and loves the known form to which it pertains.

And so if to one who seeks, for example, what “metheglin” is (for I had put this for the sake of example), it is said, What does it matter to you? he will answer, Lest perhaps I hear a speaker and not understand him, or perhaps somewhere read it and not know what the writer meant. Who, then, would say to this person, Do not understand what you hear, do not know what you read? For to almost all rational souls the beauty of this skill is ready at hand to be seen, by which the thoughts of people are made known to one another by the utterance of significant sounds: on account of this known grace, and loved because known, that unknown word is eagerly sought.

And so when he has heard and learned that “metheglin” was called wine by the ancients,[^2] but that, by the usage of speech which we now have, this word is dead, he will reckon it necessary to himself perhaps on account of certain books of the ancients. But if he holds even those to be superfluous, perhaps he now judges it not even worth committing to memory, because he sees that it pertains not at all to that form of learning which he beholds in his mind as known and loves.

## No one at all loves what is unknown.

**1.** 3. Wherefore all the love of a mind eager to learn, that is, of one wishing to know what it does not know, is not love of the thing it does not know, but of the thing it knows, on account of which it wishes to know what it does not know.[^3] Or if he is so curious that he is seized, not on account of some other known cause, but by the love alone of knowing the unknown; this curious person is indeed to be distinguished from the name of the eager learner, but he too does not love the unknown—rather, it is more fittingly said, He hates the unknown, which he wishes were nothing, since he wishes all things to be known.

But lest anyone bring back to us a harder question, asserting that one can as little hate what he does not know as he cannot love what he does not know, we do not resist the truth: but it must be understood that it is not the same thing to say, He loves to know the unknown, as if it were said, He loves the unknown. For this can happen, that one love to know the unknown: but that he love the unknown, cannot. For not in vain is “to know” put there: since one who loves to know the unknown loves, not the unknown itself, but knowing itself.

And unless he had this as known, no one could confidently say either that he knows, or that he does not know. For not only must one who says, I know, and says it truly, know what it is to know: but also one who says, I do not know, and says this confidently and truly, and knows that he says the truth, surely knows what it is to know: because he both distinguishes the one who does not know from the one who knows, when, truthfully beholding himself, he says, I do not know; and when he knows that he says this truly, from where would he know it, if he did not know what it is to know?

## Examples.

**2.** 4. Every eager learner, then, every curious person, does not love the unknown, even when he presses with the most ardent appetite to know what he does not know. For either he already holds as known by its kind what he loves, and longs to know it also in some single thing, or in single things, which are perhaps praised to him though not yet known, and he fashions in his soul an imaginary form by which he is stirred to love.

But from where does he fashion it, except from those things he already knew? Yet if he finds the thing that was praised to be unlike that form, fashioned in his soul and most well known in his thought, perhaps he will not love it. But if he loves it, he will begin to love it from the moment he learned of it. For a little before, what was loved was something other than what the soul, forming it for itself, had been wont to present. But if he finds it like that form which fame had proclaimed, to which he could truly say, I loved you already; not even then did he love something unknown, which he had known in that likeness.

Or in the form of the eternal reason we see something and there love it, which, when it is expressed in the likeness of some temporal thing, we believe on the word of those who have experienced it as they praise it, and love it; we do not love something unknown—of which we have already discoursed enough above:

or we love something known, on account of which we seek something unknown: the love of which unknown thing does not in any way hold us, but that of the known thing, to which we know it pertains, that we may come to know that too which we still seek as unknown; as I spoke a little before of the unknown word.

Or each one loves knowing itself, which can be unknown to no one who desires to know anything. For these reasons those seem to love the unknown who wish to know something they do not know, and who, on account of their more ardent appetite for seeking, cannot be said to be without love.

But how the matter stands otherwise, and that nothing unknown is loved at all, I think I have persuaded those who look carefully at the truth. But because the examples we have given are of those who desire to know something that they themselves are not, we must see whether perhaps some new kind appears, when the mind desires to know itself.[^4]

## In seeking itself, the mind already knows itself.

**3.** 5. What, then, does the mind love, when it ardently seeks to know itself, while it is unknown to itself? For see, the mind seeks itself in order to know itself, and is inflamed with this zeal. It loves, then: but what does it love? Itself? How, since it does not yet know itself, and no one can love what he does not know? Or has fame proclaimed its appearance to it, as we are wont to hear of absent things? Perhaps, then, it does not love itself, but what it fashions of itself, this it loves, perhaps far other than it itself is:

or if the mind fashions for itself something like itself, and therefore, when it loves this figment, loves itself before it knows itself, because it beholds that which is like itself: it knows, then, other minds, from which it fashions itself, and is known to itself by its very kind. Why, then, when it knows other minds, does it not know itself, since nothing can be more present to it than it itself?

But if, as with the eyes of the body, other eyes are better known than they are to themselves; then let it not seek itself, since it will never find itself.[^5] For the eyes will never see themselves except in a mirror: nor is it in any way to be supposed that, even in the contemplation of incorporeal things, some such thing is employed, so that the mind might know itself *as in a mirror*.[^6]

Or does it see in the reason of eternal truth how beautiful it is to know oneself, and love this which it sees, and is eager that it come to be in itself? for although it is not known to itself, yet it is known to it how good it is, that it be known to itself. And this indeed is most wonderful, not yet to know itself, and yet already to know how beautiful it is to know itself. Or does it see some best end, that is, its own security and blessedness, through a certain hidden memory, which did not desert it when it had advanced into far regions, and does it believe that it cannot reach that same end unless it knows itself?[^7] So, while it loves the one, it seeks the other: and it loves as known the one on account of which it seeks the unknown. But why was the memory of its blessedness able to last, and the memory of itself not able to last along with it, so that it would know itself, which wishes to arrive, as well as it knows that at which it wishes to arrive?

Or, when it loves to know *itself*,[^8] does it love, not itself which it does not yet know, but knowing itself; and does it more bitterly bear that it itself is lacking to its own knowledge, by which it wishes to comprehend all things? It knows, however, what it is to know, and while it loves this which it knows, it desires also to know *itself*. Where, then, does it know its knowing, if it does not know itself?[^9] For it knows that it knows other things,[^10] but does not know itself: for from this it knows also what it is to know.

In what way, then, does it know itself to know something, which does not know itself? For it does not know another mind to be knowing, but itself. It knows itself, therefore. Then, when it seeks itself in order to know itself, it already knows itself seeking. It already knows itself, then. Wherefore it cannot at all not know itself, which, while it knows itself not knowing, surely knows itself. But if it did not know itself not knowing, it would not seek itself in order to know. Wherefore by the very fact that it seeks itself, it is convicted of being more known than unknown to itself. For it knows itself seeking and not knowing, while it seeks itself in order to know itself.

## It knows the whole of itself.

**4.** 6. What, then, shall we say?[^11] that in part it knows itself, in part does not know itself?[^12] But it is absurd to say that it does not know the whole of what it knows. I do not say, It knows the whole; but what it knows, it knows wholly. So when it knows something about itself, which it cannot do except as a whole, it knows the whole of itself. But it knows itself to be knowing something, and it cannot know anything except as a whole. It knows the whole of itself, therefore.

Then, what of itself is so known to it as that it lives? But it cannot both be a mind and not live, since it has, moreover, the power to understand: for the souls of beasts also live, but do not understand. Just as, then, the whole mind is mind, so it lives wholly. But it knows that it lives. It knows the whole of itself, therefore. Finally, when the mind seeks *to know itself*,[^13] it already knows itself to be a mind: otherwise it does not know whether it is seeking itself, and perhaps seeks one thing for another. For it can happen that it is not itself a mind, and so, while it seeks to know the mind, it does not seek itself. Wherefore, since when the mind seeks what mind is, it knows that it seeks itself, it surely knows that it itself is a mind. Further, if it knows this about itself, that it is a mind, and is wholly a mind, it knows the whole of itself.

But see, suppose it did not know itself to be a mind, and, when it seeks itself, knew only this much, that it seeks itself. For even so it can seek one thing for another, if it does not know this: but that it may not seek one thing for another, it without doubt knows what it seeks. And if it knows what it seeks, and seeks itself, it surely knows itself. What, then, does it still seek? But if in part it knows itself, and in part still seeks itself,[^14] it seeks not itself, but a part of itself. For when it is called *itself*, the whole is meant.

Then, since it knows that it has not yet found the whole of itself by itself, it knows how great the whole is. And so it seeks what is lacking, as we are wont to seek, so that what has slipped away may come into the mind, and yet has not entirely slipped away; because it can be recognized, when it comes, to be the very thing that was sought.

But how does the mind come into the mind, as if the mind could not be in the mind? Add to this, that if, a part having been found, it does not seek the whole of itself, yet it seeks itself as a whole. The whole, then, is present to itself, and there is nothing further to be sought: for it is what is sought that is lacking, not the one who seeks. So when it seeks the whole of itself, nothing of it is lacking.

Or if it does not seek the whole of itself, but the part that has been found seeks the part that has not yet been found; then the mind does not seek itself, no part of which seeks itself. For the part that has been found does not seek itself: and the part that has not yet been found does not seek itself either, since it is sought by the part that has already been found. Wherefore, since neither the whole mind seeks itself, nor any part of it seeks itself, the mind does not seek itself at all.

## Why the soul is commanded to know itself. To know is one thing, to think is another. Hence the errors of the mind concerning itself.

**5.** 7. Why, then, is it commanded that it know itself? I believe, that it may think of itself and live according to its nature, that is, that it may seek to be ordered according to its nature, namely, under him to whom it ought to be subjected, above those things to which it ought to be preferred; under him by whom it ought to be ruled, above those which it ought to rule. For it does many things through a depraved desire, as though it had forgotten itself.

For it sees certain inwardly beautiful things, in that more excellent nature which is God: and whereas it ought to stand fast that it may enjoy them, wishing to attribute them to itself, and to be of itself what he is, not from him and like him, it turns away from him, and is moved and slips into less and less, which is thought to be more and more; because neither does it itself suffice for itself, nor does anything suffice for it when it withdraws from him who alone suffices:[^15] and therefore, through want and difficulty, it becomes too intent on its own actions and the restless pleasures it gathers through them; and so, by the desire of acquiring knowledge from those things that are outside, whose known kind it loves and feels can be lost unless held by expended care, it loses its security, and thinks of itself the less, the more secure it is that it cannot lose itself.

Thus, since it is one thing not to know oneself, another not to think of oneself (for we do not say that one skilled in many branches of learning is ignorant of grammar, when he is not thinking of it, because he is then thinking of the art of medicine): since, then, it is one thing not to know oneself, another not to think of oneself, so great is the force of love, that the things which it has long thought of with love, and to which it has clung by the glue of care, it draws along with itself even when it returns, in a way, to thinking of itself.

And because those things are bodies, which it fell in love with outside through the senses of the flesh, and has become entangled with by a kind of long-standing familiarity, and it cannot bring the bodies themselves inward with it, as it were into the region of incorporeal nature, it rolls together their images and seizes them, made within itself out of itself. For it gives something of its own substance to the forming of them: but it keeps something by which it may freely judge of the form of such images, and this is more properly the mind, that is, the rational understanding, which is kept that it may judge. For those parts of the soul which are formed by the likenesses of bodies, we feel that we have in common even with the beasts.

**6.** 8. But the mind errs when it joins itself to these images with so great a love that it even reckons itself to be something of this kind. For it is in a way conformed to them, not by existing as that, but by supposing it: not that it supposes itself to be the image; but that it is altogether that very thing of which it has the image within itself.

For there flourishes in it the judgment of distinguishing the body it leaves outside from the image of it which it carries about with itself: except when these same images are so expressed as to be perceived as though outside, not thought of within, as is wont to happen to those who are asleep, or raving, or in some ecstasy.

## The false opinions of the philosophers concerning the substance of the mind.

**7.** 9. So when it supposes itself to be some such thing, it supposes itself to be a body. And because it is well aware of its own primacy by which it rules the body, hence it came about that some inquired[^16] what part of the body had the greatest power in the body, and reckoned this to be the mind, or indeed the whole soul.[^17] And so some thought the blood, some the brain, some the heart—not as Scripture says, *I will confess to you, Lord, with my whole heart*;[^18] and, *You shall love the Lord your God with your whole heart*:[^19] for this word is drawn, by misuse or transference, from the body to the mind—but they thought that the very particle of the body which we see among the entrails when they are torn apart was the mind. Others believed it to be put together out of the most minute and indivisible little bodies, which they call atoms,[^20] running together and cohering with one another. Others said its substance was air, others fire. Others said it was no substance, because they could conceive of no substance but body, and did not find it to be a body: but they supposed it to be the very tempering of our body or the framing-together of those first elements by which this flesh is, as it were, knit together. And so all these held it to be mortal, because, whether it were a body or some composition of body, it certainly could not abide immortally.[^21]

But those who held its substance to be a certain life by no means corporeal—since they found that life is what animates and gives life to every living body—tried, consequently, to prove that it is also immortal, because life cannot lack life, as each one was able.[^22] For about that fifth body, I know not what, which some, joining it to the four most familiar elements of this world, said the soul to be from,[^23] I do not think we need discourse at length in this place. For either they call that a body which we do, of which one part in a span of place is less than the whole, and they are to be numbered among those who believed the mind to be corporeal: or, if they call either all substance, or all changeable substance, a body, although they know that not all substance is contained in spans of place by some length and breadth and height, there is no need to contend with them about a question of words.

## The error comes from this, that the mind, in thinking of itself, adds to itself something alien.

**7.** 10. In all these opinions, whoever sees that the nature of the mind is both a substance and not corporeal—that is, that it does not occupy a smaller span of place with a smaller part of itself, and a greater with a greater—must at the same time see that those who hold it to be corporeal err, not because the mind is lacking to their knowledge, but because they add things without which they cannot conceive any nature. For without the phantasms of bodies, whatever they are bidden to think of, they reckon to be nothing at all. And therefore let not the mind seek itself as though it were lacking to itself. For what is so present to knowledge as that which is present to the mind? or what is so present to the mind as the mind itself?

Hence too that which is called discovery (*inventio*), if we go back to the origin of the word, what else does it sound like but that to discover is to *come* upon (*in* ... *venire*) what is sought? Therefore the things that come, as it were, of their own accord into the mind are not usually called discoveries, although they can be called known; because we were not tending toward them by seeking, that we might *come upon* them, that is, *discover* them. Wherefore, just as the things sought with the eyes or with any other bodily sense, it is the mind itself that seeks them (for it is the mind that directs even the sense of the flesh, and it finds when that same sense comes upon the things sought): so other things which it ought to know, not through a bodily sense as go-between, but through itself, it discovers when it comes upon them; either in the higher substance, that is, in God, or in the other parts of the soul, as when it judges of the very images of bodies; for within, in the soul, it discovers them, impressed through the body.

## How the mind inquires after itself.

**8.** 11. How, then, the mind seeks and finds itself is a wonderful question: where it tends in order to seek, or where it comes in order to find. For what is so in the mind as the mind is? But because it is among the things that it thinks of with love, and has grown accustomed, with love, to sensible, that is, bodily things, it is unable to be in itself without the images of them.

Hence arises in it the disgrace of error, while it cannot separate from itself the images of things sensed, so as to see itself alone. For they have wondrously clung to it by the glue of love: and this is its uncleanness, since, while it strives to think of itself alone, it supposes itself to be that without which it cannot think of itself.

When, therefore, it is commanded to know itself, let it not seek itself as though it had been withdrawn from itself; but let it withdraw that which it has added to itself.[^24] For it is itself more inward, not only than those sensible things which are manifestly outside, but even than the images of them which are in a certain part of the soul, which the beasts too have, although they lack the understanding which is proper to the mind.

Since, then, the mind is inward, it in a way goes out from itself, when it puts forth the affection of love into these, as it were, traces of its many intentions. And these traces are, as it were, impressed on the memory, when the bodily things that are outside are sensed, so that even when these are absent, their images are nonetheless ready at hand to those thinking of them.

Let it, then, know itself,[^25] and not seek itself as though absent, but set upon itself the intention of the will by which it was wandering through other things, and think of itself.

So it will see that it never did not love itself, never was ignorant of itself: but, by loving something else along with itself, it confused itself with that and in a way grew together with it; and so, while it embraces diverse things as though one, it supposed those things to be one which are diverse.

## The mind knows itself by the very fact that it understands the command to know itself.

**9.** 12. Let it not, then, seek to discern itself as though absent, but take care to discern itself as present. Nor let it know itself as though it did not know itself, but distinguish itself from that which it knows to be other.[^26]

For the very thing it hears, *Know yourself*,[^27] how will it take care to do, if it does not know either what *Know* is, or what *Yourself* is? But if it knows both, it knows itself also; because it is not said to the mind, *Know yourself*, in the way it is said, Know the Cherubim and Seraphim: for of those, as absent, we believe according to what is proclaimed, that they are certain heavenly powers. Nor as it is said, Know the will of that man: which is in no way ready at hand for us either to perceive or to understand, except by bodily signs put forth; and that in such a way that we believe rather than understand.[^28] Nor as it is said to a man, See your face: which can be done only in a mirror. For our own face too is absent from our gaze, since it is not there where our gaze can be directed. But when it is said to the mind, *Know yourself*,[^29] by that very stroke by which it understands what is said, *Yourself*, it knows itself; and for no other reason than that it is present to itself. But if it does not understand what is said, it surely does not do it. This, then, is commanded it to do, which, when it understands the command itself, it does.

## Every mind knows three things about itself for certain: that it understands, that it exists, and that it lives.

**10.** 13. Let it not, then, add anything to that by which it knows itself, when it hears that it should know itself. For it certainly knows that the command is spoken to it, namely to itself, which exists, and lives, and understands. But a corpse too exists, and a beast lives: a corpse, however, does not understand, nor does a beast. So, then, it knows that it exists and lives in the way that intelligence exists and lives. When, therefore, for example, the mind supposes itself to be air, it supposes that air understands, yet it knows that it itself understands: but that it is air it does not know, but supposes.

Let it separate what it supposes itself to be, let it discern what it knows itself to be: let there remain to it that of which not even those doubted who supposed the mind to be one body or another.[^30] For not every mind reckons itself to be air, but some fire, some the brain, and others another body, and one another, as I mentioned above: yet all have known that they understand, and exist, and live; but they refer understanding to that which they understand,[^31] while existing and living they refer to themselves.

And no one doubts that neither does anyone understand who does not live, nor does anyone live who does not exist. Therefore, consequently, that which understands also exists and lives, not as a corpse exists which does not live, nor as a soul lives which does not understand, but in a certain proper and likewise more excellent way.

Likewise they know that they will, and they know equally that no one can do this who does not exist and who does not live: and likewise they refer the will itself to something which they will by that will. They know too that they remember; and at the same time they know that no one would remember unless he existed and lived: but we also refer memory itself to something which we remember by it. Of two of these three, then, memory and understanding, the knowledge and science of many things is comprised: will, however, is present, by which we may enjoy them or use them. For we enjoy things known, in which the will, delighted in them for their own sake, comes to rest: but we use those which we refer to something else that is to be enjoyed. Nor is there any other vicious and blameworthy life of human beings than one that uses badly and enjoys badly. But there is no place now for discoursing on this matter.

## One who doubts is alive.

**10.** 14. But since we are dealing with the nature of the mind, let us remove from our consideration all the knowledge that is taken in from without through the senses of the body; and let us attend more diligently to what we have laid down, that all minds know and are certain about themselves. For people have doubted whether the power of living, of remembering, of understanding, of willing, of thinking, of knowing, of judging belongs to air, or to fire, or to the brain, or to the blood, or to atoms, or to some fifth body, I know not what, besides the four familiar elements, or whether the framing-together or tempering of our very flesh has the power to bring these about: and one has tried to affirm this, another that. Yet who would doubt that he lives, and remembers, and understands, and wills, and thinks, and knows, and judges? Since, even if he doubts, he lives: if he doubts, he remembers why he doubts; if he doubts, he understands that he doubts; if he doubts, he wishes to be certain; if he doubts, he thinks; if he doubts, he knows that he does not know; if he doubts, he judges that he ought not rashly to assent. Whoever, then, doubts about anything else, ought not to doubt about all these things: which, if they were not, he could not doubt about anything.

**10.** 15. All these things, those who suppose the mind to be either a body or the composition or tempering of a body wish to be seen as in a subject, so that the substance is air, or fire, or some other body which they suppose the mind to be; but understanding is in this body as its quality: so that the body is the subject, and understanding is in the subject; the subject, namely, being the mind which they reckon to be a body, but in the subject is understanding, or whatever else of those things which we mentioned to be certain to us. Those too hold a like opinion who deny that the mind itself is a body, but say it is the framing-together or tempering of a body. For there is this difference, that the former say that the mind itself is the substance in which subject understanding is: but the latter say that the mind itself is in a subject, namely the body, of which it is the composition or tempering. Whence consequently what else do they reckon understanding to be but in the same subject body?

## When the mind knows itself, it knows its own substance.

**10.** 16. All of whom do not notice that the mind knows itself even when it seeks itself, as we have already shown. But in no way is a thing rightly said to be known while its substance is unknown. Wherefore, when the mind knows itself, it knows its own substance; and when it is certain about itself, it is certain about its own substance. But it is certain about itself, as the things said above prove. And it is in no way certain whether it is air, or fire, or some body, or something of a body. It is, therefore, none of those things:

and that whole which it is bidden to know about itself pertains to this, that it be certain that it is not any of those things about which it is uncertain, and that it be certain that it is that alone which alone it is certain that it is. For thus it thinks of fire or air, and whatever else of a body it thinks of. And it could in no way come about that it should think of that which it itself is in the way it thinks of that which it itself is not. For it thinks of all these things—fire, or air, or this or that body, or that part, or the framing-together and tempering of a body—by an imaginary phantasy; nor is it called all these things, but is said to be some one of them.

But if it were any of these things, it would think of it otherwise than the rest, namely, not by an imaginary figment, as absent things are thought of which have been touched by the sense of the body, whether the things themselves, or some of the same kind; but by a certain inner presence, not feigned but true (for nothing is more present to it than it itself); as it thinks that it lives, and remembers, and understands, and wills.

For it knows these things in itself, and does not imagine them as though it had touched them by sense outside itself, as bodily things are touched. From the thinking of which, if it fashions nothing for itself so as to suppose itself to be some such thing, then whatever remains to it of itself, this alone is what it itself is.

## Memory, understanding, will.

**11.** 17. Setting aside for a little, then, the other things of which the mind is certain about itself, let us consider and treat especially these three: memory, understanding, will.[^32] For in these three it is usual to discern what kind of native talent even little children display. For the more tenaciously and easily a boy remembers, and the more keenly he understands, and the more ardently he applies himself, the more praiseworthy is his talent. But when one inquires about anyone’s learning, it is not asked with what firmness and ease he remembers, or with what acuteness he understands; but what he remembers, and what he understands. And because a praiseworthy soul is considered not only as to how learned it is, but also as to how good it is: not only what it remembers and what it understands, but also what it wills is attended to; not with what burning it wills, but what it wills first, then how much it wills. For then a soul vehemently loving is to be praised, when what it loves is vehemently to be loved.

When, therefore, these three are spoken of—native talent, learning, use[^33]—the first of these is considered in those three, what each one is capable of by memory, understanding, will. The second of them is considered, what each one has in memory and understanding, to which he has come by a zealous will. But the third, use, is in the will, which handles the things that are contained in memory and understanding, whether it refers them to something else, or rests, delighted in them as an end. For to use is to take up something into the power of the will: but to enjoy is to use with joy, no longer of hope, but now of the thing itself.[^34] Accordingly, everyone who enjoys, uses: for he takes up something into the power of the will, with the end of delight: but not everyone who uses, enjoys; if he has sought what he takes up into the power of the will not for its own sake, but for the sake of something else.

## They are one essentially, three relatively.

**11.** 18. These three, then, memory, understanding, will, since they are not three lives, but one life; nor three minds, but one mind: consequently they are surely not three substances, but one substance.[^35] For memory, in that it is called life and mind and substance, is said with reference to itself: but in that it is called memory, it is said relatively to something. This I would say also of understanding and of will: for both understanding and will are said with reference to something. But each is life with reference to itself, and mind, and essence. Wherefore these three are one in this, that they are one life, one mind, one essence: and whatever else each is said with reference to itself, even taken together they are said not in the plural, but in the singular. But they are three in this, that they are referred to one another: and if they were not equal, not only each to each, but each to all, they would surely not contain one another. For not only is each contained by each, but all are contained by each. For I remember that I have memory, and understanding, and will; and I understand that I understand, and will, and remember; and I will that I will, and remember, and understand; and I remember together my whole memory, and understanding, and will.[^36] For what of my memory I do not remember is not in my memory. But nothing is so in the memory as memory itself is. I remember the whole, therefore. Likewise, whatever I understand, I know that I understand, and I know that I will whatever I will: but whatever I know, I remember. I remember, therefore, my whole understanding, and my whole will.

Similarly, when I understand these three, I understand them all together. For there is nothing of intelligible things that I do not understand, except what I do not know. But what I do not know, I neither remember nor will. Whatever, then, of intelligible things I do not understand, consequently I neither remember nor will. But whatever of intelligible things I remember and will, I consequently understand. My will too embraces my whole understanding and my whole memory, while I use the whole of what I understand and remember. Wherefore, when by each both each and all are mutually contained, the wholes are each equal to the wholes each, and the wholes each at once to all the wholes; and *these three are one*,[^37] one life, one mind, one essence.

## The mind is an image of the Trinity in its memory, understanding, and will of itself.

**12.** 19. Is it, then, now time to ascend, with whatever powers of attention we may, to that supreme and highest essence, of which the human mind is an unequal image, but nonetheless an image? or are these same three still to be set forth more distinctly in the soul, through those things which we take in from without by the sense of the body, where the knowledge of bodily things is impressed in time? For we found the mind itself, in its memory and understanding and will of its own self, to be such that, since it was grasped to know itself always and always to will itself, it was at the same time grasped also always to remember itself, and always to understand and love itself; although it does not always think of itself as distinct from those things which it is not, which it itself is:

and through this it is difficult to distinguish in it the memory of itself and the understanding of itself. For as though these were not two, but one called by two names, so it appears in that matter where these are very closely joined, and neither is preceded by the other in any time: and love itself is not felt to be there, when no want betrays it, since what is loved is always ready at hand. Wherefore even to the slower these things can become clear, while those are treated which come to the soul in time and which befall it in time, when it remembers what it did not remember before, and when it sees what it did not see before, and when it loves what it did not love before. But this treatment now demands another beginning, on account of the measure of this little book.
