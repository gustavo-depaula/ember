# Revisions I

## Chapter 14 (13): The Advantage of Believing, to Honoratus, one book

**1**. While I was already a priest at Hippo Regius, I wrote the book *The Advantage of Believing* for a friend of mine, whom I knew to have been deceived by the Manicheans and still to be held fast in that error, and to be mocking the discipline of the Catholic faith because people were commanded to believe, but were not taught by some most certain reasoning what the truth was.

In this book I said: *yet in those precepts and commandments of the Law which a Christian is now not permitted to observe, such as the Sabbath, or circumcision, or sacrifices, and anything else of this kind, such great mysteries are contained that every devout person understands there is nothing more harmful than to take whatever is there according to the letter, that is, according to the word, and nothing more wholesome than for it to be revealed by the Spirit. Hence it is said: the letter kills, but the Spirit gives life*[^1].

But I have expounded these words of the Apostle Paul otherwise and, as it seems to me, or rather as the matters themselves make plain, much more fittingly in the book entitled *The Spirit and the Letter*—although this sense too is not to be rejected.

**2**. Likewise I said: *there are two kinds of persons praiseworthy in religion. One is of those who have already found, who must also be judged most blessed; the other is of those who seek most earnestly and most rightly. The first, then, are already in possession itself; the others are on the way, by which nevertheless one most surely arrives*. In these words of mine, if those who have already found—whom we said are already in possession itself—are taken to be most blessed in such a way that they are so not in this life but in the life we hope for and toward which we strive by the way of faith, then this sense contains no error.

For they are to be judged to have found what is to be sought who are already there where we, by seeking and believing—that is, by holding to the way of faith—desire to arrive. But if these people are thought to be, or to have been, such in this life, that does not seem to me to be true—not because nothing true at all can be found in this life that is discerned by the mind and not believed by faith, but because whatever there is of it is only so much that it does not make people most blessed. For what the Apostle says, *now we see through a glass in a dark manner*[^2], and *now I know in part*[^3], is indeed not discerned by the mind; it is plainly discerned, but it does not yet make most blessed. For that does make most blessed which he says: *but then face to face*[^4], and *then I shall know even as I am known*[^5]. Those who have found this are the ones to be said to stand in possession of blessedness, to which the way of faith we hold leads, and to which we desire by believing to arrive. But just who those most blessed are who are already in that possession to which this way leads is a great question. That the holy angels are there, there is no question.

But concerning the holy people already dead, whether at least they are to be said to stand already in that possession is rightly asked. For they have indeed been stripped of the corruptible body by which the soul is weighed down; but they too still *await the redemption of their body*[^6], and their *flesh rests in hope*[^7], not yet shining in the incorruption that is to come. But whether, with respect to contemplating the truth with the eyes of the heart, as it was said, *face to face*[^8], they have anything less on this account, this is not the place to inquire by disputation.

Likewise, what I said—*for to know great and honorable, or even divine, things is most blessed*—we ought to refer to that same blessedness. For in this life, however much of it may be known, it is not yet most blessed, since beyond comparison far more remains unknown of it.

**3**. And what I said—*that it makes a great difference whether something is held by the mind with certain reason, which we call knowing, or is usefully entrusted to those who come after by report or in writing as something to be believed*—and a little later—*what we know, therefore, we owe to reason; what we believe, to authority*—is not to be taken in such a way that in more ordinary speech we should be afraid to say that we know what we believe on the testimony of suitable witnesses.

Strictly speaking, indeed, when we speak we say that we know only what we grasp by the firm reason of the mind. But when we speak in words better suited to common usage, as Sacred Scripture itself also speaks, let us not hesitate to say that we know both what we perceive by the senses of our body and what we believe on the testimony of trustworthy witnesses, provided that we understand what difference lies between these and that.

**4**. Likewise, what I said—*no one doubts that all people are either foolish or wise*—can seem to be contrary to what is read in the third book of *On Free Will*: *as though human nature admitted no middle disposition apart from folly and wisdom*. But that was said there where the question was about the first man, whether he was made wise or foolish or neither, since we could in no way call foolish one who was made without fault, seeing that folly is a great fault; and how we might call wise one who could be led astray was not sufficiently clear. And so for the sake of brevity I chose to say: *as though human nature admitted no middle disposition apart from folly and wisdom*. For I had in view also little children, who, although we confess that they contract original sin, nevertheless we cannot properly call either wise or foolish, since they do not yet exercise free choice either for good or for evil. But here I said that all people are either wise or foolish, meaning to have understood those who already exercise reason, by which they are distinguished from beasts so as to be human beings—just as we say that all people want to be happy. For surely in a statement so true and manifest we need not fear that little children too, who cannot yet want this, are meant.

**5**. In another place, when I had recalled the miracles that the Lord Jesus performed while he was here in the flesh, I added, saying: *why, you ask, do these things not happen now?* And I answered: *because they would not move us unless they were marvelous, and if they were customary, they would not be marvelous*. But I said this because they do not happen now in such number nor all of them—not because none happen even now.

**6**. At the end of the book I said: *but since this discourse of ours has gone on much longer than I thought, let us here make an end of the book, in which I want you to remember that I have not yet begun to refute the Manicheans, and have not yet attacked those trifles of theirs, nor disclosed anything great about the Catholic Church itself, but have wished only to root out of you, if I could, the false opinion about true Christians maliciously or ignorantly instilled in us, and to rouse you toward learning certain great and divine things. Therefore let this volume be as it is; and once your mind has been made more at peace, I shall perhaps be more ready in what remains*.

I did not say these things as though I had as yet written nothing against the Manicheans, or had committed nothing of Catholic doctrine to writing, since so many volumes published earlier on both subjects bear witness that I had not been silent; but in this book written to him I had *not yet begun to refute the Manicheans*, and had *not yet attacked those trifles*, nor disclosed *anything great about the Catholic Church itself*, because I hoped that, this beginning having been made, I would go on to write to him those very things that I had not yet written here.

This book begins thus: *if it seemed to me, Honoratus, to be one and the same thing.*
