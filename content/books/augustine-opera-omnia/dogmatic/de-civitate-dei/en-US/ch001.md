# Letter 212/a

> *Written in 426. Augustine, sending Firmus the books of The City of God along with a short summary of the whole work, instructs him at the same time how they are to be divided and to whom they ought to be given to be copied.*

To my distinguished lord, deservedly honorable and to be welcomed son Firmus, Augustine sends greetings in the Lord.

**1**. The books of *The City of God* that you so eagerly demanded of me I have sent, reread by me as I had promised; and that this should be done my son, your own brother Cyprian, with God's help indeed truly pressed me, just as I wanted to be pressed. There are twenty-two quires, which it is a great deal to gather into a single volume. If you wish two codices to be made, they are to be divided so that one has ten books and the other twelve. For in those ten the vanities of the impious are refuted, while in the rest our religion is set forth and defended—although this was also done in the former where it was more fitting, and the other in the latter.

But if you prefer the volumes to be more than two, then you ought to make five codices. The first would contain the first five books, in which the argument is made against those who contend that the worship not exactly of the gods but of the demons is advantageous for the happiness of this life; the second the next five, which hold that gods—whether such as those or however many—are to be worshiped through sacred rites and sacrifices for the sake of the life to come after death. The other three codices that follow ought to have four books each. For we have divided that part in such a way that four show the rise of that city, as many its progress—or, as we preferred to say, its course—and the last four its appointed ends.

If you are as diligent in reading these books as you were in obtaining them, you will learn how much they help more by your own experience than by my promise. Yet since our brothers there at Carthage do not yet have the ones belonging to this work, which is *The City of God*, I ask that you graciously and gladly grant them to those who ask, to be copied. For you will not give them to many but scarcely to one or two, and they will then give them to the rest. As for your friends—whether among the Christian people they desire to be instructed, or are held by some superstition from which through this labor of ours they will seem able to be freed by the grace of God—you yourself will see how to share them.

For my part, if the Lord wills, I will take care to inquire often through my writings how far you have advanced in reading. It does not escape you, as a learned man, how much the repetition of a reading helps toward understanding what is read. For there is either no difficulty, or certainly very little, in understanding where there is ease in reading, which grows the greater the more it is repeated, so that by perseverance what had been unripe through negligence may ripen.

My distinguished lord, deservedly honorable and to be welcomed son Firmus, as for those books that I composed *On the Academics* at the time of my recent conversion, since you showed in your earlier letter to me that they had come to your notice, I ask you to write back how you came by them. And the short summary I have sent will indicate how much the composition of the twenty-two books has gathered together.
