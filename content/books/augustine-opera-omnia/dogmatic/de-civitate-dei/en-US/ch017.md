# [The progress of the two cities]

## Book XV: What is contained in this book

**1.** 1. Concerning the happiness of paradise, or paradise itself, and the life of the first human beings there, and their sin and punishment, many have thought many things, said many things, committed many things to writing. We too, according to the holy Scriptures, have said in the earlier books what we read in them or could understand from them, in agreement with their authority, about these matters. But if these things are inquired into more minutely, they give birth to manifold and many-sided discussions, which would need to be woven into more volumes than this work and time demand—which we do not have so abundantly that we ought to linger over all the things that the idle and scrupulous can require, more ready to question than capable of understanding.

Yet I think we have now done enough with the great and most difficult questions about the beginning of the world, or of the soul, or of the human race itself, which we have distributed into two kinds: one of those who live according to man, the other of those who live according to God; which we also mystically call two cities, that is, two societies of human beings, of which one is predestined to reign eternally with God, the other to undergo eternal punishment with the devil. But this is their end, of which we must speak afterward[^1].

But now, since enough has been said about their rise, whether in the angels, whose number is unknown to us, or in the two first human beings, it now seems to me that I must take up the course of their progress, from the time when those two began to generate until human beings cease to generate. For this whole time or age, in which the dying give way and the newborn succeed, is the course of these two cities of which we are discussing.

### From Cain and Abel the two cities begin.

**1.** 2. There was born, then, first Cain, of those two parents of the human race, belonging to the city of men, and afterward Abel, to the city of God[^2]. For just as in one man we experience what the Apostle said, that *not first is that which is spiritual, but that which is natural, afterward the spiritual*[^3]—whence each one, since he arises from a condemned stock, must first be from Adam evil and carnal; but if, by being reborn in Christ, he makes progress, he will afterward be good and spiritual: so in the whole human race, when these two cities first began to run their course by being born and dying, the citizen of this age was born first, but afterward the one a pilgrim in this age and belonging to the city of God, predestined by grace, chosen by grace, by grace a pilgrim below, by grace a citizen above.

For as far as concerns himself, he arises from the same mass which is, in its origin, wholly condemned; but God, as a potter (for the Apostle introduces this likeness not shamelessly but prudently), from the same mass made one vessel unto honor, another unto dishonor[^4]. But first the vessel unto dishonor was made, and afterward the other unto honor, because even in that one man, as I have already said, what is reprobate is first—whence we must begin, but where we need not remain—and afterward what is approved, to which by making progress we come, and at which by arriving we abide. Accordingly, not indeed every evil man will become good, yet no one will be good who was not evil; but the sooner anyone is changed for the better, the more he makes the name he attains to be applied to himself the more quickly, and covers the earlier name with the later.

It is written, then, of Cain that he founded a city[^5]; but Abel, as a pilgrim, founded none. For the city of the saints is on high, although here it brings forth citizens in whom it is a pilgrim until the time of its kingdom comes, when it will gather all who rise again in their bodies, when the kingdom promised them will be given, where with their prince, the *king of the ages*, they will reign without any end of time.

### From Sarah and Hagar the two cities are prefigured.

**2.** A certain shadow, indeed, of this city, and a prophetic image serving to signify rather than to present it, appeared on earth at the time when it had to be shown forth, and was itself also called the holy city, by the merit of the signifying image, not of the expressed truth, as it will be.

Concerning this serving image, and that free city which it signifies, the Apostle speaks thus to the Galatians, *tell me*, he says, *you who wish to be under the law, have you not heard the law? For it is written that Abraham had two sons, one of the slave woman and one of the free. But he indeed who was of the slave woman was born according to the flesh; but he who was of the free, by promise. Which things are an allegory. For these are the two testaments: one indeed from Mount Sinai, bringing forth unto bondage, which is Hagar; for Sinai is a mountain in Arabia, which is joined to that which now is Jerusalem; for it serves with its children. But that Jerusalem which is above is free, which is our mother. For it is written: rejoice, you barren, who do not bear; break forth and cry, you who do not travail; for many are the children of the desolate, more than of her who has a husband*[^6]. *But we, brethren, like Isaac, are children of promise. But as then, he who was born according to the flesh persecuted him who was according to the spirit, so also now. But what does the Scripture say? Cast out the slave woman and her son; for the son of the slave woman shall not be heir with the son of the free*[^7]. *But we, brethren, are not children of the slave woman, but of the free, with which freedom Christ has freed us*[^8].

This form of understanding, descending from apostolic authority, opens to us a way in which we ought to receive the Scriptures of the two Testaments, the Old and the New. For a certain part of the earthly city was made an image of the heavenly city, not signifying itself, but the other, and therefore serving. For it was instituted not for its own sake, but for the sake of signifying another, and, another signification preceding, it too, prefiguring, was prefigured. For Hagar, the slave woman of Sarah, and her son were a certain image of this image; and since the shadows were to pass away when the light came, therefore the free woman Sarah, who signified the free city—which that shadow too, in another way to be signified, served—said, *cast out the slave woman and her son; for the son of the slave woman shall not be heir with my son Isaac*[^9], which the Apostle renders, *with the son of the free*.

### [The two cities, the one born of nature and the one born of grace]

We find, then, in the earthly city two forms: one demonstrating its own presence, the other by its presence serving to signify the heavenly city. But the citizens of the earthly city are brought forth by nature vitiated by sin, while the citizens of the heavenly city are brought forth by grace freeing nature from sin; whence the former are called *vessels of wrath*, the latter *vessels of mercy*[^10]. This was signified also in the two sons of Abraham: that one, Ishmael, was born according to the flesh of the slave woman, who was called Hagar, but the other, Isaac, was born of the free woman Sarah according to promise. Each indeed was of the seed of Abraham; but the one custom, demonstrating nature, begot, while the other promise, signifying grace, gave; there the human use is shown, here the divine benefit is commended.

**3.** For Sarah was barren, and, in despair of offspring, desiring to have at least from her slave woman what she saw she could not have from herself, gave her to her husband to be impregnated—by whom she had wished, but had not been able, to bear. And so even thus she exacted her due from her husband, using her own right in another's womb[^11]. Ishmael, then, was born as men are born, by the mingling of both sexes, by the usual law of nature.

Therefore it was said, *according to the flesh*[^12]—not that these are not benefits of God, or that God does not work them, whose artificer Wisdom *reaches*, as it is written, *from end to end mightily and disposes all things sweetly*[^13]; but where it had to be signified that God's gift, which grace bestows freely as undue to men, the son had to be given in such a way as was not owed to the courses of nature.

For nature already denies children to such a union of male and female as that of Abraham and Sarah could be at that age, with the woman's barrenness also added, who could not bear even then, when it was not age that was lacking to fecundity, but fecundity to age.

That, then, the fruit of posterity was not owed to a nature so disposed signifies that the nature of the human race, vitiated by sin and on this account justly condemned, deserved no true happiness in the future. Rightly, then, Isaac, born by promise, signifies the children of grace, the citizens of the free city, companions of eternal peace, where there is not love of one's own and, in a manner, private will, but a love rejoicing in the common and likewise unchangeable good, and making one heart out of many—that is, a perfectly harmonious obedience of charity.

### In the earthly city peace is mingled with war and victories.

**4.** The earthly city, moreover, which will not be everlasting (for it will no longer be a city when it has been condemned to the extreme punishment), has its good here, in whose fellowship it rejoices, such as the joy of such things can be. And since it is not such a good as to cause no straits to its lovers, therefore this city is for the most part divided against itself by litigating, warring, and fighting, and by seeking either deadly or at least mortal victories. For from whatever part of itself it rises in war against another part of itself, it seeks to be victor over nations, while it is captive to vices; and if, when it has conquered, it is the more proudly exalted, the victory is even deadly; but if, considering its condition and the common chances, it is more anxious over the adversities that can befall than puffed up by the prosperity that has come, this victory is only mortal. For it will not be able always to dominate by abiding over those whom it could subjugate by conquering.

But it is not rightly said that the things this city desires are not goods, since it too is better in its own human kind. For it desires a certain earthly peace for the sake of the lowest things; for it longs to arrive at this peace by warring, since, if it conquers and there is no one to resist, there will be peace, which the mutually opposing parties did not have, contending in unhappy want for those things which they could not have together. This peace toilsome wars seek, this peace what is thought a glorious victory obtains.

But when those conquer who were fighting for a juster cause, who would doubt that the victory is to be rejoiced over and a desirable peace has come about? These are goods, and without doubt gifts of God. But if, the better goods being neglected, which pertain to the city above, where there will be victory in an eternal and supreme secure peace, these goods are so desired that they are believed either to be the only goods, or are loved more than those which are believed better: misery must follow, and that which was already present must be increased.

**5.** The first founder of the earthly city, then, was a fratricide; for, overcome by envy, he killed his brother, a citizen of the eternal city, a pilgrim on this earth[^14]. Whence it is not to be wondered at that, so long afterward, in the founding of that city which was to be the head of this earthly city of which we speak, and to reign over so many nations, a certain image of its kind answered to this first example and, as the Greeks call it, archetype. For there too, as a certain poet recalled the crime itself, *the first walls were drenched with a brother's blood*[^15]. For so was Rome founded, when Roman history testifies that Remus was killed by his brother Romulus[^16]; except that these were both citizens of the earthly city. Both sought glory from the founding of the Roman commonwealth; but both could not have as much of it as if there were only one. For he who wished to glory in dominating would surely dominate less if his power were diminished by a partner with him. In order, then, that one might have the whole dominion, the partner was removed, and by crime that grew worse which by innocence would have been less and better.

But these brothers Cain and Abel did not both have between them a like desire of earthly things, nor did the one who killed the other envy him in this, that his dominion would become straiter if both ruled (for Abel did not seek dominion in that city which was being founded by his brother), but it was that diabolical envy by which the evil envy the good for no other cause than because they are good and the others evil. For in no way is the possession of goodness made less by a partner coming or remaining; rather the possession is goodness, which the undivided love of partners possesses the more widely the more harmoniously. In short, he will not have this possession who is unwilling to have it in common, and he will find it the more ample the more he can love a partner there.

### [One city against the other, of the flesh against the spirit]

That, then, which arose between Remus and Romulus shows how the earthly city is divided against itself; but that which arose between Cain and Abel demonstrated the enmities between the two cities themselves, of God and of men. The evil, then, fight among themselves; likewise the evil and the good fight among themselves; but the good and the good, if they are perfect, cannot fight among themselves. But those making progress and not yet perfect can fight in such a way that any good man fights against another from that part by which he also fights against himself;

for indeed in one man *the flesh lusts against the spirit and the spirit against the flesh*[^17]. The spiritual desire of one, then, can fight against the carnal desire of another, or the carnal desire of one against the spiritual of another, as the good and the evil fight among themselves; or indeed the carnal desires themselves of two good men, not yet perfect, fight among themselves, as the evil and the evil fight among themselves, until the soundness of those who are being cured is brought to the final victory.

**6.** For this languor—that is, that disobedience of which we discoursed in the fourteenth book—is the punishment of the first disobedience, and therefore not nature but vice; on account of which it is said to the good making progress and living from faith in this pilgrimage, *bear one another's burdens, and so you will fulfill the law of Christ*[^18]; likewise it is said elsewhere, *rebuke the unquiet, console the fainthearted, support the weak, be patient toward all; see that no one render evil for evil to anyone*[^19];

likewise in another place, *if a man be overtaken in any fault, you who are spiritual instruct such a one in the spirit of meekness, considering yourself, lest you also be tempted*[^20]; and elsewhere, *let not the sun go down upon your anger*[^21]; and in the Gospel, *if your brother sin against you, rebuke him between you and him*[^22]; likewise concerning sins in which the offense of many is guarded against, the Apostle says, *those who sin, reprove before all, that the rest may have fear*[^23].

On this account many things are commanded too about giving pardon to one another and with great care for the sake of holding peace, without which no one will be able to see God[^24]; whence that terror, when the servant is commanded to repay the debts of ten thousand talents which had been remitted to him, because he did not remit the debt of a hundred denarii to his fellow servant; which likeness having been set forth, the Lord Jesus added and said, *so also will my heavenly Father do to you, if you do not each forgive his brother from your hearts*[^25]. In this way the citizens of the city of God, pilgrims on this earth and sighing for the peace of the heavenly fatherland, are cured.

But the Holy Spirit works within, that the medicine which is applied without may avail something. Otherwise, even if God himself, using a creature subject to him, should address the human senses in some human form—whether these senses of the body or those most like these which we have in dreams—and should not rule and move the mind by inner grace, all preaching of the truth profits a man nothing.

But God does this, discerning the *vessels of mercy* from the *vessels of wrath*[^26] by a dispensation that he himself knows, very hidden, but nevertheless just. For with his help, in marvelous and latent ways, when the sin that dwells in our members—which is rather already the punishment of sin—does not, as the Apostle prescribes, reign in our mortal body to obey its desires, nor do we present our members as weapons of iniquity to it[^27], it is turned to the mind, which does not consent to it for evil, God ruling; and the mind will now hold it more tranquilly, and afterward, soundness being perfected and immortality received, man will reign without any sin in eternal peace.

### Led by envy, Cain sinned.

**7.** 1. But this very thing which, as we could, we have set forth—when God had spoken to Cain in that manner in which, as their companion, he spoke with the first human beings through a subject creature in a fitting form—what did it profit him? Did he not fulfill the crime conceived in killing his brother even after the word of divine admonition?

For when he had distinguished the sacrifices of both, regarding that of the one, despising that of the other—which it is not to be doubted could be known by some attesting visible sign—and had done this because the works of the one were evil, but those of his brother good: Cain was greatly saddened, and his countenance fell. For so it is written, *and the Lord said to Cain: why have you become sad, and why has your countenance fallen? Have you not sinned, if you offer rightly but do not rightly divide? Be at rest; for its turning is toward you, and you shall rule over it*[^28].

In this admonition or warning which God uttered to Cain, that indeed which was said, *have you not sinned, if you offer rightly but do not rightly divide?*—because it does not shine clear why or whence it was said—its obscurity has given birth to many meanings, while each interpreter of the divine Scriptures tries to expound it according to the rule of faith. For a sacrifice is rightly offered when it is offered to the true God, to whom alone sacrifice is to be made. But it is not rightly divided when the places or times or the very things which are offered, or he who offers and he to whom it is offered, or those to whom what has been offered is distributed for eating, are not rightly discerned—so that here we understand by *division* discernment;

whether when it is offered where it ought not, or what ought not there but elsewhere, or when it is offered when it ought not, or what ought not then but at another time, or when there is offered what nowhere and never at all ought to be offered,

or when a man keeps for himself the choicer things of the same kind than are those which he offers to God, or when one becomes a partaker of the thing that has been offered who is profane or anyone whom it is not lawful to become so.

But in which of these Cain displeased God cannot easily be found out. But since the apostle John, when he was speaking of these brothers, says, *not as Cain, who was of the evil one and killed his brother; and for what cause did he kill him? Because his own works were evil, but his brother's just*[^29]: it is given to understand that God did not regard his gift for this reason, that by this very thing he was dividing badly, giving to God something of his own, but to himself, himself. This all do who, following not God's will but their own—that is, living not with a right but a perverse heart—nevertheless offer God a gift by which they think he is bought off, so that he may help them not in healing their depraved desires but in fulfilling them.

And this is proper to the earthly city, to worship God or gods, that by their help it may reign in earthly victories and peace, not from the love of taking counsel, but from the desire of dominating. For the good use the world for this, that they may enjoy God; but the evil, on the contrary, wish to use God, that they may enjoy the world—those, at least, who already believe either that he exists or that he cares for human affairs. For far worse are those who do not believe even this.

When Cain had learned, then, that God had regarded his brother's sacrifice but not his own, he ought, being changed, to have imitated his good brother, not, being elated, to have rivaled him. But he was saddened, and his countenance fell. This sin God especially reproves—sadness over another's goodness, and that of a brother. For in reproving this he questioned him, saying, *why have you become sad, and why has your countenance fallen?* For because he envied his brother, God saw it and reproved this.

For to men, from whom another's heart is hidden, it could be ambiguous and quite uncertain whether that sadness grieved over his own malice, in which he had learned that he had displeased God, or over his brother's goodness, which pleased God when he looked upon his sacrifice. But God, giving the reason why he was unwilling to accept his offering, so that he might rather deservedly displease himself than his brother undeservedly displease him, showed that, being unjust by not rightly dividing—that is, by not rightly living—and unworthy that his offering should be approved, he was the more unjust in that he hated his just brother without cause.

**7.** 2. Yet he did not dismiss him without a holy, just, and good command, *be at rest*, he says; *for its turning is toward you, and you shall rule over it*. "Of his brother's, perhaps?" Far be it. Of what, then, but of sin? For he had said, *you have sinned*, and then he added, *be at rest; for its turning is toward you, and you shall rule over it*. It can indeed be understood thus, that the turning of sin ought to be toward the man himself, so that he may know he must attribute to none other than himself that he sins.

For this is the wholesome medicine of repentance and a not unfitting petition for pardon, so that, where he says, *for its turning is toward you*, we do not supply "shall be," but "let it be"—in the manner, namely, of one commanding, not of one foretelling. For then will each one rule over sin, if he does not, by defending it, set it before himself, but, by repenting, subject it; otherwise he will also serve it as it dominates, if he provides patronage for it when it comes upon him.

But that by sin may be understood the carnal concupiscence itself, of which the Apostle says, *the flesh lusts against the spirit*[^30], among the fruits of which flesh he recalls envy too, by which surely Cain was goaded and kindled to his brother's destruction: it is well to supply "shall be," that is, *for its turning shall be toward you, and you shall rule over it*.

For when that carnal part itself has been stirred, which the Apostle calls *sin*, where he says, *it is not I who work it, but the sin that dwells in me*[^31]—which part of the soul even the philosophers say is vicious, not such as ought to drag the mind, but such as the mind ought to command and restrain by reason from unlawful works—when, then, it has been stirred to commit something wrongly, if one is at rest and obeys the Apostle saying, *nor present your members as weapons of iniquity to sin*[^32], it is turned to the mind, tamed and conquered, so that reason may rule over it as subject.

This God commanded to him who was inflamed with the torches of envy against his brother and desired the removal of him whom he ought to have imitated.

*Be at rest*, he says; hold back your hand from crime, lest sin reign in your mortal body[^33] to obey its desires, and do not present your members as weapons of iniquity to sin. *For its turning is toward you*, while it is not aided by being relaxed, but reined in by being at rest; *and you shall rule over it*, so that, when it is not permitted to work outwardly, it may grow accustomed, under the power of the ruling and benevolent mind, not to be moved even inwardly.

Something of the kind was said in the same divine Book about the woman too, when, after sin, with God questioning and judging, they received the sentences of condemnation—the devil in the serpent, and she and her husband in themselves. For when he had said to her, *multiplying I will multiply your sorrows and your groaning*, and *in sorrows you shall bring forth children*, then he added, *and your turning shall be toward your husband, and he shall rule over you*[^34].

What was said to Cain about sin or about the vicious concupiscence of the flesh, this is said in that place about the sinful woman; where it must be understood that the husband, in ruling his wife, ought to be like the mind ruling the flesh. On account of which the Apostle says, *he who loves his wife loves himself; for no one ever hated his own flesh*[^35].

For these things are to be healed as our own, not to be condemned as another's. But Cain received that command of God as a transgressor. For, the vice of envy prevailing, he lay in wait for his brother and killed him. Such was the founder of the earthly city. But how he also signified the Jews, by whom Christ was killed, the shepherd of the sheep of men—whom the shepherd of the sheep of cattle, Abel, prefigured—since the matter is in a prophetic allegory, I forbear now to say, and I recall that I have said some things on this in Answer to Faustus the Manichee.

### Cain founded the first city . . .

**8.** 1. But now the history seems to me to need defending, lest the Scripture be thought incredible which says that a city was built by one man at that time when there appear to have been not more than four, or rather three, men on earth, after one brother killed another—that is, the first man, the father of all, and Cain himself, and his son Enoch, from whose name that city was named. But those whom this moves consider too little that the writer of this sacred history did not have to name all the human beings who could then have existed, but only those whom the plan of the work undertaken required. For the purpose of that writer, through whom the Holy Spirit was doing this, was, through the successions of certain generations propagated from one man, to come to Abraham, and then from his seed to the people of God, in whom, distinct from the other nations, there should be prefigured and foretold all the things that were foreseen in the Spirit as to come concerning the city whose kingdom will be eternal, and concerning its king and likewise founder, Christ; in such a way that the other society of human beings too should not be passed over in silence, which we call the earthly city, as far as was enough for recalling it, that the city of God might shine the clearer even by comparison with its adversary.

Since, then, divine Scripture, where it also recalls the number of years that those human beings lived, so concludes as to say of him of whom it was speaking, *and he begot sons and daughters, and all the days* of this man or that, which he lived, were so many years, *and he died*[^36]: must we not, because it does not name those same sons and daughters, nevertheless understand that through so many years, during which they then lived in this first age of the world, very many human beings could be born, by whose gatherings even very many cities could be founded?

But it pertained to God, by whose inspiration these things were written, first to arrange and distinguish these two societies in their diverse generations, so that the generations of men—that is, of those living according to man—should be woven together separately, and separately those of the children of God—that is, of human beings living according to God—up to the flood, where the separation and the mingling of both societies is narrated;

the separation, indeed, in that the generations of both are recalled separately—of the one, the fratricide Cain, of the other, of him who was called Seth (for he too had been born of Adam in place of the one whom his brother killed); but the mingling, in that, the good declining for the worse, they had all become such that they were to be destroyed by the flood, except one just man, whose name was Noah, and his wife and three sons and as many daughters-in-law, which eight human beings deserved to escape through the ark from that devastation of all mortals.

**8.** 2. That, then, which is written, *and Cain knew his wife, and conceiving she bore Enoch; and he was building a city in the name of his son Enoch*[^37]: it does not indeed follow that he is to be believed to have begotten this as his first son. For this is not to be supposed from the fact that he is said to have known his wife, as though he then first joined himself to her by lying with her. For even of the father of all, Adam, this was said not only then, when Cain was conceived, whom he seems to have had as firstborn; but afterward also the same Scripture says, *Adam knew Eve his wife, and she conceived and bore a son, and he called his name Seth*[^38]. Whence it is understood that this Scripture is wont so to speak, although not always when in it conceptions of human beings are read to have happened, yet not only when the two sexes first mingle with each other. Nor is that a necessary argument that we should think Enoch the firstborn of his father, because that city was named after him. For it is not beside the point that, for some cause, his father, though he had others too, loved him more than the rest. For neither was Judah the firstborn, from whom Judea was named, and the Jews.

But even if this son was born first to the founder of that city, it is not therefore to be supposed that his name was then imposed by the father on the city when he was born, because a city could not then be established by one man—a city which is nothing other than a multitude of human beings bound together by some bond of society; but when that man's family grew to so great a number that it now had the size of a people, then it could surely come about both that he established a city and imposed the name of his firstborn on the established city. For so long was the life of those human beings that, of those mentioned there, whose years too are not passed over in silence, the one who lived least before the flood reached seven hundred and fifty-three[^39]. For most even passed nine hundred years, although no one reached a thousand.

Who, then, would doubt that, in the lifetime of one man, the human race could be multiplied so greatly that there would be whence to establish not one but very many cities? Which can most easily be conjectured from this, that from one Abraham, in not much more than four hundred years, so great a numerousness of the Hebrew nation was begotten that, at the exodus of that people from Egypt, six hundred thousand men of military youth are reported to have existed[^40]—to say nothing of the nation of the Idumeans, not belonging to the people of Israel, whom his brother Esau, grandson of Abraham, begot[^41], and other nations born of the seed of Abraham himself, not begotten through his wife Sarah[^42].

### Then human beings were exceptional in size and age.

**9.** Wherefore no prudent appraiser of things would doubt that Cain could have founded not only some, but even a great city, when the life of mortals was extended to so long a time; unless perhaps some unbeliever should press us with a question from the very numerousness of years, during which it is written in our authorities that human beings then lived, and should deny that this is to be believed. So too they do not believe that the sizes of bodies were far greater then than they are now. Whence their most noble poet Virgil, concerning a huge stone fixed in the boundary of fields, which a strong man of those times, fighting, both snatched and ran with and whirled and hurled, says, *scarcely would twice six chosen men raise it on their necks, such bodies of men as the earth now produces*[^43], signifying that the earth was then wont to produce greater bodies. How much more, then, in the more recent times of the world before that noble and far-famed flood!

But concerning the size of bodies, tombs laid bare by age or by the force of rivers and various accidents very often convince the incredulous, where the bones of the dead, of incredible size, have appeared or fallen out. I myself saw, and not I alone but several with me, on the shore of Utica, a man's molar tooth so huge that, if it were cut up piece by piece into the size of our teeth, it would, it seemed to us, have made a hundred. But I would believe that to have been some giant's. For besides that the bodies of all were then much greater than ours, the giants far surpassed the rest; just as in later times, and our own, such have indeed been rare, but have almost never wholly failed, which greatly exceed the measure of others. Pliny the Second, a most learned man, testifies that, the more the course of the ages passes on, the smaller bodies nature bears[^44]; and he recalls that Homer too often complained of this in his song[^45], not deriding these things as poetic fictions, but receiving them into historical credence as a writer of natural marvels. But, as I said, bones found, very often, since they are long-lasting, betray the sizes of the ancients' bodies even to much later ages[^46].

But the numerousness of years of each human being, which existed in those times, can now come into experimental proof by no such documents. Nor yet is faith on this account to be denied to this sacred history, whose narratives we disbelieve the more shamelessly the more certainly we behold its predictions fulfilled. Yet the same Pliny also says that there is still a nation where men live two hundred years[^47].

If, then, places unknown to us are believed today to have durations of human lives which we have not experienced, why should not times too be believed to have had them? Or is it credible that there is somewhere what is not here, and incredible that there once was what is not now?

### What the number of years was in the generations of the ancients.

**10.** Wherefore, although between the Hebrew manuscripts and ours there seems to be some difference as to the very number of years—for what reason it came about, I do not know—yet it is not so great that they disagree that those human beings were so long-lived. For the first man himself, Adam, before he begot the son who was called Seth, is found to have lived two hundred and thirty years in our manuscripts, but in the Hebrew is reported to have lived a hundred and thirty; but after he begot him, he is read to have lived seven hundred years in ours, but eight hundred in those; and thus in both the sum of the total agrees.

And then, through the following generations, before the one is begotten who is recorded as begotten, the father is found among the Hebrews to have lived a hundred years less; but after he himself was begotten, a hundred fewer are found in ours than among the Hebrews; and thus on both sides the total of the numbers agrees. In the sixth generation the two manuscripts nowhere disagree. But in the seventh, where he who was born, Enoch, is related to have been not dead but translated, because he pleased God, there is the same discrepancy as in the previous five concerning the hundred years before he begot the son who is recorded there, and in the total a like agreement. For before he was translated he lived, according to both manuscripts, three hundred and sixty-five years.

The eighth generation indeed has some diversity, but less and unlike the rest. For Methuselah, whom Enoch begot, before he begot the one who follows in that order, lived, according to the Hebrews, not a hundred less but twenty more years; which again in ours are found added after he begot him, and in both the sum of the whole number comes out the same for itself. In the ninth generation alone—that is, in the years of Lamech, son of Methuselah and father of Noah—the total of the whole differs, but not very much. For he is found to have lived twenty-four years more in the Hebrew than in our manuscripts. For before he begot the son who was called Noah, he has six fewer in the Hebrew than in ours; but after he begot him, thirty more in the same than in ours. Whence, those six being subtracted, twenty-four remain, as was said.

**11.** But through this discrepancy of the Hebrew manuscripts and ours there arises that most famous question, where Methuselah is reckoned to have lived fourteen years after the flood, although Scripture relates that, of all who were then on earth, only eight human beings escaped the destruction of the flood in the ark, among whom Methuselah was not[^48]. For according to our manuscripts Methuselah, before he begot the one he called Lamech, lived a hundred and sixty-seven years; then Lamech himself, before Noah was born of him, lived a hundred and eighty-eight years[^49], which together make three hundred and fifty-five; to these are added the six hundred of Noah, in whose six-hundredth year the flood happened: which make nine hundred and fifty-five from when Methuselah was born up to the year of the flood. But all the years of Methuselah's life are reckoned nine hundred and sixty-nine, because, when he had lived a hundred and sixty-seven years and begotten the son who was called Lamech, after him begotten he lived eight hundred and two years; which all, as we said, make nine hundred and sixty-nine. Whence, nine hundred and fifty-five being subtracted from Methuselah's birth up to the flood, there remain fourteen, in which he is believed to have lived after the flood.

On account of which some suppose that he, although not on earth, where it is established that all flesh which nature does not allow to live in the waters was destroyed, was for some time with his father, who had been translated, and there lived until the flood passed by—being unwilling to derogate from the credit of the manuscripts which the Church has received into a more celebrated authority, and believing that it is the Jews, rather than these manuscripts, that do not have what is true.

For they do not admit that there could rather have been here an error of the translators than a falsehood in that language from which the Scripture itself was translated into ours through the Greek; but they say it is not credible that the seventy translators, who interpreted at one and the same time and with one mind, could have erred, or wished to lie where nothing of theirs was at stake;

but that the Jews, while they envy us that the law and the prophets have passed over to us by translation, have changed certain things in their manuscripts, so that the authority of ours might be diminished.

Let each take this opinion or suspicion as he shall think fit; yet it is certain that Methuselah did not live after the flood, but died in that same year, if what is found about the number of years in the Hebrew manuscripts is true. But what I think about those seventy translators must be inserted more carefully in its own place, when, with the Lord's help, we come to recall the times themselves, as far as the necessity of this work demands[^50]. For the present question it suffices that, according to both manuscripts, the human beings of that age had lives so long that, in the lifetime of one man, who was born first of the two parents whom alone the earth then had, the human race could be multiplied even for the establishing of a city.

**12.** 1. For in no way are those to be heard who think that years were computed otherwise in those times—that is, of such brevity that one of our years is believed to have contained ten of theirs. Wherefore, they say, when anyone hears or reads that someone lived nine hundred years, he ought to understand ninety; for those ten years are one of ours, and ten of ours were a hundred of theirs. And by this, as they think, Adam was twenty-three years old when he begot Seth, and Seth himself was twenty years and six months when Enos was born of him, which Scripture calls two hundred and five years; for, as these whose opinion we are expounding surmise, they divided one year, such as we now have, into ten parts and called those parts years; of which parts each has the square of six, because in six days God perfected his works, that on the seventh he might rest (on which matter I have discussed, as I could, in the eleventh book[^51]); but six times six, which number makes the square of six, is thirty-six days; which, multiplied ten times, come to three hundred and sixty, that is, twelve lunar months. For on account of the five remaining days, by which the solar year is completed, and a quarter of a day, on account of which, taken four times, one day is added in the year they call bissextile, days were afterward added by the ancients, that the number of years might come out right, which days the Romans called intercalary.

Accordingly Enos too, whom Seth begot, was nineteen years old when his son Cainan was born of him, which years Scripture calls a hundred and ninety[^52]. And so on, through all the generations in which the years of human beings are recalled before the flood, scarcely any is found in our manuscripts who begot a son when he was a hundred years old, or below, or even a hundred and twenty, or not much more; but those who begot at the least age are reported to have been a hundred and sixty, and over; because no man of ten years, they say, can beget sons, which number was called a hundred years by those human beings; but at sixteen years there is mature puberty, now fit for begetting offspring, which years those times called a hundred and sixty.

But that it is not incredible that the year was then computed otherwise, they add what is found among very many writers of history: that the Egyptians had a year of four months, the Acarnanians of six months, the Lavinians of thirteen months. Pliny the Second, when he had recalled that it was reported in the records that someone had lived a hundred and fifty-two years, another ten more, others to have had a life of two hundred years, others of three hundred, some even of five hundred, others of six hundred, some even reaching eight hundred, judged that all these had happened from ignorance of the times. *For some*, he says, *determined the year by summer and another by winter, others by four-part seasons, like the Arcadians*, he says, *whose years were of three months*. He added also that the Egyptians, whose small years we said above were of four months, sometimes bounded the year by the end of the moon. *And so among them*, he says, *some are reported to have lived even single thousands of years*[^53].

**12.** 2. By these arguments, as if probable, certain men, not destroying the faith of this sacred history but striving to build it up, lest it be incredible that the ancients are reported to have lived so many years, have persuaded themselves—nor do they think they persuade shamelessly—that so small a space of time was then called a year that ten of those make one of ours, and ten of ours a hundred of theirs. But that this is most false is shown by a most evident proof. Before I produce it, it does not seem to me that I should keep silent about what suspicion may be more credible.

We could certainly refute and convince this assertion from the Hebrew manuscripts, where Adam is found to have been not two hundred and thirty but a hundred and thirty years old when he begot his third son[^54]; which years, if they are thirteen of ours, then without doubt, when he first begot, he was eleven years old, or not much more. Who can generate at this age by that usual law of nature, well known to us?

But let us pass over this one, who perhaps even when he was created could; for it is not credible that he was made as small as our infants are. His son Seth was not two hundred and five, as we read, but a hundred and five, when he begot Enos[^55]; and by this, according to these men, he did not yet have eleven years of age. What shall I say of his son Cainan, who, whereas among us he is found to be a hundred and seventy, is read among the Hebrews to have been seventy when he begot Mahalalel[^56]? What man begets at seven years, if then seventy years was the name for what were seven?

**13.** 1. But when I have said this, immediately it will be brought up that this is a Jewish lie, about which enough has been done above; for the seventy translators, men praiseworthily renowned, could not lie.

Here, if I should ask which is more credible, that the Jewish nation, so far and wide diffused, could conspire by one counsel to write this lie and, while they envy others authority, have taken from themselves the truth; or that seventy men, who were themselves also Jews, set in one place, since the Egyptian king Ptolemy had summoned them to this work, envied the very truth to foreign nations and did this by a shared counsel:

who would not see which is more readily and easily believed?

But far be it that any prudent man should believe either that the Jews, of whatever perversity and malice, could do so much in manuscripts so many and so far and wide dispersed, or that those seventy memorable men shared one counsel about envying the truth to the nations. More credibly, then, one would say that, when these things first began to be copied from the library of Ptolemy, something of the kind could then have happened in one manuscript—the first copied from there, whence it might already spread more widely—where indeed even a copyist's error could have occurred.

But this it is not absurd to suspect in that question about the life of Methuselah, and in that other where, with twenty-four years over, the sum does not agree.

But in those where a similarity of the same erroneousness is continued—so that, before the son who is inserted into the order is begotten, in one place a hundred years are over, in another lacking; but after he is begotten, where they were lacking they are over, where they were over they are lacking, so that the sum agrees; and this is found in the first, second, third, fourth, fifth, seventh generation: the error itself seems to have, if it may be so said, a certain constancy and does not smack of chance, but of design.

**13.** 2. And so that diversity of numbers standing one way in the Greek and Latin manuscripts, another way in the Hebrew, where there is not this evenness, continued through so many generations, of a hundred years first added and afterward subtracted, is to be attributed neither to the malice of the Jews nor to the diligence or prudence of the seventy translators, but to the error of the copyist who first received the manuscript to be copied from the library of the above-mentioned king. For even now, where numbers do not bear upon something that can easily be understood or that appears useful to learn, they are both negligently copied and more negligently corrected. For who would think he must learn how many thousands of men the tribes of Israel could have, each one[^57]? since it is thought to profit nothing; and how many men are there to whom the depth of this usefulness appears?

But here, where through so many connected generations a hundred years in one place are present, in another lacking, and after the son who had to be recorded is born are lacking where they were present, present where they were lacking, so that the sum may agree—no doubt, since he who did this wished to persuade that the ancients lived such very numerous years because they called them very brief, and tried to show this from the maturity of puberty at which sons could fitly be begotten,

and therefore thought that, in those hundred years, ten of ours should be intimated to the incredulous, lest they refuse to receive into belief that human beings lived so long, he added a hundred where he did not find an age fit for begetting sons, and subtracted the same after the sons were begotten, that the sum might agree. For thus he wished to make credible the suitable correspondences of ages fit for begetting offspring, yet so as not to defraud the total ages of each one living of their number.

But that he did not do this in the sixth generation is the very thing that admonishes us the more that he did it when the matter we are speaking of required it, because he did not do it where it did not require. For he found that in the same generation, among the Hebrews, Jared lived, before he begot Enoch, a hundred and sixty-two years[^58], which according to that reckoning of brief years come to sixteen years and a little less than two months; which age is now apt for begetting, and therefore it was not necessary to add a hundred brief years, so that they might become twenty-six of ours, nor after Enoch was born to subtract those which he had not added before he was born. Thus it came about that here there was no variety between the two manuscripts.

But again it moves us, why in the eighth generation, before Lamech was born of Methuselah, whereas among the Hebrews a hundred and eighty-two years are read, twenty fewer are found in our manuscripts, where rather a hundred are wont to be added; and after Lamech is begotten they are restored, to complete the sum, which in the two manuscripts does not differ.

For if he wished a hundred and seventy years to be understood as seventeen, on account of the maturity of puberty, then, as he ought to add nothing, so he ought now to subtract nothing, because he had found an age fit for the generation of sons, on account of which in others he added those hundred years where he did not find it.

But about these twenty years we would deservedly think it could have happened by chance erroneousness, were it not that, as he had first subtracted them, he afterward took care to restore them, so that the integrity might agree with the sum. Or perhaps it is to be thought to have been done more cunningly, so that the design by which a hundred years are first wont to be added and afterward subtracted might be hidden, since even there, where it was not necessary, something of the kind was done—not, indeed, about a hundred years, but about however small a number first subtracted, afterward restored?

But in whatever way this be taken, whether it be believed to have been done so or not believed, whether finally it be so or not so: I would in no way doubt that it is rightly done, that, when something diverse is found in the two manuscripts, since indeed for the faith of the things done both cannot be true, credence should rather be given to that language from which the translation was made into another by translators. For in certain Greek manuscripts too—three—and one Latin and even one Syriac, agreeing among themselves, Methuselah was found to have died six years before the flood.

**14.** 1. Now then let us see in what way it can be evidently shown that the years computed in the life of those most long-lived human beings were not so brief that ten of them were one of ours, but of as great a length as those we now have (which surely the circuit of the sun completes).

For it is written that the flood happened in the six-hundredth year of Noah's life. Why, then, is it read there, *and the water of the flood came upon the earth in the six-hundredth year of Noah's life, in the second month, on the twenty-seventh day of the month*[^59], if that smallest year, ten of which make one of ours, had thirty-six days? For so tiny a year, if it received this name in the ancient manner, either has no months, or its month is three days, that it may have twelve months.

How, then, was it here said, *in the six-hundredth year, in the second month, on the twenty-seventh day of the month*, unless because the months even then were such as they now are? For in what other way would it be said that the flood began on the twenty-seventh day of the second month? Then afterward, at the end of the flood, it is read thus, *and the ark settled in the seventh month, on the twenty-seventh day of the month, upon the mountains of Ararat. But the water was diminishing until the eleventh month; and in the eleventh month, on the first day of the month, the tops of the mountains appeared*[^60].

If, then, the months were such, surely the years too were such as we now have. For those three-day months could not have twenty-seven days. Or if the thirtieth part of three days was then called a day, so that all things are diminished in proportion: then that so great a flood, which is recorded to have happened in forty days and nights, was not made in even four whole days of ours. Who would bear this absurdity and vanity? Therefore let this error be removed, which by a false conjecture wishes so to build up the faith of our Scriptures as to destroy it elsewhere. Quite surely the day even then was as great as it now is, which twenty-four hours determine by a daily and nightly course; the month as great as it now is, which the moon, begun and ended, concludes; the year as great as it now is, which twelve lunar months, with five days and a quarter added on account of the solar course, complete: of which years the six-hundredth of Noah's life had its second month, and of that month the twenty-seventh day, when the flood began, in which forty continuous days of huge rains are recorded[^61]—days that had not two hours and a little more, but twenty-four, passed in day and night. And by this those ancients lived years so great, up to more than nine hundred, as great as those Abraham afterward lived—a hundred and seventy[^62]; after him his son Isaac, a hundred and eighty[^63]; and his son Jacob, nearly a hundred and fifty[^64]; and, after some age intervening, Moses, a hundred and twenty[^65]; and as great as even now human beings live—seventy or eighty or not much more, of whom it was said, *and more of them is labor and sorrow*[^66].

**14.** 2. But that variety of numbers which is found between the Hebrew manuscripts and ours neither disagrees about this long life of the ancients, and if it has anything so diverse that both cannot be true, the faith of the things done is to be sought from that language out of which was translated what we have. And although this opportunity is at hand everywhere among the nations for those who wish it, yet it is not without significance that no one has dared to emend the seventy translators, in the very many places where they seem to say something diverse, from the Hebrew manuscripts.

For that diversity was not thought erroneousness; nor do I in any way think it is to be thought so: but where there is no copyist's error, it must be believed that they, by the divine Spirit, where the sense was consonant with the truth and proclaiming the truth, wished to say something otherwise—not by the office of translators, but by the liberty of prophets.

Whence apostolic authority is found deservedly to use not only the Hebrew but also these very translators, when it adduces testimonies from the Scriptures. But about this I have promised to speak more carefully in a more opportune place, if God will help[^67]; now let me dispatch what is at hand. For it is not to be doubted that, by a man who was born first from the first man, when they lived so long, a city could be established—an earthly one, indeed, not that which is called the city of God, to write about which we have taken into our hands the labor of this so great work.

### How the generations of the two cities are to be counted together.

**15.** 1. Someone, then, will say, "is it so to be believed that a man going to beget children, and not having any purpose of continence, was free from the work of intercourse for a hundred years and more—or, according to the Hebrews, not much less, that is, eighty, seventy, sixty years—or, if he was not free, could beget no offspring?" This question is solved in two ways. For either puberty was so much later in proportion as the length of the whole life was greater; or—what I see to be more credible—the firstborn sons are not here recalled, but those whom the order of succession required, that one might arrive at Noah, from whom again we see that the line was carried to Abraham, and then up to a certain point of time, as far as it was fitting that there be signified, even by the generations recalled, the course of the most glorious city, a pilgrim in this world and seeking the heavenly fatherland.

For what cannot be denied is that Cain was born first of all from the union of male and female. For Adam would not have said, when he was born, what he is read to have said, *I have acquired a man through God*[^68], unless he had been, by being born, the first man added to those two. After him followed Abel, whom the elder brother killed, who first showed a certain prefiguring of the pilgrim city of God: that it would suffer unjust persecutions from the impious and, in a manner, earthborn—that is, from those loving an earthly origin and rejoicing in the earthly happiness of the earthly city. But how many years Adam was when he begot them does not appear.

From there are arranged the generations, some from Cain, others from him whom Adam begot in his succession—the one whom his brother killed—and called his name Seth, saying, as it is written, *for God has raised up for me another seed in place of Abel, whom Cain killed*[^69].

Since, then, these two series of generations, one from Seth, the other from Cain, convey by distinct orders these two cities of which we treat—one heavenly, on pilgrimage on earth, the other earthly, gaping after, or cleaving to, earthly things as if they were the only joys: no one of the progeny of Cain, when it has been numbered, with Adam counted in, up to the eighth generation, was expressed as to how many years he was when he begot the one who is recalled after him. For the Spirit of God was unwilling to mark the times in the generations of the earthly city before the flood, but preferred to do so in those of the heavenly, as though they were more worthy of memory.

Moreover, when Seth was born, the years of his father are indeed not passed over in silence[^70], but he had already begotten others; and who would dare to affirm whether only Cain and Abel? For because only these were named, on account of the orders of generations which had to be recalled, it ought not therefore to seem to follow that only these were then begotten of Adam. For when, with the names of all the rest covered in silence, it is read that he begot sons and daughters: who would presume to assert how numerous this offspring of his was, if he avoids the fault of rashness?

For Adam, divinely admonished, could say, after Seth was born, *for God has raised up for me another seed in place of Abel*, because such a one he was to be as would fulfill that one's holiness, not because he was the first to be born after him in order of time. Then, as for what is written, *and Seth lived two hundred and five years* (or, according to the Hebrews, *a hundred and five years*), *and begot Enos*[^71]: who but an inconsiderate man could assert that this was his firstborn? so that we might wonder, with reason, how through so many years he was free from marriage without any purpose of continence, or, being married, did not beget; since indeed of him too it is read, *and he begot sons and daughters, and all the days of Seth were nine hundred and twelve years, and he died*[^72].

And so on, of those whose years are recalled, it is not kept silent that they begot sons and daughters. And by this it does not appear at all whether the one who is named as begotten was himself the firstborn; nay rather, since it is not credible that those fathers at so long an age were either before puberty or lacked wives or offspring, neither is it credible that those sons of theirs were the first born to them. But since the writer of the sacred history was intent, through the successions of generations with the times marked, on coming to the birth and life of Noah, in whose time the flood happened, he surely recalled not those which were the first to their parents, but those which came into the order of propagation.

**15.** 2. By way of example, that this may be made clearer, I will insert something whereby no one may doubt that what I say could happen. The evangelist Matthew, wishing to commend to memory the generation of the Lord's flesh through the series of parents, beginning from the father Abraham and intending first to come to David, says, *Abraham begot Isaac*; why did he not say Ishmael, whom he begot first? *And Isaac*, he says, *begot Jacob*; why did he not say Esau, who was his firstborn? Because, namely, through them he could not come to David.

Then it follows, *and Jacob begot Judah and his brethren*; was Judah the firstborn? *Judah*, he says, *begot Perez and Zerah*[^73]; nor was either of these twins the firstborn of Judah, but before them he had already begotten three. So he held in the order of generations those through whom he might come to David and thence whither he intended. From which it can be understood that the ancient human beings too, before the flood, were recalled not as firstborn, but as those through whom the order of succeeding generations might be led to the patriarch Noah, lest the obscure and unnecessary question of their late puberty weary us.

### Human beings ought to be bound by the bonds of diverse relationships.

**16.** 1. Since, then, the human race, after the first coupling of the man made from dust and his wife from the man's side, had need of the union of males and females in order to multiply by begetting, and there were no human beings except those who had been born of those two: the men took their sisters as wives; which surely, the more ancient it was, compelled by necessity, the more damnable it afterward became, religion forbidding it. For a most right reckoning of love was had, so that human beings, to whom concord would be useful and honorable, might be bound by the bonds of diverse relationships,

and that one man should not have many relationships in one person, but that they should be scattered singly among single persons, and thus, for binding social life more closely, very many should be held by very many.

For "father" and "father-in-law" are names of two relationships. That each one, then, may have one man as father, another as father-in-law, love extends itself more numerously. But one Adam was compelled to be both to his sons and daughters, when brothers and sisters were joined in marriage. So too Eve his wife was to the children of both sexes both mother-in-law and mother; whereas, if there had been two women, one the mother and the other the mother-in-law, social affection would have bound itself together more copiously.

The sister herself, finally, in that she also became a wife, held two relationships in one; whereas, these being distributed among single persons, so that one was a sister, another a wife, the social kinship would be increased by the number of human beings.

But there was then nothing whence this could be done, when there were no human beings but brothers and sisters from those first two. Therefore it had to be done when it could, that, an abundance existing, wives might thence be taken who were not already sisters, and not only would there be no necessity that it be done, but, if it were done, it would be wickedness.

For if even the grandchildren of the first human beings, who could now take cousins as wives, were joined in marriage to their sisters: there would now be not two but three relationships in one man, which, for binding love by a more numerous kinship, ought singly to be disseminated among single persons.

For one man would be to his own children—that is, to a brother and sister who were spouses—both father and father-in-law and uncle; so too his wife would be to those same common children both mother and aunt and mother-in-law; and their children among themselves would be not only brothers and spouses, but also cousins, since they would be the children of brothers.

But all these relationships, which bound three persons to one man, would bind nine, if they were single in single persons, so that one man might have one as sister, another as wife, another as cousin, one as father, another as uncle, another as father-in-law, one as mother, another as aunt, another as mother-in-law; and thus the social bond would spread itself out, not constricted in fewness, but more widely and numerously, in frequent kinships.

**16.** 2. And we see this so observed, as the human race grew and multiplied, even among the impious worshipers of many false gods, that, even if by perverse laws fraternal marriages are permitted, yet a better custom prefers to abhor that very license, and, although in the first times of the human race it was altogether lawful to take sisters in marriage, it is so shunned as though it could never have been lawful.

For custom avails very much for either alluring or offending human perception; and since in this matter it restrains the immoderation of concupiscence, to transgress and corrupt it is deservedly judged to be wicked. For if it is unjust, out of greed of possessing, to transgress the boundary of fields, how much more unjust is it, out of lust of lying together, to overthrow the boundary of morals!

But we have experienced, in the marriages of cousins even in our own times, how rarely, by custom, that was done which was lawful to be done by law, because divine law did not forbid it and human law had not yet forbidden it—on account of the degree of kinship being next to the fraternal degree. Nevertheless, even what was lawful was shunned, on account of its nearness to the unlawful, and what was done with a cousin seemed almost to be done with a sister; because even these among themselves, on account of so close a consanguinity, are called brothers and sisters and are almost full siblings[^74].

But it was a matter of religious care to the ancient fathers, lest the kinship itself, dividing itself little by little through the orders of descent, should go farther off and cease to be kinship, to bind it again by the bond of marriage while it was not yet far removed, and in a manner to recall it as it fled. Whence, the world being now full of human beings, although they did not love to take as wives sisters born of their father or mother or of both their parents, yet they did love to take wives of their own kindred.

But who would doubt that in this time even the marriages of cousins have more honorably been forbidden? not only according to the things we have discussed, on account of multiplying affinities, lest one person have two relationships when two could have them and the number of kinship be increased; but also because there is somehow, in human shamefacedness, something natural and praiseworthy, that, from her to whom the cause of kinship owes a reverend honor, one should hold back lust, though it be generative—lust at which we see even conjugal chastity itself blush.

**16.** 3. The coupling, then, of male and female, as far as concerns the mortal kind, is a certain seedbed of the city; but the earthly city has need of generation only, while the heavenly has need also of regeneration, that it may escape the harm of generation. But whether there was any, or, if there was, what kind of corporeal and visible sign of regeneration before the flood—as circumcision was afterward commanded to Abraham[^75]—the sacred history is silent. Yet it is not silent that even those most ancient human beings sacrificed to God; which appeared both in the two first brothers[^76], and Noah, after the flood, when he had gone out of the ark, is read to have offered victims to God[^77]. On which matter we have already said in the preceding books that demons, arrogating divinity to themselves and desiring to be believed gods, seek sacrifice for themselves and rejoice in honors of this kind for no other reason than because they know that true sacrifice is owed to the true God[^78].

### What the signification of the two generations is.

**17.** Since, then, Adam was the father of both kinds—that is, both of the line that pertains to the earthly and of the line that pertains to the heavenly city—Abel being killed and in his slaying a marvelous mystery being commended, there came to be two fathers of the individual kinds, Cain and Seth, in whose sons, whom it was fitting to recall, the marks of these two cities in the mortal kind began to shine more evidently.

For Cain begot Enoch, in whose name he founded a city—an earthly one, namely, not a pilgrim in this world, but resting in its temporal peace and happiness. Now Cain is interpreted "possession"; whence it was said, when he was born, either by his father or his mother, *I have acquired a man through God*[^79]. But Enoch means "dedication"[^80]; for here the earthly city is dedicated where it is founded, since here it has the end which it intends and seeks. Moreover Seth is interpreted "resurrection"[^81], and "Enos," his son, is interpreted "man"[^82]; not as Adam. For that name too is interpreted "man"; but it is said to be common, in that language, that is, Hebrew, to male and female.

For so it is written of him, *male and female he made them, and blessed them, and called their name Adam*[^83]. Whence it is not doubted that the woman was so called Eve by her own proper name, yet so that Adam, which is interpreted "man," was the name of both. But Enos is so interpreted "man" that those skilled in that language assert this could not be the name of a woman—as a son, as it were, of "resurrection," where *they neither marry nor take wives*[^84]. For there will be no generation there, when regeneration shall have brought us there.

Wherefore I think this too not to be noted in vain: that in those generations which are propagated from him who was called Seth, although they are said to have begotten sons and daughters, no female there born is expressed by name; but in those which are propagated from Cain, at the very end to which they extend, the last female born is named. For so it is read, *Methushael begot Lamech; and Lamech took to himself two wives, the name of the one Adah and the name of the second Zillah, and Adah bore Jabal; he was the father of those dwelling in tents of herdsmen. And the name of his brother was Jubal; he it was who showed forth the psaltery and the cithara. And Zillah also bore Tubal; and he was a hammerer and a worker in copper and iron. And the sister of Tubal was Naamah*[^85]. Thus far were extended the generations from Cain, which are all from Adam eight, Adam himself counted in—seven, namely, up to Lamech, who was the husband of two wives, and the eighth generation is in his children, among whom a woman too is recalled.

Where it is elegantly signified that the earthly city, up to its end, will have carnal generations, which come from the union of males and females. Whence the wives too of that man who is here named the last father are expressed by their proper names—which, except for Eve, is nowhere found before the flood.

But just as Cain, which is interpreted "possession," the founder of the earthly city, and his son, in whose name it was founded, Enoch, which is interpreted "dedication," indicate that this city has both its beginning and its end earthly, where nothing more is hoped for than can be seen in this age: so Seth, which is interpreted "resurrection," being the father of the generations recalled separately, what this sacred history says of his son is to be considered.

**18.** *And to Seth*, it says, *a son was born, and he called his name Enos; he hoped to call upon the name of the Lord God*[^86]. Surely the attestation of truth cries out. In hope, then, lives the man, the "son of resurrection"; in hope lives, so long as it is a pilgrim here, the city of God, which is begotten from faith in the resurrection of Christ. For from those two men, Abel, which is interpreted "mourning"[^87], and his brother Seth, which is interpreted "resurrection," the death of Christ and his life from the dead are figured. From which faith there is begotten here the city of God, that is, the man who hoped to call upon the name of the Lord God.

*For by hope we were saved*, says the Apostle. *But hope that is seen is not hope. For what a man sees, why does he also hope for? But if we hope for what we do not see, we wait for it through patience*[^88]. For who would think this to be empty of a depth of mystery? Did not Abel hope to call upon the name of the Lord God, whose sacrifice Scripture recalls to have been so acceptable to God? Did not Seth himself hope to call upon the name of the Lord God, of whom it was said, *for God has raised up for me another seed in place of Abel*[^89]? Why, then, is that attributed properly to this man which is understood to be common to all the devout, except because it was fitting that in him, who is recalled as the first to spring from the father of the generations separated into the better part—that is, of the city above—there should be prefigured the man, that is, the society of human beings, which lives not according to man in the matter of earthly happiness, but according to God in the hope of eternal happiness?

Nor was it said, "he hoped in the Lord God"; or, "he called upon the name of the Lord God"; but, *he hoped*, it says, *to call upon the name of the Lord God*. What does this mean for itself, *he hoped to call upon*, except because it is a prophecy that there would arise a people who, according to the election of grace, would call upon the name of the Lord God? This is what the Apostle, said through another prophet[^90], understands of this people belonging to the grace of God, *and it shall be, everyone who calls upon the name of the Lord shall be saved*[^91]. For this very thing which is said, *and he called his name Enos, which is interpreted man*, and then is added, *he hoped to call upon the name of the Lord God*, sufficiently shows that "man" ought not to put hope in himself; for *cursed*, as is read elsewhere, *is everyone who puts his hope in man*[^92],

and on this account not in himself either, that he may be a citizen of that other city which is not dedicated, according to the son of Cain, in this time—that is, in the gliding passage of this mortal age—but in that immortality of everlasting blessedness.

**19.** For this line too, whose father is Seth, has in that generation the name "dedication" which is the seventh from Adam, Adam counted in. For the seventh born from him was Enoch, which is interpreted "dedication." But he is that one who was translated, since he pleased God, and by a notable number in the order of generations, that by which the sabbath was consecrated—namely, the seventh from Adam.

But from the father himself of these generations, which are distinguished from the progeny of Cain—that is, from Seth—he is the sixth; on which day man was made and God consummated all his works. But this translation of Enoch was a prefiguring of the deferral of our dedication. Which indeed has already been done in Christ our head, who so rose again as not to die anymore, but he too was translated;

but there remains another dedication of the whole house, of which Christ himself is the foundation[^93], which is deferred to the end, when there will be a resurrection of all who will die no more. But whether it be called the house of God, or the temple of God, or the city of God, it is the same thing, nor does it disagree with the usage of Latin speech. For Virgil too calls the most imperious city the *house of Assaracus*, meaning the Romans to be understood, who derive their origin from Assaracus through the Trojans[^94]; and the *house of Aeneas* those same ones, because, under his leadership, when the Trojans had come into Italy, Rome was founded by them[^95]. For that poet imitated the sacred writings, in which the now vast people of the Hebrews is called the *house of Jacob*.

**20.** 1. Someone will say, "if the writer of this history intended this in recalling the generations from Adam through his son Seth, namely to come through them to Noah, under whom the flood happened, from whom again the order of the newborn would be woven together, that he might come to Abraham, from whom the evangelist Matthew begins the generations by which he comes to Christ, the eternal king of the city of God: what did he intend in the generations from Cain, and whither did he wish to lead them?" It is answered: up to the flood, by which that whole kind of the earthly city was taken away, but was repaired from the sons of Noah. For this earthly city and society of human beings living according to man cannot fail up to the end of this age, of which the Lord says, *the children of this age generate and are generated*[^96]. But the city of God, on pilgrimage in this age, regeneration leads to another age, whose children neither generate nor are generated.

Here, then, to be generated and to generate is common to both cities; although the city of God has even here many thousands of citizens who abstain from the work of generating; but the other too has them, by a certain imitation, though of those who err. For to it belong even those who, deviating from its faith, have founded diverse heresies; for they live according to man, not according to God.

And the gymnosophists of the Indians, who are reported to philosophize naked in the wildernesses of India, are its citizens, and they hold back from generating[^97]. For this is not a good, except when it is done according to faith in the highest good, which is God. Yet no one is found to have done this before the flood; since indeed even Enoch himself, the seventh from Adam, who is reported to have been translated, not to have died, begot sons and daughters before he was translated; among whom was Methuselah, through whom the order of the generations to be recalled runs on.

**20.** 2. Why, then, is so small a number of successions recalled in the generations from Cain, if they had to be led up to the flood, and there was no long age preceding puberty, which might be free from offspring for a hundred years or more? For if the author of this book did not intend someone to whom he must necessarily lead the series of generations—as in those which come from the seed of Seth he intended to come to Noah, from whom again the necessary order would follow—what need was there to pass over the firstborn sons, that one might come to Lamech, in whose children that weaving is finished, namely in the eighth generation from Adam, the seventh from Cain, as though something were thence to be connected, whence one might come either to the Israelite people, in whom even the earthly Jerusalem offered a prophetic figure to the heavenly city, or to Christ *according to the flesh, who is over all, God blessed forever*[^98], the builder and ruler of the Jerusalem above, when the whole progeny of Cain was destroyed by the flood?

Whence it can seem that in that order of generations the firstborn were recalled. Why, then, are they so few? For they could not be so few up to the flood, the fathers not being free from the office of generating until a puberty of a hundred years, if there was not then, in proportion to that longevity, a late puberty too. For supposing they were equally thirty years old when they began to beget sons, eight times thirty (since there are eight generations with Adam and with those whom Lamech begot) are two hundred and forty years: did they then, throughout all the remaining time up to the flood, not generate?

For what cause, then, would he who wrote these things have been unwilling to recall the generations that follow? For from Adam up to the flood the years are reckoned, according to our manuscripts, two thousand two hundred and sixty-two; but according to the Hebrews one thousand six hundred and fifty-six. Granting, then, that we believe this smaller number to be truer, let two hundred and forty be subtracted from one thousand six hundred and fifty-six years: is it credible that through the one thousand four hundred years and more that remain up to the flood, the progeny of Cain could be free from generations?

**20.** 3. But let him who is moved by this remember that, when I inquired how it is to be believed that those ancient human beings could cease from begetting sons for so many years, this question was solved in two ways: either by the late puberty, in proportion to so long a life, or by the fact that the sons recalled in the generations were not the firstborn, but those through whom one could come to him whom the author of the book intended, as to Noah in the generations of Seth.

Accordingly, in the generations of Cain, if there does not occur one who must be intended, to whom, the firstborn being passed over, one must come through those who are recalled, a late puberty will remain to be understood, so that somewhat after a hundred years they became pubescent and fit for begetting,

so that the order of generations might run through the firstborn and come up to the flood at a number of years of so great a quantity.

Although it can be that, for some more hidden cause which escapes me, this city which we say is earthly was commended by the weaving of generations reaching up to Lamech and his children, and then the writer of the book ceased to recall the rest, which could have existed up to the flood. There can also be this cause why the order of generations was not led through the firstborn, so that it need not be necessary to believe in those human beings so late a puberty: namely, that this same city, which Cain founded in the name of Enoch his son, could reign far and wide and have kings—not several at once, but in their own ages single ones, whom those who reigned would have begotten to succeed them.

The first of these kings could be Cain himself; the second his son Enoch, in whose name the city was founded where it was reigned over; the third Irad, whom Enoch begot; the fourth Mehujael, whom Irad begot; the fifth Methushael, whom Mehujael begot; the sixth Lamech, whom Methushael begot, who is the seventh from Adam through Cain[^99].

But it was not necessary that the firstborn should succeed their reigning fathers in the kingdom, but those whom the merit of reigning, on account of a virtue useful to the earthly city, or some lot, might find, or that he should succeed the father by a certain hereditary right of reigning whom, before the other sons, he had loved.

But the flood could have happened while Lamech was still living and reigning, so that it might find him, with all the other human beings except those who were in the ark, to destroy. For it is not to be wondered at if, with a varied quantity of the number of years interposed through so long an age from Adam up to the flood, each progeny did not have generations of an equal number, but through Cain seven, and through Seth ten; for the seventh from Adam, as I have already said, is Lamech, the tenth Noah; and therefore not one son of Lamech, as in the others above, but several were recalled, because it was uncertain who would have succeeded him after his death, if a time of reigning had remained between himself and the flood.

**20.** 4. But in whatever way the order of generations running down from Cain stands, whether through the firstborn or through kings, this seems to me by no means to be passed over in silence: that, since Lamech was found to be the seventh from Adam, so many children of his were counted in until the number eleven was completed, by which sin is signified. For three sons and one daughter are added. But the wives can signify something else, not this which now seems worthy to be commended. For now we are speaking of the generations; but of those wives, from whom they were begotten, it is kept silent.

Since, then, the law is proclaimed by the number ten, whence comes that memorable decalogue, surely the number eleven, because it transgresses ten, signifies the transgression of the law, and on this account sin. Hence it is that in the tabernacle of testimony, which was, in the journey of the people of God, as it were a walking temple, eleven curtains of goat's hair were commanded to be made[^100]. For in goat's hair there is a recollection of sins, on account of the goats that will be on the left[^101]; for when we confess we prostrate ourselves in goat's hair, as though saying what is written in the Psalm, *and my sin is ever before me*[^102].

The progeny, then, from Adam through the wicked Cain is finished with the number eleven, by which sin is signified; and that number is closed by a woman, from which sex the beginning of sin was made, through which we all die. And it was committed so that the pleasure of the flesh, which resists the spirit, should follow. For the daughter of Lamech herself, Naamah, is interpreted "pleasure"[^103]. But through Seth, from Adam up to Noah, the legitimate number ten is intimated. To which Noah three sons are added, of whom, one having lapsed, two are blessed by their father, so that, the reprobate removed and the approved sons added to the number, even the number twelve is intimated, which is notable both in the number of the patriarchs and in that of the apostles, on account of the parts of seven multiplied one by the other. For three times four, or four times three, make it.

These things being so, I see that it must be considered and recalled how these two progenies, which by distinct generations intimate two cities—one of the earthborn, the other of the regenerate—were afterward so mingled and confused that the whole human race, except eight human beings, deserved to perish by the flood.

### By the two generations the two cities are signified.

**21.** But first it must be observed how, when the generations from Cain were enumerated, with the one in whose name the city was founded—that is, Enoch—recalled before his other descendants, the rest were woven up to that end of which I have spoken, until that race and the whole progeny was destroyed by the flood; but when one son of Seth, Enos, had been recalled[^104], before the rest were added up to the flood, a certain joint is interposed, and it is said, *this is the book of the birth of human beings, in the day God made Adam; to the image of God he made him. Male and female he made them, and blessed them, and called their name Adam, in the day he made them*[^105].

This seems to me to have been interposed for this purpose, that from here the numbering of times might begin again from Adam himself—which he who wrote these things was unwilling to make in the earthly city, as though God so recalled it that he did not count it. But why is there a return here to this recapitulation, after the son of Seth has been recalled, the man who *hoped to call upon the name of the Lord God*[^106], except because it was fitting so to set forth these two cities—one through a homicide up to a homicide (for Lamech too confesses to his two wives that he committed homicide[^107]), the other through him who *hoped to call upon the name of the Lord God*?

For this is the whole and highest business of the city of God, on pilgrimage in this world, in this mortality, which had to be commended through one man whom the "resurrection" of the slain one begot. For that one man is the unity of the whole city above, not yet indeed complete, but to be completed, this prophetic prefiguration being sent before.

Let the son of Cain, then—that is, the son of "possession" (of what but earthly?)—have a name in the earthly city, since it was founded in his name. For these are among those of whom it is sung in the Psalm, *they will call their names upon their lands*[^108]; on account of which there follows them what is written in another Psalm, *Lord, in your city you will reduce their image to nothing*[^109]. But let the son of Seth—that is, the son of "resurrection"—*hope to call upon the name of the Lord God*; for he prefigures that society of human beings which says, *but I, like a fruitful olive in the house of God, have hoped in the mercy of God*[^110]; but let him not seek the empty glories of a famous name on earth; *for blessed is* the *man whose hope is the name of the Lord, and who has not regarded vanities and lying follies*[^111].

The two cities being set forth, then—one in the reality of this age, the other in hope in God—having gone out as if from a common door of mortality, which was opened in Adam, that they might run on and run their course to their own distinct and due ends, the numbering of times begins; in which other generations too are added, a recapitulation being made from Adam, from whose condemned origin, as from one mass handed over to deserved damnation, God makes some vessels of wrath unto dishonor, others vessels of mercy unto honor[^112], rendering to the former what is owed in punishment, bestowing on the latter what is not owed in grace;

so that from the very comparison of the vessels of wrath the city above, which is on pilgrimage on earth, may learn not to trust in the freedom of its own choice, but to *hope to call upon the name of the Lord God*. Since the will in a nature which was made good by the good God, but changeable, because from nothing, can both turn aside from good to do evil—which is done by free choice—and from evil to do good—which is not done without divine help.

### Beauty too is a good, but the lowest.

**22.** With the human race, then, advancing and growing by this free choice of the will, there came to be a mingling and, iniquity being shared, a certain confusion of both cities. And this evil again found its cause from the female sex; not indeed in that manner in which it did at the beginning (for those women did not, by anyone's deceit, persuade the men to sin); but, from the beginning, those who had been of depraved morals in the earthly city—that is, in the society of the earthborn—were loved, on account of the beauty of the body, by the sons of God, the citizens, namely, of that other city, on pilgrimage in this age[^113]. Which beauty is indeed a gift of God; but for this reason he bestows it even on the evil, lest it seem a great good to the good.

And so, the great good, proper to the good, being forsaken, there was a lapse to the lowest good, not proper to the good, but common to good and evil; and thus the sons of God were captivated by love of the daughters of men, and, that they might enjoy them as wives, they sank into the morals of the earthborn society, forsaking the piety which they kept in the holy society.

For so the beauty of the body, made indeed by God, but a temporal, carnal, lowest good, is loved ill, God being set behind, the eternal, inner, everlasting good—just as, justice being forsaken, gold is loved by the avaricious, with no sin of the gold, but of the man. So it is with every creature.

For, since it is good, it can be loved both well and ill: well, namely, with the order kept; ill, with the order disturbed. This I said briefly in verses, in a certain praise of the paschal candle, *these are yours, they are good, because you, who are good, created them. Nothing of ours is in them, except that we sin by loving, the order neglected, instead of you what is created by you*[^114].

But the Creator, if he is truly loved—that is, if he himself, and not something else in place of him which is not himself, is loved—cannot be loved ill. For even love itself is to be loved in an orderly way, by which that is loved well which is to be loved, so that there may be in us the virtue by which we live well. Whence it seems to me that a brief and true definition of virtue is "the order of love"; on account of which, in the holy *Song of Songs*, the bride of Christ, the city of God, sings, *set love in order in me*[^115]. The order of this love, then—that is, of charity and of love—being disturbed, the sons of God neglected God and loved the daughters of men.

By which two names each city is sufficiently distinguished. For those men too were not, by nature, not sons of men; but they had begun to have another name by grace. For in the same Scripture, where the sons of God are said to have loved the daughters of men, they are also called angels of God. Whence many think they were not men, but angels.

**23.** 1. This question, recalled in passing in the third book of this work, we left unsolved: whether angels, since they are spirits, can corporeally have intercourse with women[^116]. For it is written, *who makes his angels spirits*, that is, those who are by nature spirits he makes to be his angels, by enjoining on them the office of announcing. For what in Greek is called ἄγγελος, which name in Latin declension is rendered *angelus*, is interpreted in the Latin tongue "messenger." But whether their bodies he consequently added, by saying, *and his ministers a burning fire*[^117], or whether it means that his ministers ought to burn with love as with a spiritual fire, is ambiguous.

Yet that angels appeared to men in such bodies that they could not only be seen but even touched, the same most truthful Scripture testifies. And since it is a very common report, and many confirm that they have experienced, or have heard from those who experienced it, of whose trustworthiness there is no doubt, that Silvani and Pans, whom they commonly call incubi, have often shown themselves wicked to women and sought and accomplished intercourse with them;

and that certain demons, whom the Gauls call Dusii, assiduously attempt and accomplish this uncleanness, so many and such persons assert, that to deny it would seem impudence: I dare not define anything from this, whether some spirits, embodied with the airy element (for this element, even when it is set in motion by a fan, is felt by the bodily sense and by touch), can also suffer this lust, so as, in whatever way they can, to mingle with women who feel it.

Yet I would believe that the holy angels of God could in no way have so fallen at that time; nor that the apostle Peter said of these, *for if God did not spare the angels that sinned, but, thrusting them down into the prisons of the murky underworld, delivered them to be reserved for punishment in judgment*[^118]; but rather of those who first, apostatizing from God, fell with their prince the devil, who first cast down the first man through envy by serpentine fraud.

But that men too were called angels of God, the same holy Scripture is a most abundant witness. For of John it is written, *behold, I send my angel before your face, who shall prepare your way*[^119], and the prophet Malachi was called an angel by a certain proper grace, that is, one imparted properly to himself[^120].

**23.** 2. But this moves some, that of those who were called angels of God and of the women whom they loved we read to have been born not, as it were, men of our kind, but giants. As if, indeed, bodies of men far exceeding our measure, which I also recalled above, were not born even in our times. Was there not, a few years ago, when the destruction which was done by the Goths to the city of Rome was approaching, at Rome a woman with her father and her mother, who, with a body in a manner gigantic, far surpassed the rest? To see her there was made a marvelous concourse from everywhere. And this was especially a matter of wonder, that both her parents were not even so tall as the tallest men we are accustomed to see.

Giants, then, could be born even before the sons of God, who were also called angels of God, mingled with the daughters of men—that is, of those living according to man—the sons of Seth, namely, with the daughters of Cain. For canonical Scripture too speaks thus, in which book we read these things, whose words are these, *and it came to pass, after men began to be many upon the earth, and daughters were born to them; but the angels of God, seeing the daughters of men, that they were good, took to themselves wives of all whom they chose. And the Lord God said: my spirit shall not remain in these men forever, because they are flesh. But their days shall be a hundred and twenty years. Now there were giants upon the earth in those days, and after that, when the sons of God went in to the daughters of men, and they begot for themselves; those were the giants, men named from of old*[^121].

These words of the divine book sufficiently indicate that there were already giants upon the earth in those days, when the sons of God took as wives the daughters of men, when they loved them as good, that is, beautiful. For it is the custom of this Scripture to call those who are fair in body too "good." But even after this was done, giants were born. For so it says, *now there were giants upon the earth in those days, and after that, when the sons of God went in to the daughters of men*. Therefore both before, in those days, and after that.

But what it says, *and they begot for themselves*, sufficiently shows that before, when the sons of God had not yet fallen thus, they begot for God, not for themselves—that is, not with the lust of intercourse dominating, but with the office of propagating serving;

not the household of their own pride, but citizens of the city of God, to whom, as angels of God, they would announce that they should put their hope in God[^122], like that one who was born of Seth, the son of "resurrection," and *hoped to call upon the name of the Lord God*; in which hope they would be, with their posterity, coheirs of eternal goods and, under God the Father, brothers of his children.

**23.** 3. But that they were not such angels of God as not to be men, as some think, but were without doubt men, Scripture itself declares without any ambiguity. For when it had been premised that *the angels of God, seeing the daughters of men, that they were good, took to themselves wives of all whom they chose*, it was soon added, *and the Lord God said: my spirit shall not remain in these men forever, because they are flesh*[^123]. For by the Spirit of God they had been made angels of God and sons of God, but, declining to lower things, they are called "men" by the name of nature, not of grace; and they are called "flesh" as deserters of the spirit and, by deserting, deserted.

And the Seventy translators indeed called these both *angels of God* and *sons of God*; which indeed not all manuscripts have, for some have only *sons of God*. But Aquila, whom the Jews prefer to the other translators, interpreted not *angels of God*, nor *sons of God*, but *sons of gods*[^124]. But both are true. For they were both sons of God, under whom as Father they were also brothers of their own fathers; and sons of gods, since they were begotten by gods, with whom they themselves too were gods, according to that of the Psalm, *I have said: you are gods, and all of you sons of the Most High*[^125]. For the Seventy translators are deservedly believed to have received the prophetic spirit, so that, if by its authority they changed anything and said it otherwise than it was which they were interpreting, neither would this be doubted to have been said divinely. Although this is reported to be ambiguous in the Hebrew, so that it could be interpreted both *sons of God* and *sons of gods*.

**23.** 4. Let us pass over, then, the fables of those scriptures which are called apocryphal, because their hidden origin was not clear to the fathers, from whom the authority of the truthful Scriptures came down to us by a most certain and most known succession. In these apocryphal writings, even if some truth is found, yet, on account of many false things, there is no canonical authority. We cannot indeed deny that Enoch, the seventh from Adam, wrote some things divinely, since the apostle Jude says this in a canonical letter[^126].

But not without reason are they not in that canon of the Scriptures which was kept in the temple of the Hebrew people by the diligence of succeeding priests, except because, on account of their antiquity, they were judged of suspect trustworthiness, nor could it be found out whether these were what he had written, since they were not produced by such men as would be found to have rightly kept them through the series of succession.

Whence those things which are produced under his name and contain those fables about the giants—that they did not have men as fathers—are rightly judged by the prudent not to be believed to be his; just as many things are produced by heretics under the names of other prophets too, and more recent ones under the names of the apostles, all of which, under the name of apocrypha, have been removed by diligent examination from canonical authority.

Therefore, according to the canonical Hebrew and Christian Scriptures, it is not doubtful that there were many giants before the flood, and that these were citizens of the earthborn society of men; but that the sons of God, who were propagated according to the flesh from Seth, declined into this society, justice being forsaken. Nor is it to be wondered at that even from these giants could be born. For not all were giants, but surely there were then more of them than in the later times after the flood. It pleased the Creator to create them for this reason, that even from this it might be shown that not only the beauties but even the sizes and strengths of bodies are not to be greatly esteemed by the wise man, who is made blessed by spiritual and immortal goods, far better and firmer and proper to the good, not common to good and evil. This thing another prophet commends, saying, *there were those famous giants, who were of stature from the beginning, knowing war. These the Lord did not choose, nor did he give them the way of knowledge; but they perished, because they had no wisdom, they perished through their thoughtlessness*[^127].

**24.** But what God said, *their days shall be a hundred and twenty years*[^128], is not to be taken as though it were foretold that after this human beings would not, by living, pass a hundred and twenty years, since even after the flood we find them to have exceeded five hundred; but it is to be understood that God said this when Noah was about five hundred years old—that is, was passing the four hundred and eightieth year of his life, which Scripture, in its manner, calls five hundred, by the name of the whole most often signifying the greatest part; for in the six-hundredth year of Noah's life, in the second month, the flood happened[^129]; and thus the hundred and twenty years were foretold as the future life of the human beings about to perish, after which, being passed, they would be destroyed by the flood.

### [The flood, Noah, and the ark]

Nor is it believed in vain that the flood happened thus, with there now not being found on earth those who were not worthy to die by such a death as was avenged on the impious; not that such a kind of death does anything to the good, who will die some time, which could harm them after death; nevertheless none of those whom holy Scripture recalls as propagated from the seed of Seth died in the flood. But the cause of the flood is narrated divinely thus, *seeing*, it says, *the Lord God that the wickednesses of men were multiplied upon the earth, and that everyone diligently ponders in his heart upon evil things all the days, God both pondered that he had made man upon the earth, and reconsidered, and God said: I will blot out man, whom I have made, from the face of the earth, from man even to beast and from the creeping things even to the birds of heaven, because I am angry that I made them*[^130].

**25.** The anger of God is not a disturbance of his mind, but a judgment by which punishment is inflicted on sin. But his pondering and reconsidering of things to be changed is an unchangeable reason. For God does not, like man, repent of any deed of his, whose sentence about all things whatsoever is as fixed as his foreknowledge is certain. But if Scripture did not use such words, it would not insinuate itself in a manner more familiarly to every kind of human beings, for whom it wishes to provide—both that it may terrify the proud and rouse the negligent, and exercise the seekers and nourish the understanding; which it would not do, unless it first inclined itself and in a manner descended to those who lie. But that it also announces the destruction of all earthly animals and birds: it utters the greatness of the future calamity, not threatening destruction to living things devoid of reason, as though they too had sinned.

**26.** 1. Now, indeed, that to Noah, a just man and, as the truthful Scripture says of him[^131], perfect in his generation (not, of course, as the citizens of the city of God are to be perfected in that immortality in which they will be made equal to the angels of God[^132], but as they can be perfect in this pilgrimage), God commands that he make an ark,

in which, with his own—that is, his wife, sons, and daughters-in-law—and with the animals which by God's command entered the ark to him[^133], he might be freed from the devastation of the flood: this is without doubt a figure of the city of God on pilgrimage in this age, that is, of the Church, which is made safe through the wood on which hung the *Mediator of God and men, the man Christ Jesus*[^134].

For the very measures of its length and height and breadth signify the human body, in whose truth he was foretold as about to come to men, and came. For the length of the human body, from the crown to the soles, is six times as much as the breadth, which is from one side to the other side, and ten times as much as the height, whose measure is in the side from back to belly; as if you measure a man lying supine or prone, he is six times as long from head to feet as he is broad from right to left or from left to right, and ten times as long as he is high from the ground. Whence the ark was made three hundred cubits in length and fifty in breadth and thirty in height.

And that it received a door in its side is surely that wound, when the side of the crucified one was pierced with a lance[^135]; for by this those who come to him enter, because thence flowed the sacraments by which believers are initiated. And that it is commanded to be made of squared timbers signifies the everywhere-stable life of the saints; for in whatever way you turn a square, it will stand; and the other things which are said in the construction of that same ark are signs of ecclesiastical realities.

**26.** 2. But to pursue these now is long; and we have already done this in the work which we wrote Answer to Faustus the Manichee[^136], who denied that anything about Christ was prophesied in the books of the Hebrews. And it can indeed be that someone may expound these things more aptly than we, or one than another, provided that the things which are said are all referred to this city of God of which we speak, on pilgrimage in this malignant age as in a flood, if he who expounds them does not wish to wander far from the meaning of him who wrote these things.

By way of example, as if someone, what is here written, *you shall make it with lower stories, two-chambered and three-chambered*[^137], should wish to be understood not as I said in that work[^138]—that, because the Church is gathered from all nations, it is called two-chambered on account of the two kinds of men, namely the circumcision and the uncircumcision, whom the Apostle in another manner calls Jews and Greeks[^139]; but three-chambered because all the nations were repaired from the three sons of Noah after the flood—but should say something else which is not alien from the rule of faith.

For since he willed the ark to have dwellings not only in the lower parts, but also in the upper (and these he called two-chambered) and in the upper of the upper (and these he called three-chambered), so that from the bottom upward a third habitation rose: there can here be understood those three things too which the Apostle commends, faith, hope, charity[^140];

there can also, much more fittingly, be understood those three evangelical abundances, thirtyfold, sixtyfold, a hundredfold, so that in the lowest dwells conjugal chastity, above it widowed, and higher than this virginal; and whatever better can be understood and said according to the faith of this city[^141].

This I would say also of the other things which are here to be expounded: that, even if they are discussed in more than one way, they are nevertheless to be brought back to the one concord of the catholic faith.

### The deeds and the significations in the flood are to be sought.

**27.** 1. Yet no one ought to think either that these things were written in vain, or that only the deeds, without any allegorical significations, are here to be sought, or, on the contrary, that these things were not done at all, but are only figures of words, or that, whatever it is, it in no way pertains to the prophecy of the Church. For who, except a man of perverse mind, would contend that books were written emptily which have been guarded through thousands of years with so great a religion and with the observance of so ordered a succession, or that only the deeds are to be looked at there,

where surely, to omit other things, if the numerousness of the animals compelled the ark to be made of so great a size, what compelled that two unclean and seven clean animals be brought in[^142], when both could have been preserved in equal number? Or was God, who commanded them to be preserved for the sake of repairing the kind, not able to restore them in the manner in which he had instituted them?

**27.** 2. But those who contend that they were not done, but are only figures of things to be signified, first suppose that so great a flood could not have happened that the water, by growing, would surpass the highest mountains by fifteen cubits, on account of the summit of Mount Olympus, above which they assert clouds cannot gather, because the heaven is already so high that there is not there that thicker air where winds, mists, and rains are generated; nor do they attend that the earth, the thickest of all the elements, could have been there.

Or do they perhaps deny that the summit of a mountain is earth? Why, then, do they contend that it was permitted for lands to be raised up to those spaces of heaven, and not permitted for waters to be so raised, when these measurers and weighers of the elements assert that waters are higher and lighter than lands? What reason, then, do they bring why the earth, heavier and lower, has invaded the place of the more tranquil heaven through the rolling of so many years, and the water, lighter and higher, was not permitted to do this even for a brief time?

**27.** 3. They say also that the size of that ark could not hold so many kinds of animals in each sex, two of the unclean, seven of the clean. They seem to me to reckon only three hundred cubits of length and fifty of breadth, and not to consider that there was another as much in the upper parts and likewise another as much in the upper of the upper, and on this account those cubits, taken three times, make nine hundred by a hundred and fifty. But if we consider what Origen not inelegantly built up—namely that *Moses, the man of God, learned*, as it is written, *in all the wisdom of the Egyptians*[^143], who loved geometry, could have signified geometric cubits, where they assert that one is worth as much as six of ours—who does not see how great a quantity of things that size could hold?

For their objection that an ark of such great size could not have been put together is a most foolish slander, since they know that vast cities have been built[^144], and they fail to consider the hundred years over which that ark was constructed;

unless perhaps stone can adhere to stone joined by mortar alone, so that a wall is carried around for so many miles, while wood cannot adhere to wood by means of dovetails, pegs, nails, and a binding of pitch, so as to build an ark extending far and wide along not curved but straight lines,

an ark that no effort of men launches into the sea, but that the wave lifts when it comes, by the natural order of weights, and that divine providence rather than human prudence steers as it floats, lest it suffer shipwreck anywhere.

**27.** 4. As for the question that is usually raised with the greatest scrupulousness about the tiniest little creatures—not only such as mice and lizards, but also such as locusts, beetles, and finally flies and fleas—whether there were not in that ark a greater number than the one that was specified when God commanded this, those whom such matters trouble must first be warned that what was said, *that creep upon the earth*[^145], is to be understood in such a way that it was not necessary to preserve in the ark those that can live in the waters, not only submerged, like fish, but also floating on the surface, like many birds.

Then, when it is said, *they shall be male and female*[^146], this is surely understood to be said with a view to restoring the kind; and for this reason it was not necessary that those creatures be there which can be born without mating, from various things or from the decay of things; or, if they were there, as they are accustomed to be in houses, they could have been there without any fixed number;

or if the most sacred mystery that was being enacted, and the figure of so great a thing, could not have been fulfilled even in the truth of the deed otherwise than by having all those creatures there in that fixed number, which could not live in the waters since nature forbids it, this was not the concern of that one man or of those men, but of God.

For Noah did not catch and bring them in, but allowed them to come and enter. For this is the force of what was said, *they shall come in to you*[^147]—not, that is, by a man's act, but by God's bidding; in such a way, indeed, that we must not believe that those creatures which lack sex were there. For it was prescribed and laid down: *they shall be male and female*.

There are indeed some creatures that are born from various things without mating in such a way that they afterward mate and reproduce, like flies; but others in which there is nothing of male and female, like bees.

Moreover, as for those that have sex in such a way that they have no offspring, like mules of either kind, it would be surprising if they were there, rather than that it should have sufficed for their parents to have been there—namely the equine and the asinine kind—and likewise any others that, by the mingling of a different kind, beget some kind of offspring. But if this too pertained to the mystery, they were there. For this kind also has male and female.

**27.** 5. Some are also accustomed to be troubled about the kinds of food that the animals could have had there which are thought to feed on nothing but flesh—whether they were there beyond the appointed number, without transgression of the command, having been forced to be shut in there by the necessity of feeding the others; or whether, as is rather to be believed, there could have been, besides flesh, some foods that would suit them all. For we know how many animals whose food is flesh feed also on grains and fruits, and especially on figs and chestnuts. What wonder is it, then, if that wise and just man, divinely instructed besides as to what was suitable for each, prepared and stored up for each kind a fitting nourishment without flesh?

And what is there that hunger would not compel one to feed on? Or what could God not make pleasant and wholesome, who could even have granted, by his divine ease, that they should live without food, were it not also fitting, for the fulfilling of the figure of so great a mystery, that they should also be fed?

That so manifold signs of the things done pertain to the prefiguring of the Church, no one is permitted to think otherwise, unless he is contentious.

For the nations have already so filled the Church, and the clean and the unclean alike are so held together within a certain framework of her unity, until the appointed end is reached, that from this one most manifest instance it is not lawful to doubt even about the rest, which were said somewhat more obscurely and can be recognized with more difficulty.

Since this is so, no one—not even a hard man—will dare to think either that these things were recorded to no purpose, or that they signify nothing although they were done, or that they were only spoken significantly and not also done, nor can it probably be said that they are foreign to the signifying of the Church;

but rather it is to be believed that they were both wisely committed to memory and to writing, and were done, and signify something, and that the very something pertains to the prefiguring of the Church.

Now that this book has been brought up to this point, it must be closed, so that the course of the two cities—the earthly, that is, which lives according to man, and the heavenly, which lives according to God—may be traced after the flood and thenceforth in the events that follow.
