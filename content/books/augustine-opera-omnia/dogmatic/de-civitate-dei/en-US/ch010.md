# Book VIII: The Religion of Christ to Be Compared with the Platonists

**1.** Now we have need of a far more attentive mind than was required in the solution of the foregoing questions and in the unfolding of the earlier books.

For concerning the theology that they call natural, we must hold our discussion not with any sort of men whatever—for it is not the fabulous or the civil theology, that is, neither the theatrical nor the urban, of which the one parades the crimes of the gods, the other discloses the still more criminal desires of the gods, and on this account those of malignant demons rather than of gods—

but the discussion is to be held with the philosophers, whose very name, if we interpret it in Latin, professes the love of wisdom.

Now if wisdom is God, through whom all things were made, as divine authority and truth have shown, then the true philosopher is a lover of God. But since the reality itself, of which this is the name, is not present in all who glory in this name—for not all who are called philosophers are forthwith lovers of true wisdom—surely from among all those whose opinions we have been able to know through their writings, we must choose those with whom this question may be treated not unworthily.

For in this work I have not undertaken to refute all the empty opinions of all the philosophers, but only those that pertain to theology—by which Greek word we understand to be signified an account or discourse concerning the divinity;

nor those of all who hold it, but only of those who, while they agree that the divinity both exists and cares for human affairs, nevertheless think that the worship of the one unchangeable God does not suffice for attaining a life blessed even after death,

but suppose that many gods, created and established by that one God, are for that reason to be worshiped.

These already surpass even the opinion of Varro by their nearness to the truth; for he was able to extend the whole of natural theology only as far as this world or its soul, whereas these confess, above every nature of the soul, a God who not only made this visible world, which is often called by the name of heaven and earth, but also made every soul whatsoever, and who makes blessed, by participation in his own unchangeable and incorporeal light, the rational and intellectual soul, of which kind is the human soul. That these philosophers were called Platonists, the word being derived from Plato their teacher, no one who has heard of these matters even slightly is ignorant. Concerning this Plato, then, I shall briefly touch on what I judge necessary to the present question, mentioning first those who preceded him in the same kind of letters in time.

## There Were Two Kinds of Philosophers: the Italic and the Ionic

**2.** For as far as concerns Greek letters, whose language is held to be more illustrious than that of the other nations[^1], two kinds of philosophers are handed down:

the one Italic, from that part of Italy which was once called Magna Graecia; the other Ionic, in those lands which even now are called Greece. The Italic kind had as its founder Pythagoras of Samos, by whom they say the very name "philosophy" arose.

For whereas before, those who seemed to excel others by some manner of praiseworthy life were called "sages"[^2], he, when asked what he professed, answered that he was a philosopher, that is, a student or lover of wisdom; for to profess oneself a sage seemed most arrogant[^3].

The first of the Ionic kind, however, was Thales of Miletus, one of those seven who were called the Sages. But those six were distinguished by their manner of life and by certain precepts suited to living well;

this Thales, however, that he might also propagate successors, examined the nature of things, and committing his disputations to writing, became eminent and most admirable above all because, having grasped the calculations of astronomy, he was able even to predict eclipses of the sun and moon.

Yet he thought that water was the first principle of things, and that from it all the elements of the world, and the world itself, and the things begotten in it, came into being. But he set nothing from the divine mind over this work, which we behold as so wonderful when we consider the world.

To him succeeded Anaximander, his hearer, and he changed his opinion concerning the nature of things. For he thought that things are born not from one thing, as Thales held from moisture, but each from its own proper principles.

These principles of individual things he believed to be infinite, and to beget innumerable worlds and whatever arises in them;

and these worlds he reckoned to be now dissolved, now begotten again, according to the length of time each was able to endure; nor did he himself attribute anything to the divine mind in these works of things.

He left as his disciple and successor Anaximenes, who assigned all the causes of things to infinite air, and neither denied nor passed over the gods in silence; yet he believed that the air was not made by them, but that they themselves arose from the air.

Anaxagoras, however, his hearer, perceived and said that a divine mind was the maker of all these things that we see, out of infinite matter, which consisted of particles, like one another, of all things; and that the individual things were made of their own proper particles, but with a divine mind doing the making.

Diogenes too, another hearer of Anaximenes, said that air was indeed the matter of things, out of which all things were made, but that it was endowed with a divine reason, without which nothing could be made from it. To Anaxagoras succeeded his hearer Archelaus.

He too thought that all things were composed of particles like one another, of which each several thing was made, in such a way that he said there was also present in them a mind which, by joining and separating the eternal bodies—that is, those particles—did all things.

Socrates is said to have been his disciple, the teacher of Plato, on whose account I have briefly reviewed all these things.

## Socrates Discoursed on Good Morals

**3.** Socrates, then, is recorded to have been the first to turn the whole of philosophy toward the correcting and ordering of morals, whereas before him all had bestowed their greatest effort on the investigation of physical, that is, natural, things.

It does not seem to me, however, that it can be gathered with clarity whether Socrates did this because, weary of obscure and uncertain matters, he bent his mind to discovering something open and certain that was necessary for the blessed life—for the sake of which alone the industry of all the philosophers seems to have kept watch and labored—or whether, as some more kindly suspect of him, he was unwilling that minds unclean with earthly desires should strain to reach out toward divine things.

For he saw that the causes of things were sought by them which he believed to be first and highest only in the will of the one and highest God;

whence he thought that they could not be grasped except by a purified mind;

and therefore he judged that one must press on to purify one's life by good morals, so that the mind, unburdened of the desires that weigh it down, might raise itself by its natural vigor to eternal things and might behold, by the purity of its understanding, the nature of the incorporeal and unchangeable light, where the causes of all created natures abidingly live[^4].

It is agreed, nonetheless, that he assailed and tossed about the folly of the unlearned, who supposed they knew something even in those very moral questions to which he seemed to have bent his whole mind, whether by confessing his own ignorance or by concealing his knowledge, with a wonderful charm of discourse and the keenest urbanity[^5].

Whence, with enmities stirred up, he was condemned by a slanderous accusation and punished with death[^6]. But afterward the city of the Athenians publicly mourned that very thing which it had publicly condemned, the people's indignation turning against his two accusers so far that one of them perished, overwhelmed by the violence of the multitude, while the other escaped a like penalty by voluntary and perpetual exile[^7].

With so illustrious a fame, then, of life and death, Socrates left very many followers of his philosophy, whose eager endeavor it was to busy themselves in the discussion of moral questions, where it is debated what the highest good is, by which a man can be made blessed.

And because in the disputations of Socrates—where, while he moves all things, he asserts and demolishes—this did not plainly appear, each took from there what pleased him, and where it seemed good to each, there he set up the end of good. Now the "end of good" is so called as that to which, when one has come, he is blessed.

But the Socratics held opinions about this end so diverse among themselves that—what is scarcely credible that the followers of one master could have done—some said the highest good was pleasure, as Aristippus did; others virtue, as Antisthenes did[^8]. So one after another held one opinion after another, whom it would be long to recount.

## What Plato Understood and Taught

**4.** But among the disciples of Socrates, not without merit, Plato shone with a glory so surpassing that it altogether eclipsed the rest.

Although he was an Athenian, born among his own people in an honorable station, and far outstripped his fellow disciples by his wonderful talent, yet, thinking himself and the Socratic teaching too little to suffice for perfecting philosophy, he traveled far and wide, wherever the fame of acquiring some renowned knowledge carried him off.

And so in Egypt he learned whatever was there held great and taught[^9], and coming thence into those parts of Italy where the fame of the Pythagoreans was celebrated, he very easily grasped whatever of Italic philosophy then flourished, having heard the more eminent teachers in it[^10]. And because he singularly loved his master Socrates, making him the speaker in almost all his discourses, he tempered with that man's charm and moral disputations even those things which he had either learned from others or himself seen with whatever understanding he could.

And so, since the pursuit of wisdom is engaged in action and in contemplation, whence one part of it may be called active and the other contemplative—of which the active pertains to the conduct of life, that is, to the establishing of morals, while the contemplative pertains to the beholding of the causes of nature and of the purest truth—

Socrates is recorded to have excelled in the active, while Pythagoras applied himself rather to the contemplative, with such powers of understanding as he could[^11]. Plato, accordingly, is praised for having perfected philosophy by joining the two, which he distributed into three parts: one moral, which is engaged chiefly in action; another natural, which is assigned to contemplation; the third rational, by which the true is marked off from the false[^12].

Although this rational part is necessary to both, that is, to action and to contemplation, yet it is contemplation most of all that claims for itself the perception of truth. Therefore this threefold division is not contrary to that distinction by which we understand that the whole pursuit of wisdom consists in action and contemplation.

But what Plato thought in these or about each of these parts—that is, where he knew or believed the end of all actions to be, where the cause of all natures, where the light of all reasonings—I judge it both long to explain by discoursing, and not a thing I judge should be rashly affirmed. For since he is bent on keeping the well-known manner of his master Socrates, whom he makes the disputant in his volumes, of dissembling his own knowledge or opinion—because that manner pleased Socrates himself—it has come about that even Plato's own views on great matters cannot easily be discerned.

Nevertheless, of those things which are read in him—whether what he himself said, or what he reported and recorded to have been said by others—such as seemed to be his own views ought to be mentioned by us and inserted into this work, both where he supports the true religion, which our faith embraces and defends, and where he seems to be contrary to it, so far as concerns this question about one God and many gods, on account of the life that is to be after death and truly blessed.

For perhaps those who are praised, with a more celebrated fame, for having more keenly and truly understood and followed Plato—far and rightly set before the other philosophers of the nations—hold some such view of God as that in him is found both the cause of subsisting, and the account of understanding, and the order of living;

of which three, the one is understood to pertain to the natural part, the second to the rational, the third to the moral. For if man was so created that through that which excels in him he may attain to that which excels all things—

that is, the one true and best God, without whom no nature subsists, no teaching instructs, no exercise profits—let him be sought, in whom all things are bound together for us; let him be discerned, in whom all things are certain for us; let him be loved, in whom all things are right for us.

## The Religion of Christ Is Fittingly Compared with the Platonists

**5.** If, then, Plato said that the wise man is one who imitates, knows, and loves this God, by participation in whom he is blessed, what need is there to examine the rest?

None have come nearer to us than these. Let there yield to them, therefore, not only that fabulous theology that delights the minds of the impious with the crimes of the gods, nor even only that civil theology, where unclean demons, seducing under the name of gods the peoples given over to earthly joys, wished to have human errors as their own divine honors,

stirring up their worshipers, by most unclean enthusiasms, to watch the plays of their own crimes as though to their worship, and exhibiting to themselves more delightful plays out of the very spectators

(where, if anything as it were honorable is done in the temples, it is befouled by the obscenity of the theaters joined to it, and whatever base things are done in the theaters are praised by comparison with the foulness of the temples joined to them),

and those things which Varro interpreted out of these rites as referring, as it were, to heaven and earth and the seeds and acts of mortal things[^13] (since not even these things which he tries to insinuate are signified by those rites, and therefore the truth does not follow him in his attempt; and even if they were so signified, yet to the rational soul those things which are set in an order of nature below it ought not to be worshiped as its God, nor ought it to set before itself, as gods, those things to which the true God preferred it),

and those things which Numa Pompilius, since they pertained in very truth to rites of this kind, took care to conceal by burying them with himself, and which, dug up by the plow, the senate ordered to be burned[^14]. (Of that kind too are those things—that we may suspect something milder of Numa—

which Alexander of Macedon writes to his mother were disclosed to him by a certain Leo, a great high priest of the Egyptian rites[^15],

where not Picus and Faunus and Aeneas and Romulus, or even Hercules and Aesculapius and Liber the son of Semele and the Tyndarid brothers and any others whom they hold as gods drawn from mortals, but even the gods of the greater nations themselves, whom Cicero in the *Tusculan Disputations* seems to touch upon with their names left unspoken[^16],

Jupiter, Juno, Saturn, Vulcan, Vesta, and very many others, whom Varro tries to transfer to the parts of the world or to the elements, are betrayed to have been men. For that priest too, fearing, and as it were begging that the mysteries he had revealed be kept secret, warns Alexander that, when he has disclosed them in writing to his mother, he should order them to be burned with flames.)

Not only, then, let these things which the two theologies, the fabulous and the civil, contain yield to the Platonist philosophers, who said that the true God is both the author of things, and the illuminator of truth, and the bestower of blessedness;

but also let those other philosophers, who supposed, with minds given over to the body, that the principles of nature were corporeal, yield to these so great men, knowers of so great a God—

such as Thales, who placed it in moisture, Anaximenes in air, the Stoics in fire, Epicurus in atoms, that is, in the most minute little bodies which can neither be divided nor perceived, and whatever others there are, on the enumeration of whom there is no need to linger, who said that bodies—whether simple or compound, whether lacking life or living, but bodies nonetheless—are the cause and principle of things.

For some of them believed that living things could be made from things not living, as the Epicureans did; others, indeed, that both living and nonliving things came from something living, but bodies nonetheless from body[^17]. For the Stoics thought that fire, that is, one body out of these four elements of which this visible world consists, was both living and wise and the fabricator of the world itself and of all things that are in it, and that this fire was altogether God.

These men and the rest like them were able to think only of that which their hearts, bound up with the senses of the flesh, fashioned for them.

For they had within themselves what they did not see, and they imagined within themselves what they had seen outside, even when they were not seeing it but only thinking of it[^18].

But this, in the sight of such thinking, is now not a body but the likeness of a body; while that by which this likeness of a body is seen in the mind is neither a body nor the likeness of a body;

and that by which it is seen, and by which it is judged whether it is beautiful or deformed, is surely better than the thing that is judged. This is the mind of man and the nature of the rational soul, which assuredly is not a body, if that very likeness of a body, when it is beheld and judged in the mind of one thinking, is itself not a body. It is, then, neither earth nor water, neither air nor fire, of which four bodies, called the four elements, we see the corporeal world to be compacted. And further, if our mind is not a body, how is God, the creator of the mind, a body?

Let these too, then, as has been said, yield to the Platonists; and let those also yield who were ashamed to say that God is a body, but yet thought that our souls were of the same nature as he is; thus they were not moved by the great mutability of the soul, which it is impious to attribute to the nature of God.

But they say: the nature of the soul is changed by the body, for in itself it is unchangeable. These men might as well say: the flesh is wounded by some body, for in itself it is invulnerable.

In short, what cannot be changed can be changed by nothing; and therefore what can be changed by a body can be changed by something, and so cannot rightly be called unchangeable.

**6.** These philosophers, then, whom we see set before the rest, not undeservedly, in fame and glory, saw that no body is God, and therefore they transcended all bodies in seeking God.

They saw that whatever is changeable is not the highest God, and therefore they transcended every soul and all changeable spirits in seeking the highest God[^19]. Then they saw that every form in any changeable thing whatever, by which it is whatever it is, in whatever manner and of whatever sort its nature is, can be only from him who truly *is*, because he is unchangeably.

And accordingly, whether it be the body of the whole world, its figures, qualities, and ordered motion, and the elements arranged from heaven down to earth and whatever bodies are in them, or every form of life—whether that which nourishes and sustains, such as is in trees, or that which has this and also feels, such as is in beasts,

or that which has these and also understands, such as is in men, or that which needs no nourishing support but only sustains, feels, and understands, such as is in angels—none of these can be except from him who simply is;

because for him it is not one thing to be and another to live, as though he could be without living; nor for him is it one thing to live and another to understand, as though he could live without understanding; nor for him is it one thing to understand and another to be blessed, as though he could understand without being blessed; but what it is for him to live, to understand, to be blessed, this it is for him to be.

On account of this unchangeableness and simplicity they understood both that he made all these things, and that he himself could be made by none. For they considered that whatever is, is either a body or a life, and that a life is something better than a body, and that the form of a body is sensible, that of a life intelligible. Accordingly they preferred the intelligible form to the sensible.

We call "sensible" those things that can be perceived by the sight and touch of the body; "intelligible" those that can be understood by the gaze of the mind.

For there is no corporeal beauty, whether in the state of a body, such as a figure, or in motion, such as a melody, about which the mind does not judge.

And this it surely could not do unless this form were better in it, without the bulk of mass, without the noise of a voice, without the extent of place or of time.

But there too, unless it were changeable, one would not judge better than another concerning the sensible form;

the more talented better than the duller, the more skilled better than the unskilled, the more practiced better than the less practiced,

and the same one person, as he advances, judges better afterward than before. But what admits of more and less is without doubt changeable.

Whence talented and learned men, practiced in these matters, easily gathered that the first form is not in those things in which it is shown to be changeable[^20].

Since, therefore, in their view both body and soul were more and less beautiful, and yet, if they could be wholly without form, they would be nothing at all,

they saw that there is something in which the first form is unchangeable and therefore not comparable;

and there, most rightly, they believed to be the principle of things, which was not made and from which all things were made.

Thus *what can be known about God* he himself *showed to them, ever since the creation of the world,* when *his invisible nature* was *clearly perceived in the things that have been made; his eternal power and deity* also[^21]; by whom too all visible and temporal things were created.

Let this be said concerning that part which they call physical, that is, natural.

**7.** As for what pertains to doctrine, in which the second part is engaged, which is called by them logical, that is, rational: far be it that there should seem to be comparable to these men those who placed the judgment of truth in the senses of the body and thought that all things which are learned must be measured by their unreliable and deceptive rules,

such as the Epicureans and whatever others of the kind[^22], and even the Stoics themselves, who, although they vehemently loved the skill of disputing which they call dialectic, thought that it must be drawn from the senses of the body,

asserting from this that the mind conceives the notions which they call ἔννοιαι, namely of those things which they unfold by defining; and that from this the whole method of learning and teaching is propagated and connected[^23]. Here I am wont to wonder greatly: since they say that none are beautiful but the wise, with what senses of the body they saw this beauty, with what eyes of the flesh they beheld the form and grace of wisdom.

But these men, whom we deservedly set before the rest, distinguished the things that are beheld by the mind from those that are reached by the senses, neither taking from the senses what they can do, nor granting them beyond what they can do. And they said that the light of minds for learning all things is that same God himself by whom all things were made[^24].

**8.** There remains the moral part, which by the Greek word they call ethics, where the inquiry concerns the highest good, to which we refer all that we do, and which we seek not for the sake of anything else but for its own sake, and which, having attained, we seek nothing further whereby to be blessed. Indeed it is called the end for this reason: that for its sake we will the rest, but it itself only for its own sake.

This beatific good, then, some said belongs to man from the body, some from the mind, some from both[^25].

For they saw that man himself consists of soul and body, and therefore they believed that it could be well with him from one or the other of these two, or from both, by a certain final good whereby they might be blessed, to which they might refer all that they did, and beyond which they should not seek that to which it was to be referred.

Whence those who are said to have added a third kind of goods, which is called external—such as honor, glory, money, and anything of the kind—did not add it so as to be final, that is, to be sought for its own sake, but for the sake of something else; and they held this kind to be good for the good, but evil for the evil[^26].

Thus those who sought the good of man either from the soul or from the body or from both thought it must be sought from nothing other than man; but those who sought it from the body, from the worse part of man; those who sought it from the soul, from the better part; and those who sought it from both, from the whole man.

Whether, then, from any part whatever, or from the whole, it is from nothing other than man. Nor did these differences, because they are three, make only three, but many disagreements and sects of philosophers, because concerning the good of the body, and the good of the soul, and the good of both, different men held different opinions.

Let them all, therefore, yield to those philosophers who said that a man is blessed not in enjoying the body, nor in enjoying the soul, but in enjoying God;

not as the soul enjoys the body or itself, nor as friend enjoys friend, but as the eye enjoys the light—if anything of likeness is to be brought from these to those—what sort of likeness this is will appear, if God himself help, in another place, so far as we can manage it.

For now let it suffice to mention that Plato determined the end of good to be to live according to virtue, and that this can come about only for one who has the knowledge and imitation of God, and that there is no other cause of being blessed;

and therefore he does not doubt that to philosophize is to love God, whose nature is incorporeal. Whence it is surely gathered that the student of wisdom—for that is what a philosopher is—will then be blessed when he has begun to enjoy God.

For although one who enjoys what he loves is not forthwith blessed—for many, by loving things that ought not to be loved, are wretched, and more wretched when they enjoy them—yet no one is blessed who does not enjoy what he loves.

For even those who love things that ought not to be loved think themselves blessed not by loving but by enjoying.

Whoever, then, enjoys what he loves, and loves the true and highest good, who but the most wretched would deny that he is blessed?

But the true and highest good itself Plato says is God, whence he would have the philosopher be a lover of God, so that, since philosophy tends toward the blessed life, he who has loved God may be blessed in enjoying God.

## Certain Truths of Wisdom Belong to All Nations

**9.** Whatever philosophers, therefore, have held these views concerning the highest and true God—that he is both the maker of created things, and the light of things to be known, and the good of things to be done;

that from him we have both the principle of nature, and the truth of doctrine, and the felicity of life—

whether they are more fittingly called Platonists, or impose any other name on their sect;

whether only those of the Ionic kind who were preeminent among them held these views, as did Plato himself and those who well understood him;

or also those of the Italic kind, on account of Pythagoras and the Pythagoreans and any others perchance of the same opinion from there;

or whether also of other nations there are found any who were held to be sages or philosophers—Atlantic Libyans, Egyptians, Indians, Persians, Chaldeans, Scythians, Gauls, Spaniards—who saw and taught this: all these we set before the rest, and we confess them to be nearer to us.

**10.1.** For although a Christian man, instructed in the ecclesiastical writings only, may perhaps be ignorant of the name of the Platonists, and may not know whether two kinds of philosophers existed in the Greek tongue, the Ionic and the Italic,

he is nevertheless not so deaf in human affairs as not to know that philosophers profess either the pursuit of wisdom or wisdom itself.

Yet he is on guard against those who philosophize according to the elements of this world, not according to God, by whom the world itself was made.

For he is admonished by the apostolic precept and faithfully hears what was said: *see to it that no one makes a prey of you by philosophy and empty deceit, according to the elements of the world*[^27].

Then, lest he think that all are such, he hears the same Apostle say of certain men:

*for what can be known about God is plain to them, because God has shown it to them. Ever since the creation of the world his invisible nature has been clearly perceived in the things that have been made, his eternal power and deity*[^28];

and where, speaking to the Athenians, after he had said a great thing about God, one that can be understood by few—that *in him we live and move and have our being*—he added and said: *as even some of your poets have said*[^29].

He knows well, indeed, to be on guard against them too in the matters in which they err; for where it was said that through the things that have been made God showed them his invisible attributes to be beheld by the understanding,

there too it was said that they did not rightly worship God himself, because they paid to other things, to which they ought not, the divine honors owed to him alone: *for although they knew God they did not honor him as God or give thanks to him, but they became futile in their thinking and their senseless minds were darkened. Claiming to be wise, they became fools, and exchanged the glory of the immortal God for images resembling mortal man or birds or animals or reptiles*[^30];

where he gave us to understand both the Romans and the Greeks and the Egyptians, who gloried in the name of wisdom. But about this we shall dispute with them by and by. As for that in which they agree with us concerning the one God, the author of this universe, who is not only incorporeal above all bodies, but also incorruptible above all souls, our principle, our light, our good, in this we set them before the rest.

**10.2.** And if a Christian, ignorant of their writings, does not use in disputation the words he has not learned—calling either "natural" in Latin or "physical" in Greek that part in which the investigation of nature is treated, and "rational" or "logical" that in which it is asked in what way truth can be perceived, and "moral" or "ethical" that in which morals are dealt with, and the ends of good things to be sought and of evil things to be shunned—

he is not therefore ignorant that from the one true and best God we have both the nature by which we were made in his image, and the doctrine by which we know him and ourselves, and the grace by which, in cleaving to him, we are blessed.

This, then, is the reason why we prefer these men to the rest: that, whereas other philosophers wore out their talents and labors in seeking the causes of things, and what might be the method of learning and of living, these, having come to know God, found where there is both the cause of the established universe, and the light by which truth is perceived, and the fountain from which felicity is to be drunk.

Whether, then, these Platonists, or any other philosophers of whatever nations, hold these views concerning God, they hold them with us. But it has pleased us to treat this matter rather with the Platonists, because their writings are better known. For both the Greeks, whose language is preeminent among the nations, celebrated them with great praise, and the Latins, moved by either their excellence or their glory, learned them the more gladly and, by translating them into our own speech, made them nobler and more illustrious.

**11.** But some who are associated with us in the grace of Christ wonder, when they hear or read that Plato held these views about God, which they recognize to agree greatly with the truth of our religion. Whence some have thought that, when he went on to Egypt, he heard the prophet Jeremiah or read the prophetic Scriptures in that same journey[^31]; and this opinion indeed I set down in certain of my books[^32]. But the reckoning of times, diligently computed, which is contained in chronological history, shows that Plato was born nearly a hundred years after the time when Jeremiah prophesied; and since Plato lived eighty-one years, from the year of his death down to that time when Ptolemy, king of Egypt, requested from Judea the prophetic Scriptures of the Hebrew nation and saw to their being translated and kept by seventy Hebrew men who also knew the Greek tongue, there are found nearly sixty years. Wherefore in that journey of his Plato could neither see Jeremiah, who had died so long before, nor read those Scriptures, which had not yet been translated into the Greek tongue in which he was strong;

unless perhaps, because he was of the keenest zeal, he learned these Scriptures, as he did the Egyptian ones, through an interpreter—not so as to translate them in writing

(which Ptolemy is reported to have merited as an enormous benefit, he who by his royal power could even be feared)[^33], but so as to learn by conversation what they contained, as far as he could grasp it.

That this may be supposed, those indications seem to persuade, that the book of Genesis thus begins: *in the beginning God made heaven and earth. The earth was invisible and unformed, and darkness was over the abyss, and the Spirit of God was borne over the water*[^34];

while in the *Timaeus*, the book Plato wrote on the constitution of the world, he says that God in that work first joined earth and fire[^35]. Now it is manifest that he assigns to fire the place of heaven; this view, then, has a certain likeness to that by which it was said: *in the beginning God made heaven and earth*.

Then he says that the two means, by whose interposition these two extremes were coupled to each other, are water and air[^36]; whence he is thought[^37] to have so understood what is written: *the Spirit of God was borne over the water*.

For paying too little attention to the manner in which that Scripture is wont to call the Spirit of God—since air too is called "spirit"—he can seem to have supposed that the four elements were mentioned in that passage.

Then, as for Plato's saying that the philosopher is a lover of God, nothing so glows in those sacred Scriptures[^38]; and especially that passage—which most of all leads me almost to agree that Plato was not without acquaintance with those books—where, when the words of God are conveyed by an angel to holy Moses in such a way that, to him asking what was the name of the one who was commanding him to go to the Hebrew people to be freed from Egypt, it is answered: *I am who I am, and you shall say to the sons of Israel: he who is has sent me to you*[^39],

as though, in comparison with him who truly is because he is unchangeable, the things that were made changeable are not—Plato vehemently held this and most diligently commended it[^40]. And I do not know whether this is anywhere found in the books of those who lived before Plato, except where it was said: *I am who I am, and you shall say to them: he who is has sent me to you*.

**12.** But from wherever he learned these things—whether from the books of the ancients that preceded him, or rather, as the Apostle says, *because what can be known about God is plain to them, for God has shown it to them; ever since the creation of the world his invisible nature has been clearly perceived in the things that have been made, his eternal power and deity*[^41]—I have now, as I think, sufficiently set forth that not without reason have I chosen the Platonist philosophers as those with whom to treat this matter; because in this question, which we have just now undertaken, the discussion is about natural theology, whether, on account of the felicity that is to be after death, sacred rites ought to be performed to one God or to many.

For I chose these above all because, by however much better they thought concerning the one God who made heaven and earth, by so much are they held more glorious and illustrious than the rest, set so far above the others by the judgment of posterity

that, although Aristotle, Plato's disciple, a man of excellent talent and, in eloquence indeed unequal to Plato, yet easily surpassing many, had founded the Peripatetic sect—because he was wont to dispute while walking about—and, excelling in illustrious fame, had gathered very many disciples into his school while his teacher was still alive,

and although after Plato's death Speusippus, his sister's son, and Xenocrates, his beloved disciple, had succeeded him in his school, which was called the Academy, and on this account both they and their successors were called Academics—nevertheless the more recent philosophers, the most noble, who chose to follow Plato, were unwilling to be called Peripatetics or Academics, but Platonists. Among them are the greatly ennobled Greeks Plotinus, Iamblichus, and Porphyry; while in both languages, that is, both Greek and Latin, Apuleius the African stood forth as a noble Platonist. But all these and others of the kind, and Plato himself, thought that sacred rites must be performed to many gods.

## Whether the Gods Are Good, or Also Evil

**13.** Although, then, they differ from us in many other and great matters, yet in this, which I have just now set down—since it is no small matter

and from it the present question arises—I first ask them to which gods they think this worship is to be shown: whether to good ones, or to evil ones, or to both good and evil.

But we have the opinion of Plato saying that all the gods are good, and that there is no god at all that is evil[^42]. It follows, therefore, that these things are to be understood as shown to the good; for it is then that they are shown to gods, since they will not even be gods if they are not good.

If this is so—for what else is it fitting to believe about gods?—then surely that opinion is emptied by which some think that evil gods must be appeased by sacred rites, lest they do harm, while the good are to be invoked, that they may help[^43]. For there are no evil gods; but to the good, as they say, the due honor of sacred rites is to be paid.

Who, then, are those who love the scenic plays and demand that they be joined to divine things and shown in their own honor? Their power does not show them to be no gods at all, but this disposition of theirs assuredly shows them to be evil.

For what Plato thought about the scenic plays is well known, since he judges that the poets themselves, because they composed songs so unworthy of the majesty and goodness of the gods, must be driven from the city[^44]. Who, then, are those gods who contend over the scenic plays with Plato himself? For he does not suffer the gods to be defamed by false crimes, while these command that their own honors be celebrated by those very crimes.

Finally, when these gods ordered that those same plays be reestablished, demanding base things, they did even malignant deeds,

taking away the son of Titus Latinius and sending disease upon him because he had refused their command, and then withdrawing that disease when he had fulfilled their orders[^45];

but Plato thinks that even gods so evil are not to be feared; rather, holding most steadfastly to the strength of his own opinion, he does not hesitate to remove from a well-ordered people all the sacrilegious trifles of the poets, in which those gods delight by the fellowship of their uncleanness.

Now this Plato—as I have already mentioned in the second book[^46]—Labeo places among the demigods. And this Labeo holds that the evil divinities are to be appeased by bloody victims and supplications of this kind, but the good by plays and such things as pertain, as it were, to gladness[^47].

What is it, then, that Plato the demigod so steadfastly dares to take away—not from demigods, but from gods, and these good ones—those delights, because he judges them base? These gods surely refute Labeo's opinion; for in the case of Latinius they showed themselves not only wanton and sportive, but even savage and terrible. Let the Platonists, then, explain these things to us, who think that all the gods, according to the opinion of their author, are good and honorable and partners in the virtues of the wise, and hold it impious to think otherwise of any of the gods. We will explain, they say. Let us, then, listen attentively.

## What the Teaching of the Platonists Was Concerning the Demons

**14.1.** Of all animals, they say, in which there is a rational soul, there is a threefold division: into gods, men, and demons. The gods hold the highest place, men the lowest, the demons the middle.

For the seat of the gods is in heaven, of men on earth, of the demons in the air. As they have a different dignity of places, so also of natures.

Accordingly the gods are superior to men and demons; men, however, are set below gods and demons, both by the order of the elements and by the difference of their merits.

The demons, therefore, in the middle, are, just as they are to be ranked below the gods, beneath whom they dwell, so to be set before men, above whom they dwell. For they have in common with the gods the immortality of their bodies, but the passions of their souls in common with men. Wherefore it is no wonder, they say[^48], if they are delighted even by the obscenities of the plays and the figments of the poets, since indeed they are caught by human affections, from which the gods are far removed and in every way estranged. From this it is gathered that Plato, by detesting and forbidding the poetic figments, deprived not the gods, who are all good and exalted, of the pleasure of the scenic plays, but the demons.

**14.2.** If these things are so—which, although they are found in others too, yet Apuleius the Platonist of Madaura wrote one book on this subject alone, which he chose to entitle *On the God of Socrates*, where he discusses and sets forth from what kind of divinities Socrates had one attached to him and reconciled by a certain friendship, by whom he is reported to have been wont to be warned to desist from acting whenever what he wished to do was not going to turn out prosperously; for he says most plainly and asserts most copiously that this was not a god but a demon[^49], treating with diligent disputation that opinion of Plato concerning the sublimity of the gods, the lowliness of men, and the intermediate state of the demons—

if, then, these things are so, in what way did Plato dare, by driving the poets from the city, to take away the theatrical pleasures—if not from the gods, whom he set apart from all human contagion, then certainly from the demons themselves—

except because by this means he admonished the human mind, though still placed in these mortal members, for the splendor of honor to despise the impure commands of the demons and to detest their uncleanness?

For if Plato most honorably reproved and forbade these things, then surely the demons most basely demanded and commanded them.

Either, then, Apuleius is mistaken, and Socrates had a friend not from that kind of divinities; or Plato holds contrary views among themselves, now honoring the demons, now removing their delights from a well-mannered city; or the demon's friendship with Socrates is not to be congratulated,

of which Apuleius himself was so far ashamed that he entitled his book *On the God of Socrates*, which, according to his own disputation, by which he so diligently and copiously distinguishes the gods from the demons, he ought not to have called *On the God*, but *On the Demon of Socrates*.

But he preferred to put this in the disputation itself rather than in the title of the book.

For so, through the sound teaching that has shone upon human affairs, all or almost all shudder at the name of demons, so that whoever, before Apuleius's disputation by which the dignity of the demons is commended, should read the title of the book *On the Demon of Socrates*, would by no means think that that man had been of sound mind.

But what did Apuleius himself find to praise in the demons, beyond the subtlety and firmness of their bodies and the loftier place of their habitation? For about their morals, when he was speaking of them all in general, he not only said nothing good, but even very much that was evil.

In short, once that book is read, no one at all wonders that they wished to have even scenic baseness among divine things, and that, while they wish to be thought gods, they could be delighted by the crimes of the gods,

and that whatever in their rites is either laughed at or shuddered at, in obscene solemnity or base cruelty, accords with their affections.

**15.1.** Wherefore, far be it that a mind truly religious and subject to the true God, in considering these things, should think the demons better than itself because they have better bodies.

Otherwise it must set even many beasts before itself, which surpass us in keenness of senses, in most easy and swift motion, in strength of powers, and in the long-lasting firmness of their bodies. What man will equal eagles and vultures in seeing? What man will equal dogs in smelling? Who will equal hares, stags, and all birds in swiftness? Who lions and elephants in great strength? Who serpents in length of life, which are said even to lay aside their old age along with their slough and return to youth[^50]?

But just as we are better than all these by reasoning and understanding, so also we ought to be better than the demons by living well and honorably.

For it is for this reason that divine providence gave to those things over which we are agreed to be superior certain superior gifts of body, so that in this way too it might be commended to us that what we are set above must be cultivated with much greater care than the body,

and that we might learn to despise the very corporeal excellence which we knew the demons to have, in comparison with the goodness of life by which we are set before them—we too being destined to have an immortality of bodies, not one which an eternity of punishments torments, but one which a purity of souls precedes.

**15.2.** But now, as for the loftiness of place—that the demons dwell in the air, while we dwell on earth—to be so moved by this that on this account we should think them to be set before us, is altogether ridiculous. For by this reasoning we set even all flying things before ourselves.

But, they say, when flying things are wearied by flying, or have to refresh the body with food, they seek the earth again, either for rest or for feeding, which the demons do not do. Does it then please them that the flying things excel us, and the demons excel even the flying things?

But if it is most senseless to think so, there is no reason why we should think the demons, on account of their habitation in a higher element, worthy that we should subject ourselves to them by the disposition of religion.

For just as it could come about that the birds of the air are not only not preferred to us earthly creatures but even subjected to us, on account of the dignity of the rational soul which is in us, so it could come about that the demons, although they are more airy, are not for that reason better than us earthly creatures because air is higher than earth; but rather men are to be preferred to them, since the despair of those demons is by no means to be compared with the hope of pious men.

For even that reasoning of Plato's, by which he weaves and orders the four elements in proportion, inserting between the two extremes, most mobile fire and immobile earth, the two means, air and water[^51],

so that, by however much air is higher than water and fire than air, by so much also the waters are higher than the lands—sufficiently admonishes us not to estimate the merits of animals according to the grades of the elements.

For Apuleius himself, with the rest, calls man an earthly animal, who nevertheless is far preferred to the aquatic animals, although Plato sets the very waters before the lands[^52]: so that we may understand that the same order is not to be held, when the merits of souls are in question, that seems to be the order in the grades of bodies; but that it can come about that a better soul inhabits a lower body, and a worse one a higher.

## What Nature Apuleius Says the Demons Have

**16.** Concerning the morals of the demons, then, when this same Platonist was speaking, he said that they are agitated by the same disturbances of soul as men, irritated by injuries, appeased by compliances and gifts, that they rejoice in honors, are delighted by the various rites of the sacred ceremonies, and are roused if anything in them has been neglected[^53].

Among other things he also says that to them belong the divinations of augurs, soothsayers, seers, and dreams; and that from them too are the miracles of the magicians[^54].

But defining them briefly, he says that the demons are, in kind, animals; in soul, subject to passion; in mind, rational; in body, airy; in time, eternal. Of these five, the first three are theirs which are ours, the fourth is proper to them, and the fifth they have in common with the gods[^55].

But I see that of the three higher things which they have with us, they have two even with the gods. For he says that the gods too are animals, and, distributing to each its own elements, he placed us among the earthly animals along with the rest that live and feel on earth,

among the aquatic, fishes and other swimming things, among the airy, the demons, among the ethereal, the gods.

And accordingly, that the demons are in kind animals, this is common to them not only with men but also with gods and beasts;

that they are in mind rational, with gods and men; that they are in time eternal, with the gods alone; that they are in soul subject to passion, with men alone; that they are in body airy, they alone are.

Accordingly, that they are in kind animals is no great thing, because the beasts are this too; that they are in mind rational is not above us, because we are this too; that they are in time eternal—what good is this, if it is not blessed?

For a temporal felicity is better than a wretched eternity. That they are in soul subject to passion—how is this above us, when we too are this, and it would not be so unless we were wretched?

That they are in body airy—how is this to be esteemed, since the nature of the soul, of whatever sort, is preferred to every body, and therefore the worship of religion, which is owed from the soul, is by no means owed to a thing that is lower than the soul?

Further, if among those things which he says belong to the demons he had numbered virtue, wisdom, and felicity, and had said that they have these eternal and in common with the gods, then surely he would be naming something to be longed for and greatly prized;

and yet not even so ought we, on account of these things, to worship them as God, but rather him from whom we knew them to have received these things.

How much less now are airy animals worthy of divine honor—rational only so far as to be able to be wretched, subject to passion only so far as to be wretched, eternal only so far as to be unable to bring their wretchedness to an end!

**17.1.** Wherefore, to pass over the rest and treat only this, which he said the demons have in common with us, that is, the passions of the soul: if all four elements are full of their own animals—of immortal ones fire and air, of mortal ones water and earth—I ask why the souls of the demons are agitated by the disturbances and storms of the passions.

For a "disturbance" is what in Greek is called πάθος; whence he chose to call them subject to passion in soul, because, word for word from πάθος, "passion" would be called a motion of the soul against reason[^56]. Why, then, are these things in the souls of the demons, which are not in the beasts? For if anything similar appears in a beast, it is not a "disturbance," because it is not against reason, of which beasts are devoid.

But that these disturbances should be in men, this is brought about by folly or wretchedness; for we are not yet blessed in that perfection of wisdom which is promised to us at the end, when we have been freed from this mortality. The gods, however, they say do not suffer these disturbances for this reason: that they are not only eternal but also blessed. For they assert that the gods too have these same rational souls, but most pure from every stain and pestilence[^57].

Wherefore, if the gods are not disturbed for this reason, that they are animals blessed, not wretched, and the beasts are not disturbed for this reason, that they are animals which can be neither blessed nor wretched, it remains that the demons, like men, are disturbed for this reason, that they are animals not blessed, but wretched.

**17.2.** By what folly, then, or rather madness, do we subject ourselves to the demons through some religion, when through the true religion we are freed from that viciousness in which we are like them? For whereas the demons—as even this Apuleius, although he spares them very much and judges them worthy of divine honors[^58], is nevertheless compelled to confess—are goaded by anger, the true religion bids us not be goaded by anger, but rather resist it[^59].

Whereas the demons are invited by gifts, the true religion bids us show favor to no one for the acceptance of gifts[^60]. Whereas the demons are soothed by honors, the true religion bids us be moved by such things in no way[^61].

Whereas the demons are haters of some men and lovers of others, not by a prudent and tranquil judgment, but by a soul which he himself calls subject to passion, the true religion bids us love even our enemies[^62]. Finally, every motion of the heart and surge of the mind and all the disturbances and storms of the soul[^63], by which he asserts the demons to seethe and toss[^64], the true religion bids us lay aside.

What cause is there, then, but folly and a pitiable error, that you make yourself humble by venerating one to whom you desire to be unlike in your living;

and that you worship with religion one whom you are unwilling to imitate, when the sum of religion is to imitate the one you worship?

**18.** In vain, therefore, did Apuleius, and whoever holds such views, confer this honor on them, setting them thus in the middle, in the air between the ethereal heaven and the earth, so that—*since no god mingles with man*, which they report Plato to have said[^65]—these demons may carry the prayers of men to the gods, and bring back thence to men the things they ask, once granted.

For those who believed these things thought it unworthy that men should mingle with gods and gods with men;

but worthy that the demons should mingle both with gods and with men, to allege to the one the requests sought here, and to bring back from the other the things granted there;

so that, namely, a chaste man, a stranger to the crimes of the magic arts, should employ as patrons those through whom the gods may hear him—gods who love these things

by not loving which he becomes the more worthy, one whom they ought the more easily and gladly to hear.

For those demons love the scenic basenesses, which chastity does not love; in the malefices of the magicians they love *the thousand arts of harming*[^66], which innocence does not love.

Therefore both chastity and innocence, if they should wish to obtain anything from the gods, will not be able to do so by their own merits except through the intervention of their own enemies. There is no reason for this man to try to justify the poetic figments and the theatrical mockeries.

We have against these things their own master, and one of such authority among them, Plato—if human modesty so ill deserves of itself as not only to love base things but even to judge them pleasing to the divinity.

**19.** Further, against the magic arts, in which certain men, too unhappy and too impious, even like to glory, shall I not cite the very public light as my witness? For why are these things punished so gravely by the severity of the laws, if they are works of divinities to be worshiped? Or did the Christians perchance establish these laws by which the magic arts are punished?

In what other sense, except that it is not doubtful that these malefices are pernicious to the human race, does the most illustrious poet say: *I call to witness the gods, dear sister, and you, and your sweet life, that against my will I gird myself for the magic arts*[^67]? That too which he says in another place about these arts: *and I have seen him carry over the sown crops to another place*[^68],

because by this pestilent and wicked teaching the crops of another are said to be transferred into other lands—does not Cicero mention that it was written in the *Twelve Tables*, that is, the most ancient laws of the Romans, and that a punishment was appointed for him who should do this[^69]?

Finally, was not Apuleius himself accused of the magic arts before judges who were not Christians? And if he had known these arts, with which he was charged, to be divine and pious and in accord with the works of the divine powers, he ought not only to have confessed them, but even to have professed them, blaming rather the laws by which these things were forbidden and thought worthy of condemnation, which ought to be held as wonderful and venerable.

For thus he would either persuade the judges of his own opinion, or, if they were minded according to the iniquitous laws and punished him with death for proclaiming and praising such things, the demons would repay worthy gifts to his soul, for proclaiming whose divine works he had not feared to have human life taken from himself;

just as our martyrs, when the Christian religion was charged against them as a crime—the religion by which they knew themselves to be made safe and most glorious forever—did not choose to escape temporal punishments by denying it, but rather, by confessing, professing, proclaiming it, and faithfully and bravely enduring all things for it, and dying with pious security, compelled the laws by which it was forbidden to blush, and made them be changed.

But there exists a most copious and most eloquent oration of this Platonist philosopher, by which he defends himself as a stranger to the crime of the magic arts, and he is unwilling to be seen as innocent in any other way than by denying those things which cannot be committed by an innocent man.

But all the miracles of the magicians, whom he rightly judges to be worthy of condemnation, are wrought by the teachings and works of the demons,

whom let him consider why he should judge worthy of honor, asserting them to be necessary for the conveying of our prayers to the gods—when we ought rather to shun their works, if we wish our prayers to reach the true God.

Then I ask, what kind of prayers of men does he think are alleged to the good gods through the demons: magical or lawful? If magical, they do not want such; if lawful, they do not want them through such.

But if a sinner, repenting, pours out prayers—especially if he has committed something magical—does he then at last receive pardon by the intercession of those at whose impelling or favoring he laments that he fell into fault?

Or do the demons themselves, that they may be able to merit indulgence for the penitent, first do penance because they deceived them? This no one ever said of the demons,

because, if it were so, they would by no means dare to seek divine honors for themselves, who by repenting would desire to belong to the grace of pardon. For there is detestable pride; here, pitiable humility.

**20.** But, they say, a pressing and most urgent cause compels the demons to act as intermediaries between gods and men, so as to bring from men the things desired, and to bring back from the gods the things obtained[^70]. What, after all, is this cause, and how great the necessity? Because, they say, no god mingles with man.

A splendid sanctity of God, then, who does not mingle with a suppliant man, and mingles with an arrogant demon; does not mingle with a penitent man, and mingles with a deceiving demon;

does not mingle with a man fleeing for refuge to the divinity, and mingles with a demon feigning divinity; does not mingle with a man asking indulgence, and mingles with a demon urging wickedness;

does not mingle with a man who, through philosophical books, drives the poets from a well-ordered city, and mingles with a demon who, through the princes and pontiffs of the city, seeks by the scenic plays the mockeries of the poets;

does not mingle with a man forbidding the feigning of crimes of the gods, and mingles with a demon delighting in the false crimes of the gods;

does not mingle with a man punishing the crimes of the magicians by just laws, and mingles with a demon teaching and fulfilling the magic arts; does not mingle with a man fleeing the imitation of the demon, and mingles with a demon hunting for the deception of man.

**21.1.** But there is, no doubt, a great necessity for so great an absurdity and indignity as this,

namely that what the earthly men were doing would surely be hidden from the ethereal gods, who care for human affairs, unless the airy demons announced it;

because the ether is far from the earth and hung high aloft, while the air is contiguous to both the ether and the earth.

O wonderful wisdom! What else do these men think about the gods, whom they wish all to be most good,

than both that they care for human affairs, lest they seem unworthy of worship, and that, on account of the distance of the elements, they are ignorant of human affairs,

so that the demons may be believed necessary, and on this account they too be thought worthy of worship, through whom the gods can both learn what is being done in human affairs and where they must come to men's aid?

If this is so, the demon is better known to these good gods through his neighboring body than is a man through his good soul.

O necessity much to be deplored—or rather, vanity to be derided or detested, that the divinity may not be vain! For if the gods can see our soul with a mind free from the obstacle of the body, they do not need the demons as messengers for this;

but if the ethereal gods perceive through their own body the corporeal indications of souls—such as speech, countenance, motion—and from these gather what the demons too announce, then they can also be deceived by the lies of the demons.

And further, if the divinity of the gods cannot be deceived by the demons, then by that same divinity what we do cannot be unknown.

**21.2.** But I would that these men told me whether the demons announced to the gods that the poetic figments concerning the crimes of the gods displeased Plato, and concealed that they themselves were pleased by them,

or hid both and preferred the gods to be ignorant of this whole matter, or disclosed both—both Plato's pious prudence toward the gods and their own desire injurious to the gods—or whether they wished Plato's opinion, by which he was unwilling that the gods be defamed by false crimes through the impious license of the poets, to be unknown to the gods, but were not ashamed or afraid to betray their own wickedness, by which they love the scenic plays in which those disgraces of the gods are celebrated.

Of these four things which I have put forward by questioning, let them choose whichever they will, and in each of them let them note how much evil they think of the good gods.

For if they choose the first, they will have to confess that it was not permitted to the good gods to dwell with the good Plato when he forbade their injuries, and that they dwelt with the evil demons when they exulted in their injuries; since

the good gods could know a good man set far from them only through the evil demons, whereas they could not know the demons who were their neighbors.

But if they choose the second and say that both were concealed by the demons, so that the gods were wholly ignorant both of Plato's most religious law and of the demons' sacrilegious delight: what can the gods usefully know in human affairs through the demons as go-betweens,

when they are ignorant of those things which are decreed in honor of the good gods, by the religion of good men, against the desire of the evil demons?

But if they choose the third and answer that not only Plato's opinion forbidding the injuries of the gods, but also the demons' wickedness exulting in the injuries of the gods, became known to the gods through these same demons as messengers: is this to announce, or to insult?

And do the gods hear both, and so know both, that they not only do not bar from access to them the malignant demons who desire and do things contrary to the dignity of the gods and the religion of Plato, but even transmit gifts through those evil neighbors to the good but distant Plato?

For thus the chained series, as it were, of the elements has bound them, that they can be joined to those by whom they are accused, but cannot be joined to him by whom they are defended, knowing both, but unable to alter the weights of air and earth.

Now, for what remains, if they choose the fourth, it is worse than the rest. For who could bear it, if the demons announced to the gods the criminous figments of the poets about the immortal gods, and the unworthy mockeries of the theaters,

and their own most ardent desire and most sweet pleasure in all these things, but kept silent about Plato's judgment, with philosophical gravity, that all these things must be removed from the best republic;

so that now the good gods are compelled to know, through such messengers, the evils of the worst beings—not the evils of others, but of those same messengers—and are not permitted to know the good things of the philosophers, contrary to these, although those things are to the injury, and these to the honor, of the gods themselves?

**22.** Since, therefore, none of these four is to be chosen, lest in any of them so ill a view be held of the gods, it remains that we must in no way believe what Apuleius strives to persuade, and whatever other philosophers are of the same opinion,

that the demons are thus intermediaries between gods and men, as go-betweens and interpreters, who carry our petitions hence, and bring back thence the aid of the gods;

but rather that they are spirits most greedy to do harm, utterly estranged from justice, swollen with pride, livid with envy, crafty in deceit, who indeed dwell in this air because, cast down from the height of the higher heaven for the deserts of their irreversible transgression, they have been condemned beforehand to this fitting prison, as it were;

nor yet, because the place of air is above the lands and waters, are they on that account themselves superior in merits to men,

who very easily surpass them, not by an earthly body, but by a pious mind that has chosen the true God for its help.

But they domineer over many, plainly unworthy of a share in the true religion, as over captives and subjects,

to the greatest part of whom they have, by wonderful and deceitful signs, whether of deeds or of predictions, persuaded that they are gods.

But upon certain men who looked somewhat more attentively and diligently at their vices, they could not impose the belief that they are gods, and so they feigned themselves to be go-betweens between gods and men, and obtainers of benefits;

if, that is, men thought that at least this honor should be paid to them—men who did not believe them to be gods, because they saw them to be evil, but wished all the gods to be good, and yet did not dare to call them altogether unworthy of divine honor,

chiefly lest they offend the peoples, whom they saw to be in bondage to them by an inveterate superstition, through so many sacred rites and temples.

## Hermes Bears Witness That Men Made the Gods and Animated Them...

**23.1.** For Hermes the Egyptian, whom they call Trismegistus, thought and wrote differently about them.

For Apuleius indeed denies that they are gods; but when he says that they are engaged, by a certain intermediate state, between gods and men in such a way that they seem necessary to men in relation to the gods themselves, he does not separate their worship from the religion of the gods above.

That Egyptian, however, says that some gods were made by the highest God, others by men.

One who hears this, as I have set it down, thinks it is said of images, because they are the works of men's hands; but he asserts that the visible and tangible images are, as it were, the bodies of the gods, and that there are present in them certain spirits, invited in, which have power either to do harm or to fulfill some of the desires of those by whom divine honors and the homage of worship are paid to them.

These invisible spirits, then, to couple by a certain art to visible things of corporeal matter, so that they become as it were animated bodies, images dedicated and subjected to those spirits—this, he says, is to make gods, and men have received this great and wonderful power of making gods.

I will set down the words of this Egyptian, as they have been translated into our language. *And since the discourse is appointed for us concerning the kinship and fellowship of men and gods,* he says, *know, O Asclepius, the power and might of man. The Lord*, he says, *and Father, or what is highest, God, as he is the maker of the heavenly gods, so man is the fashioner of the gods who are in the temples, content with human nearness.* And a little after: *thus humanity*, he says, *ever mindful of its nature and origin, perseveres in that imitation of the divinity, so that, just as the Father and Lord made the eternal gods that they might be like himself, so humanity has fashioned its gods after the likeness of its own countenance*[^71].

Here, when Asclepius, to whom he was chiefly speaking, had answered him and said: *do you mean statues, O Trismegistus?*

then he said: *statues, O Asclepius—you see how far even you yourself distrust; statues, animated, full of sense and spirit,*

*and doing such and so great things, statues foreknowing things to come and foretelling them by lot, by seer, by dreams, and by many other things, bringing weaknesses upon men and curing them, sadness and gladness according to their deserts.*

*Or do you not know, O Asclepius, that Egypt is the image of heaven, or, what is truer, the transference or descent of all things that are governed and exercised in heaven? And if it is to be said more truly, our land is the temple of the whole world. And yet, since it befits the prudent man to foreknow all things, it is not right that you be ignorant of this: there will be a time when it will appear that the Egyptians have guarded the divinity with a pious mind and sedulous religion in vain, and all their holy veneration will be frustrated, falling into nothing*[^72].

**23.2.** Then Hermes pursues this passage with many words, in which he seems to foretell this time, in which the Christian religion, by however much it is more truthful and more holy, by so much the more vehemently and freely overturns all the deceitful figments, so that the grace of the most truthful Savior may free man from those gods whom man makes, and subject him to the God by whom man was made.

But Hermes, when he foretells these things, speaks as a friend to these same mockeries of the demons, and does not clearly express the Christian name,

but, as though those things were being taken away and abolished by whose observance the heavenly likeness was kept in Egypt, so he testifies, lamenting these things as about to be, with a kind of mournful prediction. For he was one of those of whom the Apostle says that *although they knew God they did not honor him as God or give thanks to him, but they became futile in their thinking and their senseless minds were darkened; claiming to be wise, they became fools, and exchanged the glory of the incorruptible God for images resembling mortal man*[^73] and the rest, which it would be long to recount.

For he says many such things about the one true God, the maker of the world, such as the truth holds;

and somehow, by that darkening of the heart, he slides down to these things, so that he ever wishes men to be subject to the gods whom he confesses to be made by men,

and laments that in a time to come these things are to be taken away, as though anything were more unhappy than a man over whom his own figments domineer;

whereas it is easier that, by worshiping as gods the things he has made, he himself should not even be a man, than that, through his worship, the things man has made should be able to be gods.

For it happens sooner that man, *being in honor, without understanding, is compared to the beasts*[^74], than that to the work of God made in his image, that is, to man himself, the work of man should be preferred.

Wherefore man deservedly falls away from him who made him, when he sets above himself what he himself made.

**23.3.** These vain, deceptive, pernicious, sacrilegious things Hermes the Egyptian, because he knew the time was coming when they would be taken away, grieved over; but he grieved as imprudently as he knew.

For the Holy Spirit had not revealed these things to him, as to the holy prophets, who, foreseeing these things, said with exultation: *can man make gods for himself? Such are no gods!*[^75];

and in another place: *and on that day, says the Lord, I will cut off the names of the idols from the land, so that they shall be remembered no more*[^76]; but properly concerning Egypt, as far as pertains to this matter, holy Isaiah thus prophesies: *and the images of Egypt will tremble at his presence, and the hearts of the Egyptians will melt within them*[^77], and the rest of this kind.

Of this kind too were those who rejoiced that what they knew was to come had come;

such as Simeon, such as Anna, who recognized the newly born Jesus[^78];

such as Elizabeth, who recognized him even when conceived, in the Spirit[^79]; such as Peter, saying, by the revelation of the Father: *you are the Christ, the Son of the living God*[^80].

But to this Egyptian those spirits had indicated the future times of their own destruction, who also, when the Lord was present in the flesh, said trembling: *have you come to destroy us before the time?*[^81]

whether because it was sudden to them, what they supposed indeed to be future, but more slowly,

or because they called this their very destruction, by which it came about that, once recognized, they were spurned,

and this was *before the time*, that is, before the time of judgment, when they are to be punished with eternal damnation, together with all the men too who are held in their fellowship,

as the religion speaks which neither deceives nor is deceived—not like this man, who, as it were, blown to and fro by every wind of doctrine[^82], and mingling true things with false, grieves over a religion as though about to perish, which he afterward confesses to be error.

## Hermes Says That His Forefathers Erred Concerning the Nature of the Gods

**24.1.** For after many things he returns to this very point, to speak again of the gods whom men have made, speaking thus: *but let what has been said about such things be enough.*

*Let us return again*, he says, *to man and to reason, by which divine gift man has been called a rational animal. For the things that have been said about man, though wonderful, are less to be wondered at. For of all wonders this overcame all wonder: that man was able to discover the divine nature and to produce it.*

*Since, therefore, our forefathers erred greatly concerning the nature of the gods, being unbelieving and not heeding the worship and divine religion, they invented an art by which they might make gods. To this invention they joined a power, fitting, drawn from the nature of the world, and mingling it—since they could not make souls—calling up the souls of demons or of angels, they put them into the holy images and divine mysteries, through which the idols might have powers both of doing good and of doing harm*[^83].

I do not know whether the demons themselves, when adjured, would confess so much as this man has confessed. *Since*, he says, *our forefathers erred greatly concerning the nature of the gods, being unbelieving and not heeding the worship and divine religion, they invented an art by which they might make gods*.

Did he say that they erred even moderately, to invent this art of making gods, or was he content to say *they erred*, without adding and saying that they erred *greatly*? This great error, then, and the unbelief of those not heeding the worship and divine religion, invented an art by which it might make gods.

And yet, that great error and unbelief and the turning away of the mind from the worship and divine religion invented this—that man should by art make gods—the wise man grieves, as though a divine religion were being taken away at a certain time to come.

See whether he is not compelled, both by a divine power, to betray the past error of his elders, and by a diabolical power, to grieve over the future punishment of the demons. For if their forefathers, by erring greatly concerning the nature of the gods, through unbelief and the turning away of the mind from the worship and divine religion, invented an art by which they might make gods: what wonder is it if this detestable art, in whatever it made through turning away from the divine religion, is taken away by the divine religion, when truth amends error, faith refutes unbelief, and a turning toward corrects a turning away?

**24.2.** For if he had said, with the causes left unspoken, that his forefathers invented an art by which they might make gods: it would surely have been ours, if we had any right and pious understanding, to attend and see that they would by no means have come to this art, by which man makes gods, if they had not strayed from the truth, if they had believed the things that are worthy of God, if they had heeded the worship and divine religion;

and yet, if we ourselves were to name the causes of this art as the great error of men and their unbelief and the turning away of an erring and faithless mind from the divine religion, the impudence of those resisting the truth would somehow have to be borne.

But since this same man, who admires the power of this art above all else in man, by which it was granted him to make gods, and grieves that a time is to come when all these figments of gods, established by men, are even by laws ordered to be taken away, nevertheless confesses and expresses the causes why these things were come to, saying that his forefathers, by great error and unbelief and by not heeding the worship and divine religion, invented this art by which they might make gods: what ought we to say,

or rather what ought we to do, but give as great thanks as we can to the Lord our God, who took these things away by causes contrary to those by which they were established? For what the multitude of error established, the way of truth took away; what unbelief established, faith took away; what the turning away from the worship of the divine religion established, the turning toward the one true and holy God took away;

and not in Egypt alone, which alone the demons' spirit laments in this passage, but in all the earth, which sings to the Lord a new song, as the truly sacred and truly prophetic Scriptures foretold, where it is written: *sing to the Lord a new song, sing to the Lord, all the earth*[^84].

For the title of this psalm is: *when the house was being built after the captivity*. For a house is built for the Lord, the city of God, which is the holy Church, in all the earth, after that captivity by which the demons possessed those men—captives—out of whom, as out of living stones, believing in God, the house is built[^85].

For not because man made gods was he therefore not possessed by them—he who made them—when by worshiping he was brought over into their fellowship; fellowship, I say, not of the stupid idols, but of the cunning demons.

For what are the idols, but what the same Scripture says: *they have eyes, but they do not see*[^86], and whatever such thing had to be said of materials, even though skillfully shaped, yet lacking life and sense? But the unclean spirits, bound to those same images by that nefarious art, had wretchedly taken their worshipers captive, reducing their souls into their own fellowship.

Whence the Apostle says: *we know that an idol is nothing; but what the nations sacrifice they sacrifice to demons and not to God; I do not want you to be partners with demons*[^87]. After this captivity, then, by which men were held by the malignant demons, the house of God is built in all the earth; whence that psalm received its title, where it is said: *sing to the Lord a new song, sing to the Lord, all the earth. Sing to the Lord, bless his name, tell the good news of his salvation from day to day. Declare his glory among the nations, his marvelous works among all the peoples; for great is the Lord and greatly to be praised, he is terrible above all gods. For all the gods of the nations are demons, but the Lord made the heavens*[^88].

**24.3.** He, therefore, who grieved that a time was to come when the worship of idols and the domination of the demons over those who worshiped them would be taken away,

instigated by an evil spirit, ever wished that this captivity might remain, after the passing of which the psalm sings that the house is built in all the earth.

Hermes foretold those things grieving; the prophet foretold these things rejoicing. And because the Spirit is victor, who sang these things through the holy prophets,

even Hermes himself was, in wonderful ways, compelled to confess that those things which he was unwilling and grieved to have taken away had been established, not by the prudent and faithful and religious, but by the erring and unbelieving and those turned away from the worship of the divine religion.

For although he calls them gods, yet, when he says that they were made by such men as we surely ought not to be, he shows, will he or not, that they are not to be worshiped by those who are not such as were those by whom they were made,

that is, by the prudent, the faithful, the religious; at the same time also showing that those very men who made them brought it upon themselves to have as gods those who were not gods. For that prophetic word is *true*: *can man make gods for himself? Such are no gods!*[^89].

When Hermes, then, had called such gods—gods of such men—made by art by such men, that is, when he called demons, bound to idols by some art through the bonds of their own desires, gods made by men,

he did not nevertheless grant them what the Platonist Apuleius did (about which we have already said enough, and shown how unfitting and absurd it is)—that they themselves were interpreters and intercessors between the gods whom God made and the men whom the same God made, bringing prayers from the one side and bringing back gifts from the other. For it is too foolish to believe that the gods whom men made avail more with the gods whom God made than do the very men whom that same God made.

For a demon, bound by an impious art to an image, has been made a god by man, but for such a man, not for every man.

What sort of god, then, is this, which man would not make unless he were erring and unbelieving and turned away from the true God?

Further, if the demons who are worshiped in the temples, put by some art into images, that is, into visible likenesses, by those men who by this art made gods while they were straying and turned away from the worship and divine religion,

are not go-betweens or interpreters between men and gods—both on account of their most wicked and most base morals, and because men, though erring and unbelieving and turned away from the worship and divine religion, are nevertheless without doubt better than those whom they themselves made gods by art:

it remains that what they can do, they can do as demons,

either by bestowing benefits, as it were, doing the more harm because they are the more deceiving, or by doing open harm—yet neither of these except when they are permitted, by the deep and secret providence of God—

and not that, as intermediaries between men and gods, they avail much among men through the friendship of the gods.

For they can in no way be friends to the good gods—whom we call holy angels and rational creatures of the holy heavenly habitation, *whether thrones or dominions or principalities or powers*[^90]—from whom they are as far removed in the disposition of soul as vices are far from virtues and malice from goodness.

**25.** In no way, therefore, must we seek, through the intermediary status, as it were, of the demons, after the goodwill or beneficence of the gods, or rather of the good angels, but through a likeness of good will,

by which we are with them and live with them and worship with them the God whom they worship, even though we cannot see them with the eyes of the flesh;

but in so far as we are wretched by the unlikeness of will and the frailty of weakness, so far are we far from them by the merit of our life, not by the place of our body.

For it is not because we dwell on earth by the condition of the flesh, but only if by uncleanness of heart we relish earthly things, that we are not joined to them. But when we are healed, so that we are such as they are, we draw near to them, for the time being, by faith, if we believe that we are made blessed, even by their favor, by him by whom they too were made.

## Hermes Grieves That Egypt Will Be Full of the Tombs of Martyrs...

**26.1.** It is indeed worth noting how this Egyptian, when he grieved that a time was coming when those things would be taken away from Egypt which he confesses to have been established by men greatly erring and unbelieving and turned away from the worship of the divine religion, says among other things:

*then this land, the most holy seat of shrines and temples, will be most full of tombs and of the dead*[^91]; as though, if those things were not taken away, men would not be going to die, or the dead would be placed anywhere but in the earth; and surely, by however much more time and days were rolled on, by so much greater would be the number of tombs, on account of the greater number of the dead.

But he seems to grieve at this: that the memorials of our martyrs would succeed to their temples and shrines, so that, namely, those who read these things with a mind averse and perverse from us might think that the pagans worshiped gods in temples, while we worship the dead in tombs.

For with so great a blindness do impious men, as it were, stumble against mountains, and refuse to see the things that strike their eyes, that they do not attend to the fact that in all the writings of the pagans either there are not found, or there are scarcely found, gods who were not men, and to whom, when dead, divine honors were paid. I pass over what Varro says, that all the dead are esteemed by them to be Manes-gods[^92], and proves it by those rites which are offered to almost all the dead, where he also mentions the funeral games, as though this were the greatest indication of divinity, that games are not wont to be celebrated except for divinities.

**26.2.** Hermes himself, of whom we now treat, in that very book where, lamenting as though foretelling things to come, he says: *then this land, the most holy seat of shrines and temples, will be most full of tombs and of the dead*, testifies that the gods of Egypt are dead men.

For when he had said that his forefathers, greatly erring concerning the nature of the gods, unbelieving and not heeding the worship and divine religion, invented an art by which they might make gods: *to this invention*, he says, *they joined a power, fitting, drawn from the nature of the world, and mingling it—since they could not make souls—calling up the souls of demons or of angels, they put them into the holy images and divine mysteries, through which the idols might have powers both of doing good and of doing harm*.

Then he continues, as though about to prove this by examples, and said: *for your grandfather, O Asclepius, the first inventor of medicine, to whom a temple is consecrated on the mountain of Libya near the shore of the crocodiles, in which lies his worldly man, that is, his body; for the rest of him, or rather the whole, if a man is wholly in the sense of life, has returned the better to heaven, still even now furnishing all aids to sick men by his divinity, which he was wont to provide by the art of medicine*[^93].

Behold, he said that a dead man is worshiped as a god in that place where he had his tomb—false, and deceiving, in saying that he has returned to heaven.

Then adding another: *Hermes*, he says, *whose ancestral name is mine, does he not, dwelling in the country of like name to himself, help and preserve all mortals coming from everywhere?*[^94]

For this elder Hermes, that is, Mercury, whom he says was his grandfather, is reported to be at Hermopolis, that is, in the city of his own name. Behold, he says that two gods were men, Aesculapius and Mercury.

But about Aesculapius both Greeks and Latins hold this same view; Mercury, however, many do not think was mortal, whom nevertheless this man testifies to have been his grandfather. But that one is one person, this another, although they are called by the same name.

I do not much contend whether that one is one person and this another; this one too, like Aesculapius, was a god from a man, according to the testimony of so great a man among his own people, this Trismegistus, his grandson.

**26.3.** He still adds and says: *but Isis, the wife of Osiris—how many good things we know she bestows when propitious, how greatly she harms when angry!*[^95] Then, to show that the gods whom men make by that art are of this kind (whence he gives us to understand that he supposes the demons to have arisen from the souls of dead men, whom, through the art that men greatly erring, unbelieving, and irreligious invented, he says were put into images, because those who made such gods could certainly not make souls), when he had said of Isis what I have mentioned, *how greatly she harms when angry*, he went on to add: *for it is easy for the earthly and worldly gods to be angry, inasmuch as they are made and composed by men out of either nature.*

By *either nature* he means out of soul and body, so that the demon stands for the soul, the image for the body.

*Whence it has come about*, he says, *that by the Egyptians these are called holy animals, and that throughout their several cities the souls of those whose souls were consecrated while living are worshiped, so that they are governed by their laws and called by their names*[^96].

Where is that mournful complaint, as it were, that the land of Egypt, the most holy seat of shrines and temples, would be most full of tombs and of the dead? Surely the deceitful spirit, by whose prompting Hermes said these things, was through him compelled to confess that even then that land was most full of the tombs and of the dead whom they worshiped as gods. But it was the grief of the demons that spoke through him, who mourned that their future punishments were impending at the memorials of the holy martyrs. For in many such places they are tormented and confess and are cast out from the possessed bodies of men.

## We Do Not Institute Sacred Rites for the Martyrs...

**27.1.** And yet we do not set up for these same martyrs temples, priesthoods, sacred rites, and sacrifices, since not they, but their God, is our God.

We honor, indeed, their memorials as those of holy men of God, who strove unto the death of their bodies for the truth, that the true religion might become known, the false and feigned ones being convicted; whereas even any who had felt this before, repressed it through fear.

But who among the faithful ever heard a priest, standing at the altar—even one built over the holy body of a martyr to the honor and worship of God—say in the prayers: "I offer sacrifice to you, Peter, or Paul, or Cyprian,"

when at their memorials it is offered to God, who made them both men and martyrs and associated them with his holy angels in heavenly honor, so that by that celebration we both give thanks to the true God for their victories and exhort ourselves to the imitation of such crowns and palms, with him invoked for help, by the renewal of their memorial?

Whatever acts of homage by the religious are employed, then, in the places of the martyrs, are adornments of memorials, not sacred rites or sacrifices to the dead as to gods. And whoever bring even their own feasts there (which indeed is not done by the better Christians, and in most lands there is no such custom);

yet whoever do this, when they have set them out, pray and take them away, that they may eat of them or even bestow some on the needy, and they wish them to be sanctified for themselves through the merits of the martyrs in the name of the Lord of the martyrs. But that these are not sacrifices to the martyrs is known to one who knows the one sacrifice of Christians, which is offered even there.

**27.2.** We, therefore, honor our martyrs neither with divine honors nor with human crimes, as those men honor their gods; nor do we offer sacrifices to them, nor turn their disgraces into their sacred rites. For concerning Isis, the wife of Osiris, the Egyptian goddess, and concerning their parents, who are all written to have been kings (to which her parents, when she was sacrificing, she found a crop of barley, and from it showed ears of grain to her husband the king and to his counselor Mercury, whence they would have her to be also Ceres)[^97],

what and how great evils were committed to memory not by the poets but by their mystic writings, as Alexander writes to his mother Olympias, with the priest Leo disclosing them,

let those read who wish and are able, and let those who have read recall, and let them see for what dead men, or about what deeds of theirs, sacred rites were instituted as to gods.

Far be it that, though they hold them as gods, they should dare in any part to compare them with our holy martyrs, whom nevertheless we do not hold as gods.

For we so do not appoint priests nor offer sacrifices to our martyrs—because it is unfitting, undue, unlawful, and owed only to the one God—that we do not delight them either with their own crimes or with most base plays, where these men either celebrate the shameful deeds of their gods, if, while they were men, they committed such things, or the contrived delights of the harmful demons, if they were not men.

Of this kind of demons Socrates would not have had a god, if he had a god; but perhaps those who wished to excel in that art of making gods imported such a god to a man who was a stranger to that art and innocent.

Why say more? No one even moderately prudent doubts that these spirits are not to be worshiped on account of the blessed life that is to be after death.

But perhaps they will say that all the gods indeed are good, but that of the demons some are evil, some good, and they will judge that those through whom we may come to the life blessed forever are to be worshiped—those whom they suppose to be good. Of what sort this is must now be seen in the following book.
