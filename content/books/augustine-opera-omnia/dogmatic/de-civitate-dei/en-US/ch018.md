# Book XVI: Shem and Japheth Are Blessed

## [The Prophetic Significance of Noah and His Sons]

**1.** Whether after the flood the traces of the holy city as it ran its course were continuous, or were interrupted by intervening times of impiety, so that no man existed who was a worshiper of the one true God, it is difficult to find out clearly from what the Scriptures say, because in the canonical books, after Noah—who together with his wife and three sons and as many daughters-in-law deserved to be delivered through the ark from the devastation of the flood—we do not find, down to Abraham, anyone's piety proclaimed by an explicit divine word, except that Noah commends his two sons Shem and Japheth with a prophetic blessing, beholding and foreseeing what was to come long afterward.

Hence it came about also that he cursed his middle son—that is, the one younger than the firstborn and older than the last, who had sinned against his father—not in him, but in his son, Noah's own grandson, with these words: *cursed be the boy Canaan; a servant shall he be to his brothers*.

Now Canaan had been born of Ham, who had not covered, but rather had exposed, the nakedness of his sleeping father. Hence too what follows, where he added the blessing of his two sons, the greatest and the least, saying, *blessed be the Lord God of Shem, and let Canaan be his boy; may God enlarge Japheth, and let him dwell in the houses of Shem*[^1]—just as Noah's own planting of the vineyard, and his becoming drunk from its fruit, and the uncovering of him as he slept, and the other things there done and recorded, are laden with prophetic meanings and veiled under coverings.

**2.** 1. But now that the outcome has followed in the descendants, the things that had been covered are sufficiently uncovered. For who, considering these things diligently and intelligently, would not recognize them in Christ? For Shem, of whose seed Christ was born in the flesh, is interpreted "named."[^2]

And what is more named than Christ, whose name now spreads its fragrance everywhere, so that in the *Song of Songs*, the prophecy itself going before, *it is compared to ointment poured out*[^3]; in whose houses, that is, the churches, the breadth of the nations dwells? For Japheth is interpreted "breadth."

Ham, moreover, which is interpreted "hot,"[^4] the middle son of Noah, as though separating himself from both and remaining between the two, neither in the firstfruits of the Israelites nor in the fullness of the nations, what does he signify but the breed of heretics, hot not with the spirit of wisdom but with that of impatience, by which the hearts of heretics are wont to seethe and to disturb the peace of the saints?

But these things turn to the advantage of those who make progress, according to that saying of the Apostle: *there must be heresies, that those who are approved may be made manifest among you*[^5]. Hence too it is written: *an instructed son will be wise, but he will use an imprudent servant*[^6]. For many things pertaining to the catholic faith, while they are stirred up by the hot restlessness of heretics, are, in order that they may be defended against them, both considered more diligently, and understood more clearly, and preached more insistently; and the question raised by the adversary becomes an occasion of learning.

And yet not only those who are most openly separated, but all who glory in the Christian name and live ruinously, can not unreasonably be seen to be figured in Noah's middle son; for the passion of Christ, which was signified by that man's nakedness, they both announce by their profession and dishonor by their evil deeds. Of such, therefore, it was said: *by their fruits you shall know them*[^7].

Therefore Ham was cursed in his son, as though in his fruit, that is, in his work. Hence too his son Canaan is fittingly interpreted "their movement"; and what else is this than "their work"? But Shem and Japheth—as it were the circumcision and the uncircumcision, or, as the Apostle calls them in another way, the Jews and the Greeks[^8], but called and justified—having in some way come to know the nakedness of their father, by which the passion of the Savior was signified, took a garment and laid it upon their backs and entered backward and covered the nakedness of their father, and did not see what they reverently covered[^9].

For in a certain way, in the passion of Christ, we both honor what was done for us and turn away from the crime of the Jews. The garment signifies the sacrament, the backs the memory of things past, because the Church now celebrates the passion of Christ as already accomplished—at the very time, that is, when Japheth dwells in the houses of Shem and the evil brother in their midst[^10]—and does not still look forward to it as something future.

**2.** 2. But the evil brother, in his son—that is, in his work—is a boy, that is, a servant, of his good brothers, since the good knowingly make use of the evil for the exercise of patience or for the advancement of wisdom. For there are some, as the Apostle testifies, who announce Christ not chastely; but, says he, *whether in pretense or in truth, Christ is announced*[^11].

For he himself planted the vineyard of which the prophet says, *the vineyard of the Lord of hosts is the house of Israel*[^12], *and he drank of its wine*: whether that cup is to be understood here of which he says, *can you drink the cup that I am about to drink?*[^13] and, *Father, if it be possible, let this cup pass*[^14], by which he without doubt signifies his passion; or whether, because wine is the fruit of the vine, this is rather signified by that, namely that from the vineyard itself, that is, from the race of the Israelites, he took flesh and blood for us, that he might be able to suffer;

*and he was made drunk*, that is, he suffered, *and was made naked*[^15]; for there his weakness was made naked, that is, appeared, of which the Apostle says, *though he was crucified out of weakness*[^16]. Hence the same Apostle says, *the weakness of God is stronger than men, and the foolishness of God is wiser than men*[^17]. And when it had been said, *and he was made naked*, Scripture added, *in his house*[^18], elegantly showing that it was by the people of his own flesh and by the household of his own blood—namely the Jews—that he was to suffer the cross and death.

This passion of Christ the reprobate announce outwardly only in the sound of the voice; for they do not understand what they announce. But the upright have so great a mystery in the inner man and honor inwardly in the heart the weak and foolish thing of God, because it is stronger and wiser than men. A figure of this is the fact that Ham, going out, announced this abroad; but Shem and Japheth, in order to veil it, that is, to honor it, went in, that is, they did it inwardly[^19].

**2.** 3. These secrets of divine Scripture we search out as we can, one more fittingly and another less so, yet holding faithfully as certain that these things were neither done nor recorded without some prefiguring of things to come, and are to be referred to nothing other than Christ and his Church, which is the city of God; and the proclamation of that city, from the beginning of the human race, has not been lacking, and we see it being fulfilled in all things.

When Noah's two sons, then, had been blessed and the one in their midst cursed, thereafter, down to Abraham, there is silence for more than a thousand years concerning the mention of any of the just who worshiped God piously. Nor would I believe that they were lacking; but if they were all mentioned, it would become too long, and this would be a matter of historical diligence rather than of prophetic foresight. The writer of these sacred Scriptures, then—or rather the Spirit of God through him—pursues those things by which not only are past events narrated, but future ones also foretold, which nevertheless pertain to the city of God; for whatever is said here even about men who are not its citizens is said for this purpose, that the other city may, by the contrast, either profit or stand out. Not, of course, that all the things narrated as done are to be thought to signify something also; but for the sake of those that do signify something, even those that signify nothing are woven in.

For the earth is broken up by the ploughshare alone; but in order that this may be done, the other parts of the plough are also necessary; and the strings alone in lyres and musical instruments of this kind are fitted for song; but in order that they may be fitted, the other parts too are present in the frames of the instruments, which are not struck by those who play, but to which are connected the parts that, when struck, give back their sound.

So in prophetic history some things too are said that signify nothing, but to which are attached, and in a certain way bound, the things that do signify.

## The Generations of Shem and Ham Are Enumerated

**3.** 1. The generations, then, of the sons of Noah are next to be considered, and what seems fit to be said about them must be woven into this work, in which the course of the two cities through the ages—the earthly, that is, and the heavenly—is set forth. For their enumeration was begun from the youngest son, who was called Japheth, of whom eight sons are named, and seven grandsons from two of his sons, three from one, four from the other; so they amount to fifteen in all. The sons of Ham, that is, of Noah's middle son, are four, and five grandsons from one of his sons, and two great-grandsons from one grandson; their sum comes to eleven[^20]. When these have been enumerated, the account returns as it were to the head and says, *and Cush begot Nimrod; he began to be a giant upon the earth. He was a giant hunter against the Lord God. For this reason they say: Like Nimrod, a giant hunter against the Lord. And the beginning of his kingdom was Babylon, Erech, Accad, and Calneh, in the land of Shinar. Out of that land went forth Asshur and built Nineveh and the city of Rehoboth and Calah and Resen, between the midst of Nineveh and Calah: this is the great city*[^21].

Now this Cush, the father of the giant Nimrod, was named first among the sons of Ham, whose five sons had already been reckoned and two grandsons. But either he begot this giant after his grandsons were born, or, what is more credible, Scripture spoke of him separately on account of his eminence; since indeed his kingdom too was mentioned, the beginning of which was that most renowned city Babylon, and the cities or regions that were mentioned alongside it. But what was said—that out of that land, that is, the land of Shinar, which belonged to the kingdom of Nimrod, Asshur went forth and built Nineveh and the other cities that it strung together—was done long afterward, which it touched on from this occasion on account of the renown of the kingdom of the Assyrians, which Ninus, the son of Belus, the founder of the great city of Nineveh, wonderfully extended; and the name of that city was derived from his name, so that from Ninus it was called Nineveh.

But Asshur, from whom the Assyrians, was not among the sons of Ham, Noah's middle son, but is found among the sons of Shem, who was Noah's greatest son. From this it appears that those sprang from the offspring of Shem who afterward took possession of that giant's kingdom and went forth from there and founded other cities, the first of which was called Nineveh from Ninus.

From here the account returns to another son of Ham, who was called Mizraim, and those whom he begot are mentioned, not as individual men, but as seven nations. And from the sixth, as from a sixth son, a people is mentioned as having gone forth which is called Philistim; so they come to eight. Then it returns again to Canaan, in whom as a son Ham was cursed, and those whom he begot are named, eleven of them. Then, certain cities being mentioned, it is told to what bounds they reached. And thus, when the sons and grandsons are reckoned, thirty-one are reported as begotten of the offspring of Ham[^22].

**3.** 2. It remains to mention the sons of Shem, Noah's greatest son; for it is to him that this narrative of the generations, which began from the youngest, has come by degrees. But where the sons of Shem begin to be mentioned has something of obscurity, which has been cleared up by explanation, because it bears much on the matter we are inquiring into. For it reads thus: *and Shem too was born, the father of all the sons of Eber, the brother of Japheth the elder*[^23].

The order of the words is, *and to Shem too was born Eber*—that is, to Shem himself was born Eber, *which Shem is the father of all the sons*. By Shem, then, he wished to be understood the patriarch of all who sprang from his stock, whom he is about to mention, whether they be sons, or grandsons and great-grandsons and those thereafter sprung from the same line. Shem did not, of course, beget this Eber directly, but Eber is found fifth from him in the line of progenitors. For Shem, among other sons, begot Arphaxad, Arphaxad begot Cainan, Cainan begot Shelah, Shelah begot Eber.

Shem did not, in fact, beget this Eber; rather, Eber is found fifth from him in the line of ancestry. For among his other sons Shem begot Arphaxad, Arphaxad begot Cainan, Cainan begot Shelah, and Shelah begot Eber.

Not without reason, then, is Eber himself named first in the offspring coming from Shem, and even preferred to Shem's sons, although he is the fifth in descent, unless it be that what is handed down is true—that from him the Hebrews were named, as it were Heberews; though there could also be another opinion, that they seem to have been called Hebrews from Abraham, as it were Abrahews; but doubtless this is true, that from Eber they were called Heberews, and then, with one letter worn away, Hebrews—the language which the people of Israel alone was able to keep, among whom the city of God both sojourned in the saints and was foreshadowed in a mystery in them all.

So then the sons of Shem are first named, six of them; then from one of them four of his grandsons were born, and likewise another of Shem's sons begot his grandson, and from him in turn a great-grandson was born, and thence a great-great-grandson, who is Eber. Now Eber begot two sons, one of whom he called Peleg, which is interpreted "dividing." Then Scripture, adding and giving the reason for this name, says, *because in his days the earth was divided*[^24]. What this means will appear later. But the other son who was born of Eber begot twelve sons; and so all those begotten of Shem come to twenty-seven. In sum, then, all those begotten of the three sons of Noah—that is, fifteen of Japheth, thirty-one of Ham, twenty-seven of Shem—come to seventy-three.

Then Scripture continues, saying, *these are the sons of Shem in their tribes, according to their languages, in their regions and in their nations*; and likewise of them all it says, *these are the tribes of the sons of Noah, according to their generations, according to their nations. From these the islands of the nations were scattered over the earth after the flood*[^25]. From this it is gathered that there were then seventy-three nations, or rather (as will be shown later) seventy-two, not men. For earlier too, when the sons of Japheth had been mentioned, it was concluded thus: *from these the islands of the nations were separated in their land, each one according to his language, in their tribes and in their nations*[^26].

**3.** 3. Now indeed, among the sons of Ham, in one place the nations are mentioned more openly, as I showed above. *Mizraim begot those who are called the Ludim*[^27]; and in the same way the rest, up to seven nations. And after enumerating them all, it concludes, saying, *these are the sons of Ham in their tribes, according to their languages, in their regions and in their nations*[^28]. The reason, then, that the sons of many were not mentioned is that they were joined by their birth to other nations and could not themselves make nations.

For what other reason is there that, although eight sons of Japheth are enumerated, the sons born of only two of them are mentioned; and although four sons of Ham are named, those born of only three are added; and although six sons of Shem are named, the posterity of only two is woven in? Did the rest remain without sons? Far be it to believe this; but they did not make nations on whose account they would be worthy to be mentioned, because, as they were born, they were added to other nations.

## What Babylon Did in the Confusion of Tongues

**4.** Since, then, these nations are reported to have been in their own languages, the narrator nevertheless returns to that time when there was one language for all, and from there he now sets forth what happened so that a diversity of languages arose. *And the whole earth*, it says, *had one lip and one voice for all. And it came to pass, when they moved from the east, they found a plain in the land of Shinar, and dwelt there. And a man said to his neighbor: Come, let us make bricks and bake them in fire. And bricks served them for stone, and bitumen was their mortar; and they said: Come, let us build for ourselves a city and a tower, whose top shall reach to heaven, and let us make our name before we are scattered over the face of all the earth. And the Lord came down to see the city and the tower which the sons of men had built. And the Lord God said: Behold, there is one race and one lip of them all; and this they have begun to do, and now nothing of all that they have set out to do will fail them;*

*come, and going down let us there confound their language, that they may not understand, each one, the voice of his neighbor. And the Lord scattered them thence over the face of all the earth, and they ceased building the city and the tower. For this reason its name was called Confusion, because there the Lord confounded the lips of all the earth; and from there the Lord God scattered them over the face of all the earth*[^29].

This city, which was called Confusion, is Babylon itself, whose wonderful construction even the history of the nations praises. For Babylon is interpreted "confusion." From this it is gathered that that giant Nimrod was its founder, which had been briefly intimated above, where, when Scripture spoke of him, it said that the beginning of his kingdom was Babylon—that is, the city which held the primacy over the rest, where, as in a metropolis, would be the seat of the kingdom; although it was not completed to the full extent that impious pride imagined[^30]. For an excessive height was being planned, which was said to be up to heaven—whether of one tower of his which they were laboring on as the chief among the others, or of all the towers, which were thus signified by the singular number, as one says "soldier" and means thousands of soldiers; as "frog" and "locust"; for thus the multitude of frogs and locusts was named in the plagues with which the Egyptians were struck through Moses[^31].

But what would human and vain presumption have accomplished, however great a height of mass it might have raised toward heaven against God, when it would surpass all the mountains, when it would get beyond the space of this cloudy air[^32]? What harm, in short, would any elevation, whether spiritual or bodily, do to God? Humility builds a safe and true way to heaven, lifting the heart up to the Lord, not against the Lord, as that giant was called *a hunter against the Lord*[^33].

Some, not understanding this, were misled by the Greek ambiguity, so that they did not translate *against the Lord*, but *before the Lord*; for ἐναντίον signifies both "against" and "before." For this word is in the Psalm, *and let us weep before the Lord who made us*[^34]; and this word is also in the book of Job, where it is written, *you have broken out in fury against the Lord*[^35]. So, then, this giant is to be understood as *a hunter against the Lord*. But what is signified here by this name "hunter," if not a deceiver, oppressor, and destroyer of the creatures born of the earth? He was raising, then, with his peoples a tower against God, by which impious pride was signified.

Now an evil disposition is rightly punished, even one to which no effect follows. But what was the kind of punishment itself? Since the dominion of one who commands lies in language, there pride was condemned, so that the man who refused to understand in order to obey God's command was not understood when he gave a command.

Thus that conspiracy was dissolved, since each one withdrew from him whom he did not understand and joined himself only to one with whom he could speak; and through their languages the nations were divided and scattered through the lands, as it pleased God, who did this in ways hidden and incomprehensible to us.

**5.** As for what is written, *and the Lord came down to see the city and the tower which the sons of men had built*—that is, not the sons of God, but that society living according to man, which we call the earthly city—God is not moved from place to place, who is always wholly everywhere, but he is said to *come down* when he does something on earth which, done wonderfully beyond the usual course of nature, in some way shows his presence;

nor does he learn for a time by seeing, who can never be ignorant of anything, but he is said to see and to know for a time what he causes to be seen and known. So that city was not seen in the way in which God caused it to be seen, when he showed how greatly it displeased him.

Yet it can be understood that God came down to that city because his angels, in whom he dwells, came down; so that what was added, *and the Lord God said: behold, there is one race and one lip of them all*, and so forth, and then what was further added, *come, and going down let us there confound their language*[^36], is a recapitulation, showing in what manner was done what had been said, *the Lord came down*.

For if he had already come down, what does this mean, *come, and going down let us confound* (which is understood as said to the angels), unless that he was coming down through the angels, he who was in the descending angels? And he well does not say, "Come, and going down do you confound"; but, *let us there confound their language*—showing that he so works through his ministers that they too are God's co-workers, as the Apostle says, *for we are God's co-workers*[^37].

**6.** 1. That too, when man was made, could be understood of the angels, what was said, *let us make man*, since he did not say, "Let me make"; but because there follows *after our image*, and it is not lawful to believe that man was made after the image of angels, or that the image of angels and of God is the same, it is rightly understood there as the plurality of the Trinity. Yet because this Trinity is one God, even though he had said *let us make*, it says, *and God made man after the image of God*[^38]; he did not say, "the gods made," or, "after the image of the gods."

Here too the same Trinity could be understood, as though the Father said to the Son and the Holy Spirit, *come, and going down let us there confound their language*[^39], if there were anything to forbid the angels from being understood, who rather are suited to come to God by holy movements—that is, by pious thoughts—by which the unchangeable Truth is consulted by them, as an eternal law, in that supernal court of theirs. For they are not the truth to themselves, but, as partakers of the creative Truth, they are moved toward it as toward the fountain of life, that they may receive from it what they do not have of themselves. And this movement by which they come, who do not withdraw, is stable.

Nor does God speak to the angels as we speak to one another, or to God, or to angels, or as the angels themselves speak to us, or as God speaks to us through them, but in his own ineffable way; while to us it is here indicated in our way. For God's more sublime speech, before his deed, is the unchangeable account of his very deed, which has no resounding and passing sound, but a power eternally abiding and operating in time. By this he speaks to the holy angels, but to us, set far away, otherwise.

But when we too grasp something of such speech with our inner ears, we draw near to the angels. So in this work I need not continually give an account of God's utterances. For either the unchangeable Truth speaks ineffably, through itself, to the minds of the rational creature, or it speaks through a changeable creature, whether by spiritual images to our spirit or by bodily voices to our bodily sense.

**6.** 2. Indeed, what was said, *and now nothing of all that they have set out to do will fail them*[^40], was not said by way of affirmation, but as if by way of question, as those who threaten are accustomed to speak, as a certain poet says, *will they not get out their arms and follow from the whole city?*[^41] So, then, it is to be taken as if he had said, "Shall not all the things they have set out to do fail them?" But if it is said in that way, it does not express one who threatens. Yet for the sake of slower readers we added the particle, that is, "not," so as to say, "shall not," since we cannot write the tone of the speaker.

From those three men, then, the sons of Noah, seventy-three nations, or rather, as the reckoning will show, seventy-two, and as many languages began to be throughout the lands, which by increasing also filled the islands. But the number of nations grew much greater than that of languages. For in Africa too we know of very many barbarian nations with one language.

**7.** And that men, when the human race had multiplied, could have crossed by ship to inhabit the islands, who would doubt? But the question concerns every kind of beast which is not under the care of men, nor born from the earth as frogs are, but propagated only by the mingling of male and female—such as wolves and the rest of this kind—how, after the flood, by which all that were not in the ark were destroyed, they could have come to be even in the islands, if they were not restored except from those whose kinds the ark preserved in both sexes.

They can indeed be believed to have crossed to the islands by swimming, but only to the nearest ones. But there are some so far removed from the mainlands that none of the beasts seems to have been able to swim to them. But if men caught them and carried them along with them, and in that way established their kinds where they dwelt, out of zeal for hunting, it is not incredible that this could have happened; though it is not to be denied that, by God's command or permission, they could also have been transported by the work of angels.

But if they arose from the earth according to their first origin, when God said, *let the earth bring forth the living soul*[^42], it appears much more clearly that all the kinds were in the ark not so much for the sake of restoring the animals as for figuring the various nations, on account of the mystery of the Church, if on the islands, to which they could not cross, the earth brought forth many animals.

## Whether the Monstrous Races of Men Are Descended from Adam

**8.** 1. The question is also asked whether it is to be believed that certain monstrous races of men, of which the history of the nations tells, were propagated from the sons of Noah—or rather from that one man from whom they too descended—such as those said to have one eye in the middle of the forehead; some who have their feet turned backward behind their legs; some who have the nature of both sexes, a male right breast and a female left, and who, mating with each other in turn, both beget and bear; others who have no mouths and live only by breathing through the nostrils; others who are a cubit in height, whom the Greeks call Pygmies from the cubit; and elsewhere women who conceive at five years and do not pass the eighth year of life.

Likewise they report that there is a race that have single legs on their feet and do not bend the knee, and are of wonderful swiftness; these they call Sciopodes, because in the heat they lie on their backs on the ground and shade themselves with the shadow of their feet; some without a neck, having their eyes in their shoulders; and the other kinds of men or quasi-men which are depicted in mosaic on the seaside square of Carthage, drawn from books of, as it were, a more curious history. What shall I say of the Cynocephali, whose dog-like heads and very barking proclaim them beasts rather than men?

But it is not necessary to believe in all the kinds of men said to exist. Yet whoever is born anywhere as a man—that is, a rational, mortal animal—however unusual to our senses the form, or color, or motion, or sound of his body may be, or by whatever force, in whatever part, of whatever quality his nature, let no one of the faithful doubt that he draws his origin from that one first-formed man. It is apparent, nevertheless, what nature has established in the greater number, and what is wonderful by its very rarity.

**8.** 2. But whatever account is given of monstrous births of men among us, such an account can be given of certain monstrous races. For God is the creator of all things, who himself knows where and when it is or was fitting for anything to be created, knowing of what parts, whether by likeness or by diversity, he weaves the beauty of the universe. But one who cannot inspect the whole is offended, as it were, by the deformity of a part, because he does not know to what it is suited or to what it is to be referred.

We know men to be born with more than five fingers on hands and feet; and this is a slighter divergence than any other; but yet far be it that anyone be so foolish as to think that the Creator erred in the number of human fingers, even though he does not know why he did this. So even if a greater diversity arises, he knows what he has done, whose works no one justly blames.

At Hippo Diarrhytus there is a man who has, as it were, crescent-shaped feet, and on them only two toes each; and his hands are similar. If there were some race like this, it would be added to that curious and wonderful history. Shall we then on this account deny that this man is descended from that one who was first created?

Androgynes, whom they also call Hermaphrodites, although they are quite rare, are nevertheless hardly ever lacking in any age, in whom both sexes so appear that it is uncertain from which they ought rather to take their name; but the custom of speech has prevailed that they should be named from the better, that is, from the masculine. For no one ever called them Androgynaecae or Hermaphroditae.

Some years ago, certainly within our own memory, there was born in the East a man double in his upper members, single in his lower. For there were two heads, two chests, four hands, but one belly and two feet, as for a single man; and he lived so long that report drew many to see him.

But who could mention all the human offspring far unlike those of whom it is most certain that they were born?

As, then, these cannot be denied to draw their origin from that one man, so whatever races, in the diversities of their bodies, are reported to have, as it were, deviated from the usual course of nature which the majority and almost all keep—if they are included in that definition, that they are rational and mortal animals—it must be confessed that they draw their stock from that same one first father of all, provided that the things handed down about the variety of those nations, and their great diversity from one another and from us, are true.

For if we did not know that apes and long-tailed monkeys and sphinxes are not men but beasts, those historians, glorying in their curiosity, could, with unpunished vanity, lie to us about them as though they were some races of men[^43]. But if those of whom those marvels are recorded are men—what if God for this reason willed to create even some races in this way, lest in these monsters which must be born of men among us we should think that his wisdom, by which he fashions human nature, had erred, as the craft of some less perfect workman? It ought not, therefore, to seem absurd to us that, just as in any individual races there are certain monsters of men, so in the whole human race there are certain monstrous races.

Therefore, to conclude this question step by step and cautiously: either the things written of this sort about certain races are altogether nothing; or if they exist, they are not men; or, if they are men, they are descended from Adam.

**9.** But that there are also antipodes—that is, men on the opposite part of the earth, where the sun rises when it sets for us, who tread with their feet opposite to ours—this is on no account to be believed. Nor do those who affirm this say that they have learned it by any historical knowledge, but they conjecture it, as it were, by reasoning, on the ground that the earth is suspended within the vault of heaven, and that the world has the same place as both its lowest and its middle; and from this they suppose that the other part of the earth, which is below, cannot lack the habitation of men.

And they do not consider that, even if the world is believed to be of a globed and round figure, or is shown to be so by some reasoning, it nevertheless does not follow that on that part too the earth is bare of the mass of waters; and then, even if it is bare, that it is not for that reason at once necessary that it have men. Since in no way does that Scripture lie, which gains belief for its account of past events by the fact that its predictions are fulfilled; and it is too absurd to say that some men could have sailed, crossing the immensity of the Ocean, from this part to that, and arrived, so that there too the human race should be established from that one first man.

## [The Line of Generations from Shem]

Therefore among those peoples of men of that time, who are gathered to have been divided into seventy-two nations and as many languages, let us seek, if we can find it, that city of God sojourning on earth, which was brought down to the flood and the ark and is shown to have persevered in the sons of Noah through their blessings—chiefly in the greatest, who was called Shem—since Japheth was so blessed as to dwell in the houses of Shem, his brother.

**10.** 1. The line of generations, then, must be traced from Shem himself, that it may show the city of God after the flood, as the line of generations from him who was called Seth showed it before the flood. For this reason, then, divine Scripture, when it had shown the earthly city in Babylon—that is, in confusion—returns by recapitulation to the patriarch Shem and begins from him the generations down to Abraham, mentioning also the number of years, at what age each begot a son belonging to this line and how long he lived. Here certainly is to be recognized what I promised before, so that it may appear why it was said of the sons of Eber, *the name of one was Peleg, because in his days the earth was divided*[^44]. For what else is to be understood by the earth being divided than by the diversity of languages?

Omitting, then, the other sons of Shem who do not pertain to this matter, those are connected in the order of generations through whom one may arrive at Abraham; just as before the flood those were connected through whom one might arrive, by the generations propagated from that son of Adam who was called Seth, at Noah. So, then, this string of generations begins: *and these are the generations of Shem. Shem was a son of a hundred years when he begot Arphaxad, in the second year after the flood. And Shem lived, after he begot Arphaxad, five hundred years, and begot sons and daughters, and died*[^45].

Thus it goes on through the rest, saying in what year of his life each begot a son belonging to this order of generations which extends to Abraham, and how many years he afterward lived, indicating that he begot sons and daughters; so that we may understand whence the peoples could have grown,

lest, occupied with the few men who are mentioned, we should childishly hesitate as to whence such great expanses of lands and kingdoms could have been filled from the stock of Shem, especially on account of the kingdom of the Assyrians, whence that Ninus, the subduer of the eastern peoples, reigned everywhere with vast prosperity and propagated to his posterity a very broad and very firmly founded kingdom, which would be carried on for a long time.

**10.** 2. But we, lest we delay longer than is needful, set down to be recorded in this order only—not how many years each one in this line of generations lived, but in what year of his life he begot a son—so that we may both reckon the number of years from the past flood down to Abraham and briefly and in passing touch on other matters, besides those in which necessity compels us to delay.

In the second year after the flood, then, Shem begot Arphaxad; Arphaxad, when he was a hundred and thirty-five years old, begot Cainan; he, when he was a hundred and thirty, begot Shelah; furthermore Shelah himself was of the same number of years when he begot Eber; and Eber was passing his hundred and thirty-fourth year when he begot Peleg, in whose days the earth was divided; Peleg himself lived a hundred and thirty and begot Reu; and Reu a hundred and thirty-two, and begot Serug; and Serug a hundred and thirty, and begot Nahor; and Nahor seventy-nine, and begot Terah; and Terah seventy, and begot Abram[^46], whom God afterward named Abraham, changing his name[^47]. So the years from the flood down to Abraham come to one thousand and seventy-two according to the common edition, that is, of the Septuagint translators. But in the Hebrew codices they report that far fewer years are found, of which they give either no account or a very difficult one.

## Whether the City of God Existed from Noah to Abraham

**10.** 3. When, then, we seek the city of God among those seventy-two nations, we cannot affirm that at the time when they had one lip—that is, one speech—the human race was already estranged from the worship of the true God, so that true piety remained only in those generations which descend from the seed of Shem through Arphaxad and lead to Abraham; but from that pride of building a tower up to heaven, by which impious elevation is signified, there appeared the city—that is, the society—of the impious. Whether, then, it did not exist before, or lay hidden, or rather both persisted—the pious, that is, in the two sons of Noah who were blessed and their posterity, but the impious in him who was cursed and his offspring, where there also arose a *giant hunter against the Lord*—it is not easy to judge.

For perhaps—what is indeed more credible—both in the sons of those two there were already then, before Babylonia had begun to be established, despisers of God, and in the sons of Ham there were worshipers of God; yet it is to be believed that neither kind of men was ever lacking on earth. Indeed, even when it was said, *all have turned aside, together they have become unprofitable; there is none that does good, no, not one*[^48], in both Psalms where these words occur, this too is read: *shall not all who work iniquity know, who devour my people as they eat bread?*[^49]. There was, then, even then a people of God.

Hence what was said, *there is none that does good, no, not one*, was said of the sons of men, not of the sons of God. For it was prefaced, *God looked down from heaven upon the sons of men, to see if there were one understanding or seeking after God*[^50], and then those words were added which show that all the *sons of men*—that is, those belonging to the city which lives according to man, not according to God—are reprobate.

## Whether the Hebrew Language Came from Eber

**11.** 1. Wherefore, just as, though there was one language for all, the sons of pestilence were not on that account lacking—for before the flood too there was one language, and yet all except the one house of the just Noah deserved to be destroyed by the flood—so, when by the deserts of more elevated impiety the nations were punished and divided by the diversity of languages, and the city of the impious received the name of "confusion," that is, was called Babylon, the house of Eber was not lacking, in which what had previously been the language of all might remain.

Hence, as I mentioned above[^51], when the sons of Shem began to be enumerated, who each begot individual nations, Eber was commended first, although he is Shem's great-great-grandson—that is, he is found to have sprung fifth from him.

Because, then, this language remained in his family, the other nations being divided into other languages—the language which is not without reason believed to have been previously common to the human race—it was thereafter called Hebrew. For then there was need to distinguish it from the other languages by a proper name, just as the others too were called by proper names. But when it was one, it was called nothing other than human language or human speech, by which alone the whole human race spoke.

**11.** 2. Someone may say, "If in the days of Peleg, son of Eber, the earth was divided through the languages—that is, the men who were then on the earth—that language which had previously been common to all ought rather to have been called from his name." But it must be understood that Eber himself imposed such a name on his son, so that he was called Peleg, which is interpreted "division," for the reason that he was born to him when the earth was divided through the languages—that is, at that very time, so that this is what was said, *in his days the earth was divided*[^52]. For unless Eber were still living when the multitude of languages came about, the language which was able to remain with him would not receive its name from his name.

And therefore it is to be believed that this very language was that first common one, since that multiplication and change of languages came from punishment, and surely the people of God ought to have been free from this punishment.

Nor is it without reason that this is the language which Abraham held, and which he could not pass on to all his sons, but only to those who, propagated through Jacob and coalescing more notably and eminently into the people of God, were able to have the Covenants of God and the stock of Christ. Nor did Eber himself pour that same language into his whole offspring, but only into that one whose generations are brought down to Abraham.

Wherefore, even though it is not evidently expressed that there was some pious race of men when Babylonia was being founded by the impious, this obscurity did not serve to defraud the inquirer, but rather to exercise his attention. For when one reads that there was at first one language for all, and Eber is commended before all the sons of Shem, although he springs fifth from him, and the language is called Hebrew which the authority of the Patriarchs and Prophets preserved not only in their discourses but also in the sacred Scriptures—

then surely, when it is asked, in the division of the languages, where that language could have remained which had previously been common—and where, without any doubt, it did remain, there was not that punishment which was effected by the change of languages—what else occurs to us than that it remained in the nation of him from whose name it received its name, and that no small trace of the justice of this nation appeared in this, that, when the other nations were chastised by the change of languages, such a punishment did not reach this one?

## At What Time the Diversity of Languages Arose

**11.** 3. But this still troubles us: how could Eber and his son Peleg each make individual nations, if one language remained to them both. And certainly there is one Hebrew nation, propagated from Eber down to Abraham, and through him thereafter, until the great people of Israel came to be. How then did all the sons of the three sons of Noah who were mentioned make individual nations, if Eber and Peleg did not each make one? Doubtless this is more probable, that that giant Nimrod too made his own nation, but on account of the excellence of his dominion and of his body was named separately and more eminently, so that the number of seventy-two nations and languages may stand. But Peleg was mentioned, not because he made a nation (for his nation is that same Hebrew one and his language the same), but on account of the notable time, that in his days the earth was divided.

Nor ought it to trouble us how the giant Nimrod could meet that age at which Babylon was founded and the confusion of languages made, and from this the division of the nations. For it is not the case that, because Eber is sixth from Noah, while he is fourth, they could not therefore have come together at the same time by living.

For this happened when men lived longer where the generations are fewer, less where they are more; or were born later where they are fewer, earlier where they are more.

It must indeed be understood that, when the earth was divided, the other sons of the sons of Noah who are mentioned as fathers of nations were not only already born, but were also of such an age as to have numerous families worthy of the names of nations. Hence it is by no means to be thought that they were begotten in the order in which they are read to be mentioned. Otherwise, how could the twelve sons of Joktan, who was another son of Eber, brother of Peleg, already make nations, if Joktan was born after his brother Peleg, as he is mentioned after him—since at the time when Peleg was born the earth was divided? Accordingly it must be understood that, though Peleg was named first, Joktan, his brother, was born long after him, Joktan whose twelve sons already had families so large that they could be divided into their own languages. For one who was later in age could be mentioned first, just as earlier there were mentioned, of those begotten of the three sons of Noah, the sons of Japheth, who was the youngest of them; then the sons of Ham, who was the middle one; lastly the sons of Shem, who was the first and greatest.

Now the names of those nations partly remained, so that even today it appears whence they were derived—as from Asshur the Assyrians and from Eber the Hebrews; partly they were changed by the antiquity of time, so that even the most learned men, scrutinizing the most ancient histories, have scarcely been able to find the origins, not of all, but of some of these nations. For as to the fact that from Ham's son, who was called Mizraim, the Egyptians are said to have sprung, no origin of the name resounds here; just as none does for the Ethiopians, who are said to belong to that son of Ham who was called Cush. And if all things are considered, more names appear changed than remaining.

## The Generations from Shem to Abraham Are Enumerated

**12.** Now let us look at the course of the city of God even from that point of time which was made in the father Abraham, whence the knowledge of it begins to be more evident, and where clearer divine promises are read, which we now see being fulfilled in Christ. As, then, we have learned from the indication of Holy Scripture, Abraham was born in the region of the Chaldeans[^53], which land belonged to the Assyrian kingdom.

But among the Chaldeans even then impious superstitions already prevailed, as among the other nations. There was, then, one house of Terah, from whom Abraham was born, in which there was worship of the one true God, and—as is credible—in which alone the Hebrew language too had already remained; although he himself, like the now more manifest people of God in Egypt, is reported by the account of Joshua the son of Nun to have served strange gods in Mesopotamia[^54], the rest of the offspring of that Eber gradually flowing off into other languages and other nations.

Accordingly, just as through the flood of waters one house of Noah had remained to restore the human race, so in the flood of many superstitions throughout the whole world one house of Terah had remained, in which the planting of the city of God was preserved. Finally, as there, when the generations down to Noah had been enumerated above together with the numbers of the years, and the cause of the flood had been set forth, before God began to speak to Noah about building the ark, it is said, *now these are the generations of Noah*[^55]:

so here too, when the generations have been enumerated from him who was called Shem, son of Noah, down to Abraham, then a notable point is similarly set down, so that it says, *these are the generations of Terah. Terah begot Abram and Nahor and Haran, and Haran begot Lot. And Haran died in the presence of Terah his father, in the land in which he was born, in the region of the Chaldeans. And Abram and Nahor took wives for themselves; the name of Abram's wife was Sarai, and the name of Nahor's wife was Milcah, the daughter of Haran*[^56]. This Haran was the father of Milcah and the father of Iscah, which Iscah is believed to be the same as Sarah, Abraham's wife.

**13.** Then it is narrated how Terah with his people left the region of the Chaldeans and came into Mesopotamia and dwelt in Haran. But it is silent about one of his sons, who was called Nahor, as though he did not take him along with him. For it is narrated thus: *and Terah took Abram his son and Lot the son of Haran, his grandson, and Sarai his daughter-in-law, the wife of Abram his son, and led them out of the region of the Chaldeans to go into the land of Canaan; and he came to Haran and dwelt there*[^57]. Nowhere here is Nahor named, nor his wife Milcah. But we find afterward, when Abraham sent his servant to take a wife for his son Isaac, this written: *and the servant took ten camels of his master's camels and of all his master's goods with him, and arising he set out for Mesopotamia, to the city of Nahor*[^58].

By this and other testimonies of this sacred history it is shown that Nahor too, Abraham's brother, went out of the region of the Chaldeans and set up his dwelling in Mesopotamia, where Abraham had dwelt with his father. Why, then, did Scripture not mention him, when Terah set out from the Chaldean nation with his people and dwelt in Mesopotamia—where not only his son Abraham, but also his daughter-in-law Sarah and his grandson Lot are mentioned, as those whom he led along with him? Why, do we suppose, unless perhaps because he had fallen away from his father's and brother's piety and had clung to the superstition of the Chaldeans, and afterward emigrated from there as well, whether by repenting or by suffering persecution, because he was held suspect?

For in the book which is entitled Judith, when Holofernes, the enemy of the Israelites, asked what that nation was, whether war was to be made against it, Achior, leader of the Ammonites, answered him thus: *let our lord hear a word from the mouth of his servant, and I will report to you the truth about the people that dwells near you, in this hill country, and no lie shall come out of the mouth of your servant. For this people is descended from the Chaldeans, and they formerly dwelt in Mesopotamia, because they were unwilling to follow the gods of their fathers, who were glorious in the land of the Chaldeans, but turned aside from the way of their parents and worshiped the God of heaven, whom they had come to know; and their fathers cast them out from the presence of their gods, and they fled to Mesopotamia and dwelt there many days. And their God told them to go out from their dwelling and go into the land of Canaan, and there they dwelt*[^59], and the rest that Achior the Ammonite relates. Hence it is manifest that the house of Terah suffered persecution from the Chaldeans for the sake of the true piety by which the one and true God was worshiped by them.

**14.** Now when Terah had died in Mesopotamia, where he is said to have lived two hundred and five years, the promises of God made to Abraham now begin to be indicated, for it is written thus: *and the days of Terah in Haran were two hundred and five years, and Terah died in Haran*[^60]. But this is not to be taken as though he spent all these days there, but that he completed all the days of his life, which were two hundred and five years, there; otherwise it would not be known how many years Terah lived, since it is not read in what year of his life he came to Haran; and it is absurd to suppose that, in this line of generations, where it is diligently mentioned how many years each one lived, the number of this man's years of life alone was not committed to memory. For as to the fact that the years of certain men whom that same Scripture mentions are passed over in silence, they are not in this order, in which the reckoning of times is woven by the death of those who beget and the succession of those begotten. But this order, which is directed from Adam down to Noah and thence down to Abraham, contains no one without the number of his years of life.

## The Years of Abraham's Life Are Reckoned

**15.** 1. But as for the fact that, the death of Terah, Abraham's father, having been mentioned, it is then read, *and the Lord said to Abram: go out from your land and from your kindred and from your father's house*[^61], and so forth—it is not because this follows in the discourse of the book that it is to be supposed to follow also in the time of the events. For if it is so, there will be an insoluble question. For after these words of God, which were made to Abraham, Scripture speaks thus: *and Abram went out, as the Lord had spoken to him, and Lot went with him. Now Abram was seventy-five years old when he went out from Haran*[^62]. How can this be true, if he went out from Haran after the death of his father? For when Terah was seventy years old, as was indicated above, he begot Abraham; and if to that number are added the seventy-five years that Abraham was passing when he went out from Haran, they come to one hundred and forty-five years. Terah, then, was of so many years when Abraham went out from that city of Mesopotamia; for he was passing the seventy-fifth year of his age, and for this reason his father, who had begotten him in his seventieth year, was passing, as has been said, his hundred and forty-fifth. He did not, then, go out from there after his father's death—that is, after the two hundred and five years that his father lived; but the year of his departure from that place, since it was his seventy-fifth, was without doubt, it is gathered, the hundred and forty-fifth of his father, who had begotten him in his seventieth year.

And for this reason it must be understood that Scripture, in its usual manner, returned to a time which that narrative had already passed; just as above, when it had mentioned the sons of the sons of Noah, it said that they were in their languages and nations[^63], and yet afterward, as though this too followed in the order of times, it says, *and the whole earth had one lip and one voice for all*[^64].

How, then, were they according to their nations and according to their languages, if there was one for all, unless because the narrative returned by recapitulation to what had already passed? So, then, here too, when it had been said, *and the days of Terah in Haran were two hundred and five years, and Terah died in Haran*[^65], then Scripture, returning to that which it had passed over for this reason, that first what had been begun about Terah might be completed, says, *and the Lord said to Abram: go out from your land*, and so forth. After which words of God it is added, *and Abram went out, as the Lord had spoken to him, and Lot went with him. Now Abram was seventy-five years old when he went out from Haran*[^66]. This, then, was done when his father was passing the hundred and forty-fifth year of his age; for it was then his own seventy-fifth. But this question is also solved otherwise, so that the seventy-five years of Abraham, when he went out from Haran, are reckoned from the time when he was delivered from the fire of the Chaldeans, not from when he was born, as though he should be held to have been born rather then.

**15.** 2. But the blessed Stephen, in the *Acts of the Apostles*, when he narrated these things, says, *the God of glory appeared to our father Abraham, when he was in Mesopotamia, before he dwelt in Haran, and said to him: go out from your land and from your kindred and from your father's house, and come into the land which I will show you*. According to these words of Stephen, God spoke to Abraham not after his father's death, who certainly died in Haran, where his son too dwelt with him, but before he dwelt in that same city, while he was already in Mesopotamia.

He had already, then, gone out from the Chaldeans. So what Stephen adds, *then Abraham went out from the land of the Chaldeans and dwelt in Haran*, does not concern what was done after God spoke to him (for he did not go out from the land of the Chaldeans after those words of God, since he says God spoke to him while he was in Mesopotamia), but pertains to that whole time which he calls *then*—that is, "from the time when" *he went out from the Chaldeans and dwelt in Haran*. Likewise what follows, *and from there, after his father died, he removed him into this land, in which you now dwell, and your fathers*[^67]—he does not say, "after his father died, he went out from Haran"; but, from there "he removed him here, after his father died."

It must be understood, therefore, that God spoke to Abraham while he was in Mesopotamia, before he dwelt in Haran; but that he came to Haran with his father, keeping God's command with him, and went out from there in his own seventy-fifth year, but his father's hundred and forty-fifth. But his removal into the land of Canaan, not his departure from Haran, is what Stephen says was done after his father's death, because his father was already dead when he bought the land, of which there he began to be the possessor as his own property.

But as to the fact that, when he was already established in Mesopotamia—that is, already gone out from the land of the Chaldeans—God says, *go out from your land and from your kindred and from your father's house*, this is said not that he should cast his body out from there, which he had already done, but that he should tear away his mind.

For he had not gone out from there in mind if he was held by hope of returning and by desire, which hope and desire had to be cut off at God's command and with his help, and by Abraham's obedience. It is not incredibly supposed, indeed, that, since Nahor afterward followed his father, it was then that Abraham fulfilled the Lord's command, so that with Sarah his wife and Lot his brother's son he went out from Haran.

## At What Time God's Promises Were Made to Abraham

**16.** Now the promises of God made to Abraham are to be considered. For in these the clearer oracles of our God—that is, of the true God—began to appear concerning the people of the pious, which prophetic authority foretold. The first of these is read thus: *and the Lord said to Abram: go out from your land and from your kindred and from your father's house, and go into the land which I will show you; and I will make you into a great nation, and I will bless you and magnify your name, and you shall be blessed, and I will bless those who bless you and curse those who curse you, and in you shall all the tribes of the earth be blessed*[^68].

It must be noted, then, that two things were promised to Abraham: one, namely, that his seed should possess the land of Canaan, which is signified where it was said, *go into the land which I will show you, and I will make you into a great nation*; but the other far more excellent, concerning not the carnal but the spiritual seed, through which he is the father not of the one Israelite nation, but of all the nations which follow the footsteps of his faith, which began to be promised in these words: *and in you shall all the tribes of the earth be blessed*. Eusebius thinks this promise was made in the seventy-fifth year of Abraham's age[^69], as though Abraham went out from Haran as soon as it was made; since Scripture cannot be contradicted, where it is read, *Abram was seventy-five years old when he went out from Haran*[^70]. But if this promise was made in that year, Abraham was surely already lingering in Haran with his father. For he could not go out from there unless he had first dwelt there. Is Stephen, then, contradicted, who says, *the God of glory appeared to our father Abraham, when he was in Mesopotamia, before he dwelt in Haran*[^71]? But it must be understood that all these things were done in the same year—both God's promise, before Abraham dwelt in Haran, and his dwelling in Haran and his departure from there; not only because Eusebius in the *Chronicle* reckons from the year of this promise and shows that the exodus from Egypt, when the law was given, was after four hundred and thirty years, but also because the apostle Paul mentions this.

**17.** At the same time there were eminent kingdoms of the nations, in which the city of the earthborn—that is, the society of men living according to man, under the dominion of the deserter angels—stood out more notably: three kingdoms, namely, of the Sicyonians, the Egyptians, and the Assyrians. But that of the Assyrians was much more powerful and lofty. For that king Ninus, the son of Belus, had subdued the peoples of all Asia except India.

By "Asia" I now mean not that part which is one province of this greater Asia, but that which is called "all Asia," which some have placed in one of the two parts of the whole world, but most in a third part, so that the parts are Asia, Europe, and Africa; which they did not by an equal division. For this part which is called Asia extends from the south through the east up to the north; but Europe from the north up to the west, and thence Africa from the west up to the south. Hence two—Europe and Africa—seem to hold half the world, but the other half Asia alone. But the reason those two parts were made is that between both there enters from the Ocean whatever waters flow between the lands; and this makes the great sea for us.

Wherefore, if you divide the world into two parts, of the East and of the West, Asia will be in one, but in the other Europe and Africa. Hence, of the three kingdoms which then excelled, that of the Sicyonians was not under the Assyrians, because they are in Europe; but how was that of the Egyptians not subject to them, by whom all Asia was held, the Indians alone, as is reported, excepted?

In Assyria, then, the dominion of the impious city had prevailed; its head was that Babylon, whose name—that is, "confusion"—is most fitting for the city of the earthborn. There Ninus already reigned after the death of his father Belus, who had first reigned there for sixty-five years. But his son Ninus, who succeeded his deceased father in the kingdom, reigned fifty-two years, and had been in the kingdom forty-three years when Abraham was born, which was about the one thousand two hundredth year before the founding of Rome, as it were another Babylon in the west.

## Abraham Proceeded into Canaan

**18.** Abraham, then, having gone out from Haran in the seventy-fifth year of his age, but the hundred and forty-fifth of his father, proceeded with Lot his brother's son and Sarah his wife into the land of Canaan and came as far as Shechem, where he again received a divine oracle, of which it is written thus: *and the Lord appeared to Abram, and said to him: to your seed I will give this land*[^72]. Nothing is said here of that seed in which he was made the father of all the nations, but only of that one of which he is the father of the one Israelite nation; for by this seed that land was possessed.

**19.** Then, having built an altar there and called upon God, Abraham set out from there and dwelt in the wilderness, and from there was compelled by the necessity of famine to go into Egypt. There he called his wife his sister, in no way lying; for this too was true, because she was near in blood; just as Lot too, by the same kinship, being his brother's son, was called his brother.

And so he kept silent about her being his wife, he did not deny it, committing the guarding of his wife's chastity to God and, as a man, guarding against human snares; since, if he did not guard against the danger as far as it could be guarded against, he would rather be tempting God than hoping in God[^73]. Of this matter we have said enough against the slanderer Faustus the Manichee[^74].

Finally, what Abraham presumed of the Lord came to pass. For Pharaoh, king of Egypt, who had taken her to himself as a wife, being grievously afflicted, gave her back to her husband. And here far be it that we should believe she was defiled by another's embrace, because it is much more credible that Pharaoh was not permitted to do this by his great afflictions.

**20.** When Abraham, then, had returned from Egypt to the place whence he had come, then Lot, his brother's son, departed from him into the land of Sodom, their love unimpaired. For they had become rich and had begun to have many shepherds of their flocks, and when these quarreled among themselves, they in this way avoided a contentious discord of their households. For from this, as human things go, some quarrel could arise even between themselves.

Accordingly, these are the words of Abraham, guarding against this evil, to Lot: *let there be no quarrel between me and you, and between my shepherds and your shepherds, for we are men and brothers. Behold, is not the whole land before you? Depart from me: if you go to the left, I will go to the right; or if you go to the right, I will go to the left*[^75]. Hence perhaps arose that peaceable custom among men, that when something earthly is to be divided, the elder divides and the younger chooses[^76].

## Again a Promise of God Was Made to Abraham

**21.** When, then, Abraham and Lot had parted and were dwelling separately by the necessity of supporting their household, not by the foulness of discord, and Abraham was in the land of Canaan but Lot was in Sodom, the Lord said to Abraham in a third oracle: *lift up your eyes and look from the place where you now are, to the north and the south and the east and the sea, for all the land which you see I will give to you and to your seed forever, and I will make your seed like the sand of the earth. If anyone can number the sand of the earth, your seed too shall be numbered. Arise, walk through the land in its length and in its breadth, for I will give it to you*[^77].

Whether in this promise there is also that one by which he was made the father of all the nations does not evidently appear. For this may seem to pertain to it: *and I will make your seed like the sand of the earth*, which was said in that figure of speech which the Greeks call hyperbole[^78]; which is of course figurative, not proper. Yet that Scripture is wont to use this, as the other figures, no one who has learned it doubts. Now this figure—that is, manner of speech—occurs when what is said is far greater than what is signified by what is said.

For who does not see how incomparably greater the number of the sand is than the number of all men can be, from Adam himself down to the end of the age? How much greater, then, than the seed of Abraham—not only that which pertains to the Israelite nation, but also that which is, and will be, according to the imitation of his faith, throughout the whole world among all nations? Which seed, in comparison with the multitude of the impious, is surely few; although these few too make up an innumerable multitude of their own, which was signified by hyperbole through the sand of the earth. To be sure, this multitude which is promised to Abraham is not innumerable to God, but to men; but to God not even the sand of the earth is.

Accordingly, because not only the Israelite nation, but the whole seed of Abraham—where there is also the promise of many sons, not according to the flesh but according to the spirit—is more fittingly compared to the multitude of the sand, the promise of both things can here be understood to have been made. But the reason we said it does not evidently appear is that the multitude even of that one nation, which was born according to the flesh from Abraham through his grandson Jacob, grew so great as to fill almost all the parts of the world. And therefore it too could be compared by hyperbole to the multitude of the sand, because this alone too is innumerable to man.

That that land alone is signified which was called Canaan, no one doubts. But what was said, *I will give it to you and to your seed forever*, may trouble some, if they understand *forever* as "to eternity." But if they take *forever* in this place as we faithfully hold that the beginning of the age to come starts from the end of the present one, nothing will trouble them; because, even though the Israelites were expelled from Jerusalem, they nevertheless remain in other cities of the land of Canaan, and will remain until the end; and when that whole land is inhabited by Christians, this too is the seed of Abraham.

## Abraham Is Blessed by Melchizedek

**22.** Having received this response of promise, Abraham migrated and remained in another place of the same land, by the oak of Mamre, which was at Hebron[^79]. Then, when five kings were waging war against four, and the Sodomites being defeated, Lot too was captured, Abraham delivered him from the enemies who had fallen upon Sodom, having brought into the battle with him three hundred and eighteen of his home-born servants, and won the victory for the kings of Sodom, and was willing to carry off none of the spoils, although the king for whom he had conquered offered them.

But he was clearly then blessed by Melchizedek, who was priest of God Most High[^80]; about whom, in the *Epistle* which is entitled *to the Hebrews*—which many say is the apostle Paul's, but some deny—many great things are written[^81]. For there first appeared the sacrifice which is now offered to God by Christians throughout the whole world, and there is fulfilled that which, long after this was done, is said by prophecy to Christ, who was as yet to come in the flesh: *you are a priest forever according to the order of Melchizedek*[^82]; not, that is, according to the order of Aaron, which order was to be taken away when the things appeared which were prefigured by those shadows.

**23.** Then too the word of the Lord came to Abraham in a vision. And when he promised him protection and a very great reward, Abraham, anxious about his posterity, said that a certain Eliezer, his home-born servant, would be his heir; and at once an heir was promised him—not that home-born servant, but one who was to come forth from Abraham himself—and again an innumerable seed, not like the sand of the earth, but the stars of heaven[^83]; where it rather seems to me that a posterity sublime in heavenly happiness is promised.

For as far as multitude is concerned, what are the stars of heaven compared to the sand of the earth? Unless someone says that this comparison too is like it in this respect, that the stars also cannot be numbered, since it is not to be believed that all of them can be seen. For the more sharply one looks, the more he sees. Hence it is rightly supposed that some are hidden even from those who see most keenly, besides those stars which are reported to rise and set in another part of the world most remote from us. Finally, whoever boast that they have comprehended and recorded the whole number of the stars—such as Aratus or Eudoxus, or whatever others there are—the authority of this book holds them in contempt.

Here, to be sure, that statement is set down which the Apostle recalls for the sake of commending the grace of God: *Abraham believed God, and it was reckoned to him as righteousness*[^84]; lest the circumcision should glory and refuse to admit the uncircumcised nations to the faith of Christ. For when this was done, that faith was reckoned to the believing Abraham as righteousness, he had not yet been circumcised.

**24.** 1. In the same vision, when God spoke to him, he also said this to him: *I am the God who brought you out of the region of the Chaldeans, to give you this land, that you may be its heir*. And when Abraham had asked by what he might know that he would be its heir, God said to him: *take me a three-year-old heifer and a three-year-old she-goat and a three-year-old ram and a turtledove and a pigeon. And he took all these for him and divided them in the middle and laid them facing one another; but the birds he did not divide. And the birds came down*, as it is written, *upon the bodies that had been divided, and Abram sat down with them. But about sunset a dread fell upon Abram, and behold, a great dark fear fell upon him; and it was said to Abram: knowing you shall know that your seed shall be a sojourner in a land not its own, and they shall reduce them to slavery and afflict them for four hundred years; but the nation whom they shall serve I will judge. And after this they shall come out here with much baggage. But you shall go to your fathers in peace, nourished in a good old age. And in the fourth generation they shall return here. For the sins of the Amorites are not yet filled up until now. But when the sun was already at its setting, a flame arose, and behold a smoking furnace and torches of fire, which passed through those divided pieces. On that day the Lord God ordained a covenant with Abram, saying: to your seed I will give this land, from the river of Egypt to the great river, the river Euphrates—the Kenites and the Kenizzites and the Kadmonites and the Hittites and the Perizzites and the Rephaim and the Amorites and the Canaanites and the Hivites and the Girgashites and the Jebusites*[^85].

**24.** 2. All these things were done and said divinely in the vision; to discuss each of them in detail would be long and exceeds the intention of this work. We ought, then, to know what is enough. After it was said that Abraham believed God and it was reckoned to him as righteousness, he did not fail in faith when he said, *Lord God, by what shall I know that I shall be its heir?*[^86] for the inheritance of that land had been promised.

For he does not say, "Whence shall I know?" as though he did not yet believe; but he says, *by what shall I know?* so that to the thing which he had believed some likeness might be applied, by which its manner might be recognized; just as it is not distrust on the part of the Virgin Mary that she says, *how shall this be, since I know not a man?* for she was sure of what was to be; she was inquiring into the manner in which it would be done, and when she had asked this, she heard[^87]. And so here too a likeness was given, from the animals—the heifer and the she-goat and the ram and the two birds, the turtledove and the pigeon—so that by these he might know what was to be, which he no longer doubted would be.

Whether, then, by the heifer was signified the people placed under the yoke of the law; by the she-goat the same people about to be a sinner; by the ram the same people about also to reign (which animals are called three-year-old for this reason, that, since there are notable points of times from Adam to Noah, and thence to Abraham, and thence to David, who, Saul being rejected, was the first founded in the kingdom of the Israelite nation by the will of the Lord, in this third order, which extends from Abraham to David, that people grew up as though bearing a third age)—or whether these signify something else more fitting:

in no way, nevertheless, would I doubt that the spiritual were prefigured in it by the addition of the turtledove and the pigeon. And therefore it was said, *but the birds he did not divide*, since the carnal are divided among themselves, but the spiritual in no way, whether they withdraw themselves from the busy ways of men, like the turtledove, or live among them, like the pigeon; yet both birds are simple and harmless, signifying that even in that Israelite people, to whom that land was to be given, there would be undivided sons of the promise and heirs of the kingdom, destined to abide in eternal happiness.

But the birds coming down upon the bodies which had been divided indicate not anything good, but the spirits of this air, seeking a certain food of their own from the division of the carnal. But that Abraham sat down with them signifies that even among those divisions of the carnal there would be true faithful who would persevere to the end. And that about sunset a dread fell upon Abraham, and a great dark fear, signifies that about the end of this age there would be a great disturbance and tribulation of the faithful, of which the Lord says in the Gospel, *for then there shall be great tribulation, such as has not been from the beginning*[^88].

**24.** 3. But as for what was said to Abraham, *knowing you shall know that your seed shall be a sojourner in a land not its own, and they shall reduce them to slavery and afflict them for four hundred years*[^89], this was most plainly prophesied of the people of Israel, who were to be in slavery in Egypt; not that that people was to spend four hundred years in that same slavery under the afflicting Egyptians, but it was foretold that this would come to pass within those four hundred years.

For just as it was written of Terah, Abraham's father, *and the days of Terah in Haran were two hundred and five years*[^90], not because all were spent there, but because they were completed there: so here too, for this reason, it was inserted, *and they shall reduce them to slavery and afflict them for four hundred years*, since that number was completed in that affliction, not because the whole was spent there. Four hundred years, to be sure, are said for the fullness of the number, although they are somewhat more, whether they are reckoned from the time when these things were promised to Abraham, or from when Isaac was born, on account of the "seed" of Abraham, of whom these things are foretold.

For they are reckoned, as we said above, from the seventy-fifth year of Abraham, when the first promise was made to him, down to the exodus of Israel from Egypt, four hundred and thirty years; which the Apostle mentions thus: *but this I say*, he says: *the law, which came four hundred and thirty years later, does not annul a covenant confirmed by God, so as to make the promise void*[^91]. These four hundred and thirty years, then, could already be called four hundred, since they are not much more: how much more so when some of this number had already passed, when those things were shown and said to Abraham in the vision, or when Isaac was born to his hundred-year-old father, twenty-five years after the first promise, when of these four hundred and thirty there already remained four hundred and five, which God willed to call four hundred. And no one would doubt that the rest which follow in the words of God foretelling pertain to the Israelite people.

**24.** 4. But what is added, *but when the sun was already at its setting, a flame arose, and behold a smoking furnace and torches of fire, which passed through those divided pieces*[^92], signifies that at the end of the age the carnal are to be judged by fire. For just as the affliction of the city of God, such as never was before, which is hoped to come under Antichrist, is signified by Abraham's dark fear about sunset—that is, the end of the age now drawing near—so at sunset, that is, at the very end now, by that fire is signified the day of judgment, separating the carnal who are to be saved through fire and those to be damned in the fire.

Then the covenant made with Abraham clearly designates and names the land of Canaan, with eleven nations in it, from the river of Egypt to the great river Euphrates. Not, then, from the great river of Egypt, that is, the Nile, but from the small one which divides between Egypt and Palestine, where the city of Rhinocorura is.

## There Was No Crime of Abraham with Hagar

**25.** From here now follow the times of Abraham's sons, the one by Hagar the maidservant, the other by Sarah the free woman, of whom we have already spoken in the previous book[^93]. But as for the deed itself, in no way is a crime to be branded upon Abraham concerning this concubine.

For he used her to beget offspring, not to satisfy lust, and not in insult to his wife, but rather in obedience to her, who believed it would be a solace for her own barrenness if she made the fruitful womb of her maidservant her own by her will, since she could not by nature[^94]; and by that right of which the Apostle speaks, *likewise the husband too has no power over his own body, but the wife*[^95], the wife might use her husband to bear, from another, what she could not from herself.

Here there is no desire of wantonness, no baseness of wickedness. By the wife the maidservant is handed over to the husband for the sake of offspring; by the husband she is received for the sake of offspring; by both not the indulgence of fault, but the fruit of nature, is sought.

Finally, when the pregnant maidservant grew proud toward her barren mistress, and Sarah, with womanly suspicion, imputed this rather to her husband, even there Abraham showed that he had not been a slave-lover but a free begetter, and had kept his chastity, in Hagar, for Sarah his wife, and had fulfilled not his own pleasure but her will;

that he had received and not sought, had approached and not clung, had sown and not loved. For he says, *behold, your maidservant is in your hands; use her as it pleases you*[^96]. O man, who used women in a manly way—his wife temperately, his maidservant compliantly, neither intemperately!

**26.** 1. After this Ishmael was born of Hagar, in whom Abraham might think to be fulfilled what had been promised him when he had wished to adopt his home-born servant for himself, God saying, *this one shall not be your heir; but he who shall come forth from you, he shall be your heir*[^97]. So, lest he should think this promise fulfilled in the maidservant's son, now *when he was ninety-nine years old, the Lord appeared to him and said to him: I am God; please me in my sight and be without blame, and I will set my covenant between me and you, and I will multiply you greatly.*

*And Abram fell upon his face. And God spoke to him, saying: and I, behold, my covenant is with you, and you shall be the father of a multitude of nations; and your name shall no longer be called Abram, but your name shall be Abraham, because I have made you the father of many nations; and I will increase you very, very greatly and make you into nations, and kings shall come forth from you; and I will establish my covenant between me and you and your seed after you throughout their generations, for an everlasting covenant, that I may be God to you and to your seed after you. And I will give to you and to your seed after you the land in which you are a stranger, all the land of Canaan, for an everlasting possession, and I will be God to them.*

*And God said to Abraham: but you shall keep my covenant, you and your seed after you throughout their generations. And this is the covenant which you shall keep between me and you and your seed after you throughout their generations: every male among you shall be circumcised, and you shall be circumcised in the flesh of your foreskin, and it shall be a sign of the covenant between me and you. And a boy of eight days shall be circumcised, every male among you throughout your generations. The home-born and the bought from any foreigner's son who is not of your seed shall be circumcised with circumcision—the home-born of your house and the bought. And my covenant shall be in your flesh for an everlasting covenant. And the male who is not circumcised, who shall not be circumcised in the flesh of his foreskin on the eighth day—that soul shall perish from his people, because he has broken my covenant.*

*And God said to Abraham: Sarai your wife—her name shall not be called Sarai, but Sarah shall be her name. And I will bless her and give you a son by her, and I will bless him, and he shall become nations, and kings of nations shall be from him. And Abraham fell upon his face and laughed and said in his heart, saying: shall a son be born to me, who am a hundred years old, and shall Sarah, ninety years old, bear? And Abraham said to God: let this Ishmael live in your sight. But God said to Abraham: yes, behold, Sarah your wife shall bear you a son, and you shall call his name Isaac; and I will establish my covenant with him for an everlasting covenant, to be God to him and to his seed after him. But concerning Ishmael, behold, I have heard you; behold, I have blessed him and will enlarge him and multiply him greatly. He shall beget twelve nations, and I will make him into a great nation. But my covenant I will establish with Isaac, whom Sarah shall bear you at this time in the following year*[^98].

**26.** 2. Here clearer things are promised about the calling of the nations in Isaac, that is, in the son of the promise, by whom grace is signified, not nature, because a son is promised from an old man and a barren old woman. For although God works even the natural course of procreation, yet where the work of God is evident, nature being corrupted and ceasing, there grace is more evidently understood. And because this was to be not through generation but through regeneration, therefore circumcision was now commanded, when the son was promised from Sarah. And that he commands all to be circumcised—not only the sons, but also the home-born and bought servants—testifies that this grace pertains to all. For what else does circumcision signify than nature renewed by the putting off of its oldness? And what else does the eighth day signify than Christ, who rose again when the week was completed, that is, after the Sabbath?

The names too of the parents are changed: everything resounds with newness, and in the Old Testament the New is foreshadowed. For what is that which is called the Old Testament but the concealing of the New? And what else is that which is called the New but the revealing of the Old? Abraham's laughter is the exultation of one rejoicing, not the mockery of one distrusting. His words too, in his heart, *shall a son be born to me, who am a hundred years old, and shall Sarah, ninety years old, bear?* are not those of one doubting, but of one wondering.

But if anyone is troubled by what was said, *and I will give to you and to your seed after you the land in which you are a stranger, all the land of Canaan, for an everlasting possession*[^99], as to how it is to be taken—whether as fulfilled or as still awaited to be fulfilled, since no earthly possession whatever can be everlasting for any nation—let him know that *everlasting* is the rendering by our translators of what the Greeks call αἰώνιον, which is derived from "age"; for αἰών in Greek is called "age."

But the Latins did not dare to call this "secular," lest they should send the sense far off into something else. For many things are called "secular" which are done in this age in such a way that they pass away even in a short time; but what is called αἰώνιον either has no end or extends to the end of this age.

**27.** Likewise it may trouble us how we ought to understand what was said here, *the male who shall not be circumcised in the flesh of his foreskin on the eighth day—that soul shall perish from his people, because he has broken my covenant*[^100], since this is no fault of the little child, whose soul he said would perish, nor did he himself break God's covenant, but his elders, who did not take care to circumcise him; unless because the little children too, not according to the property of their own life, but according to the common origin of the human race, all broke God's covenant in that one in whom all sinned[^101].

For many things are called covenants of God, besides those two great ones, the Old and the New, which anyone may know by reading. But the first covenant, which was made with the first man, is surely this: *on the day you eat, you shall die the death*[^102]. Hence it is written in the book which is called *Ecclesiasticus*, *all flesh grows old like a garment. For the covenant from the beginning is: you shall die the death*[^103]. For since a more evident law was given afterward, and the Apostle says, *but where there is no law, neither is there transgression*[^104], in what way is that true which is read in the Psalm, *I counted all the sinners of the earth transgressors*[^105], unless because all who are held bound by any sin are guilty of having transgressed some law?

Wherefore, if the little children too—which true faith holds—are born sinners not properly but originally, whence we confess that the grace of the remission of sins is necessary for them, surely, in the manner in which they are sinners, they are also recognized as transgressors of that law which was given in paradise; so that both are true which are written: *I counted all the sinners of the earth transgressors*, and, *where there is no law, neither is there transgression*. And for this reason, because circumcision was a sign of regeneration, and generation will not without reason destroy the little child on account of original sin, by which God's covenant was first broken, unless regeneration delivers him, these divine words are to be understood as though it had been said, "Whoever shall not have been regenerated—that soul shall perish from his people," because he broke God's covenant, when in Adam he too, with all, sinned.

For if he had said, "Because he broke this my covenant," he would have compelled it to be understood only of that circumcision; but now, since he did not express what kind of covenant the little child broke, we are free to understand it said of that covenant whose breaking could pertain to the little child.

But if anyone contends that this was said only of that circumcision—that the little child broke God's covenant in it because he was not circumcised—let him seek some manner of speech by which it can be understood, not absurdly, that he broke the covenant for this reason, that, though not by him, yet in him, it was broken. But even so it must be observed that the soul of the uncircumcised little child does not unjustly perish, by no negligence of its own, except by the bond of original sin.

**28.** A promise so great and so clear, then, having been made to Abraham, to whom it was said most evidently, *I have made you the father of many nations; and I will increase you very, very greatly and make you into nations, and kings shall come forth from you. And I will give you a son by Sarah, and I will bless him, and he shall become nations, and kings of nations shall be from him*[^106]—which promise we now see fulfilled in Christ; from then on those spouses are not called in the Scriptures, as they were before, Abram and Sarai, but, as we have called them from the beginning, since they are now so called by all, Abraham and Sarah. But why Abraham's name was changed, the reason is given: *because*, he says, *I have made you the father of many nations*. This, then, is to be understood as the meaning of "Abraham"; but "Abram," as he was called before, is interpreted "exalted father." Of the change of Sarah's name, however, no reason is given; but, as those say who wrote the interpretations of the Hebrew names contained in these sacred Scriptures, "Sarai" is interpreted "my princess," but "Sarah" "strength." Hence it is written in the *Epistle to the Hebrews*, *by faith Sarah herself received strength to conceive seed*[^107].

For both were aged, as Scripture testifies; but she was also barren and now bereft of the menstrual flow, so that she could no longer bear, even if she had not been barren. Furthermore, if a woman is of such an advanced age that the things customary to women still flow for her, she can bear by a young man, but not by an old one; though an old man can still beget, but by a young woman—as Abraham, after Sarah's death, was able to beget by Keturah, because he found her age vigorous[^108]. This, then, is what the Apostle commends as wonderful, and on this account he says that Abraham's body was already dead, since not from every woman to whom there was still some last time of bearing could he himself at that age still beget[^109]. For we ought to understand his body as dead with respect to something, not with respect to everything. For if with respect to everything, it is no longer the old age of a living man, but the corpse of a dead one.

Although this question is also wont to be solved in this way, that Abraham afterward begot by Keturah because the gift of begetting, which he received from the Lord, remained even after his wife's death. But the solution of this question which we have followed seems to me preferable, because a hundred-year-old man—but of our time—can beget by no woman; not then, when men still lived so long that a hundred years did not yet make a man of decrepit old age.

## How the Deity Appeared to Abraham and Lot

**29.** Likewise God appeared to Abraham by the oak of Mamre in three men[^110], whom there is no doubt were angels; although some suppose that one of them was the Lord Christ, asserting that he was visible even before the clothing of the flesh. It is indeed within the divine power, and that of an invisible, incorporeal, and unchangeable nature, to appear even to mortal eyes without any change of itself—not through that which it is, but through something subject to it; and what is not subject to it? But yet, if they affirm that one of these three was Christ for this reason, that, although he saw three, he spoke to the Lord in the singular—for it is written thus: *and behold, three men stood over him, and seeing them he ran to meet them from the door of his tent, and bowed himself to the ground and said: Lord, if I have found favor before you*[^111], and so forth—why do they not also notice this, that two of them came to destroy the Sodomites, while Abraham still spoke to one, calling him Lord and interceding lest he should destroy the just together with the impious in Sodom?

But those two Lot received in such a way that even he himself, in his conversation with them, calls them Lord in the singular. For when he had said to them in the plural, *behold, lords, turn aside into the house of your servant*, and the rest that is said there[^112], yet afterward it is read thus: *and the angels took his hand and his wife's hand and the hands of his two daughters, because the Lord spared him. And it came to pass, as soon as they had led him outside, that they said: save your soul; do not look back, nor stay anywhere in the whole region; save yourself in the mountain, lest you be caught. But Lot said to them: I pray, Lord, because your servant has found mercy before you*[^113], and what follows. Then, after these words, the Lord too answers him in the singular, although he was in the two angels, saying: *behold, I have admired your face*[^114], and so forth.

Hence it is much more credible that both Abraham in the three and Lot in the two men recognized the Lord, to whom they spoke in the singular number, even while they supposed them to be men; for it was for no other reason that they so received them as to minister to them as to mortals needing human refreshment; but there was surely something by which they so excelled, though as men, that those who showed them hospitality could not doubt that the Lord was in them, as he is wont to be in the Prophets; and therefore they called both them sometimes in the plural and the Lord in them sometimes in the singular.

But that they were angels Scripture testifies, not only in this book of *Genesis*, where these deeds are narrated, but also in the *Epistle to the Hebrews*, where, when hospitality was being praised, it says, *by this some, without knowing it, have received angels into their lodging*[^115].

Through those three men, then, when a son, Isaac, was again promised to Abraham by Sarah, this divine response too was given, so that it was said, *Abraham shall become a great and numerous nation, and in him shall all the nations of the earth be blessed*[^116]. And here those two things were most briefly and most fully promised: the nation of Israel according to the flesh, and all the nations according to faith.

**30.** After this promise, when Lot had been delivered from Sodom and a rain of fire came from heaven, that whole region of the impious city was turned to ashes, where defilements with males had grown into such a custom as the laws are wont to grant license for other deeds. But this punishment of theirs too was a specimen of the future divine judgment. For to what does it pertain that those who were being delivered by the angels were forbidden to look back, unless because one must not return in mind to the old life, which one who is regenerated by grace puts off, if we are minded to escape the last judgment? Finally Lot's wife, where she looked back, remained behind, and, turned into salt, furnished faithful men a certain seasoning, by which they may have some taste whereby that example may be guarded against[^117].

Then again Abraham did at Gerar, before Abimelech the king of that city, what he had done in Egypt concerning his wife, and she was likewise restored to him untouched. Here indeed Abraham, to the king who rebuked him for keeping silent about his wife and calling her his sister, opening what he had feared, added this too: *for indeed she is truly my sister by my father, but not by my mother*[^118], because Sarah was Abraham's sister on her father's side, on which side she was his kinswoman. But she was of such beauty that even at that age she could be loved.

## When Isaac Was Promised, Sarah Laughed

**31.** After this a son was born to Abraham, according to God's promise, by Sarah, and he named him Isaac, which is interpreted "laughter." For the father too had laughed when he was promised to him, wondering in joy; and the mother too had laughed when he was again promised through those three men, doubting in joy; although, the angel reproaching her that that laughter, even if it was of joy, was nevertheless not of full faith, she was afterward confirmed by the same angel even in faith. From this, then, the boy received his name. For that that laughter pertained not to mocking reproach but to celebrating joy, Sarah showed when Isaac was born and called by that name; for she says, *the Lord has made laughter for me; for whoever hears will rejoice with me*[^119]. But after a little time the maidservant is cast out of the house with her son, and those two Testaments, the Old and the New, are signified, according to the Apostle, where that Sarah bears the figure of the heavenly Jerusalem, that is, of the city of God[^120].

**32.** 1. Amid these things, which all to mention would be too long, Abraham is tested concerning the sacrificing of his most beloved son Isaac himself, that his pious obedience might be proved, to be brought to the knowledge of the ages, not of God[^121]. For not every testing is to be blamed, since that too is to be rejoiced over by which a proving is made. And for the most part the human mind cannot become known to itself otherwise than by answering its own strength, as it were, when testing questions it, not by word but by experience; where, if it recognizes the gift of God, then it is pious, then it is made solid by the firmness of grace, not puffed up by the emptiness of boasting.

Abraham would never, to be sure, believe that God delighted in human victims; although, when a divine command thunders, it is to be obeyed, not disputed. Yet Abraham is to be praised for having believed that his son, when sacrificed, would rise again. For God had said to him, when concerning the casting out of the maidservant and her son he was unwilling to fulfill his wife's will, *in Isaac shall seed be called for you*. And there it certainly follows and says, *but the son of this maidservant too I will make into a great nation, because he is your seed*[^122]. How, then, was it said, *in Isaac shall seed be called for you*, when God called Ishmael too his seed? But the Apostle, explaining what *in Isaac shall seed be called for you* means, says, *that is, not those who are the children of the flesh, these are children of God, but the children of the promise are reckoned as seed*[^123].

And for this reason the children of the promise, that they may be the seed of Abraham, are called in Isaac—that is, they are gathered in Christ by calling grace. The pious father, then, faithfully holding this promise, because it had to be fulfilled through him whom God commanded to be slain, did not hesitate that he who could be given when not hoped for could be restored when sacrificed.

So it was understood also in the *Epistle to the Hebrews*, and so expounded. *By faith*, it says, *Abraham, when tested, offered Isaac, and he who had received the promises offered his only son, to whom it was said: in Isaac shall seed be called for you, considering that God is able to raise even from the dead*. Accordingly it added, *whence he received him also as a likeness*[^124]; a likeness of whom, unless of him of whom the Apostle says, *who did not spare his own Son, but delivered him up for us all*[^125]? Therefore Isaac too, as the Lord carried his cross, so carried himself the wood to the place of the victim, on which he too was to be laid.

Finally, because Isaac was not to be slain, after the father was forbidden to strike, who was that ram, by whose sacrifice the sacrifice was fulfilled with significant blood? Surely, when Abraham saw it, it was held by its horns in a thicket[^126]. Who, then, was figured by it, but Jesus, crowned with Jewish thorns before he was sacrificed?

**32.** 2. But let us rather hear the divine words through the angel. For Scripture says, *and Abraham stretched out his hand to take the sword, that he might slay his son. And the angel of the Lord called him from heaven and said: Abraham! And he said: behold, here I am. And he said: do not lay your hand upon the boy, nor do anything to him; for now I have known that you fear your God, and have not spared your beloved son for my sake*[^127]. *Now I have known* is said for "now I have made known"; for God did not as yet not know this. Then, that ram being sacrificed instead of his son Isaac, *Abraham called*, as it is read, *the name of that place: The Lord saw; so that they say today: on the mountain the Lord appeared*. As it was said, *now I have known*, for "now I have made known," so here, *the Lord saw*, for "the Lord appeared," that is, "made himself to be seen."

*And the angel of the Lord called Abraham a second time from heaven, saying: by myself I have sworn, says the Lord, because you have done this thing and have not spared your beloved son for my sake, blessing I will bless you, and multiplying I will multiply your seed like the stars of heaven and like the sand which is by the shore of the sea. And your seed shall possess as an inheritance the cities of its adversaries, and in your seed shall all the nations of the earth be blessed, because you have obeyed my voice*[^128]. In this way that promise about the calling of the nations in the seed of Abraham, after the holocaust by which Christ was signified, was confirmed even by the oath of God. For he had often promised, but had never sworn. But what is the swearing of the true and truthful God but a confirmation of the promise and a certain rebuke of the unbelieving?

**32.** 3. After this Sarah died, in the hundred and twenty-seventh year of her life, but the hundred and thirty-seventh of her husband[^129]. For he was ten years older than she in age; as he himself, when a son was promised him by her, says, *shall a son be born to me, who am a hundred years old, and shall Sarah, ninety years old, bear?*[^130]. Then Abraham bought a field in which he buried his wife[^131]. Then, according to Stephen's narrative, he was established in that land, since he began to be a possessor there; after the death, that is, of his father, who is reckoned to have died two years before[^132].

**33.** Then, when Isaac was forty years old, he took as wife Rebecca, granddaughter of Nahor his uncle—in the hundred and fortieth year of his father's life, that is, three years after his mother's death. But that he might take her, when a servant was sent by his father into Mesopotamia, what else was shown, when Abraham said to that same servant, *put your hand under my thigh, and I will adjure you by the Lord God of heaven and the Lord of the earth, that you take not a wife for my son Isaac from the daughters of the Canaanites*[^133], unless that the Lord God of heaven and the Lord of the earth was to come in the flesh, which was drawn from that thigh? Are these small indications of the foretold truth, which we see fulfilled in Christ?

## What Keturah and Her Sons Signify

**34.** But what does it mean that Abraham, after Sarah's death, took Keturah as wife[^134]? Here far be it that we should suspect incontinence, especially at that age and in that holiness of faith. Or were sons still being sought to be procreated, when by God's promise so great a multiplication of sons from Isaac, by the stars of heaven and the sand of the earth, was held by a faith most thoroughly proved? But surely, if Hagar and Ishmael, with the Apostle teaching, signified the carnal of the Old Testament[^135], why should not Keturah too and her sons signify the carnal who think they belong to the New Testament? For both were called both wives and concubines of Abraham; but Sarah was never called a concubine. For even when Hagar was given to Abraham, it was written thus: *and Sarai, Abram's wife, took Hagar the Egyptian, her maidservant, after she had dwelt ten years in the land of Canaan, and gave her to Abram her husband as a wife for himself*[^136]. But of Keturah, whom he took after Sarah's death, it is read thus: *and Abraham, adding to it, took a wife whose name was Keturah*[^137]. Behold, both are called wives; both, moreover, are found to have been concubines, Scripture afterward saying, *and Abraham gave all his property to Isaac his son, but to the sons of his concubines Abraham gave gifts and sent them away from Isaac his son, while he was still living, to the east, into the land of the east*[^138].

The sons of the concubines, then, have some gifts, but they do not reach the promised kingdom—neither the heretics nor the carnal Jews—because besides Isaac there is no heir, and *not those who are the children of the flesh, these are children of God, but the children of the promise are reckoned as seed*[^139], of whom it was said, *in Isaac shall seed be called for you*[^140]. For I do not see why Keturah too, taken after his wife's death, was called a concubine, except on account of this mystery.

But whoever does not wish to take these things in these significations, let him not slander Abraham. For what if this too was provided against future heretics, opponents of second marriages, so that in the father of many nations himself it might be shown that to marry again after a spouse's death is no sin?

And Abraham died, when he was a hundred and seventy-five years old[^141]. He left, then, his son Isaac, whom he begot at a hundred, seventy-five years old.

## Isaac's Twins Foretell Two Peoples

**35.** Now from here let us see how through the descendants of Abraham the times of the city of God run their course. From the first year of Isaac's life, then, down to the sixtieth, in which sons were born to him, this is memorable: that, when the Lord had granted him, who asked God that his wife, who was barren, might bear, what he asked, and she had conceived, the twins still enclosed in her womb struggled. And when she was distressed by this trouble, she inquired of the Lord and received the answer, *two nations are in your womb, and two peoples shall be separated from your belly, and one people shall overcome the other, and the elder shall serve the younger*[^142]. The apostle Paul wishes this to be understood as a great proof of grace, because, when they were not yet born and had done nothing good or evil, without any good merits the younger is chosen, the elder being rejected[^143]; when without doubt, as far as original sin is concerned, both were equal; but as for their own, neither of them had any.

But to say something more at length about this matter the plan of the work I have undertaken does not allow, whence we have already said much in others[^144]. But what was said, *the elder shall serve the younger*, almost none of our writers has understood otherwise than that the greater people of the Jews would serve the lesser Christian people.

And indeed, although in the nation of the Idumeans, which was born of the elder—who had two names (for he was called both Esau and "Edom," whence "Idumeans")—this may seem fulfilled, because it was afterward to be overcome by the people who sprang from the younger, that is, the Israelite, and was to be subject to it: yet it is more fittingly believed that this prophecy, by which it was said, *one people shall overcome the other, and the elder shall serve the younger*, was aimed at something greater. And what is this, but what is evidently fulfilled in the Jews and the Christians?

## In Abraham the Promise Was to Isaac

**36.** Isaac too received such an oracle as his father had received several times. Of which oracle it is written thus: *and there was a famine over the land, besides the famine which had happened before in the time of Abraham. And Isaac went to Abimelech king of the Philistines, to Gerar. And the Lord appeared to him and said: do not go down into Egypt; but dwell in the land which I shall tell you, and sojourn in this land; and I will be with you and bless you. For to you and to your seed I will give all this land, and I will establish my oath which I swore to Abraham your father; and I will multiply your seed like the stars of heaven, and I will give to your seed all this land, and in your seed shall all the nations of the earth be blessed, because Abraham your father obeyed my voice and kept my precepts and my commandments and my ordinances and my statutes*[^145].

This patriarch had neither another wife nor any concubine, but was content with the posterity of the two twins procreated from one union. He too, indeed, feared danger from the beauty of his wife, while he dwelt among strangers, and did what his father had done—to call her his sister and keep silent about her being his wife; for she was his kinswoman both by his father's and by his mother's blood; but she too remained untouched by the strangers, once it was known that she was his wife[^146]. Nor, however, ought we for this reason to prefer him to his father, because he knew no woman besides his one wife. For without doubt the merits of his father's faith and obedience were greater—so much so that for his sake God says he does to Isaac the good things he does. *In your seed*, he says, *shall all the nations of the earth be blessed, because Abraham your father obeyed my voice and kept my precepts and my commandments and my ordinances and my statutes*; and again in another oracle, *I am*, he says, *the God of Abraham your father; do not fear, for I am with you and have blessed you and will multiply your seed for the sake of Abraham your father*[^147];

so that we may understand how chastely Abraham did what, to shameless men seeking patronage from the holy Scriptures for their own wickedness, seems to have been done out of lust; and then that we may know this too, that we are not to compare men with one another by single good qualities, but in each one consider the whole. For it can happen that someone has something in his life and morals by which he surpasses another, and that this is far more excellent than that by which he is surpassed by the other.

And for this reason, by a sound and true judgment, although continence is preferred to marriage, yet a faithful married man is better than a continent unbeliever. But an unbelieving man is not only less to be praised, but even most to be detested. Let us set both as good men; even so, surely, a married man most faithful and most obedient to God is better than a continent man of less faith and less obedience. But if other things are equal, who would doubt to prefer the continent man to the married one?

## What Isaac's Blessing of Jacob Instead of Esau Signifies

**37.** Isaac's two sons, Esau and Jacob, then, grow up together. The birthright of the elder is transferred to the younger by a compact and agreement between them, in that the elder desired too immoderately the lentils which the younger had prepared as food, and at that price, with an oath interposed, sold his birthright to his brother[^148]. Here we learn that in eating each is to be blamed not for the kind of food, but for immoderate greediness.

Isaac grows old, and his sight is taken from his eyes through old age. He wishes to bless his elder son, and unknowingly blesses the younger instead of him—the younger who put himself under his father's hands in place of the elder brother, who was hairy, and who, with kidskins fitted to himself, carried, as it were, the sins of another. Lest this trick of Jacob be thought a fraudulent deceit, and lest the mystery of a great matter not be sought in it, Scripture said beforehand above, *and Esau was a man knowing how to hunt, of the field; but Jacob was a simple man, dwelling at home*[^149].

Some of our writers have translated this "without deceit." But whether it be called "without deceit" or *simple* or rather "without feigning," which is in Greek ἄπλαστος, what deceit of a man without deceit is there in the receiving of that blessing? What is the deceit of a simple man, the feigning of one not lying, but the profound mystery of truth?

But the blessing itself—what is it? *Behold*, he says, *the smell of my son is like the smell of a full field which the Lord has blessed. And may God give you of the dew of heaven and of the fatness of the earth, and an abundance of grain and wine; and may nations serve you, and princes adore you, and may you become lord of your brother, and may the sons of your father adore you. He who curses you shall be cursed; and he who blesses you shall be blessed*[^150]. The blessing of Jacob, then, is the proclamation of Christ among all nations. This is being done, this is being accomplished.

Isaac is the Law and Prophecy; even by the mouth of the Jews Christ is blessed by it, as by one not knowing, because it itself does not know. With the fragrance of the name of Christ, like a field, the world is filled; his is the blessing *of the dew of heaven*, that is, of the rain of divine words, and *of the fatness of the earth*, that is, the gathering of peoples; his is the *abundance of grain and wine*, that is, the multitude which grain and wine gather in the sacrament of his body and blood. Nations serve him, princes adore him.

He is the lord of his brother, because his people rules over the Jews. The sons of his father adore him, that is, the sons of Abraham according to faith; because he too is a son of Abraham according to the flesh.

Whoever curses him is cursed, and whoever blesses him is blessed. Christ, I say, our Christ is blessed—that is, truly spoken of—even by the mouth of the Jews, though they err, yet sing the Law and the Prophets; and another is thought to be blessed, the one whom they in their error await.

Behold, when the elder repeats his request for the promised blessing, Isaac is alarmed and recognizes that he has blessed one instead of the other; he wonders and asks who that one was; yet he does not complain that he was deceived; on the contrary, at once, a great mystery being revealed to him inwardly in his heart, he avoids indignation and confirms the blessing. *Who, then*, he says, *hunted game for me and brought it to me? And I ate of all before you came; and I blessed him, and let him be blessed*[^151]. Who would not here rather expect the curse of an angry man, if these things were done not by heavenly inspiration but in earthly fashion?

O deeds done, but done prophetically; on earth, but from heaven; through men, but divinely! If the single things, so fruitful in such great mysteries, were examined, many volumes would have to be filled; but the measure that must moderately be imposed on this work compels us to hasten on to other things.

## The Seed of Abraham Is Blessed in Jacob

**38.** 1. Jacob is sent by his parents into Mesopotamia, that he may take a wife there. The words of his father sending him are these: *you shall not take a wife from the daughters of the Canaanites; arise, flee into Mesopotamia, to the house of Bethuel, your mother's father, and take yourself a wife from there, from the daughters of Laban, your mother's brother. And may my God bless you and increase you and multiply you; and you shall become a company of nations; and may he give you the blessing of Abraham your father, to you and to your seed after you, that you may become heir of the land of your sojourning, which God gave to Abraham*[^152].

Here now we understand the seed of Jacob set apart from the other seed of Isaac, which came about through Esau. For when it was said, *in Isaac shall seed be called for you*[^153], the seed pertaining, of course, to the city of God, there was separated from it the other seed of Abraham, which was in the maidservant's son, and which was to be in the sons of Keturah. But it was still ambiguous concerning the two twin sons of Isaac whether that blessing pertained to both or to one of them; and if to one, which of them. This was now declared, when Jacob is prophetically blessed by his father and it is said to him, *and you shall become a company of nations, and may he give you the blessing of Abraham your father*.

**38.** 2. So Jacob, proceeding into Mesopotamia, received an oracle in his sleep, of which it is written thus: *and Jacob went out from the well of the oath and set out for Haran and came to a place and slept there; for the sun had set; and he took of the stones of the place and put them at his head and slept in that place and dreamed. And behold, a ladder set firm upon the earth, whose top reached to heaven; and the angels of God were ascending and descending by it, and the Lord rested upon it and said: I am the God of Abraham your father and the God of Isaac; do not fear; the land on which you sleep, I will give it to you and to your seed; and your seed shall be like the sand of the earth, and it shall be spread abroad to the sea and to the south and to the north and to the east; and in you and in your seed shall all the tribes of the earth be blessed. And behold, I am with you, keeping you in every way wherever you go, and I will bring you back into this land, for I will not forsake you until I do all that I have spoken to you. And Jacob arose from his sleep and said: surely the Lord is in this place, and I did not know it. And he was afraid and said: how terrible is this place! this is nothing but the house of God, and this is the gate of heaven. And Jacob arose and took the stone which he had put there under his head, and set it up as a pillar and poured oil on its top; and Jacob called the name of that place: the House of God*[^154].

This pertains to prophecy; nor did Jacob pour oil on the stone in the manner of idolatry, as though making it a god; for he neither adored that same stone nor sacrificed to it; but since the name of Christ is from "chrism," that is, from "anointing," surely something was here figured which pertains to a great mystery. And that ladder the Savior himself is understood to recall to our memory in the Gospel, where, when he had said of Nathanael, *behold a true Israelite, in whom there is no guile*[^155], because Israel had seen that vision (for he is Jacob), he says in the same place, *amen, amen, I say to you, you shall see heaven opened and the angels of God ascending and descending upon the Son of man*[^156].

**38.** 3. Jacob, then, proceeded into Mesopotamia, that he might take a wife from there. But how it befell him to have four women, of whom he procreated twelve sons and one daughter, though he had unlawfully desired none of them, divine Scripture indicates. For he had come to take one; but when another had been substituted for the one, he did not dismiss even her, whom he had unknowingly used in the night, lest he should seem to have held her in mockery, and—at a time when no law forbade several wives for the sake of multiplying posterity—he took also the one to whom alone he had already pledged faith of future marriage. And when she was barren, she gave her maidservant to her husband, that she herself might receive sons by her; which her elder sister too, although she had borne, imitated, since she desired to multiply offspring.

Jacob is not read to have sought any woman besides one, nor to have used several except in the duty of begetting offspring, the conjugal right being preserved, so that he did not even do this unless his wives demanded that it be done, who had lawful power over their husband's body[^157]. He begot, then, twelve sons and one daughter by four women[^158]. Then he entered into Egypt through his son Joseph, who, sold by his envious brothers, had been brought there and there exalted.

**39.** But Jacob was also called Israel, as I said a little before, which name the people procreated from him rather obtained. And this name was imposed on him by the angel who had wrestled with him on the way as he returned from Mesopotamia[^159], bearing most evidently a type of Christ. For that Jacob prevailed over him—who of course willed it, that he might figure a mystery—signifies the passion of Christ, where the Jews seemed to prevail over him. And yet he obtained a blessing from that same angel whom he had overcome; and thus the imposition of this name was a blessing. But "Israel" is interpreted "seeing God," which will be in the end the reward of all the saints. Furthermore, that same angel touched the breadth of his thigh, as though of one prevailing, and in this way made him lame. And so one and the same Jacob was both blessed and lame; blessed in those who from that same people believed in Christ, and lame in the unbelievers. For the breadth of the thigh is the multitude of the race. For there are very many in that stock of whom it was prophetically foretold, *and they went lame from their paths*[^160].

**40.** And so it is reported that seventy-five persons entered into Egypt together with Jacob himself, reckoning him in with his sons. In which number only two women are mentioned, one a daughter, the other a granddaughter. But the matter, diligently considered, does not indicate that there was such a number in Jacob's offspring on the day or in the year in which he entered Egypt. For there are mentioned among them even great-grandsons of Joseph, who could in no way have existed then, since Jacob was then a hundred and thirty years old, but his son Joseph thirty-nine; and since it is established that he took a wife in his thirtieth year or later, how could he, within nine years, have great-grandsons by the sons whom he received from that same wife?

Since, then, Ephraim and Manasseh, Joseph's sons, did not have sons, but Jacob, on entering Egypt, found them boys under nine years old, in what manner are not only their sons but also their grandsons numbered among those seventy-five who then entered Egypt with Jacob? For there is mentioned there Machir, the son of Manasseh, grandson of Joseph, and that same Machir's son, that is, Gilead, grandson of Manasseh, great-grandson of Joseph; there too is one whom Ephraim, Joseph's other son, begot, that is, Utalaam, grandson of Joseph, and the son of that same Utalaam, Edem, grandson of Ephraim, great-grandson of Joseph; who could in no way have existed when Jacob came into Egypt and found Joseph's sons, his grandsons, the grandfathers of these, boys under nine years old.

But doubtless Jacob's entry into Egypt, when Scripture mentions him among the seventy-five persons, is not one day or one year, but is that whole time as long as Joseph lived, through whom it came about that they entered. For of Joseph himself the same Scripture speaks thus: *and Joseph dwelt in Egypt, he and his brothers and all the household of his father, and he lived a hundred and ten years, and Joseph saw Ephraim's children to the third generation*. He is that great-grandson of his, third from Ephraim. For by the third generation it means son, grandson, great-grandson. Then it follows: *and the children of Machir, the son of Manasseh, were born upon Joseph's thighs*[^161]. And here is that same grandson of Manasseh, great-grandson of Joseph. But they were called in the plural, as Scripture is accustomed, which called Jacob's one daughter too *daughters*; as in the usage of the Latin language children are called in the plural "sons," even if there is no more than one.

Since, then, the happiness of Joseph himself is proclaimed, that he was able to see his great-grandsons, they are in no way to be thought to have existed already in the thirty-ninth year of their great-grandfather Joseph, when Jacob his father came to him in Egypt. But what deceives those who look at these things less diligently is that it is written, *now these are the names of the sons of Israel who entered into Egypt together with Jacob their father*[^162]. For this was said because the seventy-five are reckoned together with him, not because all were already together when he himself entered Egypt; but, as I said, the whole time during which Joseph lived, through whom the entry was made, is held to be the time of his entry.

**41.** Therefore, for the sake of the Christian people, in which the city of God sojourns on earth, if we seek the flesh of Christ in the seed of Abraham, the sons of the concubines being removed, we come upon Isaac; if in the seed of Isaac, Esau being removed, who is also Edom, we come upon Jacob, who is also Israel; if in the seed of Israel himself, the rest being removed, we come upon Judah, because from the tribe of Judah Christ arose.

And for this reason, when Israel, about to die in Egypt, was blessing his sons, let us hear how he prophetically blessed Judah: *Judah*, he says, *your brothers shall praise you. Your hands shall be upon the back of your enemies; the sons of your father shall adore you. A lion's cub is Judah; from a tender plant, my son, you have gone up; reclining you have slept like a lion and like a lion's cub; who shall rouse him? The prince shall not fail from Judah, nor a leader from his thighs, until there come the things that are laid up for him; and he himself is the expectation of the nations; binding his colt to the vine and his ass's colt to the choice vine, he shall wash his robe in wine and his garment in the blood of the grape. His eyes are darker than wine and his teeth whiter than milk*[^163].

I have expounded these things in disputing against Faustus the Manichee[^164], and I think it enough, so far as the truth of this prophecy shines forth; where both the death of Christ is foretold by the word of sleeping, and not necessity but power in death by the name of the lion. Which power he himself proclaims in the Gospel, saying, *I have power to lay down my life, and I have power to take it up again. No one takes it from me; but I lay it down of myself, and take it up again*[^165]. So the lion roared, so he fulfilled what he said.

For to that power pertains what was added concerning his resurrection: *who shall rouse him?* that is, that no man would, except he himself, who also said of his body, *destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up*[^166].

But the very kind of death—that is, the loftiness of the cross—is understood in one word, where he says, *you have gone up*. And what he added, *reclining you have slept*, the Evangelist expounds, where he says, *and bowing his head he gave up the spirit*[^167]; or else his burial is recognized, in which he reclined sleeping, and from which no man roused him, as the Prophets roused certain ones, or as he himself roused others, but he himself rose as from sleep.

Furthermore his robe, which he washes in wine—that is, cleanses from sins in his own blood, of which blood the baptized know the sacrament, whence he also adds, *and his garment in the blood of the grape*—what is it but the Church? *And his eyes are darker than wine*: his spiritual ones, made drunk with his cup, of which the Psalm sings, *and how glorious is your inebriating cup!*[^168]. *And his teeth whiter than milk*, which in the Apostle the little ones drink—that is, nourishing words—not yet fit for solid food[^169].

He himself, then, is the one in whom were laid up the promises to Judah, and until they should come the princes—that is, the kings of Israel—never failed from that stock. *And he himself is the expectation of the nations*: which is clearer in the seeing than in the expounding.

But as Isaac's two sons, Esau and Jacob, furnished a figure of the two peoples in the Jews and the Christians (although, as far as the propagation of the flesh is concerned, neither did the Jews come from the seed of Esau, but the Idumeans; nor the Christian nations from Jacob, but rather the Jews; for the figure availed only for this, that it was said, *the elder shall serve the younger*[^170]), so it came about also in the two sons of Joseph; for the elder bore the type of the Jews, the younger of the Christians.

When Jacob was blessing them, placing his right hand on the younger, whom he had on his left, and his left on the elder, whom he had on his right, it seemed grievous to their father, and he admonished his father, as though correcting his error, and showing which of them was the elder. But Jacob refused to change his hands, and said, *I know, my son, I know. And this one shall become a people, and this one shall be exalted; but his younger brother shall be greater than he, and his seed shall become a multitude of nations*[^171]. Here too he shows those two promises. For the one becomes *a people*, the other *a multitude of nations*. What is more evident than that by these two promises are contained the people of the Israelites and the whole world in the seed of Abraham—the one according to the flesh, the other according to faith?

## Moses Delivered the People

**43.** 1. Jacob having died, and Joseph also having died, throughout the remaining hundred and forty-four years, until the departure from the land of Egypt, that nation grew in an incredible manner, though worn down by such great persecutions that at one time the males that were born were killed, when the Egyptians, wondering, were terrified by the excessive increase of that people[^172].

Then Moses, withdrawn by stealth from the slayers of the little ones, came to the royal house, God preparing great things through him[^173],

and, nourished and adopted by Pharaoh's daughter (which name belonged to all the kings in Egypt), he grew into so great a man that he himself led that wonderfully multiplied nation out from the hardest and heaviest yoke of slavery which it was bearing there—or rather, God did so through him, who had promised this to Abraham.

For first, fleeing from there because, in defending an Israelite, he had killed an Egyptian and had been frightened[^174], he was afterward divinely sent, and in the power of the Spirit of God[^175] overcame Pharaoh's resisting magicians. Then through him ten memorable plagues were brought upon the Egyptians, when they were unwilling to let the people of God go: water turned into blood, frogs and gnats, the dog-fly, the death of the cattle, sores, hail, locusts, darkness, the death of the firstborn[^176]. Finally the Israelites, whom the Egyptians, at last broken by so many and so great plagues, had let go, were pursued by the Egyptians and destroyed in the Red Sea. For as the Israelites departed, the divided sea made a way; but as the Egyptians pursued, the wave, returning upon itself, drowned them[^177].

Then for forty years, with Moses as leader, the people of God was led through the wilderness, when the tabernacle was called the tabernacle of the testimony, where God was worshiped with sacrifices foretelling things to come, after the law had now been given on the mountain with much terror; for the divinity bore witness most evidently by wonderful signs and voices.

This was done as soon as the departure from Egypt had been made and the people had begun to be in the wilderness, on the fiftieth day after the Passover was celebrated by the sacrifice of the lamb; which is so far a type of Christ, foretelling that he would, by the victim of his passion, pass over from this world to the Father ("Passover," in the Hebrew language, being interpreted "passage"[^178]), that, now when the New Testament was being revealed, after our Passover, Christ, was sacrificed[^179], on the fiftieth day the Holy Spirit came from heaven[^180], who was called in the Gospel the finger of God[^181], to recall our remembrance to the memory of the first prefigured deed, because those tablets of the law too are reported to have been written by the finger of God[^182].

**43.** 2. Moses having died, Joshua the son of Nun ruled the people and led them into the land of promise and divided it among the people. By these two wonderful leaders wars too were waged most prosperously and wonderfully, God attesting that those victories of his came about not so much on account of the merits of the Hebrew people as on account of the sins of the nations that were being subdued.

After these leaders there were judges, the people being now settled in the land of promise, so that the first promise to Abraham, concerning the one nation, that is, the Hebrew, and the land of Canaan[^183], began meanwhile to be rendered—not yet concerning all nations and the whole world; which the coming of Christ in the flesh, and the faith of the Gospel, not the observances of the Old Law, was to fulfill. A prefiguring of this was made in the fact that not Moses, who had received the law for the people on Mount Sinai, but Joshua, whose name too had been changed at God's command so that he was called Joshua[^184], led the people into the land of promise. But in the times of the judges, according as the sins of the people and the mercy of God stood, prosperity and adversity in wars alternated[^185].

**43.** 3. Thence one came to the times of the kings, the first of whom to reign was Saul; and when he was rejected and laid low by defeat in war[^186], and his stock cast off, so that no kings should arise from it, David succeeded to the kingdom, of whom especially Christ was called the son. In him a certain point was made, and, as it were, the beginning of the youth of the people of God; of which race a certain adolescence, as it were, was led from Abraham himself down to this David. For not without reason did the evangelist Matthew so mention the generations that he commended this first interval by fourteen generations, namely from Abraham to David[^187].

For from adolescence a man begins to be able to beget; therefore he took the beginning of the generations from Abraham, who was also constituted father of nations when he received his changed name. Before him, then, there was, as it were, the boyhood of this race of the people of God, from Noah down to Abraham himself; and therefore it was found in a language, that is, the Hebrew.

For from boyhood a man begins to speak after infancy, which was so called for this reason, that he cannot speak. And that first age oblivion surely sinks, just as the first age of the human race was blotted out by the flood. For how few there are who remember their infancy?

Wherefore, in this course of the city of God, as the previous book contained one and the same first age, so let this book contain two, the second and the third; in which third age, on account of the three-year-old heifer, the three-year-old she-goat, and the three-year-old ram, both the yoke of the Law was imposed, and an abundance of sins appeared, and the beginning of the earthly kingdom arose, where there were not lacking spiritual men, whose mystery was figured in the turtledove and the pigeon[^188].
