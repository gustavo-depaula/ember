# Book V: Whether there is a rational account of events or not.

## Preface

Since it is agreed that the fullness of all things to be desired is felicity, which is not a goddess but a gift of God, and that therefore no god ought to be worshipped by men except him who can make them happy (whence, if she were a goddess, she alone would deservedly be said to be worshipped): let us now consequently see for what cause God, who can give also those goods that even the not-good, and through this the not-happy, can have, willed the Roman empire to be so great and so long-lasting. For that this was not done by that multitude of false gods whom they worshipped, we have already said much, and we will say more where it shall seem opportune.

## What some think about fate and the stars . . .

**1.** The cause of the greatness of the Roman empire, then, is neither fortuitous nor fatal, according to the view or opinion of those who say that those things are fortuitous which either have no causes or causes not coming from any rational order, and those fatal which happen, apart from the will of God and of men, by the necessity of a certain order[^1]. Human kingdoms are established outright by divine providence. And if anyone ascribes this to fate for the reason that he calls the very will or power of God by the name of "fate," let him hold his opinion but correct his language. For why does he not first say what he is going to say afterward, when someone asks him what he meant by fate? For when men hear it, by the usual habit of speech they understand nothing but the force of the position of the stars, such as is when one is born or conceived; which some sever from the will of God[^2], while others affirm that this too depends on it[^3].

But those who think that the stars decree, without the will of God, what we do or what goods we have or evils we suffer, are to be driven from the ears of all—not only of those who hold the true religion, but also of those who wish to be worshippers of gods of whatever kind, however false. For what else does this opinion do but bring it about that no god at all be worshipped or entreated? It is not against these that our disputation is now set up, but against those who, in defense of those whom they think gods, oppose the Christian religion.

But those who suspend the position of the stars—which in a manner decrees what sort each person is and what good befalls him or what evil happens to him—upon the will of God, if they think those same stars have this power handed down to them by the supreme power of that God, so that they decree these things at their own will, do a great injury to heaven, in whose, as it were, most illustrious senate and most splendid council chamber they imagine that crimes are decreed to be done—such crimes as, had any earthly state decreed them, it would have had to be overthrown by the decree of the human race. What judgment, then, about the deeds of men is left to God, on whom a heavenly necessity is brought to bear, when he is the Lord both of the stars and of men? Or if they do not say that the stars, having received power from the supreme god, decree these things at their own discretion, but in imposing such necessities altogether fulfill his commands: is it thus to be thought of God himself, what seemed most unworthy to think of the will of the stars?

But if the stars are said to signify these things rather than to do them, so that that position is, as it were, a kind of speech foretelling the future, not effecting it (for this was the opinion of men not moderately learned[^4]): the astrologers are not indeed wont to speak so, as to say, for example, "Mars positioned thus signifies a murderer," but "makes a murderer"[^5];

yet, granting that they do not speak as they ought, and that they ought to receive from the philosophers the rule of speech for foretelling those things which they think they find in the position of the stars: how is it that they have never been able to say why, in the life of twins—in their actions, in their fortunes, in their professions, arts, honors, and the other things pertaining to human life, and in death itself—there is for the most part so great a diversity, that, as far as these things are concerned, many strangers are more like them than the twins themselves are like each other, twins separated in being born by a very tiny interval of time, but in conception sown by one act of intercourse and at one and the same moment?

how is it that they have never been able to explain why twins so often differ so greatly in their lives, actions, experiences, occupations, skills, honors, and everything else belonging to human life, and even in their deaths, that in these respects many strangers resemble them more closely than the twins resemble each other, although their births were separated by a very brief interval, while at conception they were begotten through a single act of intercourse and at the very same moment?

**2.** Cicero says that Hippocrates, the most noble physician, left it in writing that he suspected certain brothers to be twins, because, when they had begun to be ill at the same time and their illness grew worse at the same time, it was relieved at the same time[^6]; whom Posidonius the Stoic, much given to astrology, used to assert had been born and conceived under the same constellation of the stars[^7]. Thus what the physician believed to pertain to a most similar temperament of health, this the astrologer-philosopher ascribed to the force and constellation of the stars which had existed at the time when they were conceived and born. In this case the medical conjecture is much more acceptable and more credible at first hand,

since the parents, as they were affected in body while they were having intercourse, could so affect the first beginnings of the conceived, that, with the earlier increments following from the mother's body, they were born of equal health; then, nourished in one house with the same food—where medicine attests that the air and the position of the place and the force of the waters avail very much for the body's being well or ill disposed—accustomed also to the same exercises, they bore bodies so similar that they were moved similarly even to falling ill at one time and from the same causes.

But to wish to draw the constellation of heaven and the stars, which existed when they were conceived or born, to this parity of falling ill—when so many things of the most diverse kind, with the most diverse effects and outcomes, could be conceived and born at the same time in the land of one region subject to the same heaven—I do not know of what arrogance this is.

But we know of twins who not only have diverse acts and travels, but even suffer disparate illnesses. Of which matter Hippocrates would give a most easy account, as it seems to me: that dissimilar states of health could befall them from diverse foods and exercises, which come not from the body's temperament but from the will of the mind. But Posidonius, or any asserter of the fatal stars, it would be a wonder if he can find here what to say, unless he is willing to play upon the minds of the inexperienced in matters they do not know.

For what they try to make of the small interval of time which twins had between themselves while they were being born, on account of that little part of the heaven where the marking of the hour is placed, which they call the horoscope: either it does not avail as much as the diversity found in the wills, acts, morals, and chances of twins, or it avails even more than the equal lowliness or nobility of birth of the twins, whose greatest diversity they place only in the hour in which each is born[^8]. And through this, if one is born so quickly after the other that the same part of the horoscope remains, I ask for all things equal, which can be found in no twins; but if the slowness of the later one changes the horoscope, I ask for diverse parents, whom twins cannot have.

**3.** In vain, then, is that noble fiction about the potter's wheel brought forward, which they say Nigidius answered, troubled by this question, whence he was even called Figulus. For when he had spun the potter's wheel with as much force as he could[^9], while it was running he struck it twice with ink, as though in one place of it, with the utmost speed; then, when the motion ceased, the marks which he had fixed were found to be distant by no small interval on the edge of that wheel. *So,* he said, *in so great a swiftness of the heaven, even if one is born after another with as great a speed as that with which I struck the wheel twice, in the space of the heaven it is a very great deal: hence,* he said, *come whatever things are reported as most dissimilar in the morals and chances of twins*[^10].

This fiction is more fragile than the vessels that are fashioned by that spinning. For if there is so much difference in the heaven—which cannot be comprehended by the constellations—that to one of the twins an inheritance falls and to the other it does not: why do they dare, when they have examined the constellations of others who are not twins, to pronounce such things as pertain to that secret which no one can comprehend and note down in the moments of those being born? But if they say such things in the nativities of others for the reason that these pertain to more extended spaces of time, while those moments of tiny parts, which twins being born can have between themselves, are assigned to the smallest matters, about which astrologers are not wont to be consulted (for who consults them about when he should sit, when walk, when or what he should lunch on?): do we say these things, when we show very many and very diverse things in the morals, works, and chances of twins?

**4.** Two twins were born in the ancient memory of the fathers (to speak of notable cases) one after the other in such a way that the later held the heel of the earlier[^11]. So diverse were things in their life and morals, so great the disparity in their acts, so great the dissimilarity in their parents' love, that the very distance between them made them even enemies to each other.

Is this said because, while one walked, the other sat, and while one slept, the other was awake, and while one spoke, the other was silent—things which pertain to those minute matters which cannot be comprehended by those who write down the constellation of the stars under which each is born, from which the astrologers are consulted?

One led a hired servitude, the other did not serve; one was loved by his mother, the other was not loved; one lost the honor which was held great among them, the other obtained it. What of their wives, what of their children, what of their possessions—how great a diversity! If, then, these things pertain to those minute portions of time which twins have between themselves, and are not ascribed to the constellations: why, when the constellations of others are examined, are these things said? But if they are said for the reason that they pertain not to incomprehensible minute moments, but to spaces of time which can be observed and noted: what is that potter's wheel doing here, except that men with hearts of clay are sent spinning in a circle, lest the empty talk of the astrologers be refuted?

**5.** As for those very brothers whose illness—because it appeared at the same time worse and lighter in both—Hippocrates, examining it medically, made him suspect that they were twins: do they not sufficiently refute those who wish to give to the stars what came from a similar temperament of the bodies? For why were they ill similarly and at the same time, not one earlier and one later, as they had been born—since the two certainly could not be born at the same time? Or if it brought no weight, toward their being ill at diverse times, that they were born at diverse times: why do they contend that a diverse time in being born avails toward the diversities of other things? Why could they travel at diverse times, marry wives at diverse times, beget children at diverse times, and do many other things, for the reason that they were born at diverse times, and could not, by the same cause, also be ill at diverse times?

For if the disparate delay in being born changed the horoscope and introduced disparity into the other matters: why did that remain in their illnesses which it had in the equality of the time of conception? Or if the fates of health are in the conception, but those of other matters are said to be in the birth, they ought not, with the natal constellations examined, to say anything about health, when the hour of conception is not given them to examine. But if they foretell illnesses without examining the horoscope of conception, because the moments of those being born indicate them: how would they tell either of those twins, from the hour of his nativity, when he would fall ill, when the other too, who did not have the same hour of nativity, would necessarily fall ill equally?

Then I ask: if there is so great a distance of time in the nativity of twins that, on account of it, diverse constellations must be made for them—because of the diverse horoscope and on this account diverse cardinal points[^12], where so great a force is placed that even their fates are diverse from this—how could this come about, when their conception cannot have a diverse time? Or if of two conceived in one moment of time the fates for being born could be disparate, why could not the fates for living and dying also be disparate of two born in one moment of time? For if the one moment in which both were conceived did not hinder the one from being born earlier and the other later: why, if two are born in one moment, does anything hinder the one from dying earlier and the other later? If a conception of one moment allows twins to have diverse fortunes in the womb, why should not a nativity of one moment allow any two persons whatever to have diverse fortunes on earth—and so all the fabrications of this art, or rather of this vanity, be done away with? What is this—why do those conceived at one time, in one moment, under one and the same position of the heaven, have diverse fates which lead them to a nativity at diverse hours, while two born together in one moment of time, under one and the same position of the heaven, from two mothers, cannot have diverse fates which lead them to a diverse necessity of living or dying?

Or do those conceived not yet have fates, which they cannot have unless they are born? What is it, then, that they say—that if the hour of conception is found, many things can be told by them more divinely? Whence this too is proclaimed by some, that a certain wise man chose the hour at which he should lie with his wife, that he might beget a marvelous son.

Whence, finally, comes also this, which Posidonius, the great astrologer and likewise philosopher, used to answer about those twins who were ill alike: that it came about because they had been born at the same time and conceived at the same time. For he certainly added the conception for this reason, lest it be said to him that they could not clearly have been born at the same time, whom it was agreed had altogether been conceived at the same time; so that the fact that they were ill similarly and together he might attribute not to a nearby equal temperament of the body, but might bind that same parity of health also to the bonds of the stars.

If, then, there is so great a force in the conception toward the equality of fates, the same fates ought not to have been changed by being born. Or if the fates of twins are changed for the reason that they are born at diverse times, why should we not rather understand that they had already been changed, so that they were born at diverse times? Does the will of the living, then, not change the fates of nativity, when the order of those being born changes the fates of conception?

**6.** And yet, even in the very conceptions of twins, where certainly the moments of time of both are the same, how does it come about that under the same fatal constellation the one is conceived male, the other female? We know twins of diverse sex; both still live, both are still vigorous in age; and although there is between them a likeness of bodily appearance, as much as is possible in a diverse sex, yet in the manner and purpose of life they are so disparate that—besides the acts in which feminine ones must necessarily differ from masculine (for the one serves in the office of a count and is almost always traveling away from his home, while the other does not depart from her native soil and her own estate)—moreover (what is more incredible, if astral fates are believed, but not wonderful, if the wills of men and the gifts of God are considered) the one is married, the other a consecrated virgin; the one has begotten numerous offspring, the other has not even married.

But, they say, the force of the horoscope avails very much. How little this is, I have already sufficiently discussed. But whatever it be, they say it avails in the birth; does it also in the conception?

Where it is both manifest that there is one act of intercourse, and the force of nature is so great that, when a woman has conceived, she cannot then conceive another at all; whence the moments of conception must necessarily be the same in twins. Or perhaps, because they were born under a diverse horoscope, either he was changed into a male, or she into a female, while they were being born?

Since, then, it could be said not altogether absurdly that certain breathings of the stars avail only toward the differences of bodies—as in the solar approaches and recessions we see even the seasons of the year itself varied, and by lunar increases and decreases certain kinds of things increased and diminished, such as sea urchins and shellfish and the marvelous tides of the ocean—but that the wills of the mind are not subject to the positions of the stars: now these men, when they try to bind even our acts to them, admonish us to ask whence this account of theirs cannot hold good for them even in bodies themselves. For what is so pertinent to the body as the body's sex? And yet under the same position of the stars twins of diverse sex could be conceived. Whence what more foolish thing can be said or believed than that the position of the stars, which at the hour of conception was the same for both, could not bring it about that he should not have a sex diverse from his sister, with whom he had the same constellation; while the position of the stars, which was at the hour of their birth, could bring it about that she should differ from him so much in virginal sanctity?

## There is no reason why days should be chosen for doing things.

**7.** And who could bear this, that in choosing days they contrive certain new fates for their acts? That man, evidently, was not born so as to have an admirable son, but rather so as to beget a contemptible one, and therefore the learned man chose the hour at which he should be joined to his wife. He made, then, a fate that he did not have, and from his own deed there began to be fated what had not been in his nativity.

O singular folly! A day is chosen that a wife may be married; I believe for this reason, because one can fall upon a day not good, unless it is chosen, and marry unhappily. Where, then, is what the stars already decreed for one being born? Or can a man, by the choice of a day, change what has already been appointed for him, while what he himself has appointed in choosing the day cannot be changed by another power?

Then, if only men, and not all the things that are under heaven, are subject to the constellations, why do they choose some days as suited for setting vines or trees or crops, other days for giving over cattle or for putting the males to them, that the herds of mares or oxen may be impregnated, and other things of this kind? But if chosen days avail for these things, because the position of the stars dominates all earthly bodies or living things according to the diversities of temporal moments: let one consider how innumerable things are born or arise or begin under one point of time, and have such diverse outcomes, that they persuade any boy that these observations are to be laughed at. For who is so witless as to dare to say that all trees, all plants, all beasts, serpents, birds, fishes, little worms have, one by one, diverse moments of being born? Yet men are wont, to test the skill of the astrologers, to bring to them the constellations of dumb animals, whose birth they diligently observe at home for this exploration, and they prefer those astrologers to the rest who, the constellations examined, say that it is not a man that was born, but a beast. They dare even to say what kind of beast—whether fit for wool, or for carrying, or for the plow, or for guarding the house. For they are tested even with the fates of dogs, and answer these things with great cries of those who admire.

Men dote so far as to suppose that, when a man is born, the other births of things are so inhibited that not even a fly is born with him under the same region of the heaven. For if they admit the fly, the reasoning proceeds which, step by step, by modest advances, leads them from flies to camels and elephants. Nor do they wish to notice this: that on a day chosen for sowing a field, so many grains come into the earth at once, sprout at once, and, the crop having come up, grow green at once, ripen, turn yellow; and yet, of the ears that are coeval with the rest and, so to speak, of the same sprouting, some the blight destroys, some the birds ravage, some men pull up.

How will they say that these had diverse constellations, which they behold to have such diverse outcomes? Or will they regret choosing days for these things, and deny that they pertain to a heavenly decree, and subject to the stars only men, to whom alone on earth God gave free wills?

All these things being considered, it is not without reason believed that, when astrologers answer marvelously many true things, it comes about by the hidden instigation of spirits that are not good, whose care it is to insert and confirm these false and harmful opinions about astral fates in human minds—not by any art of the horoscope noted and examined, which is none at all.

## The Stoics are the authors of the view that fate is a connection of causes.

**8.** But those who call by the name of fate not the constitution of the stars, such as it is when anything is conceived or born or begun, but the connection and series of all causes by which everything that comes to be comes to be: with these there is no need to labor and contend much about a controversy of words, since indeed they ascribe the very order and a certain connection of causes to the will and power of the most high God, who is most rightly and most truly believed both to know all things before they come to be and to leave nothing unordered; from whom are all powers, although the wills of all are not from him. That it is the very will, above all, of the most high God—whose power is extended insuperably through all things—that they call fate, is proved thus. These verses are, if I am not mistaken, by Annaeus Seneca: *Lead me, supreme father and ruler of the high heaven, wherever it has pleased you; there is no delay in obeying. I am present, unwearied. Make me unwilling, I shall follow groaning, and as a bad man shall suffer what I might have done as a good one. The fates lead the willing, the unwilling they drag*[^13]. Surely in this last verse he most evidently called *fates* what above he had called the will of the supreme father; whom he says he is prepared to obey, that he may be led willing, lest he be dragged unwilling; since, namely, *the fates lead the willing, the unwilling they drag.*

Those Homeric verses too support this opinion, which Cicero turned into Latin: *such are the minds of men as the father himself, Jupiter, has lit up the fruit-bearing lands with his light*[^14]. Nor would a poetic opinion have authority in this question; but since he says that the Stoics, asserting the force of fate, are wont to use these verses from Homer, it is treated not of that poet's opinion, but of these philosophers', since through these verses, which they apply to the disputation they hold about fate, what they think fate to be is most openly declared—since they call Jove, whom they think the most high god, him from whom they say the connection of the fates depends.

## Cicero, for free will against fate . . .

**9.** 1. Cicero strives to refute these men in such a way that he thinks he can avail nothing against them unless he does away with divination. This he tries to do away with by denying that there is any knowledge of future things, and contending with all his might that there is none at all, whether in man or in God, and that there is no prediction of things. Thus he both denies God's foreknowledge and tries to overthrow all prophecy, clearer than light, by empty argumentations and by setting against himself certain oracles that can easily be refuted; which, however, he does not even refute. But in refuting these conjectures of the astrologers his discourse reigns, because they are truly such as to destroy and refute themselves.

But those who establish even astral fates are much more tolerable than this man who does away with the foreknowledge of future things. For both to confess that there is a god and to deny that he foreknows the future is the most open insanity.

And when he himself saw this, he attempted even that which is written: *the fool said in his heart: there is no God*[^15]; but not in his own person. For he saw how invidious and troublesome it was, and therefore he made Cotta dispute about this matter against the Stoics in the books *On the Nature of the Gods,* and preferred to give his verdict for Lucilius Balbus, to whom he gave the part of the Stoics to defend, rather than for Cotta, who contended that there is no divine nature[^16]. But in the books *On Divination* he most openly, in his own person, attacks the foreknowledge of future things[^17].

But he seems to do all this lest he should consent that there is fate and lose free will. For he thinks that, with the knowledge of future things granted, fate so follows that it cannot be denied at all.

But however the most tortuous contentions and disputations of the philosophers may stand, we, as we confess the most high and true God, so we confess his will and supreme power and foreknowledge; nor do we fear that on that account we do not by will what we do by will, because he foreknew that we would do it, whose foreknowledge cannot be deceived—which Cicero feared, so that he attacked foreknowledge, and the Stoics feared, so as not to say that all things come to be by necessity, although they contended that all things come to be by fate.

**9.** 2. What is it, then, that Cicero feared in the foreknowledge of future things, so that he strove to undermine it by a detestable disputation? Evidently because, if all future things are foreknown, they will come in the order in which they were foreknown to be going to come; and if they will come in this order, the order of things is certain to the foreknowing God; and if the order of things is certain, the order of causes is certain; for nothing can come to be which some efficient cause has not preceded; but if the order of causes, by which everything that comes to be comes to be, is certain, *by fate,* he says, *all things that come to be come to be.* And if this is so, nothing is in our power and there is no free choice of the will;

*and if we grant this,* he says, *all human life is overthrown; in vain are laws given, in vain are reproaches, praises, censures, exhortations applied, nor have any rewards been established by justice for the good and punishments for the bad.* Lest, then, these unworthy and absurd things, ruinous to human affairs, should follow, he is unwilling that there be foreknowledge of future things; and he confines the religious mind into these straits, that it should choose one of two—either that there is something in our will, or that there is foreknowledge of future things—since he judges that both cannot be, but that if one is confirmed, the other is taken away; if we choose foreknowledge of future things, free choice of the will is taken away; if we choose free choice of the will, foreknowledge of future things is taken away. He himself, then, as a great and learned man, consulting most and most expertly for human life, chose of these two the free choice of the will; and that this might be confirmed, he denied the foreknowledge of future things—and so, while he wishes to make men free, he made them sacrilegious.

But the religious mind chooses both, confesses both, and by the faith of piety confirms both. How? he says; for if there is foreknowledge of future things, all those things will follow which are connected, until it is reached that nothing is in our will. And further, if there is something in our will, by retracing the same steps it is reached that there is no foreknowledge of future things.

For through all those things it is thus retraced: if there is free choice of the will, not all things come to be by fate; if not all things come to be by fate, there is not a certain order of all causes; if there is not a certain order of causes, neither is there a certain order of things to the foreknowing God, things which cannot come to be except by preceding and efficient causes; if the order of things is not certain to the foreknowing God, not all things come as he foreknew that they would come; and further, if not all things come as they were foreknown by him to come, there is not, he says, in God the foreknowledge of all future things.

## What is the faith concerning freedom and foreknowledge.

**9.** 3. We, against these sacrilegious and impious daring deeds, both say that God knows all things before they come to be, and that we do by will whatever we feel and know is done by us only when we are willing. But we do not say that all things come to be by fate; rather, we say that nothing comes to be by fate; since we show that the name of "fate," where it is wont to be placed by those who speak—that is, in the constitution of the stars when each is conceived or born—avails nothing, because the thing itself is asserted in vain.

But the order of causes, where the will of God avails most, we neither deny nor call by the term "fate," unless perhaps we understand "fate" (*fatum*) as said from *fando,* that is, from *speaking*[^18]; for we cannot deny that it is written in the holy Scriptures: *God has spoken once, these two things I have heard, that power belongs to God, and to you, O Lord, belongs mercy, for you render to each according to his works*[^19]. For what is said, *He has spoken once,* is understood "immovably," that is, unchangeably, "he has spoken," just as he knows unchangeably all things that are to come and that he himself is going to do. For this reason, then, we could call "fate" from *speaking,* were it not that this name is now wont to be understood of another matter, toward which we do not wish the hearts of men to be inclined.

But it does not follow that, if to God the order of all causes is certain, therefore nothing is in the choice of our will. For our wills themselves are in that order of causes which is certain to God and contained in his foreknowledge, since human wills too are causes of human works; and so he who foreknew all the causes of things certainly could not, among those causes, be ignorant also of our wills, which he foreknew to be the causes of our works.

**9.** 4. For even that which Cicero himself grants—that nothing comes to be unless an efficient cause precedes—is enough to refute him in this question[^20]. For what does it help him that he says nothing indeed comes to be without a cause, but that not every cause is fatal, because there is a fortuitous cause, a natural one, a voluntary one? It suffices that he confesses that everything which comes to be comes to be only by a preceding cause. For we say that those causes which are called fortuitous—whence "fortune" too received its name—are not none, but hidden, and we attribute them to the will either of the true God or of any spirits; and the natural ones themselves we by no means sever from the will of him who is the author and founder of all nature. But the voluntary causes are either of God or of angels or of men or of any animals, if indeed those movements of the souls devoid of reason are to be called wills, by which they do something according to their nature, when they either seek or avoid anything. By the wills of angels I mean either of the good, whom we call the angels of God, or of the bad, whom we call the angels of the devil or even demons: so too of men, both good and bad.

And through this it is gathered that there are no efficient causes of all the things that come to be except voluntary ones—of that nature, namely, which is the spirit of life. For this air too, or wind, is called "spirit"; but since it is a body, it is not the spirit of life. The spirit of life, then, which gives life to all things and is the creator of every body and of every created spirit, is himself God, a spirit certainly not created. In his will is the supreme power, which aids the good wills of created spirits, judges the bad, orders all, and grants powers to some, to others does not grant them. For as he is the creator of all natures, so he is the giver of all powers, not of wills. For the bad wills are not from him, since they are against nature, which is from him.

Bodies, therefore, are more subject to wills—some to ours, that is, of all mortal living things and of men more than of beasts; some to those of angels; but all are above all subject to the will of God, to whom all wills too are subjected, because they have no power except what he grants. The cause of things, then, which makes and is not made, is God; but other causes both make and are made, such as are all created spirits, especially rational ones. But corporeal causes, which are more made than make, are not to be numbered among the efficient causes, since they can only do what the wills of spirits do out of them.

How, then, does the order of causes, which is certain to the foreknowing God, bring it about that nothing is in our will, when our wills have a great place in that very order of causes? Let Cicero, then, contend with those who say that this order of causes is fatal, or rather call it itself by the name of fate—which we shrink from especially on account of the word, which is not wont to be understood in the true matter. But because he denies that the order of all causes is most certain and most known to the foreknowledge of God, we detest him more than the Stoics. For either he denies that there is a God—which indeed, under the person of another introduced in the books *On the Nature of the Gods,* he attempted to do; or, if he confesses that there is a god whom he denies to be foreknowing of future things, even so he says nothing other than what *the fool said in his heart: There is no God*[^21]. For he who is not foreknowing of all future things is certainly not God.

Wherefore our wills too avail just as much as God willed and foreknew that they would avail; and therefore whatever they avail, they avail most certainly, and what they are going to do, they themselves will altogether do, because he foreknew that they would avail and do it, whose foreknowledge cannot be deceived. Wherefore, if it pleased me to apply the name of "fate" to anything, I would rather say that the fate of the weaker is the will of the more powerful, who has him in his power, than that the choice of our will is taken away by that order of causes which the Stoics, not by usual custom but by their own, call fate.

**10.** 1. Whence neither is that necessity to be dreaded, by dreading which the Stoics labored to distinguish the causes of things in such a way as to withdraw some from necessity and subject others to it, and among those which they were unwilling to be under necessity they placed our wills too, lest, namely, they should not be free, if they were subjected to necessity. For if that is to be called our necessity which is not in our power, but which, even if we are unwilling, effects what it can—such as the necessity of death—it is manifest that our wills, by which we live rightly or wrongly, are not under such a necessity. For we do many things which, if we were unwilling, we certainly would not do. To which the very act of willing primarily pertains; for if we will, it is; if we do not will, it is not; for we would not will, if we were unwilling.

But if that is defined to be necessity, according to which we say that it is necessary that something be so or come to be so, I do not know why we should fear it, lest it take from us the freedom of the will. For we do not place either the life of God or the foreknowledge of God under necessity, if we say that it is necessary for God always to live and to foreknow all things; just as his power is not diminished when it is said that he cannot die or be deceived. For he cannot do this in such a way that, rather, if he could, he would certainly be of lesser power. For he is rightly called almighty, who nevertheless cannot die or be deceived. For he is called almighty by doing what he wills, not by suffering what he does not will; and if this should befall him, he would by no means be almighty. Whence for this reason there are certain things he cannot do, because he is almighty.

So too, when we say that it is necessary that, when we will, we will by free choice: we both say what is without doubt true, and we do not for that reason subject free choice itself to a necessity that takes away freedom.

Our wills, then, exist, and they themselves do whatever we do by willing, which would not come to be if we were unwilling. But whatever anyone suffers unwilling by the will of other men, even so the will avails—if not his, yet a man's will;

but the power is God's. (For if there were only a will and it could not do what it willed, it would be hindered by a more powerful will; nor even so would the will be anything but a will, and not another's, but his who willed, even if he could not fulfill what he willed.) Whence whatever a man suffers against his own will, he ought to attribute not to the wills of men or angels or of any created spirit, but rather to him who gives power to the willing.

**10.** 2. It is not, then, on this account nothing in our will, because God foreknew what would be in our will. For he who foreknew this foreknew not nothing. And further, if he who foreknew what would be in our will foreknew not nothing, but something: surely, even with him foreknowing, there is something in our will. Wherefore we are in no way compelled either, retaining the foreknowledge of God, to take away the choice of the will, or, retaining the choice of the will, to deny (which is impious) that God is foreknowing of future things;

but we embrace both, both faithfully and truly confess both: the one, that we may believe well; the other, that we may live well. But one lives badly if one does not believe well about God. Whence far be it from us to deny his foreknowledge, in order that we may will freely—he by whose aid we are or shall be free.

Accordingly laws, reproaches, exhortations, praises, and censures are not in vain, because he foreknew that these too would be, and they avail very much, as much as he foreknew that they would avail; and prayers avail to obtain those things which he foreknew he would grant to those who pray; and rewards have been justly established for good deeds and punishments for sins.

For a man does not sin because God foreknew that he would sin; rather, it is for this reason not doubted that he himself sins when he sins, because he whose foreknowledge cannot be deceived foreknew that not fate, not fortune, not anything else, but that the man himself would sin. And if he is unwilling, he certainly does not sin; but if he is unwilling to sin, this too God foreknew.

## God disposes all things.

**11.** The most high and true God, then, with his Word and the Holy Spirit—which three are one—the one almighty God, the creator and maker of every soul and every body, by participation in whom all are happy who are happy by truth and not by vanity, who made man a rational animal of soul and body, who, when man sinned, neither permitted him to go unpunished nor abandoned him without mercy; who gave to good and bad alike existence, even with the stones, seminal life, even with the trees, sentient life, even with the beasts, intellectual life, with the angels alone; from whom is every mode, every form, every order; from whom is measure, number, weight; from whom is whatever exists naturally, of whatever kind it is, of whatever value it is; from whom are the seeds of forms, the forms of seeds, the movements of seeds and of forms; who gave to the flesh too its origin, beauty, health, the fecundity of propagation, the disposition of the members, the well-being of concord; who gave to the irrational soul also memory, sense, appetite, but to the rational soul, besides these, mind, intelligence, will; who did not leave—not only heaven and earth, not only angel and man, but not even the entrails of a tiny and contemptible living thing, nor the little feather of a bird, nor the little flower of a plant, nor the leaf of a tree—without the harmony of its parts and a certain peace, as it were: he is in no way to be believed to have willed the kingdoms of men and their dominions and servitudes to be alien from the laws of his providence.

## The Romans had an immense love of liberty.

**12.** 1. Accordingly let us see what morals of the Romans, and for what cause, the true God—in whose power even earthly kingdoms are—deigned to help toward enlarging the empire. That we might discuss this more completely, we wrote the preceding book, which pertains to this point: that in this matter there is no power of those gods whom they thought ought to be worshipped even with trifling things; and the earlier parts of the present volume, which we have carried thus far, on doing away with the question of fate, lest anyone who had already been persuaded that the Roman empire was not propagated and preserved by the worship of those gods should attribute it rather to some fate than to the most powerful will of the most high God.

The ancient and earliest Romans, then, as far as their history teaches and commends, although—like the other nations, with the one exception of the people of the Hebrews—they worshipped false gods and sacrificed not to God but to demons, were nevertheless *greedy of praise, generous with money; they wanted immense glory and honest riches*[^22]; this they loved most ardently, for this they wished to live, for this they did not hesitate to die; their other desires they repressed by the immense desire of this one. Their very homeland, finally, because to serve seemed inglorious but to dominate and command glorious, they longed first with all zeal to be free, and then to be mistress.

Hence it is that, not bearing royal domination, *they made for themselves annual commands and two commanders*[^23], *who were called consuls from consulting (consulendo), not kings or lords from reigning and lording*[^24]; although "kings" too certainly seem better named from "ruling" (regendo), as "kingdom" (regnum) from "kings" (reges), and "kings" in turn, as has been said, from "ruling"; but the royal pomp was thought not the discipline of one ruling or the goodwill of one consulting, but the pride of one dominating.

And so, King Tarquinius being expelled and consuls instituted, there followed what the same author set down among the praises of the Romans, that *the state, having attained liberty, grew incredibly, so much in a short time; so great a desire of glory had come over them*[^25]. This greed of praise, then, and desire of glory did those many wonderful things—praiseworthy, that is, and glorious according to the estimation of men.

**12.** 2. The same Sallust praises great and renowned men of his own times, Marcus Cato and Gaius Caesar, saying that for a long time that republic had no one great in virtue, but that within his own memory there were those two of immense virtue, though of diverse morals. And among the praises of Caesar he set this down: that he longed for himself a great command, an army, a new war, where his virtue could shine forth. Thus it came about, in the wishes of men great in virtue, that Bellona would stir up wretched nations to war and drive them with her bloody scourge, so that there might be a place where their virtue could shine forth. This, assuredly, that greed of praise and desire of glory did. Therefore, first by love of liberty, and afterward also of domination, and by desire of praise and glory, they did many great things. Their own distinguished poet too renders testimony to them of both; for thence he says: *and Porsenna bade them receive the banished Tarquin and pressed the City with a mighty siege; the sons of Aeneas rushed upon the sword for liberty*[^26]. Then, accordingly, it was a great thing for them either to die bravely or to live free.

But when liberty had been attained, so great a desire of glory had come over them that liberty alone was too little, unless domination too were sought, while it is held a great thing that the same poet says, as though Jove were speaking: *nay, harsh Juno, who now wearies sea and lands and heaven with fear, shall change her counsels for the better and shall cherish with me  
the Romans, lords of the world and the toga-wearing race. So it is decreed. An age shall come, as the lustra glide by, when the house of Assaracus shall press in servitude Phthia and famed Mycenae and shall lord it over conquered Argos*[^27]. Which things Virgil, introducing Jove as foretelling them as though future, was himself already recalling as done and beholding as present; but I wished to recount them for this reason, to show that the Romans held domination, after liberty, in such a way that it was set among their great praises. Hence too is that of the same poet, who, when he preferred to the arts of other nations those very arts proper to the Romans, of reigning and commanding and subjugating and warring down peoples, said: *others shall hammer out the breathing bronze more softly, I grant it indeed, shall draw living faces from the marble, shall plead causes better, and with the rod shall trace the courses of the heaven and tell the rising stars: you, O Roman, remember to rule the peoples with your sway (these shall be your arts), to impose the custom of peace, to spare the conquered and to war down the proud*[^28].

**12.** 3. These arts they exercised the more expertly the less they gave themselves to pleasures and to the enervation of mind and body in coveting and increasing riches, and, through these, in corrupting their morals—by plundering wretched citizens, by lavishing on base actors.

Whence those who already surpassed and abounded in such a stain of morals, when Sallust wrote these things and Virgil sang, sought after honors and glory not by those arts, but by tricks and deceits. Whence the same author says: *but at first ambition rather than avarice exercised the minds of men, which vice, however, was nearer to virtue. For glory, honor, command the good man and the cowardly alike long for themselves; but the one,* he says, *strives by the true way, while the other, because good arts are lacking to him, contends by tricks and deceits*[^29].

These are those "good arts," namely, to come to honor and glory and command through virtue, not through deceitful ambition; which, however, the good man and the cowardly alike long for themselves; but the one, that is, the good man, strives by the true way. The *way* is virtue, by which he strives as toward the end of his possession, that is, toward glory, honor, command.

That the Romans had this implanted in them even the temples of the gods among them indicate, which they set up most closely joined, of Virtue and Honor[^30], holding as gods the things that are given by God. Whence it can be understood what end they wished virtue to have, and to what those who were good referred it—to honor, namely; for the bad did not even have it, although they desired to have honor, which they tried to obtain by bad arts, that is, by tricks and deceits.

**12.** 4. Cato is praised the better. For of him he says: *the less he sought glory, the more it followed him*[^31]. Since indeed glory, with desire of which they burned, is the judgment of men who think well of men; and therefore virtue is better, which is content with no human testimony but that of its own conscience. Whence the Apostle says: *for this is our glory: the testimony of our conscience*[^32]; and in another place: *but let each one prove his own work, and then he will have glory in himself alone and not in another*[^33].

Glory, then, and honor and command, which they longed for themselves and to which the good strove to come by "good arts," virtue ought not to follow, but rather they should follow virtue. For there is no true virtue except that which tends toward that end where is the good of man, than which there is no better. Whence too the honors which Cato sought he ought not to have sought, but the state ought to have given them, on account of his virtue, to him who did not seek them.

**12.** 5. But since within that memory there were two Romans great in virtue, Caesar and Cato, the virtue of Cato seems to have been far nearer to truth than that of Caesar. Accordingly, what sort of state there was at that time, and what sort it had been before, let us see in the very opinion of Cato: *Do not think,* he says, *that our ancestors made the republic great from small by arms. If it were so, we should have it by far the most beautiful. For of allies and citizens, and besides of arms and horses, we have a greater abundance than they had.*

*But there were other things that made them great, which are none of ours: at home industry, abroad just rule, a mind free in counsel, subject neither to wrongdoing nor to lust. In place of these we have luxury and avarice, public want, private opulence; we praise riches, we follow sloth; between the good and the bad there is no distinction; ambition possesses all the rewards of virtue. Nor is it any wonder: where you each take counsel separately for yourselves, where at home you are slaves to pleasures, here to money or favor, it comes about that an assault is made upon a republic left empty*[^34].

**12.** 6. Whoever hears these words of Cato or of Sallust thinks that the ancient Romans who are praised were then all such, or that most were. It is not so; otherwise the things would not be true which the same author likewise writes, the things I recounted in the second book of this work[^35], where he says that the injuries of the more powerful, and because of them the withdrawal of the common people from the senators, and other dissensions, had existed at home from the very beginning, and that no more equitable and moderate justice was practiced than when the kings had been expelled, so long as there was fear of Tarquin, until the grave war which had been undertaken with Etruria on his account should be finished;

but that afterward the senators exercised the common people with a servile rule, scourged them after the manner of kings, drove them from their land, and, with the rest having no share, alone held the command[^36]; and that of these discords—while the one party wished to dominate, the other was unwilling to serve—the end was the Second Punic War, because again a grave fear began to press, and to restrain their restless minds, by another and greater concern, from those disturbances, and to recall them to civil concord.

But great things were administered by certain few men who were good in their own manner; and, those evils being tolerated and tempered, by the providence of a few good men that state grew; as the same historian says that, when he read and heard the many renowned deeds which the Roman people did at home and in war, by sea and land, it pleased him to attend to what thing especially had sustained such great undertakings[^37]; since he knew that very often the Romans had contended with a small band against great legions of the enemy, and had learned that wars had been waged with small forces against opulent kings; and, pondering many things, he said it was clear to him that the extraordinary virtue of a few citizens had accomplished everything, and that thereby it came about that poverty surpassed riches, fewness a multitude. *But after the state was corrupted by luxury and sloth,* he says, *again the republic by its own greatness sustained the vices of the commanders and magistrates*[^38].

The virtue, then, of a few who strove toward glory, honor, command by the true way—that is, by virtue itself—was praised even by Cato. Hence came that industry at home which Cato recounted, so that the treasury was opulent, private fortunes slender. Whence, the morals being corrupted, he set the vice on the contrary side: public want, private opulence[^39].

## The hero of the nations seeks glory for himself.

**13**. Wherefore, when the kingdoms of the East had long been illustrious, God willed that there be a western one too, later in time but more illustrious in the breadth and greatness of empire;

And he granted it especially to such men, for taming the grave evils of many nations—men who for the sake of honor, praise, and glory consulted for their homeland, in which they sought that very glory, and did not hesitate to set its safety before their own safety, repressing, for the sake of this one vice, that is, the love of praise, the desire of money and many other vices.

For he sees more soundly who recognizes that even the love of praise is a vice, which did not escape the poet Horace, who says: *are you swollen with the love of praise? There are sure expiations that can restore you, the book being read through three times with a pure heart*[^40]. And the same poet, in a lyric song, to repress the lust of domination, sang thus: *you would reign more widely by taming a greedy spirit than if you joined Libya to far-off Gades and either Carthaginian served one master*[^41].

Yet those who do not curb their baser lusts by the faith of piety, the Holy Spirit obtained, and by the love of intelligible beauty, are at least better, by the desire of human praise and glory—not indeed now holy, but less base. Cicero too could not dissemble about this in those same books which he wrote *On the Republic,* where he speaks of forming the leader of the state, whom he says must be nourished by glory, and consequently recounts that his ancestors did many marvelous and renowned things by the desire of glory[^42]. To this vice, then, they not only did not resist, but even judged that it ought to be stirred up and kindled, thinking this useful to the republic. And yet not even in his books of philosophy does Cicero dissemble about this plague, where he confesses it clearer than light. For when he was speaking of such pursuits, which certainly ought to be followed with the end of the true good, not by the windiness of human praise, he brought in this universal and general opinion: *honor nourishes the arts, and all are kindled to pursuits by glory, and those things always lie neglected which are disapproved among any people*[^43].

**14.** This desire, then, is without doubt better resisted than yielded to. For each one is the more like God, the cleaner he is of this uncleanness. And although in this life it is not utterly rooted out of the heart, because it does not cease to tempt even minds that are progressing well: let at least the desire of glory be overcome by the love of justice, so that, if anywhere there lie neglected things that are disapproved among any people—if they are good, if they are right—even the love of human praise itself may blush and yield to the love of truth.

For so much is this vice an enemy of pious faith, if the desire of glory in the heart be greater than the fear or love of God, that the Lord said: *how can you believe, who expect glory from one another and do not seek the glory which is from God alone?*[^44] Likewise of certain men who had believed in him and feared to confess it openly, the evangelist says: *they loved the glory of men more than that of God*[^45].

This the holy apostles did not do; who, when they preached the name of Christ in those places where it was not only disapproved (as he says: *and those things always lie neglected which are disapproved among any people*), but even held in the utmost detestation, holding what they had heard from the good Teacher and Physician of minds: *if anyone denies me before men, I will deny him before my Father who is in heaven,* or *before the angels of God*[^46]—amid curses and reproaches, amid the gravest persecutions and cruel punishments, were not deterred from the preaching of human salvation by so great a roar of human offense. And because, doing and saying divine things and living divinely, they in a manner warred down hard hearts and introduced the peace of justice, an immense glory followed in the Church of Christ:

they did not rest in it as in the end of their own virtue, but, referring that very thing too to the glory of God, by whose grace they were such, they kindled also by this tinder those for whom they consulted to the love of him by whom they too might be made such. For lest they should be good for the sake of human glory, their Teacher had taught them, saying: *beware of doing your justice before men, to be seen by them; otherwise you will have no reward with your Father who is in heaven*[^47].

But again, lest understanding this perversely they should fear to please men and profit less by hiding the fact that they are good, showing with what end they ought to become known, he says: *let your works shine before men, that they may see your good deeds and glorify your Father who is in heaven*[^48]. Not, then, *that you may be seen by them,* that is, with this intention, that you should wish them to be turned toward you, since through yourselves you are nothing; but *that they may glorify your Father who is in heaven,* being turned toward whom they may become what you are.

These the martyrs followed, who surpassed the Scaevolas and Curtiuses and Deciuses—not by inflicting punishments on themselves, but by bearing those inflicted on them—and by true virtue, because by true piety, and by an innumerable multitude.

But since those men were in the earthly city, to whom the end of all their offices on its behalf was set as its safety and a kingdom not in heaven but on earth—not in eternal life but in the passing away of the dying and the succession of those about to die—what else would they love but glory, by which they wished, even after death, to live, as it were, in the mouth of those who praise?

**15.** To those, then, to whom God was not going to give eternal life with his holy angels in his heavenly city—to whose society true piety leads, which renders the service of religion, which the Greeks call λατρεία, to none but the one true God—if he did not grant them even this earthly glory of a most excellent empire: a reward would not be rendered to their good arts, that is, to the virtues by which they strove to come to so great a glory. For of such men, who seem to do something good for this purpose, that they may be glorified by men, the Lord too says: *amen I say to you, they have received their reward*[^49]. So these too despised their own private affairs for the common thing, that is, the republic, and for its treasury; resisted avarice; consulted for their homeland with free counsel; were subject neither to wrongdoing according to their own laws nor to lust; by all these arts they strove, as though by the true way, toward honors, command, glory; they were honored among almost all nations, imposed the laws of their empire on many nations, and to this day are glorious in letters and history among almost all nations: there is no reason for them to complain of the justice of the most high and true God; *they have received their reward.*

**16.** But the reward of the saints—who even here endure reproaches for the truth of God, which is hateful to the lovers of this world—is far otherwise. That city is everlasting; there no one is born, because no one dies; there is true and full felicity—not a goddess, but a gift of God; thence we have received the pledge of faith, while, on pilgrimage, we sigh for its beauty; there the sun does not rise upon the good and the bad[^50], but the sun of justice protects only the good; there there will be no great industry to enrich the public treasury from straitened private fortunes, where the common treasure is truth.

Accordingly, the Roman empire was extended to human glory not only that such a reward might be rendered to such men, but also that the citizens of that eternal city, while they are here on pilgrimage, might diligently and soberly look upon those examples and see how great a love is owed to the supernal homeland for the sake of eternal life, if so great a love was given to the earthly one by its citizens for the sake of human glory.

**17.** 1. For as far as concerns this life of mortals, which is led and ended in a few days, what does it matter under whose rule a man who is to die lives, if those who rule do not compel him to impious and unjust things? Or did the Romans really harm the nations whom, once subjugated, they laid their laws upon, except in that this was done by an immense slaughter of wars?

And if this had been done with concord, the same thing would have been done with better success; but there would have been no glory for those who triumphed. For the Romans too did not fail to live under those laws which they imposed on the rest. If this had been done without Mars and Bellona, so that Victory too had no place, none conquering where none had fought: would not the Romans and the other nations be in one and the same condition?

especially if presently that were done which was afterward most gratefully and most humanely done, that all who belonged to the Roman empire received the fellowship of the city and were Roman citizens, and so that became of all which was before of a few;

only that that common people which had no lands of its own would live off the public; and this nourishment of theirs would be furnished more gratefully by men in concord, through good administrators of the republic, than extorted from the conquered.

**17.** 2. For what it matters to safety and good morals—and certainly to the very dignities of men—that some have conquered and others been conquered, I do not see at all, except for that most empty pomp of human glory, in which those received their reward who burned with an immense desire of it and waged burning wars. For do not their fields pay tribute? Is it permitted them to learn what is not permitted to others? Are there not many senators in other lands who do not know Rome even by sight? Take away boastfulness, and what are all men but men? And if the perversity of the age allowed that the better men should be the more honored: not even thus ought human honor to be held a great thing, since smoke is of no weight.

But let us use, even in these matters, the benefit of the Lord our God; let us consider how great things they despised, what things they endured, what desires they subdued for human glory—those who deserved to receive it as the reward, so to speak, of such virtues; and let even this avail us toward suppressing pride: that, since that city in which it is promised us to reign is as far from this one as heaven is from earth, eternal life from temporal joy, solid glory from empty praises, the society of angels from the society of mortals, the light of him who made the sun and moon from the light of the sun and moon,

the citizens of so great a homeland may seem to themselves to have done nothing great if, to attain it, they have done some good work or endured some evils, when those men, for this earthly one already attained, did such great things, suffered such great things—especially because the remission of sins, which gathers the citizens to the eternal homeland, has something to which, by a certain shadow, that asylum of Romulus was similar, by which the impunity of crimes of every kind gathered the multitude by which that city was to be founded.

**18.** 1. What great thing is it, then, for the sake of that eternal and heavenly homeland to despise all the allurements of this age, however pleasant, if for this temporal and earthly one Brutus could even kill his sons[^51]—which that homeland compels no one to do? But surely it is harder to slay one's sons than what must be done for that homeland: either to give to the poor those things which seemed to be gathered and kept for one's sons, or, if a temptation arise which compels it to be done for faith and justice, to lose them.

For it is not earthly riches that make either us or our sons happy—riches to be lost either while we live or, when we are dead, to be possessed by we know not whom, or perhaps by those whom we would not; but God makes us happy, who is the true wealth of minds. But to Brutus, because he killed his sons, even the poet who praises him gives testimony of unhappiness. For he says: *and the father, unhappy, will call his sons, who stir up new wars, to punishment for the sake of fair liberty, however posterity shall report those deeds.* But in the following verse he consoled the unhappy man: *love of country prevails, and the immense desire of praise*[^52].

These are those two things, liberty and the desire of human praise, which drove the Romans to wonderful deeds. If, then, for the liberty of men who are to die and the desire of praises which are sought from mortals, sons could be killed by a father: what great thing is it, if, for the sake of true liberty, which makes us free from the dominion of iniquity and death and the devil, and not by desire of human praises but by love of the men to be freed—not from King Tarquin, but from the demons and the prince of demons—not sons are killed, but the poor of Christ are counted among sons?

**18.** 2. If another Roman leader too, surnamed Torquatus, killed his son—not because he had fought against the homeland, but even for the homeland, yet because he had fought against his command, that is, against what his father the commander had commanded, provoked by an enemy and fighting in youthful ardor, although he had conquered[^53]—lest there be more evil in the example of a command despised than good in the glory of an enemy slain:

why should they boast who, for the laws of an immortal homeland, despise all earthly goods, which are loved much less than sons?

If Furius Camillus freed even an ungrateful homeland—from whose neck he had driven the yoke of its bitterest enemies, the Veientes, and by whose rivals he had been condemned—from the Gauls a second time, because he had no better one where he could live more gloriously[^54]:

why should he be exalted, as though he had done some great thing, who, having perhaps suffered in the Church from carnal enemies the gravest injury of dishonor, did not transfer himself to her enemies the heretics, or himself found some heresy against her, but rather defended her, as far as he could, from the most ruinous depravity of the heretics—since there is no other, not where one may live in the glory of men, but where eternal life may be acquired?

If Mucius, that peace might be made with King Porsenna, who was pressing the Romans in a most grievous war—because he could not kill Porsenna himself and, deceived, killed another in his place—stretched his right hand into the burning altar before his eyes, saying that many such as the one he saw had conspired for his destruction, and Porsenna, shuddering at his fortitude and the conspiracy of such men, without any hesitation, peace being made, withdrew himself from that war[^55]:

who is going to charge his merits to the kingdom of heaven, if for it he has spent his whole body in the flames—not one hand, and not doing this to himself of his own accord, but enduring it while someone else persecutes him?

If Curtius, armed, with his horse spurred, hurled himself headlong into the broken chasm of the earth, serving the oracles of his gods, since they had commanded that the best thing the Romans had be sent there[^56], and they could understand nothing else than that they excelled in men and arms—whence, namely, it behooved that, at the gods' command, an armed man be cast headlong into that destruction:

what great thing for the eternal homeland will he say he has done, who, having suffered some enemy of his faith—not casting himself of his own accord into such a death, but, sent by that enemy, has died—since indeed from his Lord and the king of his homeland he has received a more certain oracle: *do not fear those who kill the body but cannot kill the soul*[^57]?

If the Decii, in a manner consecrating themselves to be killed by certain words, devoted themselves, so that, as they fell and appeased the wrath of the gods with their blood, the Roman army might be freed[^58]:

in no way will the holy martyrs be proud, as though they had done something worthy for the sake of sharing in that homeland where there is eternal and true felicity, if, even to the shedding of their own blood, loving not only their brethren, for whom it was shed, but also their very enemies, by whom it was shed, as it was commanded them, they have striven by the faith of love and the love of faith?

If Marcus Pulvillus, dedicating the temple of Jove, Juno, and Minerva, when the death of his son was falsely announced to him by enviers, so that, disturbed by that message, he might withdraw and his colleague thus obtain the glory of the dedication, despised it so far that he ordered the son to be even cast out unburied (so far had the desire of glory conquered the grief of bereavement in his heart)[^59]:

what great thing will he say he has done for the preaching of the holy Gospel, by which the citizens of the supernal homeland are freed from various errors and gathered, to whom, when anxious about the burial of his father, the Lord said: *follow me, and let the dead bury their dead*[^60]?

If Marcus Regulus, lest he should deceive his most cruel enemies by his oath, returned to them from Rome itself, because, as he is reported to have answered the Romans who wished to keep him, after he had served the Africans he could not have there the dignity of an honorable citizen—and the Carthaginians, because he had pleaded against them in the Roman senate, killed him with the gravest tortures[^61]:

what torments are not to be despised for the sake of the faith of that homeland to whose blessedness faith itself leads? *Or what shall be rendered to the Lord for all that he has rendered*[^62], if a man has suffered, for the faith owed to him, such things as Regulus suffered for the faith he owed to his most ruinous enemies?

How will a Christian dare to exalt himself over his voluntary poverty, so that in the pilgrimage of this life he may walk the more unencumbered along the way that leads to the homeland, where the true riches is God himself, when he hears or reads that Lucius Valerius, who died in his consulship, was so poor that his burial was provided for by money collected from the people[^63]? Let him hear or read that Quintius Cincinnatus, when he possessed four acres and cultivated them with his own hands, was brought from the plow to be made dictator—an office certainly greater in honor than consul—and, having attained immense glory by conquering the enemy, remained in the same poverty[^64]?

Or what great thing will he proclaim he has done, who has been seduced by no reward of this world from the fellowship of that eternal homeland, when he has learned that Fabricius could not be torn away from the Roman state by the great gifts of Pyrrhus, king of the Epirotes—even a fourth part of the kingdom being promised—and preferred to remain there a private man in his own poverty[^65]?

For as to the fact that, while they had the republic—that is, the affair of the people, the affair of the homeland, the common affair—most opulent and most rich, they themselves were so poor in their own houses that one of them, who had already been twice consul, was driven from that senate of poor men by the censorial mark, because he was found to have ten pounds of silver in his vessels[^66]; thus those very men were poor by whose triumphs the public treasury was enriched:

do not all Christians who, by a more excellent purpose, make their riches common according to what is written in the *Acts of the Apostles*[^67]—that distribution be made to each as each has need, and that no one call anything his own, but that all things be common to them—understand that they ought to be puffed up by no boastfulness on this account, doing this to obtain the fellowship of the angels, when those men did almost some such thing to preserve the glory of the Romans?

**18.** 3. These and other such things, if any of this kind are found in their writings, when would they become so known, when would they be proclaimed with so great a fame, were the Roman empire not extended far and wide and increased by magnificent successes? Accordingly, through that empire, so broad and so long-lasting, renowned and glorious by the virtues of such great men, both the reward which they sought was rendered to their intention, and to us are set examples of a necessary admonition,

so that, if we have not held, for the most glorious city of God, the virtues to which these of theirs—which they held for the glory of the earthly city—are in some way similar, we may be pricked with shame; and if we have held them, we may not be exalted with pride; since, as the Apostle says, *the sufferings of this time are not worthy of the future glory which will be revealed in us*[^68]. But for human glory and the present time their life was judged worthy enough.

Whence the Jews too, who killed Christ—the New Testament revealing what in the Old was veiled, namely that the one true God is to be worshipped not for earthly and temporal benefits, which divine providence grants mingled to good and bad, but for eternal life and perpetual gifts and the fellowship of that very supernal city—were most rightly given over to the glory of these men, so that those who by virtues of whatever kind sought and acquired earthly glory should conquer those who by great vices killed and spurned the giver of true glory and of the eternal city.

## What the virtue of the citizens, and what the virtue of the saints, is.

**19.** There is indeed a difference between the desire of human glory and the desire of domination. For although it is an easy slope, that he who is too much delighted by human glory should also ardently affect to dominate, yet those who desire true glory—even of human praises—give heed not to displease those who judge well. For there are many good things in morals, of which many judge well, although many do not have them; through these good qualities of morals men strive toward glory and command or domination, of which Sallust says: *but the one strives by the true way*[^69]. But whoever, without the desire of glory by which a man fears to displease those who judge well, longs to dominate and command, seeks for the most part to obtain what he loves even by the most open crimes. Accordingly, he who desires glory either strives by the true way or certainly *contends by tricks and deceits,* wishing to seem to be good, which he is not.

And therefore for one who has the virtues it is a great virtue to despise glory, because the contempt of it is in the sight of God, but is not laid open to human judgment. For whatever he does before the eyes of men, that he may appear a despiser of glory, if it is believed that he does it for greater praise—that is, for greater glory—there is no way for him to show those who suspect this that he is otherwise than they suspect.

But he who despises the judgments of those who praise also despises the rashness of those who suspect; whose salvation, however, if he is truly good, he does not despise, since one who has his virtues from the Spirit of God is of such great justice that he loves even his very enemies, and so loves them that he wishes those who hate or disparage him to be corrected and to be his consorts, not in the earthly homeland, but in the supernal one;

but as for those who praise him, although he sets little store by the fact that they praise him, yet he does not set little store by the fact that they love him, nor does he wish to deceive those who praise, lest he beguile those who love; and therefore he ardently insists that rather he be praised from whom a man has whatever in him is rightly praised.

But he who, a despiser of glory, is greedy of domination surpasses the beasts, whether in the vices of cruelty or of luxury. Such were certain Romans. For, losing the care of reputation, they did not lack the desire of domination. That there were many such, history reveals; but the summit, and as it were a certain citadel, of this vice Nero Caesar first attained, whose luxury was so great that nothing manly was thought to be feared from him; whose cruelty was so great that nothing soft would have been believed to be in him, had it not been known[^70].

Yet even to such men the power of dominating is not given except by the providence of the most high God, when he judges human affairs worthy of such masters. The divine voice is open about this matter, the Wisdom of God speaking: *through me kings reign, and tyrants through me hold the earth*[^71]. But lest "tyrants" be thought to mean not the worst and wicked kings, but, by the old name, the powerful—whence Virgil says: *part of my peace will be to have touched the right hand of the tyrant*[^72]—it is most openly said in another place of God: *because he makes a hypocrite to reign on account of the perversity of the people*[^73].

Wherefore, although I have set forth as well as I could for what cause the one true and just God helped the Romans, who were good according to a certain pattern of the earthly city, to attain the glory of so great an empire: there can yet be another, more hidden cause, on account of the diverse merits of the human race, more known to God than to us—while this stands agreed among all the truly pious: that no one without true piety, that is, without the true worship of the true God, can have true virtue, and that it is not true when it serves human glory; yet that those who are not citizens of the eternal city, which in our sacred Writings is called the city of God[^74], are more useful to the earthly city when they have virtue even of that kind than if they have not even that.

But those who, endowed with true piety, live well, if they have the knowledge of ruling peoples, nothing is happier for human affairs than that, by God's mercy, they should have the power. But such men attribute their virtues, however great they can have them in this life, to nothing but the grace of God, because he gave them to the willing, the believing, the asking; and at the same time they understand how much is lacking to them for the perfection of justice, such as there is in the society of those holy angels, to which they strive to fit themselves. But however much that virtue be praised and proclaimed which serves the glory of men without true piety, it is by no means to be compared to the tiny beginnings of the saints, whose hope is placed in the grace and mercy of the true God.

## It is virtue that serves pleasure and glory.

**20.** The philosophers who place the end of the human good in virtue itself are wont, in order to bring shame upon certain philosophers who approve the virtues[^75] but measure them by the end of bodily pleasure and think that pleasure is to be sought for its own sake[^76], while these others think virtue is to be sought for its own sake, to paint, as it were, a certain picture in words,

where Pleasure sits on a royal throne like some delicate queen, and the virtues are subjected to her as handmaids, watching her nod[^77], to do what she has commanded: she bids Prudence inquire vigilantly how Pleasure may reign and be safe; she bids Justice furnish what benefits she can, to procure the friendships necessary for bodily comforts, and do injury to no one, lest, the laws being offended, Pleasure cannot live secure; she bids Fortitude, if pain befall the body which does not drive it to death, hold its mistress, that is, Pleasure, bravely in the thought of the mind, so that by the recollection of its former delights it may soothe the stings of present pain; she bids Temperance take only so much of food, and of whatever else delights, lest by excess something harmful disturb the health and Pleasure—which the Epicureans place at its greatest even in bodily health—be gravely offended[^78]. Thus the virtues, with all the dignity of their glory, will serve Pleasure as some imperious and dishonorable little woman. Nothing, they say, is more ignominious and more deformed than this picture, and nothing which the gaze of good men can less bear; and they say the truth.

But I do not think there would be a picture of sufficiently due decency, if such a one too were feigned, where the virtues serve human glory. For although Glory herself is not a delicate woman, she is puffed up and has much emptiness. Whence a certain solidity and firmness of the virtues does not worthily serve her, so that Prudence should foresee nothing, Justice distribute nothing, Fortitude endure nothing, Temperance moderate nothing, except for what may please men and serve windy glory.

Nor will those defend themselves from this foulness who, while they spurn the judgments of others as despisers of glory, seem wise to themselves and please themselves. For their virtue, if indeed there is any, is in another way subjected to human praise; for he himself who pleases himself is not other than a man.

But he who by true piety believes and hopes in God, whom he loves, gives more heed to those things in which he displeases himself than to those things, if any are in him, which please not so much himself as Truth; nor does he attribute that for which he can now please anyone to anything but the mercy of him whom he fears to displease—giving thanks for the things healed, pouring out prayers for the things to be healed.

## God gives dominion in the series of times . . .

**21.** Since these things are so, let us attribute the power of giving kingdom and empire to none but the true God, who gives felicity in the kingdom of heaven to the pious alone, but the earthly kingdom both to the pious and the impious, as it pleases him, to whom nothing pleases unjustly. For although we have said something which he willed to be open to us, yet it is too much for us, and greatly surpasses our powers, to discuss the hidden things of men and to judge by a clear examination the merits of kingdoms.

That one true God, then, who neither in judgment nor in aid deserts the human race, when he willed and as much as he willed gave the kingdom to the Romans; who gave it to the Assyrians, or even to the Persians, by whom only two gods are worshipped, one good, the other bad, as their writings contain[^79]—to say nothing of the Hebrew people, of whom I have already said as much as seemed sufficient[^80], who worshipped none but the one God even when they reigned.

He, then, who gave the Persians crops without the worship of the goddess Segetia, who gave the other gifts of the earth without the worship of so many gods whom these men set, one over single things, or even several over single things: he gave them their kingdom too without the worship of those by whose worship these men believed they had reigned.

So too with men: he who gave it to Marius gave it to Gaius Caesar; he who gave it to Augustus gave it also to Nero; he who gave it to the Vespasians, father or son, most agreeable emperors, gave it also to the most cruel Domitian;

and—lest it be necessary to go through them one by one—he who gave it to Constantine the Christian gave it also to Julian the apostate,

whose excellent natural gifts a sacrilegious and detestable curiosity, in the love of dominating, deceived—a curiosity to whose vain oracles he was given over, when, relying on the security of victory, he burned the ships in which necessary food was carried;

then, hotly pressing on with immoderate daring deeds, and soon, by the merit of his rashness, slain in hostile territory, he left the army destitute, so that there was no other way to escape thence except that, contrary to that auspice of the god Terminus, of which we spoke in the previous book, the bounds of the Roman empire should be moved[^81]. For the god Terminus yielded to necessity, who had not yielded to Jove.

These things plainly the one true God rules and governs, as it pleases him; and if by hidden causes, are they therefore unjust?

**22.** So too the very times of wars—just as it is in his discretion and just judgment and mercy either to grind down or to console the human race—so that some end more quickly, others more slowly. The war of the pirates was finished by Pompey, the Third Punic War by Scipio, with incredible speed and brevity of time. The war of the fugitive gladiators too, although many Roman generals and two consuls were conquered and Italy horribly ground down and laid waste, was nevertheless, after much was consumed, consumed in the third year. The Picentes, Marsi, and Peligni—nations not foreign but Italian—after a long and most devoted servitude under the Roman yoke, tried to raise their head into liberty, when already many nations had been subjugated to the Roman empire and Carthage destroyed; in which Italian war, the Romans being very often conquered—where both two consuls perished and other most noble senators—this evil was nevertheless not drawn out for a long time; for the fifth year gave it an end.

But the Second Punic War, with the greatest losses and calamity to the republic, for eighteen years weakened and almost consumed the Roman forces; in two battles nearly seventy thousand of the Romans fell. The First Punic War was carried on through twenty-three years; the Mithridatic war for forty years. And lest anyone suppose that the early Romans were stronger for carrying on wars more quickly, the Samnite war, in those earlier times much praised in every virtue, was drawn out for nearly fifty years; in which war the Romans were so conquered that they were even sent under the yoke. But because they did not love glory for the sake of justice, but seemed to love justice for the sake of glory, they broke the peace made and the treaty.

These things I recount for this reason, because many, ignorant of past things—some even dissemblers of their own knowledge—if in Christian times they see some war drawn out a little longer, at once leap most insolently upon our religion, exclaiming that, if it did not exist and the deities were worshipped by the old rite, that war too would now most quickly be finished by that Roman virtue which, with the aid of Mars and Bellona, so quickly finished such great wars.

Let those, then, who have read recall how long-lasting were the wars, with how various outcomes, with how mournful disasters, waged by the ancient Romans—just as the world is wont to be tossed, like a most stormy sea, by the varied tempest of such evils; and let them sometimes confess what they are unwilling to, and not slay themselves with mad tongues against God and deceive the inexperienced.

**23.** Yet what God did marvelously and mercifully in our own memory, in most recent time, they do not recount with thanksgiving, but, as much as in them lies, they try, if it can be done, to bury in the oblivion of all men; which, if it is passed over in silence by us, we shall likewise be ungrateful. When Radagaisus, king of the Goths, already established in the neighborhood of the City with a huge and savage host, was threatening the necks of the Romans, in one day he was conquered with such speed that, without even one of the Romans—I do not say slain, but even wounded—his army of much more than a hundred thousand was laid low, and he himself, soon captured, was killed with the punishment due.

For if that man, so impious, with such great and so impious forces, had entered Rome, whom would he have spared? To what places of the martyrs would he have shown honor? In what person would he have feared God? Whose blood would he not have wished shed, whose chastity untouched? But what voices would these men have for their gods, with how great insult would they boast, that he had therefore conquered, had therefore been able to do such great things, because by daily sacrifices he appeased and invited the gods, which the Christian religion did not allow the Romans to do?

For when he was now approaching these places, where by the nod of the supreme majesty he was crushed, while his fame everywhere grew frequent, it was said to us at Carthage that the pagans believed, spread, and boasted this: that he, protected and aided by friendly gods, to whom he was reported to sacrifice daily, could in no way be conquered by those who did not make such sacrifices to the Roman gods, nor permit them to be made by anyone. And the wretches do not give thanks to so great a mercy of God, who, when he had determined to chastise by a barbarian incursion the morals of men deserving to suffer graver things, tempered his indignation with such great gentleness,

that he first made that man marvelously conquered, lest glory be given, for overthrowing the minds of the weak, to the demons whom it was agreed he supplicated; and then Rome was taken by these barbarians who, contrary to all the custom of wars waged before, protected, out of reverence for the Christian religion, those who fled to holy places, and so opposed, in the Christian name, the very demons and the rites of impious sacrifices on which that man had presumed, that they seemed to wage a war far more atrocious with them than with men;

thus the true Lord and Governor of affairs both scourged the Romans with mercy and showed, by the supplicators of demons being so incredibly conquered, that those sacrifices are not even necessary to the safety of present things—so that, by those who do not contend obstinately but attend prudently, the true religion may not be deserted on account of present necessities, and may rather be held by a most faithful expectation of eternal life.

## What is to be sought from the best prince.

**24.** For we Christians do not call certain emperors happy for this reason, that they reigned longer, or, reigning, left their sons by a peaceful death, or tamed the enemies of the republic, or could both guard against and crush hostile citizens rising up against them. These and other gifts or solaces of this toilsome life even certain worshippers of demons deserved to receive, who do not pertain to the kingdom of God, to which these pertain; and this was done by his very mercy, lest those who believed in him should desire these things from him as the highest goods.

But we call them happy if they rule justly; if, amid the tongues of those who honor them on high and the obsequies of those who salute them too humbly, they are not exalted, and remember that they are men; if they make their power a handmaid to his majesty, for spreading the worship of God as widely as possible; if they fear, love, and worship God; if they love more that kingdom where they do not fear to have consorts; if they avenge more slowly, forgive easily; if they exert that same vengeance for the necessity of ruling and guarding the republic, not to satisfy the hatreds of enmities; if they grant that same pardon not for the impunity of iniquity, but in hope of correction; if what they are for the most part harshly compelled to decree they compensate with the gentleness of mercy and the bounty of benefits; if their luxury is the more chastened the more it could be free; if they prefer to rule over depraved desires rather than over any nations whatever, and if they do all these things not for the ardor of empty glory, but for the love of eternal felicity; if for their sins they do not neglect to offer to their true God the sacrifice of humility and compassion and prayer.

Such Christian emperors we say are happy—for the present in hope, afterward in the very reality—when that which we await shall have come.

**25.** For the good God, lest men who believed that he was to be worshipped for the sake of eternal life should suppose that no one could attain these high places and earthly kingdoms unless he supplicated the demons, because these spirits availed much in such matters, filled the emperor Constantine—who did not supplicate the demons but worshipped the true God himself—with such great earthly gifts as no one would dare to wish for; to whom he even granted to found a city, an ally of the Roman empire, as it were a daughter of Rome herself, but without any temple or image of demons. He reigned long; alone, as Augustus, he held and defended the whole Roman world; in administering and waging wars he was most victorious; in crushing tyrants he was prospered in all things; he died, full of years, of sickness and old age; he left his sons reigning.

But again, lest any emperor should be a Christian for the purpose of deserving the felicity of Constantine—since everyone ought to be a Christian for the sake of eternal life—he took away Jovian much more quickly than Julian; he permitted Gratian to be slain by a tyrant's sword, far more gently indeed than Magnus Pompey, who worshipped the Roman gods, as it were. For Pompey could not be avenged by Cato, whom he had left, in a manner, the heir of the civil war; but Gratian, although pious souls do not require such solaces, was avenged by Theodosius, whom he had made a partner in the kingdom—when he had a little brother—more eager for faithful fellowship than for excessive power[^82].

## Theodosius was renowned for virtue and faith.

**26.** 1. Whence he too not only kept toward him, while alive, the faith he owed, but even after his death received as a ward, like a Christian, into his own part of the empire, Valentinian, his little brother, driven out by his slayer Maximus, and guarded him with a paternal affection—him whom, destitute of all resources, he could have taken away with no trouble, if he had burned more with the desire of reigning more widely than with the love of doing good; whence rather, his imperial dignity preserved, having received him, he consoled him with very humanity and grace. Then, when that success made Maximus terrible, he, in the straits of his cares, did not slip into sacrilegious and unlawful curiosities, but sent to John, established in the desert of Egypt—whom, by a growing fame, he had learned to be a servant of God endowed with the spirit of prophecy—and received from him a most certain message of victory. Soon, the destroyer of the tyrant Maximus, he restored the boy Valentinian, with most merciful veneration, to the parts of his empire whence he had been put to flight,

Then, when that success made Maximus formidable, Theodosius, amid the pressures of his anxiety, did not lapse into sacrilegious and unlawful divination. Instead, he sent to John, who lived in the Egyptian desert and whom he had learned, through his growing reputation, to be a servant of God endowed with the spirit of prophecy; from him he received a most certain announcement of victory. Soon, having destroyed the usurper Maximus, he restored the young Valentinian, with the most compassionate respect, to the portion of his empire from which he had been driven,

and when he, whether by treachery or by some other means or chance, had soon after been destroyed, Theodosius, having again received a prophetic response and being certain by faith, crushed another tyrant, Eugenius, who had been illegitimately substituted in that emperor's place—against whose most robust army he fought more by praying than by striking. Soldiers who were present reported to us that whatever they hurled was wrested from their hands, when from Theodosius's side a vehement wind went against the adversaries and not only most violently snatched whatever was thrown at them, but even turned their own weapons back into their bodies. Whence the poet Claudian too, although a stranger to the name of Christ, said in his praises: *O too much beloved of God, for whom the ether wars, and the conspiring winds come at the trumpet-call!*[^83]

And as victor, as he had believed and foretold, he took down the images of Jove, which had been, by I know not what rites, as it were consecrated against him and set up in the Alps; and their thunderbolts, because they were of gold, he cheerfully and kindly bestowed on his couriers, who were joking (which that joy permitted) and saying that they wished to be struck by those thunderbolts.

The sons of his enemies, whom the onslaught of war—not by his command—had carried off, fleeing to the Church even while not yet Christians, he wished to become Christians on this occasion, and loved them with Christian love, and did not deprive them of their goods, but increased them in honors.

He permitted private enmities to avail against no one after victory. The civil wars they were not willing, like Cinna and Marius and Sulla and others such, to finish even when they were finished, but he grieved more that they had arisen than he wished to harm anyone when they were ended.

Amid all these things, from the very beginning of his empire, he did not cease to come, with most just and most merciful laws, to the aid of the Church, laboring against the impious—the Church which the heretic Valens, favoring the Arians, had vehemently afflicted; of which Church he rejoiced to be a member more than to reign on earth. He commanded the images of the gentiles to be everywhere overthrown, understanding well enough that earthly gifts too are placed not in the power of the demons, but of the true God.

But what was more marvelous than his religious humility, when, in the case of the most grievous crime of the Thessalonians—for which, the bishops interceding, he had already promised indulgence—he was compelled, by the tumult of certain men who clung to him, to avenge it, and, restrained by ecclesiastical discipline, so did penance that the people, praying for him, wept more to see the imperial loftiness prostrate than they had feared it angry when they sinned?

These and any like good works, which it is long to recount, that man bore away from this temporal vapor of any human summit and loftiness; of which works the reward is eternal felicity, whose giver is God to the truly pious alone. But the other heights or supports of this life—like the world itself, light, the airs, lands, waters, fruits, and the very man's soul, body, senses, mind, life—he bestows on good and bad alike; among which is also any greatness of empire whatever, which he dispenses for the governance of the times.

**26.** 2. Accordingly I now see that an answer must be given even to those who, refuted and convicted by the most evident proofs—by which it is shown that toward those temporal things which alone fools desire to have, the numerousness of false gods avails nothing—try to assert that the gods are to be worshipped not for the sake of the present life's usefulness, but for the sake of that which will be after death. For to those who, for the sake of the friendships of this world, wish to worship vain things and complain that they are not permitted their childish notions, I think enough has been answered in these five books.

When I had published the three earlier of these and they had begun to be in the hands of many, I heard that some were preparing to write I know not what response against them. Then it was reported to me that they had already written, but were seeking a time when they could publish without danger. These I admonish not to wish for what is not expedient for them. For it is easy for anyone to seem to have answered, who has been unwilling to keep silent. Or what is more talkative than vanity? Which cannot do what truth can, because, if it wishes, it can even cry out more than truth. But let them consider everything diligently, and if perhaps, judging without partisan zeal, they perceive that such things are of a kind that can rather be stirred up than uprooted by a most shameless garrulity and an as it were satiric or mimic levity, let them check their trifles and choose rather to be corrected by the prudent than praised by the shameless.

For if they wait for a time not for the freedom of speaking the truth but for the license of speaking ill, far be it that there should befall them what Cicero says of a certain man who was called happy because of the license of sinning: *O wretched man, to whom it was permitted to sin*[^84]!

Whence whoever thinks himself happy by the license of speaking ill will be far happier if this is not permitted him at all, since, laying aside the emptiness of boastfulness, he can even at this time, as though out of a zeal for consulting, contradict whatever he wishes, and, as far as they can, hear from those whom he consults, in friendly disputation, honorably, gravely, freely, what he ought to hear.
