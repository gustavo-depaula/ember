# Book X: The angels are pleased that we sacrifice to the one God.

**1.** It is the settled opinion of all who can in any way use reason, that all men wish to be blessed[^1]. But as to who the blessed are, or how they come to be so, while the weakness of mortals inquires, many and great controversies have been stirred up, on which the philosophers have worn out their zeal and their leisure—controversies which it would be both lengthy and unnecessary to bring forward and discuss.

For if the one who reads this recalls what we did in the eighth book in choosing the philosophers with whom this question about the blessed life that is to come after death might be treated—whether we can attain to it by serving with religion and rites the one true God, who is also the maker of the gods, or many gods—he does not expect the same things to be repeated here as well, especially since, by rereading, if perchance he has forgotten, he can aid his memory[^2].

For we chose the Platonists, the most renowned of all philosophers by their merit, on this account, that they were able to discern that the immortal and rational, or intellectual, soul of man cannot be blessed except by participation in the light of that God by whom both it and the world were made; and thus they deny that anyone will attain that which all men seek, namely, the blessed life, who has not cleaved, with the purity of a chaste love, to that one supreme good which is the unchangeable God[^3].

But because they too, whether yielding to the vanity and error of the peoples, or, as the Apostle says, *becoming vain in their thoughts*[^4], thought, or wished it to be thought, that many gods are to be worshiped, so that some of them held that even to demons the divine honors of rites and sacrifices are to be offered—

to whom we have already in no small part replied: now we must see and discuss, as far as God grants, how the immortal and blessed beings, established in the heavenly seats among the dominions, principalities, and powers[^5]—whom these men call gods, and some of whom they name either good demons or, with us, angels[^6]—are to be believed to wish that religion and piety be observed by us; that is, to put it more plainly, whether it is their pleasure that we perform rites and sacrifices to themselves too, or only to their God, who is also ours, or that we consecrate by the rites of religion something of ours, or ourselves.

## The worship of God is called service.

**1.** For this is the worship owed to the divinity, or, if it must be said more exactly, to the deity; and to signify it by one word, since no sufficiently suitable Latin word occurs to me, I indicate by a Greek word, where necessary, what I mean to say. For our translators, wherever *latreia* is set in the holy Scriptures, have rendered it "service"[^7].

But the service that is owed to men, according to which the Apostle directs that slaves ought to be subject to their masters[^8], is usually called by another name in Greek; whereas *latreia*, according to the usage of those who composed the divine eloquence for us, is either always, or so frequently as to be almost always, used of that service which pertains to the worship of God.

Accordingly, if "worship" alone be spoken of, it does not seem to be owed to God alone. For we are said to "worship" (*colere*) men too, whom we honor by frequent remembrance or presence. Nor are only those things "worshiped" to which we subject ourselves in religious humility, but certain things also that are subject to us are said to be "tended."

For from this word both "farmers" (*agricolae*) and "settlers" (*coloni*) and "inhabitants" (*incolae*) are named, and they call the gods themselves "sky-dwellers" (*caelicolas*) for no other reason than that they "inhabit" (*colant*) the sky—not, of course, by venerating it, but by dwelling in it, as though certain settlers of the sky[^9];

not as those are called "settlers" who, for the sake of agriculture, owe a condition to their native soil under the dominion of the owners, but as a certain great author of Latin eloquence says: *there was an ancient city; Tyrian settlers held it*[^10].

For he called them "settlers" from inhabiting, not from agriculture. Hence too cities founded from greater cities, like swarms of peoples, are called colonies. And by this it is altogether most true that "worship," in a certain proper sense of this word, is owed to none but God; but because the worship of other things too is spoken of, the worship owed to God cannot be signified in Latin by one word.

For even "religion" (*religio*) itself, although it seems to signify more distinctly not just any worship but the worship of God (whence by this name our translators rendered that which is called in Greek *threskeia*[^11])—yet because, by the Latin custom of speaking, not of the unlearned but even of the most learned, "religion" is said to be shown both to human relationships and ties of kinship and to all sorts of bonds[^12], the ambiguity is not avoided by that word when the question turns on the worship of the deity, so that we might confidently be able to say that "religion" is nothing but the worship of God, since this word seems to be unnaturally taken away from signifying the regard due to human kinship.

"Piety" (*pietas*) too is usually understood as properly the worship of God, which the Greeks call *eusebeia*[^13]. Yet it is also said to be exercised dutifully toward parents. And by the custom of the common people this name is frequently used also of works of mercy[^14]; which I think has come about because God especially commands these to be done, and testifies that they please him either in place of, or above, sacrifices. From this custom of speaking it has come about that even God himself is called "pious" (*pius*)[^15];

whom indeed the Greeks, by no usage of their own speech, call *eusebes*, although their common people too employ *eusebeia* for "mercy"[^16]. Hence in certain places of the Scriptures[^17], that a more certain distinction might appear, they preferred to say not *eusebeia*, which sounds as if compounded from "good worship," but *theosebeia*, which sounds as if compounded from "the worship of God." But neither of these can we express in one word.

That, then, which is called in Greek *latreia* and is rendered in Latin "service"—but the service by which we worship God[^18];

or that which is called in Greek *threskeia*, but in Latin "religion"—the religion that is ours toward God; or that which they call *theosebeia*, but which we cannot express in one word and can call the worship of God[^19]: this we say is owed to God alone, who is the true God and makes his worshipers gods[^20].

Whoever, then, are immortal and blessed in the heavenly dwellings, if they do not love us and do not wish us to be blessed, are surely not to be worshiped.

But if they love us and wish us to be blessed, surely they wish it from that source from which they themselves are blessed; or are they blessed from one source and we from another?

## Plotinus and John teach that we are partakers of God.

**2.** But we have no conflict with these more excellent philosophers on this question. For they saw, and in their writings recorded most copiously in many ways, that those beings are made blessed from the same source as we, by a certain intelligible light set before them, which is God for them and is other than they,

by whom they are illumined, so that they shine and, by participation in him, subsist as perfect and blessed[^21].

Plotinus often and at length asserts, in expounding the meaning of Plato, that not even that which they believe to be the soul of the universe is blessed from any other source than ours, and that this is a light which the soul itself is not, but by which it was created and by whose intelligible illumination it shines intelligibly[^22].

He gives, too, a likeness for those incorporeal things drawn from these conspicuous and ample heavenly bodies, as though that light were the sun and the soul were the moon[^23]. For they think the moon is illumined by the sun set before it[^24].

That great Platonist, then, says that the rational soul—or rather, if it should be called intellectual, from which kind he understands also the souls of the immortal and blessed beings to be, whom he does not doubt to dwell in the heavenly seats—has above itself no nature except God, who fashioned the world, by whom it too was made;

and that to those beings on high the blessed life and the light of the understanding of truth are furnished from no other source[^25] than that from which it is furnished to us as well,

agreeing with the Gospel, where it is read: *there was a man sent from God, whose name was John; he came for a testimony, to bear witness to the light, that all might believe through him. He was not the light, but came to bear witness to the light. That was the true light, which enlightens every man coming into this world*[^26]. By this distinction it is sufficiently shown that the rational or intellectual soul, such as was in John, cannot be a light to itself, but shines by participation in another, true light. This John himself confesses too, where, bearing witness to him, he says: *of his fullness we have all received*[^27].

**3.** Since this is so, if the Platonists, or whoever else held these views, knowing God, glorified him as God and gave thanks, and did not become vain in their thoughts[^28], nor partly become authors of the peoples' errors and partly not dare to resist them,

they would surely confess that both for those immortal and blessed beings and for us mortal and wretched ones, that we may be able to be immortal and blessed, the one God of gods is to be worshiped, who is both ours and theirs.

**3.** To him we owe the service which in Greek is called *latreia*, whether in any sacred rites or in ourselves. For of him we are the temple, all together and each one a temple[^29], since he deigns to dwell in the concord of all and in each one; not greater in all than in each, since he is neither extended by mass nor diminished by division.

When our heart is lifted up to him, it is his altar; we appease him through his Only-begotten as priest; we slay bloody victims to him when we contend even to blood for his truth; we burn the most fragrant incense to him[^30] when in his sight we blaze with devout and holy love;

to him we vow and render his gifts in us and our very selves; to him we dedicate and consecrate the memory of his benefits with festal solemnities and appointed days, lest by the rolling of time ungrateful forgetfulness should steal upon us; to him we sacrifice the victim of humility and praise on the altar of the heart, fervid with the fire of love[^31].

To see him, as he shall be able to be seen, and to cleave to him, we are purified from every stain of sins and evil desires and consecrated in his name. For he himself is the fountain of our blessedness, he is the end of all our striving. Choosing him, or rather re-choosing him (for we had lost him by neglecting)—re-choosing him, then, whence "religion" too is said to be named[^32]—we tend toward him by love, so that in reaching him we may rest, blessed because made perfect in that end.

For our good, about whose end there is great contention among the philosophers, is nothing other than to cleave to him, by whose incorporeal embrace alone, if it may be so said, the intellectual soul is filled and made fruitful with true virtues.

We are commanded to love this good with our whole heart, with our whole soul, and with our whole strength; to this good we ought both to be led by those who love us, and to lead those whom we love. Thus are fulfilled those two precepts on which the whole Law and the Prophets depend: *you shall love the Lord your God with your whole heart and with your whole soul and with your whole mind*, and: *you shall love your neighbor as yourself*[^33].

For that man might know how to love himself, an end was appointed for him to which he should refer all that he did, that he might be blessed; for one who loves himself wishes nothing other than to be blessed. And this end is to cleave to God[^34].

When, then, to one who already knows how to love himself, it is commanded to love his neighbor as himself, what else is commanded but that, as far as he can, he commend to him the love of God? This is the worship of God, this is true religion, this is right piety, this is the service owed to God alone.

Whatever immortal power, then, endowed with however great a virtue, if it loves us as itself, wishes us to be subject, that we may be blessed, to him to whom it too, being subject, is blessed.

If, then, it does not worship God, it is wretched, because it is deprived of God; but if it does worship God, it does not wish to be worshiped in God's place. For it rather supports that divine sentence and favors it with the powers of love, by which it is written: *he who sacrifices to gods shall be rooted out, save to the Lord alone*[^35].

**4.** For, to say nothing now of the other things that pertain to the homage of religion by which God is worshiped, there is certainly no man who would dare say that sacrifice is owed to anyone but God. Many things, indeed, have been borrowed from divine worship to be rendered as human honors, whether through excessive humility or through pestilent flattery;

yet in such a way that those to whom they were rendered were held to be men who are said to be worshiped and venerated, or, if much is added to them, even adored: but who has held that sacrifice should be offered except to him whom he either knew or supposed or feigned to be God?

How ancient, moreover, is the worship of God in sacrificing, those two brothers Cain and Abel sufficiently indicate, whose elder brother's sacrifice God rejected, but the younger's he regarded[^36].

**5.** But who would be so foolish as to think that the things offered in sacrifices are necessary for any uses of God? Although divine Scripture testifies this in many places, lest we be lengthy, it will suffice to recall that brief saying from the psalm: *I said to the Lord, you are my God, for you have no need of my goods*[^37].

Not only by cattle, then, or by any other corruptible and earthly thing, but not even by the very righteousness of man is God to be believed to be in need, and the whole of what rightly worships God profits man, not God. For no one would say that he had done a service to a fountain if he drank, or to the light if he saw.

Nor is the fact that other sacrifices in the victims of cattle were offered by the ancient fathers[^38]—which the people of God now read of, but do not perform—to be understood otherwise than that by those things were signified the things that take place within us, to this end, that we may cleave to God and serve our neighbor toward the same end.

A visible sacrifice, then, is the sacrament, that is, the sacred sign, of an invisible sacrifice. Hence that penitent in the Prophet, or the Prophet himself, seeking to have God favorable to his sins, says: *if you had wished sacrifice, I would surely have given it; you will not delight in holocausts. A sacrifice to God is a contrite spirit; a contrite and humbled heart God will not despise*[^39].

Let us observe how, where he said that God does not wish sacrifice, there he showed that God does wish sacrifice. He does not wish, then, the sacrifice of a slaughtered animal, and he does wish the sacrifice of a contrite heart.

By that, then, which he said God does not wish, is signified this, which he added that God does wish.

And so he said that God does not wish those sacrifices in the way in which they are believed by the foolish to be wished, namely, for the sake of his own pleasure. For if he did not wish the sacrifices he does wish (of which this is one: a contrite and humbled heart in the grief of repenting) to be signified by those sacrifices which he was supposed to desire as pleasing to himself, he would surely not have commanded that these be offered in the old Law[^40].

And therefore they had to be changed at a fitting and now fixed time, lest they be believed to be desirable to God himself, or at any rate acceptable in us, and not rather the things that were signified by them.

Hence in another place of another psalm he says: *if I should be hungry, I would not tell you; for mine is the world and its fullness. Shall I eat the flesh of bulls or drink the blood of goats?*[^41] As if he said: "surely, if they were necessary to me, I would not ask of you what I have in my power." Then, adding what those things signify, he says: *offer to God the sacrifice of praise and pay your vows to the Most High, and call upon me in the day of tribulation, and I will deliver you, and you shall glorify me*[^42].

Likewise in another Prophet he says: *with what shall I lay hold of the Lord, take hold of my God the most high? Shall I lay hold of him with holocausts, with year-old calves? Will the Lord accept thousands of rams, or tens of thousands of fat goats? Shall I give my firstborn for my impiety, the fruit of my womb for the sin of my soul? Has it been told you, O man, what is good? Or what does the Lord seek from you but to do judgment and to love mercy and to be ready to walk with the Lord your God?*[^43]

And in this Prophet's words both are distinguished and sufficiently declared: that God does not require for their own sake those sacrifices by which are signified these sacrifices which God does require. In the Epistle that is inscribed to the Hebrews it says: *do not forget to do good and to share; for by such sacrifices God is pleased*[^44].

And by this, where it is written, *I desire mercy rather than sacrifice*[^45], nothing else ought to be understood than that one sacrifice is preferred to another sacrifice; for that which is called sacrifice by all is a sign of the true sacrifice. But mercy is the true sacrifice; whence it was said, as I recalled a little before, *for by such sacrifices God is pleased*. Whatever, therefore, in the ministry of the tabernacle or the Temple is read to have been divinely commanded concerning sacrifices in many ways, is referred, by way of signifying, to the love of God and neighbor. For *on these two precepts*, as it is written, *the whole law and the Prophets depend*[^46].

**6.** Accordingly, a true sacrifice is every work by which it is brought about that we cleave to God in holy fellowship, referred, that is, to that end of the good by which we can be truly blessed. Hence even mercy itself, by which a man is aided, if it is not done for God's sake, is not a sacrifice. For although it is done or offered by man, yet a sacrifice is a divine thing, so that the ancient Latins called it by this very word too[^47]. Hence man himself, consecrated in the name of God and vowed to God, insofar as he dies to the world that he may live to God, is a sacrifice. For this too pertains to the mercy that each one does to himself. Therefore it is written: *have mercy on your soul by pleasing God*[^48].

Our body too, when we chastise it with temperance, if we do this for God's sake, as we ought, so as not to present our members as weapons of iniquity to sin, but as weapons of righteousness to God[^49], is a sacrifice. Exhorting to which, the Apostle says: *I beseech you, therefore, brethren, by the mercy of God, to present your bodies a living sacrifice, holy, pleasing to God, your reasonable service*[^50].

If, then, the body, which the soul uses as an inferior, like a servant or an instrument, is a sacrifice when its good and right use is referred to God, how much more is the soul itself a sacrifice when it refers itself to God, so that, kindled by the fire of his love, it loses the form of worldly desire and, subject to him as to an unchangeable form, is reformed, thereby becoming pleasing to him by what it has received from his beauty! This the same Apostle adds in what follows, saying: *and do not be conformed to this world, but be reformed in the newness of your mind, that you may prove what is the will of God, what is good and well-pleasing and perfect*[^51].

Since, then, true sacrifices are works of mercy, whether toward ourselves or toward our neighbors, which are referred to God; and works of mercy are done for no other reason than that we may be freed from misery and thereby be blessed

(which does not come about except by that good of which it was said: *but for me it is good to cleave to God*[^52]):

it surely follows that the whole redeemed city itself, that is, the congregation and fellowship of the saints, is offered to God as a universal sacrifice through the great priest, who also offered himself in his passion for us, that we might be the body of so great a head, according to the form of a servant[^53].

For this form he offered, in this form he was offered, because according to it he is mediator, in it priest, in it sacrifice.

And so, when the Apostle had exhorted us to present our bodies a living sacrifice, holy, pleasing to God, our reasonable service, and not to be conformed to this world, but to be reformed in the newness of our mind, that we may prove what is the will of God, what is good and well-pleasing and perfect—which whole sacrifice we ourselves are—he says: *for I say, through the grace of God which is given to me, to all who are among you, not to be more wise than it is fitting to be wise, but to be wise unto sobriety; according as God has imparted to each one the measure of faith. For as in one body we have many members, but all the members have not the same functions: so we, being many, are one body in Christ; and individually members one of another, having different gifts according to the grace that is given to us*[^54].

This is the sacrifice of Christians: *being many, one body in Christ*.

This too the Church celebrates in the sacrament of the altar, well known to the faithful, where it is shown to her that in the very thing she offers, she herself is offered.

**7.** Rightly, then, those immortal and blessed beings established in the heavenly seats, who rejoice together in the participation of their Creator—firm in his eternity, certain in his truth, holy by his gift—

since they mercifully love us, mortal and wretched as we are, that we may be immortal and blessed, do not wish us to sacrifice to them, but to him whose sacrifice they too, along with us, know themselves to be.

For together with them we are one city of God, to which it is said in the psalm: *glorious things are said of you, O city of God*[^55]; whose one part sojourns among us, the other gives aid among them.

For from that heavenly city, where the will of God is the intelligible and unchangeable law, from that heavenly court, in a manner of speaking (for there care is taken for us), that holy Scripture descended to us, ministered through angels, where it is read: *he who sacrifices to gods shall be rooted out, save to the Lord alone*[^56]. To this Scripture, to this law, to such precepts, miracles so great have borne witness that it sufficiently appears to whom the immortal and blessed beings, who wish us to be what they are, would have us sacrifice.

## Miracles were wrought attesting the promises of God.

**8.** For if I should recall things too ancient, I shall seem to go back further than is enough—what miracles were wrought attesting the promises of God, by which, thousands of years ago, he foretold to Abraham that in his seed all nations would have blessing[^57].

For who would not marvel that this same Abraham's barren wife bore him a son at that age of old age when even a fertile woman could no longer bear[^58], and that in the sacrifice of this same Abraham a flame, made from heaven, ran between the divided victims[^59],

and that to this same Abraham the heavenly burning of Sodom was foretold by the angels, whom he had received into his hospitality as men like himself, and through whom he had held God's promises concerning the offspring to come[^60],

and that, with the burning now imminent, the wondrous deliverance from Sodom, through those same angels, of Lot, the son of his brother—whose wife, looking back on the way and suddenly turned into salt[^61], admonished us by a great sacrament that no one in the way of his deliverance ought to long for the things left behind?

But what things, and how great, are those that were wondrously done through Moses for the people of God, to be rescued in Egypt from the yoke of bondage, where the magicians of Pharaoh—that is, of the king of Egypt, who oppressed that people with his dominion—were permitted to do certain marvels for this end, that they might be the more marvelously vanquished!

For they acted by sorceries and magical incantations, to which the evil angels, that is, the demons, are given over; but Moses, with as much more power as he had more righteousness, easily overcame them in the name of God, who made heaven and earth, with angels serving him[^62].

Finally, at the third plague, when the magicians failed, the ten plagues were completed through Moses by a great arrangement of mysteries, by which the hard hearts of Pharaoh and the Egyptians yielded to letting the people of God go. And soon he repented, and when they tried to pursue the departing Hebrews, while these passed through dry land with the sea divided, the Egyptians were covered and overwhelmed by the wave returning upon itself from this side and that[^63].

What shall I say of those miracles which, while the same people was being led through the desert, abounded by a stupendous divine power: that waters which could not be drunk, when wood was cast into them as God had commanded, lost their bitterness and satisfied the thirsty[^64];

that manna came from heaven for the hungry, and, when a fixed measure was set for those gathering it, whatever anyone had gathered beyond that bred worms and rotted, but on the day before the sabbath a double portion was gathered, because on the sabbath it was not lawful to gather, and was spoiled by no rottenness[^65];

that, when they desired to feed on flesh, which seemed that no amount could suffice for so great a people, the camp was filled with birds, and the ardor of their craving was extinguished by a loathing of satiety[^66];

that enemies who confronted them, forbidding their passage and giving battle, were laid low, with not one of the Hebrews falling, while Moses prayed and his hands were stretched out in the figure of a cross[^67];

that the seditious among the people of God, who divided themselves from the divinely ordained fellowship, were, as a visible example of an invisible punishment, swallowed up alive when the earth gaped open[^68]; that a rock struck with a rod poured out abundant streams for so great a multitude[^69];

that the deadly bites of serpents, the most just punishment of sins, were healed by a bronze serpent lifted up on wood and looked upon, so that both the afflicted people might be aided, and death, destroyed by death, might be signified, as it were, by the likeness of crucified death[^70]?

And when, afterward, the erring people had begun to worship this serpent—kept for the memory of the deed—as though it were an idol, King Hezekiah, serving God with a religious authority, broke it to pieces, with great praise for his piety[^71].

## . . . to which theurgy and magic yield.

**9.** These and many other things of this kind, all of which it would be too long to recall, were done to commend the worship of the one true God and to forbid the worship of the many false ones.

And they were done by simple faith and the confidence of piety, not by incantations and spells composed by the art of a wicked curiosity, which they call either magic or, by a more detestable name, sorcery, or by a more honorable one, theurgy[^72],

as though they try to distinguish these, and to make some of those given to unlawful arts seem damnable,

whom the common people call sorcerers (for these, they say, belong to sorcery),

but to make others seem praiseworthy, to whom they assign theurgy; when both are bound by the deceitful rites of demons under the names of angels.

**9.** For Porphyry too promises a certain purification, as it were, of the soul through theurgy, yet hesitatingly and in a discussion that is in a manner bashful; but he denies that this art bestows on anyone a return to God;

so that you may see him wavering, with alternating judgments, between the vice of sacrilegious curiosity and the profession of philosophy. For at one moment he warns that this art is to be guarded against as deceitful, and dangerous in its very practice, and prohibited by the laws;

but at another, as though yielding to its praisers, he says it is useful for purifying a part of the soul—not, indeed, the intellectual part, by which the truth of intelligible things is perceived, which have no likenesses of bodies, but the spiritual part, by which the images of corporeal things are grasped[^73].

For he says that this part is made, by certain theurgic consecrations which they call *teletai*, fit and suited to the reception of spirits and angels and to the seeing of gods. Yet he confesses that from these theurgic *teletai* no purification accrues to the intellectual soul such as would make it fit to see its God and to perceive the things that truly are.

From which it can be understood of what sort of gods, or of what sort of vision, he says is wrought by theurgic consecrations, in which the things that truly are are not seen.

Finally, he says that the rational soul—or, as he prefers to say, the intellectual—can escape into its own region, even if that which is spiritual in it has been purified by no theurgic art; but that the spiritual part is purified by the theurgist only so far that it does not thereby attain to immortality and eternity.

Although, therefore, he distinguishes angels from demons, holding the aerial regions to be the demons', the ethereal or empyrean the angels', and although he advises that one should make use of the friendship of some demon, by whose conveyance a man may, after death, be lifted even a little above the earth, while he maintains that there is another way to the heavenly fellowship of the angels:

he nonetheless testifies, by a confession in a manner explicit, that the fellowship of demons is to be guarded against, when he says that the soul, after death, paying its penalties, shudders at the worship of the demons by whom it was beset;

and he could not deny that theurgy itself, which he commends as a reconciler of angels and gods, deals with such powers as either themselves envy the purification of the soul, or serve the arts of the envious—bringing forward, on this matter, the complaint of some Chaldaean or other:

*A good man in Chaldaea complains*, he says, *that his great efforts to purify a soul were frustrated, since a man powerful in the same arts, touched by envy, had bound, with sacred prayers, the powers he had adjured, so that they would not grant what was asked. Therefore that man bound them*, he says, *and this one did not loose them*.

By which token he said it appears that theurgy is a discipline for accomplishing good as much as evil, both among gods and among men; that the gods too suffer and are led into those disturbances and emotions which Apuleius attributed in common to demons and men[^74]; yet he separates the gods from these by the height of their ethereal seat, and asserts that this is Plato's opinion in that distinction[^75].

**10.** Behold now another Platonist, whom they reckon more learned, Porphyry, says that even the gods themselves are bound by emotions and disturbances through some theurgic discipline or other,

since they could be adjured and held by sacred prayers not to bestow purification on a soul, and were so terrified by him who commanded the evil that they could not, by another who asked the good, be loosed from that fear through the same theurgic art and freed to grant the benefit.

Who would not see that all these things are the inventions of deceitful demons, except one who is their most wretched slave and a stranger to the grace of the true deliverer?

For if these things were done among good gods, surely the beneficent purifier of the soul would there prevail more than the malevolent hinderer.

Or if to just gods the man, on whose behalf the action was taken, seemed unworthy of purification, surely they ought not to have been terrified by the envious one nor,

as he himself says, hindered by fear of a more powerful divinity, but ought to have denied it by a free judgment.

It is strange, moreover, that that kindly Chaldaean, who desired to purify a soul by theurgic rites, did not find some higher god who would either terrify more and compel the terrified gods to do good, or restrain the one who terrified them, so that they might freely do good;

if, that is, the good theurgist lacked the rites by which he might first purify those very gods, whom he was invoking as purifiers of the soul, from that plague of fear.

For what reason is there why a more powerful god can be employed by whom they are terrified, but cannot be employed by whom they are purified?

Or is a god found who hearkens to the envious and strikes fear into the gods that they may not do good, and yet no God is found who hearkens to the well-disposed and removes fear from the gods that they may do good?

O illustrious theurgy! O purification of the soul to be proclaimed, where unclean envy commands more than pure beneficence obtains! Nay rather, the deceit of the malignant spirits is to be guarded against and detested, and the saving doctrine is to be heard.

For the fact that those who work these filthy purifications by sacrilegious rites see certain wondrously beautiful images, as this man relates, whether of angels or of gods, as though with a purified spirit (if indeed they see any such thing), is what the Apostle speaks of:

*for Satan transforms himself into an angel of light*[^76]. For those apparitions are his, who, desiring to ensnare the wretched souls of many in the deceitful rites of false gods, and to turn them away from the true worship of the true God, by which alone they are cleansed and healed,

as was said of Proteus, *turns himself into all shapes*[^77], pursuing in hostility, aiding in deceit, harmful in both.

## Porphyry, to Anebo, certain mockeries of magic.

**11.** This Porphyry was wiser when he wrote to Anebo the Egyptian, where, like one consulting and inquiring, he both betrays the sacrilegious arts and overturns them. And there, indeed, he rejects all the demons, whom he says, through their imprudence, draw to themselves a moist vapor

and are therefore not in the ether, but in the air beneath the moon and in the very globe of the moon; yet he does not dare attribute to all the demons all the deceits and malice and absurdities by which he is rightly moved. For he calls certain ones benign demons, after the manner of others, although he confesses that all in general are imprudent.

But he wonders that the gods are not only enticed by victims but are even compelled and forced to do what men wish;

and how, if the gods are distinguished from the demons by body and incorporeality, it is to be supposed that the sun and moon and the other visible things in heaven are gods, which he does not doubt to be bodies; and if they are gods,

how some are said to be beneficent and others maleficent; and how, being corporeal, they are joined to incorporeal beings.

He asks too, as though in doubt, whether in those who divine and do certain marvels there are emotions of the soul, or whether certain spirits come from outside, through whom they have power to do these things;

and he rather conjectures that they come from outside, for the reason that, with stones and herbs applied, they both bind certain persons, and open closed doors, or work some such thing wondrously.

Hence he says that others suppose there is a certain kind of being whose property it is to hearken,

deceitful by nature, of every form, of many modes, feigning gods and demons and the souls of the dead, and that this is what brings about all these things that seem to be good or evil;

but that concerning the things that are truly good it gives no help,

nay rather, does not even know these things, but both ill-reconciles and falsely accuses, and sometimes hinders, the diligent followers of virtue,

and is full of rashness and arrogance, rejoices in the reeks of sacrifice, is caught by flatteries, and the rest, which, concerning this kind of deceitful and malignant spirits, who come into the soul from outside and delude the human senses, whether asleep or awake, he does not confirm as things of which he himself is persuaded, but so faintly suspects or doubts that he asserts these to be the opinions of others[^78].

For it was difficult for so great a philosopher either to know or confidently to convict the whole diabolical fellowship, which any little Christian old woman neither hesitates to affirm exists nor scruples most freely to detest. Unless perhaps this man is reluctant to offend both Anebo himself, to whom he writes, as a most illustrious priest of such rites, and others, the admirers of such works as though they were divine and pertaining to the worship of the gods.

**11.** Yet he goes on and, as though inquiring, recalls also those things which, soberly considered, cannot be attributed except to malignant and deceitful powers. For he asks why, when the better ones are invoked, the worse ones are commanded, as it were, to carry out the unjust commands of a man;

why they do not hearken to one who has been engaged in a venereal act when he calls upon them, while they themselves do not hesitate to lead any persons whatever to all manner of incestuous couplings; why they declare that their priests must abstain from animal flesh, lest, of course, they be polluted by corporeal vapors, while they themselves are enticed both by other vapors and by the reeks of sacrificial victims,

and why, although the inspector is forbidden the touch of a corpse, those rites are for the most part celebrated with corpses; what it means that a man, liable to whatever vice, directs his threats not at a demon or some soul of the dead, but at the sun and moon themselves or at any of the heavenly bodies, and terrifies them falsely, to extort the truth from them.

For he threatens to dash the heavens together and other like things impossible for a man, so that those gods, like most foolish boys terrified by false and ridiculous threats, may do what is commanded.

He says too that a certain Chaeremon, skilled in such rites, or rather sacrileges, wrote that the things celebrated by report among the Egyptians, whether of Isis or of Osiris her husband, have the greatest power of compelling the gods to do what is commanded, when he who compels by spells threatens to betray or overthrow these things,

where he even terribly says that he will scatter the limbs of Osiris, if they neglect to do what is bidden[^79].

That a man should threaten the gods with these and such vain and insane things—and not just any gods, but the very heavenly ones, shining with starry light—and not without effect, but compelling them by a violent power and leading them by these terrors to do what he wishes, Porphyry rightly wonders at;

nay rather, under the guise of one wondering and seeking the causes of such things, he gives us to understand that those spirits do these things whose kind he described above under the opinion of others—deceitful not, as he put it, by nature, but by vice—who feign gods and the souls of the dead, but who, as demons, do not feign, as he himself says, but plainly are demons.

And as to what seems to him—that by herbs and stones and animals and certain fixed sounds and voices and figurations and figments, and even by certain movements of the stars observed in the revolution of heaven, powers fit for carrying out various effects are fashioned on earth by men—all this pertains to those same demons, the mockers of the souls subject to them, who provide themselves with pleasurable sport out of the errors of men.

Either, then, Porphyry, truly doubting and inquiring, nonetheless recalls these things by which they may be convicted and refuted, and shown to pertain not to those powers which favor us in laying hold of the blessed life, but to the deceiving demons; or—that we may suspect better of the philosopher—he wished in this way not to offend, by the proud authority, as it were, of a teacher, an Egyptian man given to such errors and supposing himself to know some great things, nor to disturb him by the altercation of an open adversary, but, by the humility, as it were, of one inquiring and desiring to learn, to turn him to ponder these things and to show how contemptible, or even how much to be avoided, they are.

Finally, near the end of the letter, he asks to be taught by him what is the way to blessedness according to Egyptian wisdom[^80]. As for those whose intercourse with the gods was for this purpose, that, in order to find a runaway slave or to acquire an estate, or for the sake of marriage or trade or some such thing, they should disquiet the divine mind, he says they seem to have cultivated wisdom in vain[^81]; and that those very divinities too, with whom they held intercourse, even if they foretold true things about other matters, yet, since concerning blessedness they gave no sure or sufficiently suitable counsel, were neither gods nor benign demons, but either he who is called the deceitful one, or wholly a human fabrication.

## The unchangeable God makes visible signs in the natural world.

**12.** But since such great things, and of such a kind, are wrought by these arts that they exceed every measure of human capacity, what remains except that those things which seem wondrously to be foretold or done as though divinely, and yet are not referred to the worship of the one God—to whom to cleave simply is, as even the Platonists admit and in many ways testify, the only beatifying good—should prudently be understood to be the mockeries of malignant demons and seductive hindrances, to be guarded against by true piety?

But, on the other hand, whatever miracles, whether through angels or in whatever way, are wrought so divinely as to commend the worship and religion of the one God, in whom alone is the blessed life, are to be believed to be truly wrought by, or through, those who love us according to truth and piety, with God himself working in them. For neither are those to be heard who deny that the invisible God works visible miracles, since even according to them he himself made the world, which surely they cannot deny to be visible.

Whatever marvel, then, is done in this world, is surely less than the whole of this world, that is, heaven and earth and all that is in them, which God certainly made. But just as he who made it, so the manner in which he made it is hidden and incomprehensible to man. And so, although the miracles of visible natures have grown cheap by the constancy of seeing them, yet, when we behold them wisely, they are greater than the most unusual and rarest things. For man himself is a greater miracle than any miracle done through man.

Wherefore God, who made the visible heaven and earth, does not disdain to work visible miracles in heaven or on earth, by which he may rouse to the worship of himself, the invisible one, a soul still given over to visible things; but where and when he does so, an unchangeable counsel rests with him, in whose disposition all things that are to come have already been made among times.

For, moving temporal things, he is not moved temporally, nor does he know the things to be done otherwise than the things done,

nor does he hearken to those who call upon him otherwise than he sees those who will call upon him.

For even when his angels hearken, he hearkens in them, as in his true temple, not made with hands, as in his holy men; and his commands are carried out in time, beheld in his eternal law[^82].

**13.** Nor ought it to disturb us that, although he is invisible, he is recorded to have often appeared visibly to the fathers[^83]. For just as the sound by which a thought set in the silence of the understanding is heard is not the same as the thought itself, so too the form in which God, set in an invisible nature, was seen was not what he himself is.

Yet he himself was seen in that same corporeal form, just as that thought itself is heard in the sound of the voice; nor were the fathers ignorant that they were seeing the invisible God in a corporeal form which he himself was not. For Moses both spoke with him as he spoke, and yet said to him: *if I have found grace before you, show me yourself, that I may knowingly see you*[^84].

Since, then, it was fitting that the law of God should be given terribly in the edicts of angels[^85], not to one man or to a few wise men, but to a whole nation and an immense people: in the presence of that same people great things were done on the mountain, where the law was given through one man[^86], while the multitude beheld the fearful and trembling things that were happening.

For the people of Israel did not believe Moses as the Lacedaemonians believed their Lycurgus, that he had received from Jupiter or Apollo the laws which he established[^87]. For when the law was being given to the people, by which the one God was bidden to be worshiped, in the sight of that same people, as far as divine providence judged sufficient, it appeared, by wondrous signs and movements of things, that the creation was serving the Creator in the giving of that same law.

## . . . that he may teach us his providence . . .

**14.** But just as the right education of a single man, so that of the human race, insofar as it pertains to the people of God, advanced by certain joints of times, as it were of ages, by stages, that it might rise from temporal things to the grasping of eternal, and from visible to invisible[^88];

yet in such a way that even at that time, when visible rewards were divinely promised, the one God was nonetheless commended as the one to be worshiped, lest the human mind, even for those very earthly benefits of this transitory life, should make itself subject to anyone but the true Creator and Lord of the soul. For whoever denies that all the things which either angels or men can bestow on men are in the power of the one Almighty is mad.

Concerning providence, certainly, the Platonist Plotinus disputes, and proves that it reaches, from the most high God, whose is an intelligible and ineffable beauty, all the way to these earthly and lowest things, by the beauty of little flowers and leaves;

all of which, as things abject, as it were, and most swiftly perishing, he affirms cannot have the most fitting numbers of their forms unless they are formed from that source where the intelligible and unchangeable form, having all things together, abides[^89].

This the Lord Jesus showed where he says: *consider the lilies of the field; they neither toil nor spin. But I say to you, that not even Solomon in all his glory was arrayed like one of these. And if God so clothes the grass of the field, which today is and tomorrow is cast into the oven, how much more you, O you of little faith?*[^90]

Most excellently, then, the human soul, still weak with earthly desires, grows accustomed to expect even those very goods which it longs for in time—the lowest and earthly things, necessary to this transitory life and to be held cheap in comparison with the everlasting benefits of that other life—from none but the one God, so that it may not depart from his worship even in desiring these things, but may attain to him by holding them cheap and by turning away from them.

**15.** And so it pleased divine providence to order the course of times in such a way that, as I have said and as is read in the *Acts of the Apostles*[^91], the law concerning the worship of the one true God should be given in the edicts of angels, in which both the person of God himself—not indeed through his own substance, which always remains invisible to corruptible eyes, but by certain signs, through the creation subject to the Creator—should appear visibly,

and should speak, syllable by syllable, through the transitory little pauses of time, in the words of human language, he who in his own nature neither begins nor ceases to speak—not corporeally but spiritually, not sensibly but intelligibly, not temporally but, so to speak, eternally[^92]; which, in his presence, his ministers and messengers hear more purely, not with the ear of the body but of the mind, who enjoy his unchangeable truth, immortally blessed; and what they hear, in ineffable ways, must be done and carried down even into these visible and sensible things, they accomplish without hesitation and without difficulty.

But this law was given by a distribution of times, which should first have, as has been said, earthly promises, by which, nonetheless, eternal things were signified, which many celebrated with visible sacraments but few understood[^93]. Yet the worship of the one God is there enjoined, with a most open attestation of both words and all things, not of one God out of a crowd, but of him who made heaven and earth and every soul and every spirit which is not what he himself is[^94]. For he made them, these were made, and that they may exist and be well, they need him by whom they were made.

## The angels are ministers of salvation even by miracles.

**16.** Which angels, then, do we judge are to be believed concerning the blessed and everlasting life? Those who wish themselves to be worshiped with the rites of religion, demanding that rites and sacrifices be offered to them by mortals, or those who say that all this worship is owed to the one God, the creator of all, and direct that it be rendered to him with true piety, by whose contemplation they too are blessed and promise that we shall be?

For that vision of God is a vision of so great a beauty and so most worthy of love that, without it, Plotinus does not hesitate to call one most unhappy, however endowed and abounding with any other goods[^95].

Since, then, certain angels rouse us by wondrous signs to worship this one God, but certain others to worship themselves with service (*latria*), and this in such a way that the former forbid these to be worshiped, while the latter do not dare to forbid him to be worshiped, which are rather to be believed?

Let the Platonists answer, let any philosophers whatever answer, let the theurgists answer—or rather the *periurgists* (the busybodies of perjury), for all those arts are worthier of this name; finally, let men answer, if any sense of their own nature, that they were created rational, lives in any part within them;

let them answer, I say, whether sacrifice is to be offered to those gods or angels who bid that sacrifice be offered to themselves, or to that one to whom those bid sacrifice be offered who forbid it both to themselves and to those others?

If neither these nor those worked any miracles, but only gave commands—some, that sacrifice be offered to themselves, others forbidding this but commanding it for the one God alone—piety itself ought to be enough to discern which of these descended from the arrogance of pride and which from true religion.

I will say even more: if only those who demand sacrifices for themselves moved human souls by wondrous deeds, while those who forbid this and bid sacrifice be offered to the one God alone never deigned to work those visible miracles, surely their authority would have to be preferred, not by the sense of the body, but by the reason of the mind.

But since God has acted, in commending the eloquence of his truth, so that, through those immortal messengers who proclaim not their own arrogance but his majesty, he has worked greater, surer, and clearer miracles, lest those who demand sacrifices for themselves should the more easily persuade the weak among the devout of a false religion by showing certain stupendous things to their senses—who, then, would take pleasure in being so foolish as not to choose the true things to follow, where he finds also the ampler things to marvel at?

For those miracles of the gentile gods which history commends (I do not mean those which, at hidden intervals of time, befall as portents through causes of the world itself, yet established and ordered under divine providence—such as unusual births of animals and uncommon appearances of things in sky and earth, whether only terrifying or even harmful, which their most deceitful cunning claims are warded off and mitigated by demonic rites;

but I mean those which evidently enough appear to be done by their force and power: such as that the images of the household gods, the Penates, which Aeneas in his flight brought from Troy, are reported to have migrated from place to place[^96]; that Tarquin cut a whetstone with a razor[^97]; that the Epidaurian serpent attached itself as a companion to Aesculapius sailing to Rome[^98];

that the ship in which the image of the Phrygian Mother was carried, rendered immovable by such great efforts of men and oxen, a single little woman, binding it with her girdle, moved and drew along as a testimony to her chastity[^99];

that a Vestal virgin, about whose corruption a charge was in dispute, with a sieve filled with water from the Tiber and not flowing through, did away with the controversy[^100])

these, then, and things of this kind are by no means to be compared, in power and greatness, with those which we read were done among the people of God; how much less those which by the laws even of those very peoples who worshiped such gods were judged to be forbidden and to be punished, namely, magical or theurgic arts!

Most of which deceive the senses of mortals only in appearance, by an imaginary illusion, such as drawing down the moon, *until, drawn nearer*, as Lucan says, *she foams upon the herbs set beneath*[^101];

while certain ones, even if they seem in their working to be made equal to some deeds of the devout, the very end by which they are distinguished shows that these our deeds incomparably excel.

For by those miracles those gods are to be worshiped with sacrifices all the less the more they demand them; but by these the one God is commended, who shows that he needs nothing of the kind, both by the testimony of his Scriptures[^102] and by the subsequent removal of those very sacrifices.

If, then, angels demand sacrifice for themselves, those are to be preferred to them who demand it not for themselves but for God, the creator of all, whom they serve.

For hereby they show with how sincere a love they love us, when by sacrifice they wish to subject us not to themselves but to him by whose contemplation they too are blessed, and to bring us to him from whom they themselves have not departed.

But if the angels who wish sacrifices to be offered not to one but to many do not wish them for themselves, but for those gods whose angels they are, even so those are to be preferred to them who are the angels of the one God of gods, to whom they bid sacrifice be offered in such a way as to forbid it to any other, while none of those others forbids it to the one to whom these bid sacrifice be offered.

Furthermore, if—as their proud deceit rather indicates—they are neither good nor the angels of good gods, but evil demons, who wish not the one and most high God, but themselves, to be worshiped with sacrifices: what greater protection against them is to be chosen than that of the one God, whom the good angels serve, who bid us serve with sacrifice not them, but him whose sacrifice we ourselves ought to be?

## What miracles are recorded in the history of the Jews.

**17.** Accordingly, the law of God, which was given in the edicts of angels[^103], in which the one God of gods was commanded to be worshiped with the rites of holy things, while all others were forbidden, was placed in the Ark[^104], which was called the Ark of the testimony.

By which name it is sufficiently signified that God, who was worshiped through all those things, was not wont to be enclosed or contained in a place, although his responses and certain signs were given to human senses from the place of that Ark, but that testimonies of his will were borne from there;

for the law itself too was written on tablets of stone and placed, as I said, in the Ark, which, during the time of their wandering in the wilderness, the priests carried with due veneration, together with the tabernacle, which was likewise called the tabernacle of the testimony[^105]; and there was a sign, that by day a cloud appeared, which by night shone like fire; and when this cloud moved, the camp was moved, and where it stood, the camp was pitched[^106].

And testimonies of great miracle were rendered to that law besides those I have mentioned, and besides the voices that were uttered from the place of that Ark. For when, as those entering the land of promise crossed, that same Ark passed over, the river Jordan, halting on the upper side and running down on the lower, furnished both to it and to the people a dry place for crossing[^107].

Then, when that same Ark had been carried around seven times, the walls of the city which first met them as hostile—worshiping, after the manner of the gentiles, many gods—suddenly fell, assaulted by no hand, struck by no battering ram[^108].

After these things, when they were now in the land of promise and that same Ark had been captured by the enemy because of their sins, those who had captured it placed it honorably in the temple of their god, whom they worshiped above the rest, and going out, shut the doors; and when they opened them the next day, they found the image to which they made supplication fallen down and shamefully broken.

Then they themselves, driven by portents and more shamefully punished, returned the Ark of the divine testimony to the people from whom they had taken it. And what a return it was! They set it on a cart and yoked to it heifers from which they had drawn away their suckling calves, and allowed them to go where they would, here too desiring to test the divine power. But those heifers, without any human leader or driver, walking persistently along the road to the Hebrews, and not called back by the lowings of their hungry offspring, carried back a great sacrament to their worshipers[^109].

These things and things of this kind are small to God, but great for terrifying and salutarily instructing mortals. For if the philosophers, and especially the Platonists, are praised for having been wiser than the rest, as I recalled a little before[^110], because they taught that divine providence administers these lowest and earthly things too, by the testimony of the numerous beauties which are produced not only in the bodies of animals but even in herbs and hay:

how much more evidently do those things attest to the divinity which are done at the hour of his proclamation, where that religion is commended which forbids sacrifice to all things heavenly, earthly, and infernal, bidding it for the one God alone, who alone, loving and loved, makes us blessed,

and who, fixing beforehand the commanded times of those sacrifices and foretelling that they were to be changed for the better through a better priest, testifies that he does not seek these things, but by them signifies other and more excellent things—not that he himself may be exalted by these honors, but that we, kindled by the fire of his love, may be roused to worship him and to cleave to him, which is a good for us, not for him.

**18.** Will anyone say that these miracles are false and were not done, but lyingly written?

Whoever says this, if he denies that any writings at all are to be believed concerning these matters, can also say that no gods care for mortal things.

For they have persuaded men to worship them in no other way than by the effects of wondrous works, of which the history of the nations too is a witness—the gods of those nations being able to show themselves marvelous rather than to prove themselves useful.

Hence in this work of ours, of which we now have this tenth book in hand, we have not undertaken to refute those who either deny that there is any divine power or contend that it does not care for human things,

but those who prefer their own gods to our God, the founder of the holy and most glorious city, not knowing that he is also the invisible and unchangeable founder of this visible and changeable world, and the most true giver of the blessed life, not out of the things he founded, but out of himself. For his most truthful Prophet says: *but for me it is good to cleave to God*[^111].

For concerning the end of the good there is a question among the philosophers, to the attaining of which all duties are to be referred[^112]. And this man did not say, "But for me it is good to abound in riches," or "to be distinguished by the purple and to excel with scepter or diadem," or, what some even of the philosophers did not blush to say, "For me bodily pleasure is the good[^113]"; or what those who seemed better said as something better, "For me the virtue of my mind is the good[^114]"; but he said, *for me it is good to cleave to God*. This he had been taught by the one to whom alone sacrifice is to be offered, as his holy angels too admonished, with the attestation even of miracles[^115]. Hence he himself had become a sacrifice to him, seized and burning with whose intelligible fire he was, and was borne by holy desire into his ineffable and incorporeal embrace.

Furthermore, if the worshipers of many gods (of whatever sort they suppose their gods to be) believe that miracles were done by them, whether on the authority of the histories of civil affairs or of magical books, or, as they more honorably think, theurgic ones: what reason is there why they should be unwilling to believe that these things were done according to those Writings, to which so much the greater faith is owed as he is great above all to whom alone they command sacrifice to be offered?

## A visible sacrifice too is offered to the one God.

**19.** But those who think that these visible sacrifices are suitable to other gods, while to him, as to the invisible one, the invisible and greater things, and to the greater and better, the better—such as the duties of a pure mind and a good will[^116]—surely do not know that these are signs of those things, just as spoken words are signs of things.

Wherefore, just as in praying and praising we direct to him significant words, to whom we offer in the heart the very things that we signify: so in sacrificing let us know that a visible sacrifice is to be offered to no other than to him whose invisible sacrifice in our hearts we ourselves ought to be.

Then the angels favor us and rejoice with us, and to this very end help us according to their powers—the powers and higher virtues, more powerful by their very goodness and piety. But if we should wish to render these things to them, they do not gladly accept them, and when they are sent to men in such a way that their presence is perceived, they most openly forbid it.

There are examples in the holy Writings. Certain persons thought that the honor owed to God should be rendered to angels, whether by adoring or by sacrificing, and they were forbidden by their admonition and bidden to render this to him to whom alone they knew it was lawful[^117]. The holy men of God too imitated the holy angels. For Paul and Barnabas in Lycaonia, when a certain miracle of healing had been done, were thought to be gods, and the Lycaonians wished to immolate victims to them; which, removing it from themselves with humble piety, they announced to them the God in whom they should believe[^118].

And for no other reason do those deceitful ones proudly demand this for themselves, except that they know it is owed to the true God. For they rejoice, not in truth, as Porphyry and some suppose, in the reeks of corpses, but in divine honors[^119].

They have, indeed, a great abundance of reeks on every side, and if they wished for more, they could provide them for themselves. Those spirits, then, who arrogate divinity to themselves, are delighted not by the smoke of any body, but by the mind of the suppliant, over whom, deceived and made subject, they may rule, blocking the way to the true God, lest a man be his sacrifice, so long as he sacrifices to anyone but him.

**20.** Hence that true Mediator, insofar as, taking the form of a servant[^120], he was made mediator of God and men, the man Christ Jesus[^121]—although in the form of God he receives sacrifice together with the Father, with whom he is also one God—yet in the form of a servant he chose rather to be a sacrifice than to receive one, lest by this occasion anyone should suppose that sacrifice was to be offered to any creature whatever.

Through this he is also priest, himself the one offering, himself also the oblation. Of this reality he willed the sacrifice of the Church to be the daily sacrament, which, since she is the body of him who is the head, learns to offer herself through him[^122].

Of this true sacrifice the ancient sacrifices of the saints were the manifold and varied signs, since this one thing was figured through many, as though one thing were said in many words, that it might be commended much without weariness[^123]. To this supreme and true sacrifice all false sacrifices have yielded.

## The martyrs are our heroes.

**21.** But within measured and predetermined times, even the power permitted to the demons—that, by stirring up the men whom they possess, they may tyrannically exercise enmities against the city of God, and may take sacrifices for themselves not only from those who offer and demand them from the willing, but may even violently extort them from the unwilling by persecution—

is not only not pernicious but is even found useful to the Church, that the number of the martyrs may be filled[^124]; whom the city of God holds as so much the more illustrious and honored citizens the more bravely they contend, even to blood, against the sin of impiety[^125].

These, far more elegantly, if the ecclesiastical custom of speaking would allow it, we would call our heroes. For this name is said to be derived from Juno, who in Greek is called *Hera*, and therefore some son of hers, according to the fables of the Greeks, was named *Heros*—the fable signifying by this, as it were, something mystical, namely, that the air is assigned to Juno, where they hold that the heroes dwell with the demons[^126]; by which name they call the souls of the dead who have some merit. But our martyrs would be named heroes in the opposite sense, if, as I said, the usage of ecclesiastical speech admitted it—not because they had a fellowship with the demons in the air, but because they conquered those same demons, that is, the aerial powers, and in them Juno herself, whatever she is thought to signify, who is not altogether unfittingly brought in by the poets as hostile to the virtues and envious of the brave men who seek heaven.

But Virgil again unhappily succumbs and yields to her, so that, when in his work she says, *I am conquered by Aeneas*[^127], Helenus admonishes Aeneas himself with what seems religious counsel and says: *chant vows to Juno freely, and overcome the mighty mistress with suppliant gifts*[^128].

From this opinion Porphyry, although not from his own judgment but from that of others, says that a good god or genius does not come into a man unless the evil one has first been appeased[^129];

as though the evil divinities were stronger among them than the good, since indeed the evil ones hinder the help of the good unless, appeased, they give place; and while the evil ones are unwilling, the good cannot profit; but the evil ones can do harm, since the good are not able to resist them.

This is not the way of the true and truly holy religion; not thus do our martyrs conquer Juno, that is, the aerial powers envious of the virtues of the devout. Not at all, if it could be said in the usual way, do our heroes overcome *Hera* by suppliant gifts, but by divine virtues. For Scipio was more fittingly surnamed Africanus because he conquered Africa by his valor than if he had appeased the enemy with gifts so that they might spare him.

**22.** By true piety the men of God cast out, by exorcizing, the aerial power that is hostile and contrary to piety[^130], not by appeasing it, and they conquer all the temptations of its adversity by praying not to it, but to their God against it.

For it conquers or subjugates no one except by fellowship in sin. It is conquered, then, in the name of him who assumed manhood and lived without sin, so that in him, both priest and sacrifice, there might be remission of sins, that is, through the Mediator of God and men, the man Christ Jesus[^131], through whom, the purgation of sins being accomplished, we are reconciled to God.

For men are separated from God only by sins, the purgation of which in this life comes about not by our own virtue but by the divine mercy, through his indulgence, not through our power; for even that little virtue, however small, which is called ours is granted to us by his goodness. And we would attribute much to ourselves in this flesh, did we not live under pardon until we lay it down. For this reason, then, grace was bestowed on us through the Mediator, that, polluted by the flesh of sin, we might be cleansed by the likeness of the flesh of sin[^132]. By this grace of God, in which he has shown his great mercy toward us[^133], we are both ruled in this life through faith, and after this life shall be led to the fullest perfection through the very vision of unchangeable truth.

## Whether Porphyry can teach that we are purified by the principles.

**23.** Porphyry says too that it was answered by divine oracles that we are not purified by the rites (*teletai*) of the moon and the sun, so that from this it might be shown that a man can be purified by the rites of no gods. For whose rites purify, if those of the sun and moon do not, whom they hold to be the chief among the heavenly gods?

Finally, he says it was expressed by the same oracle that the principles can purify, lest perhaps, when it had been said that the rites of the sun and moon do not purify, the rites of some other god out of the crowd should be believed to avail for purifying.

But what he, as a Platonist, says the principles are, we know. For he speaks of God the Father and God the Son, whom he calls in Greek the paternal intellect or the paternal mind[^134]; but concerning the Holy Spirit he says either nothing or nothing openly; although what other he calls the mean between these, I do not understand.

For if, like Plotinus, where he disputes about the three principal substances[^135], this man too wished the nature of the soul to be understood as the third, he would surely not call it the mean between these, that is, the mean between the Father and the Son.

For Plotinus ranks the nature of the soul below the paternal intellect[^136]; but this man, when he says "the mean," does not rank it below, but sets it between. And no doubt he said this, as he could or as he wished, of what we call the Holy Spirit—the Spirit not of the Father only nor of the Son only, but of both.

For philosophers speak with free words, and in matters most difficult to understand they do not fear to offend religious ears. But for us it is right to speak according to a fixed rule, lest the license of words beget an impious opinion even about the things that are signified by them.

**24.** And so we do not, when we speak of God, speak of two or three principles, just as it is not lawful for us to speak of two or three gods, although, speaking of each one—of the Father, or of the Son, or of the Holy Spirit—we confess that each individually is God, and yet do not say, as the Sabellian heretics do, that the Father is the same as the Son, and the Holy Spirit the same as both Father and Son[^137],

but that the Father is the Father of the Son, and the Son the Son of the Father, and the Holy Spirit, the Spirit of the Father and the Son, is neither the Father nor the Son.

Truly, then, it was said that a man is not purified except by a principle, although among them the principles were spoken of in the plural.

But Porphyry, being subject to the envious powers, of whom he was both ashamed and afraid to refute freely, was unwilling to understand that the Lord Christ is the principle by whose incarnation we are purified.

For he despised him in that very flesh which he assumed for the sake of the sacrifice of our purification[^138], not understanding, through that pride, the great sacrament which the true and kindly Mediator cast down by his humility, showing himself to mortals in that mortality which the malignant and deceitful mediators, by not having it, more proudly exalted themselves and promised to wretched men, as immortals to mortals, a deceptive help.

The good and true Mediator, therefore, showed that sin is the evil, not the substance or nature of the flesh, which, together with the human soul, could both be assumed without sin, and be possessed, and be laid aside in death, and be changed for the better by resurrection[^139];

and that death itself, although it was the penalty of sin—which, however, he himself paid for us without sin—is not to be avoided by sinning, but rather, if the opportunity is given, to be endured for the sake of righteousness. For he was able to loose sins by dying for this reason, that he both died and not for sin.

That Platonist did not recognize this one to be the principle; for he would have recognized him to be the purifying principle. For neither the flesh is the principle, nor the human soul, but the Word *through whom all things were made*[^140].

The flesh, then, does not cleanse of itself, but through the Word by whom it was assumed, when *the Word was made flesh and dwelt among us*[^141]. For speaking mystically of his flesh to be eaten, when those who did not understand withdrew, offended, saying, *this is a hard saying; who can hear it?*, he answered the rest who remained: *it is the Spirit that gives life; the flesh profits nothing*[^142].

The principle, then, having assumed soul and flesh, cleanses both the soul and the flesh of believers. Therefore, when the Jews asked who he was, he answered that he was the principle[^143]. Which surely we, carnal, weak, liable to sins, and wrapped in the darkness of ignorance, could in no way perceive, unless we were cleansed and healed by him through that which we were and were not. For we were men, but we were not righteous[^144]; but in his incarnation there was a human nature, yet it was righteous, not sinful. This is the mediation that was stretched out as a hand to those who had fallen and lay prostrate; this is the seed ordained through angels, in whose edicts the law too was given, by which the one God was bidden to be worshiped and this Mediator who was to come was promised[^145].

## Whether at one time the psalmist was wavering between the two lives.

**25.** By faith in this sacrament even the just men of old were able to be cleansed by living devoutly—not only before the law was given to the Hebrew people (for neither did God, as their proclaimer, nor the angels fail them), but also in the very times of the law, although it seemed to hold carnal promises in figures of spiritual things, on account of which it is called the Old Testament[^146].

For there were Prophets then too, through whom, as through angels, the same promise was proclaimed; and of their number was the one whose so great and divine sentence concerning the end of the human good I recalled a little before: *but for me it is good to cleave to God*[^147].

In which psalm, indeed, the distinction of the two Testaments, which are called Old and New, is sufficiently declared. For on account of the carnal and earthly promises, when he saw that the impious abounded in them, he says that his feet were almost moved and his steps nearly poured out into a fall,

as though he himself had served God in vain, when he saw the despisers of God flourishing in that happiness which he expected from him; and that he had labored in the inquiry of this matter, wishing to grasp why it was so, until he entered into the sanctuary of God and understood, in the last things of those who seemed happy, where he had been going astray.

Then he understood that, in that very thing in which they exalted themselves, as he says, they had been cast down and had failed and perished because of their iniquities[^148]; and that all that height of temporal happiness had become to them like the dream of one waking, who suddenly finds himself bereft of the deceitful joys he was dreaming. And since on this earth, or in the earthly city, they seemed great to themselves, he says: *Lord, in your city you will bring their image to nothing*[^149].

Yet how useful it was for him to seek even earthly things from none but the one true God, in whose power all things are, he sufficiently shows where he says: *I have become like a beast before you, and I am always with you*[^150]. By *like a beast* he surely meant "not understanding."

For "I ought to have desired from you those things which cannot be common to me with the impious; and when I saw them abounding in these, I thought I had served you in vain, since those too had these things who had been unwilling to serve you.

Yet *I am always with you*, because even in the desire of such things I did not seek other gods." And by this it follows: *you have held my right hand, in your will you have led me, and with glory you have received me*[^151]; as though to the left hand belonged all those things in which, when he saw the impious abounding, he was almost overthrown. *For what is there for me in heaven, and what have I wished from you upon earth?*[^152]

He reproved himself and was justly displeased with himself, because, having so great a good in heaven (which he afterward understood), he sought from his God upon earth a transitory, fragile, and, in a manner, muddy happiness. *My heart and my flesh have failed, O God of my heart*[^153], he says—by a good failing, surely, from lower things to higher;

whence in another psalm it is said: *my soul longs and faints for the courts of the Lord*[^154]; likewise in another: *my soul has fainted for your salvation*[^155]. Yet, when he had spoken of both, that is, of the failing heart and flesh, he did not add, "O God of my heart and my flesh," but *O God of my heart*. For through the heart the flesh is cleansed. Whence the Lord says: *cleanse the things that are within, and the things that are without will be clean*[^156].

He then says that his portion is God himself, not something from him, but himself. *God of my heart*, he says, *and God my portion forever*[^157]; that among the many things which are chosen by men, he himself was pleased to choose him to be chosen. *For behold*, he says, *those who put themselves far from you shall perish; you have destroyed everyone who fornicates away from you*[^158], that is, whoever wishes to be a prostitute of many gods. Whence follows that for the sake of which the other things from this same psalm seemed worth saying: *but for me it is good to cleave to God*[^159]—not to go far off, not to fornicate after many. But to cleave to God will then be perfect, when all that is to be set free has been set free.

But now there comes about that which follows: *to put my hope in God*. For *hope that is seen is not hope; for what a man sees, why does he also hope for?* says the Apostle. *But if we hope for what we do not see, we wait for it through patience*[^160].

But, set now in this hope, let us do what follows, and let us too, according to our small measure, be angels of God, that is, his messengers, announcing his will and praising his glory and grace. Whence, when he had said, *to put my hope in God*, he says, *that I may announce all your praises in the gates of the daughter of Zion*[^161].

This is the most glorious city of God; this knows and worships the one God; this the holy angels announced, who invited us to its fellowship and willed us to be their fellow citizens in it; who are not pleased that we should worship them as our gods, but, together with them, both their God and ours; and that we should not sacrifice to them, but, together with them, be a sacrifice to God.

And so, with no one who considers these things, laying aside malignant obstinacy, doubting it, all the immortal blessed beings who do not envy us (for if they envied, they would not be blessed), but rather love us, that we too may be blessed with them, favor us more and help us more when we worship the one God together with them—Father and Son and Holy Spirit—than if we worshiped them themselves by sacrifices.

## Certain things are true in Porphyry's teaching about the angels.

**26.** In some way, as it seems to me, Porphyry was ashamed before his theurgist friends. For he had some sense of these things, but did not freely defend them against the worship of many gods[^162]. For he said that there are some angels who, descending below, declare divine things to theurgic men, but others who declare on earth the things that are the Father's, and his height and depth.

Are these angels, then, whose ministry it is to declare the will of the Father, to be believed to wish us to be subject to anyone but him whose will they announce to us? Whence this Platonist himself most excellently admonishes that they are to be imitated rather than invoked[^163]. We ought not, therefore, to fear that we offend the immortal and blessed beings, subject to the one God, by not sacrificing to them.

For what they know is owed only to the one true God, by cleaving to whom they too are blessed, they without doubt do not wish to be rendered to themselves, neither through any signifying figure nor through the very thing that is signified by the sacraments.

This is the arrogance of the proud and wretched demons, from whom the piety of those subject to God, and blessed by cleaving to none but him, is far different. To the perceiving of which good they ought, even for us, to favor us with sincere kindness, and not to arrogate to themselves that to which we should be made subject, but to announce him under whom we may be joined to them in peace.

Why do you still tremble, O philosopher, to have a free voice against the powers that envy both true virtues and the gifts of the true God? You have already distinguished the angels who announce the will of the Father from those angels who, drawn down by some art I know not, descend to theurgic men.

Why do you still honor them, so as to say that they declare divine things? What divine things, after all, do they declare, who do not announce the will of the Father? Surely they are those whom the envious one bound with sacred prayers, lest they should bestow purification on a soul, and who could not, by the good one, as you say, desiring to purify, be loosed from those bonds and restored to their own power.

Do you still doubt that these are malignant demons—or do you perhaps feign not to know, while you are unwilling to offend the theurgists, by whom, deceived through your curiosity, you learned these pernicious and insane things as a great benefit[^164]?

Do you dare to raise this envious thing—not a power but a plague, and, I will not say a mistress, but, as you yourself confess, rather the handmaid of the envious—up into heaven beyond this air we have traversed, and to place it among your gods, even the starry ones, or even to defame the stars themselves with these reproaches?

**27.** How much more humanely and tolerably your fellow Platonist Apuleius erred[^165], who, honoring the demons—those alone ordered from the moon downward—as driven by the diseases of the emotions and the disturbances of the mind, yet, willing and unwilling, confessed it of them;

yet separated the higher gods of heaven, pertaining to the ethereal spaces—whether the visible ones, which he saw shining conspicuously, the sun and moon and the other lights there, or the invisible ones, which he supposed to exist—from every stain of those disturbances, with as much disputation as he could!

But you learned this not from Plato, but from your Chaldaean masters: to raise up human vices into the ethereal or empyrean heights of the world and the heavenly firmaments, so that your gods might declare divine things to the theurgists;

above which divine things you nonetheless make yourself higher through the intellectual life, so that, namely, to you as a philosopher the purifications of the theurgic art seem by no means necessary; yet you import them to others, so as to pay this fee, as it were, to your masters, that you seduce those who cannot philosophize to those things which you confess to be useless to you, as one capable of higher things;

so that, namely, whoever are removed from the virtue of philosophy, which is too arduous and belongs to the few, may, on your authority, seek out theurgic men by whom they may be purified—not, indeed, in the intellectual soul, but at least in the spiritual soul; and since the multitude of those who are loath to philosophize is incomparably greater, more are forced to come to your secret and unlawful masters than to the Platonic schools. For this the most unclean demons, feigning themselves to be ethereal gods, whose proclaimer and angel you have become, promised you: that those purified by the theurgic art in the spiritual soul do not indeed return to the Father, but will dwell above the aerial regions among the ethereal gods[^166].

That multitude of men does not hear these things, for whose deliverance from the dominion of the demons Christ came. For in him they have the most merciful purification of their mind and spirit and body. For this very reason he assumed the whole man without sin, that he might heal the whole of what man consists of from the plague of sins. Would that you too had known him, and had entrusted yourself for healing more safely to him than to your own virtue, which is human, fragile, and weak, or to a most pernicious curiosity!

For he would not have deceived you, whom your own oracles, as you yourself write, confessed to be holy and immortal; of whom even the most renowned poet said—poetically, indeed, because under the shadow of another person, yet truly, if you refer it to him: *under your leadership, whatever traces of our wickedness remain shall, made void, free the earth from perpetual dread*[^167].

For he said those things which, even of men who make much progress in the virtue of righteousness, can—on account of the weakness of this life—remain, if not wickednesses, yet traces of wickednesses, which are healed by none but that Savior of whom this verse was framed. For surely Virgil indicates that he did not say this of himself, in about the fourth verse of that same *Eclogue*, where he says:  
*now comes the last age of the Cumaean song*[^168]; whence it appears without hesitation that this was said by the Cumaean Sibyl.

But those theurgists, or rather the demons feigning the appearance and figures of gods, defile rather than purify the human spirit by the falsity of apparitions and the deceptive sport of empty forms.

For how should they purify a man's spirit, who have their own unclean? Otherwise they would in no way be bound by the spells of an envious man, and would not either, by fear, withhold the very empty benefit they seemed about to bestow, or, by like envy, deny it. It is enough that you say neither the intellectual soul, that is, our mind, can be purified by theurgic purification, and that the spiritual soul itself, that is, the part of our soul lower than the mind, which you assert can be purified by such an art, you nonetheless confess cannot be made immortal and eternal by this art. But Christ promises eternal life; whence the world flocks to him—to your annoyance, indeed, yet to your wonder and amazement[^169].

What does it profit that you could not deny that men err by the theurgic discipline and that very many are deceived through a blind and foolish opinion, and that it is a most certain error to run, by acting and supplicating, to the principalities and angels, and yet again, as though lest you should seem to have wasted your effort in learning these things, you send men to the theurgists, that through them the spiritual soul of those who do not live according to the intellectual soul may be purified?

## Christ calls all to salvation.

**28.** You send men, then, into a most certain error, and you are not ashamed of so great an evil, though you profess yourself a lover of virtue and wisdom;

which, if you had loved it truly and faithfully, you would have recognized *Christ, the power of God and the wisdom of God*[^170], and would not, puffed up with the swelling of vain knowledge, have recoiled from his most salutary humility.

You confess, nonetheless, that the spiritual soul too can be purified by the virtue of continence without the theurgic arts and without the rites (*teletai*) in the vain learning of which you labored.

Sometimes you even say that the rites do not raise the soul up after death, so that they now seem to profit nothing, even to that very soul which you call spiritual, after the end of this life; and yet you turn these things over in many ways and repeat them, for no other purpose, as I judge, than to appear skilled in such matters too, and to please those curious about unlawful arts, or to make men curious about them yourself.

But it is well that you say this art is to be feared, whether for the perils of the laws or of the very practice. And would that at least the wretched might hear this from you, and thence depart, lest they be swallowed up there, or not approach it at all.

Ignorance, certainly, and, on account of it, many vices, you say are purified by no rites, but by the *patrikon noun* alone, that is, the paternal mind or intellect, which is conscious of the Father's will.

But that this is Christ you do not believe; for you despise him on account of the body received from a woman and on account of the reproach of the cross, being fit, no doubt, to pluck lofty wisdom from the heights, the lowest things being scorned and cast away[^171].

But he fulfills what the holy prophets truly foretold of him: *I will destroy the wisdom of the wise, and the prudence of the prudent I will reject*[^172]. For he does not destroy and reject in them his own wisdom, which he himself gave, but that which they arrogate to themselves, who do not have his.

Whence, having recalled this prophetic testimony, the Apostle goes on and says: *where is the wise man? Where is the scribe? Where is the disputer of this age? Has not God made foolish the wisdom of this world? For since, in the wisdom of God, the world by its wisdom did not know God, it pleased God by the foolishness of preaching to save those who believe. For the Jews ask for signs and the Greeks seek wisdom; but we*, he says, *preach Christ crucified, to the Jews indeed a stumbling block, but to the gentiles foolishness, yet to those who are called, both Jews and Greeks, Christ the power of God and the wisdom of God; for the foolishness of God is wiser than men, and the weakness of God is stronger than men*[^173]. This, as foolish and weak, the wise and strong despise as though by their own virtue. But this is the grace that heals the weak, who do not proudly boast of their false blessedness, but rather humbly confess their true misery.

**29.** You proclaim the Father and his Son, whom you call the paternal intellect or mind, and the mean between these, whom we suppose you to mean the Holy Spirit[^174], and after your manner you call them three gods[^175]. Here, although you use undisciplined words, you nonetheless see, in some fashion and as though through certain shadowy outlines of a faint imagination, the goal toward which we must strive;

but the incarnation of the unchangeable Son of God, by which we are saved, so that we may be able to come to those things which we believe or in however small a part understand, you are unwilling to acknowledge. And so you see, in some way, even if from afar, even if with dimmed sight, the fatherland in which we must abide, but the way by which we must go you do not hold.

You confess grace, nonetheless, since indeed you say it has been granted to few to come to God through the virtue of understanding. For you do not say, "It pleased few," or "few wished it," but, when you say "it has been granted," you without doubt confess the grace of God, not the sufficiency of man.

You use this word even more openly where, following Plato's opinion[^176], you yourself too do not doubt that in this life a man in no way attains to the perfection of wisdom, but that for those living according to the intellect all that is lacking can be supplied, after this life, by the providence and grace of God[^177].

Oh, if you had known *the grace of God through Jesus Christ our Lord*, and his very incarnation, by which he assumed a human soul and body, you could have seen it to be the supreme example of grace!

But what shall I do? I know I speak in vain to a dead man—at least as far as concerns you; but as far as concerns those who esteem you highly and love you, whether from some love of wisdom or from curiosity about the arts which you ought not to have learned, whom I am rather addressing in this appeal to you, perhaps I do not speak in vain.

The grace of God could not have been commended more graciously than that the only Son of God himself, remaining unchangeably in himself, should be clothed with man and give the Spirit of his love to men through a man as mediator,

by which men might come to him who was so far off—the immortal from the mortal, the unchangeable from the changeable, the righteous from the impious, the blessed from the wretched.

And because he naturally implanted in us the desire to be blessed and immortal, remaining blessed and taking on a mortal nature, that he might grant us what we love, he taught us by suffering to despise what we fear.

**29.** But that you might be able to acquiesce in this truth, there was need of humility, which can most difficultly be urged upon your neck. For what incredible thing is said—especially to you who hold such views, by which you ought to admonish yourselves to believe this—what, I say, incredible thing is said to you, when it is said that God assumed a human soul and body? You certainly attribute so much to the intellectual soul, which is surely a human soul, that you say it can be made consubstantial with that paternal mind whom you confess to be the Son of God.

What, then, is incredible, if some one intellectual soul was assumed in a certain ineffable and singular way for the salvation of many? And that the body cleaves to the soul, so that the man may be whole and complete, we know with our very nature as witness.

Unless this were most familiar, it would surely be the more incredible; for it is more easily to be received into belief that—though the human cleaves to the divine, though the changeable to the unchangeable—yet spirit cleaves to spirit, or, to use the words you have in use, the incorporeal to the incorporeal, than that a body cleaves to the incorporeal.

Or does perhaps the unusual birth of a body from a virgin offend you? Neither ought this to offend; rather, it ought to lead you to receive piety, that one marvelous was marvelously born.

Or do you perhaps refuse to believe this—that he raised that very body, laid down in death and changed for the better by resurrection, now incorruptible and immortal, into the heights—looking to Porphyry in these very books from which I have set down much, which he wrote *On the Return of the Soul*, so often directing that every body is to be fled, that the soul may be able to remain blessed with God?[^178]

But he himself, holding these views, ought rather to have been corrected, especially since concerning the soul of this visible world, and of so vast a corporeal mass, you hold, along with him, things so incredible. For on Plato's authority[^179] you say that the world is a living being, and a most blessed living being, which you wish also to be everlasting.

How, then, will it neither ever be released from the body, nor ever lack blessedness, if, that the soul may be blessed, every body is to be fled?

This sun too and the other stars you confess, not only in your books, to be bodies, which all men along with you do not hesitate both to behold and to say; but also, by a higher skill, as you think, you maintain that these are most blessed living beings, and everlasting along with these bodies[^180].

What is it, then, that, when the Christian faith is urged upon you, you then forget, or feign not to know, what you are accustomed to dispute or teach? What reason is there why, on account of your own opinions, which you yourselves attack, you are unwilling to be Christians, except that Christ came humbly and you are proud? What sort of bodies the saints will have in the resurrection can be disputed somewhat more scrupulously among the most learned in the Christian Scriptures[^181];

yet that they will be everlasting we do not in the least doubt, and that they will be such as the example which Christ showed by his resurrection. But of whatever sort they may be, since they are proclaimed to be wholly incorruptible and immortal, and in no way hindering the contemplation of the soul by which it is fixed in God, and since you too say that there are in the heavens immortal bodies of the immortally blessed:

what is it that, in order to be blessed, you suppose every body must be fled, so that you seem to flee the Christian faith as though reasonably, except that it is that which I say again: Christ is humble, you are proud?

Or is it perhaps shameful to be corrected? And this vice belongs to none but the proud. It is shameful, no doubt, for learned men to become, from disciples of Plato, disciples of Christ, who taught a fisherman by his Spirit to be wise and to say:

*In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God. He was in the beginning with God. All things were made through him, and without him was made nothing that was made. In him was life, and the life was the light of men, and the light shines in the darkness, and the darkness did not comprehend it*[^182].

This beginning of the holy Gospel, which is named *according to John*, a certain Platonist, as we used to hear from the holy old man Simplician, who afterward presided as bishop over the Church of Milan[^183], used to say ought to be inscribed in golden letters and set up in the most eminent places throughout all the churches.

But that God, the teacher, grew cheap to the proud for this reason, that *the Word was made flesh and dwelt among us*[^184]; so that it is too little for the wretched that they are sick,

unless they also exalt themselves in their very sickness and blush at the medicine by which they could have been healed. For they do not do this that they may be lifted up, but that by falling they may be more grievously afflicted.

## . . . though Plato teaches, with Porphyry indeed resisting, that souls are rolled back into beasts.

**30.** If it is thought unworthy to correct anything after Plato, why did Porphyry himself correct some things, and not small ones? For it is most certain that Plato wrote that the souls of men, after death, are rolled back even into the bodies of beasts[^185]. This opinion Porphyry's teacher Plotinus too held[^186];

yet it rightly displeased Porphyry. He held, indeed, that human souls return into men—not into their own bodies which they had left, but into other, new bodies.

He was ashamed, no doubt, to believe the former, lest perhaps a mother, rolled back into a mule, should carry her own son; and he was not ashamed to believe the latter, where a mother, rolled back into a girl, might perhaps marry her son.

How much more honorably is that believed which the holy and truthful angels taught, which the prophets, moved by the Spirit of God, spoke, which he himself foretold whom the messengers sent before announced would come as Savior, which the apostles sent forth, who filled the world with the Gospel, proclaimed;

how much more honorably, I say, is it believed that souls return once to their own bodies than that they return so many times to different ones! Yet, as I said, Porphyry was for the most part corrected in this opinion, so that he held at least that human souls can be cast down only into men, but did not in the least hesitate to overthrow the beastly prisons.

He says too that God gave the soul to the world for this end, that, coming to know the evils of matter, it might run back to the Father and never thereafter be held, polluted by the contagion of such things. Here, even if he is somewhat unfitting in his view (for it was rather given to the body that it might do good things; for it would not learn evils if it did not do them), yet in this he corrected the opinion of the other Platonists, and in no small matter, that he confessed that the soul, cleansed from all evils and established with the Father, will never thereafter suffer the evils of this world[^187].

By this opinion he certainly did away with what is most often presented as the Platonic view, namely that, as the dead come to be from the living, so the living come to be perpetually from the dead[^188]; and he showed to be false what Virgil seems to have said in a Platonic vein, that the souls sent into the Elysian fields after being purified (a name by which, as in a fable, the joys of the blessed seem to be signified) are summoned to the river Lethe, that is, to forgetfulness of things past: *doubtless that, forgetful, they may revisit the vault above and once again begin to wish to return into bodies*[^189].

This rightly displeased Porphyry, since it is in truth foolish to believe[^190] that from that life, which cannot be most blessed unless it is most certain of its own eternity, souls should desire the defilement of corruptible bodies and from there return to these things, as though the highest purification accomplished this, that pollution should be sought out again.

For if their being perfectly cleansed has this effect, that they forget all evils, while the forgetting of evils produces the desire for bodies, in which they are again entangled in evils, then surely the highest happiness will be the cause of unhappiness, the perfection of wisdom the cause of folly, and the highest cleansing the cause of uncleanness.

Nor will the soul be blessed there by truth, for however long it shall be there, it must be deceived in order to be blessed. For it will not be blessed unless it is secure; but in order to be secure, it will falsely suppose that it will always be blessed, since at some time it will also be wretched. How, then, will one rejoice in truth, for whom the cause of rejoicing will be a falsehood?

Porphyry saw this[^191] and said that the purified soul returns to the Father for this reason, that it may never again be held fast by the polluting contagion of evils. Falsely, therefore, was that supposedly necessary cycle of departing from these things and returning to the same believed by certain Platonists.

And even if this were true, what good would it do to know it, unless perhaps the Platonists would dare to prefer themselves to us on this account, that in this life we did not yet know what they, in another and better life, most fully purified and most wise, were going to be ignorant of, and were going to be blessed by believing what is false?

But if it is most absurd and most foolish to say this, then surely the opinion of Porphyry is to be preferred to that of those who imagined cycles of souls forever alternating between blessedness and misery. And if this is so, behold, a Platonist dissents from Plato for the better; behold, he saw what the other did not see, and after so great and so eminent a master he did not shrink from correction, but set truth above the man.

## Plato absurdly teaches that souls existed beforehand.

**31.** Why, then, do we not rather believe the divine word concerning those matters which we cannot trace out by human ingenuity—the word which declares that the soul itself is not coeternal with God, but was created, and once was not? For when the Platonists were unwilling to believe this, they thought they were bringing forward an adequate ground for themselves, namely that what had not always existed beforehand could not thereafter be everlasting; although, even concerning the world and concerning those whom Plato writes were made gods in the world by God[^192], he says most plainly that they began to be and have a beginning, yet will have no end,

but maintains that through the most powerful will of their maker they will remain forever. Yet they have found a way to understand this, namely that this is not a beginning of time, but of substitution.

*For just as*, they say, *if a foot had been in the dust from eternity, a footprint would always have been beneath it, which footprint, nevertheless, no one would doubt was made by the one treading—nor would the one be prior to the other, although the one was made by the other—so*, they say, *both the world and the gods created in it have always existed, since the one who made them always exists, and yet they were made*[^193].

Must it then be said that, if the soul has always existed, its misery too has always existed? Further, if something in it that did not exist from eternity began to exist in time, why could it not have come about that the soul itself existed from a point in time, having not existed before?

Then too its blessedness, which after the experience of evils is to be more firmly fixed and to abide without end, as these men themselves admit, without doubt began in time, and yet will be everlasting, though before it was not. That whole argument, therefore, is undone, by which it is supposed that nothing can be without end of time unless it has no beginning of time. For the blessedness of the soul has been found, which, though it has had a beginning of time, will have no end of time.

Wherefore let human weakness yield to divine authority, and let us believe, concerning true religion, those blessed and immortal beings who do not seek for themselves the honor which they know is owed to their God, who is also ours, and who command us to offer sacrifice to none but him alone, of whom both we and they, as I have often said and must often say, ought to be the sacrifice—we who are to be offered through that priest who, in the man whom he assumed, and according to which he willed also to be a priest, deigned to become a sacrifice for us even unto death.

## On Porphyry's authority there is a way for the soul to be set free.

**32. 1.** This is the religion which contains the universal way of the soul's liberation, since by no other way can the soul be set free. For this is, in a manner, the royal road, which alone leads to a kingdom—not one tottering on a temporal height, but secure in the firmness of eternity.

But when Porphyry says, in the first book of his *On the Return of the Soul*, near the end, that no school has yet been received which contains the universal way of the soul's liberation—neither any most true philosophy, nor the customs and discipline of the Indians, nor the initiation of the Chaldeans, nor any other way whatever—and that this same way had not yet been brought to his knowledge by historical inquiry: he without doubt confesses that there is some such way, but that it had not yet come to his knowledge.

Thus all that he had most studiously learned about the soul's liberation, and seemed to himself, or rather to others, to know and to hold, was not enough for him. For he felt that he still lacked some most excellent authority which it would be proper to follow in a matter so great. But when he says that no school containing the universal way of the soul's liberation had yet come to his knowledge, not even from any most true philosophy, he shows well enough, in my judgment, either that the philosophy in which he himself philosophized is not the truest, or that such a way is not contained in it.

And how can that be the truest philosophy by which this way is not contained? For what other universal way of the soul's liberation is there than that by which all souls are set free, and through which, therefore, without it no soul is set free? And when he adds and says: *nor from the customs and discipline of the Indians, nor from the initiation of the Chaldeans, nor by any other way whatever*[^194], he testifies in the plainest words that this universal way of the soul's liberation is contained neither in what he had learned from the Indians nor in what he had learned from the Chaldeans; and he could not be silent that he had taken divine oracles from the Chaldeans, which he constantly mentions[^195].

What, then, does he mean by the universal way of the soul's liberation, not yet received either from any most true philosophy or from the teachings of those nations which were held to be great, as it were, in divine matters—because among them curiosity prevailed more strongly in coming to know and worship various angels—and not yet brought to his knowledge by historical inquiry?

What is this universal way, except the one which has been divinely bestowed, not as proper to each nation by itself, but as common to all nations?

That this exists, this man, endowed with no ordinary ability, does not doubt. For he does not believe that divine providence could have left the human race without this universal way of the soul's liberation. For he does not say that it does not exist, but only that so great a good and so great a help had not yet been received, had not yet been brought to his knowledge; and no wonder.

For Porphyry was in this world at the time when this universal way of the soul's liberation—which is none other than the Christian religion—was permitted to be assailed by the worshipers of idols and demons and by earthly kings, for the sake of establishing and consecrating the number of the martyrs, that is, of the witnesses to the truth,

through whom it might be shown that all bodily evils are to be endured for the faith of godliness and for the commendation of the truth.

Porphyry, therefore, saw these things, and thought that, through persecutions of this kind, this way would soon perish, and for that reason that it was not itself the universal way of the soul's liberation—not understanding that the very thing which moved him, and which he feared to suffer in his own choice of it, pertained rather to its confirmation and to a stronger commendation of it.

**32. 2.** This, then, is the universal way of the soul's liberation—that is, the way granted by divine mercy to all nations—and surely to whomever its knowledge has now come, and to whomever it is yet to come, it neither ought to have been nor ought to be said: Why now? and: Why so late? Since the counsel of the one who sends it is not penetrable by human ingenuity. This too that man felt, when he said that this gift of God had not yet been received and had not yet been brought to his knowledge. For he did not judge it on that account not to be true, because it had not yet been received into his faith or come to his knowledge.

This, I say, is the universal way for the liberation of believers, concerning which the faithful Abraham received the divine oracle: *in your seed shall all the nations be blessed*[^196].

He was indeed a Chaldean by nation, but, that he might receive such promises and that from him might be propagated the seed *ordained by angels in the hand of a Mediator*[^197], in whom was this universal way of the soul's liberation, that is, the way given to all nations, he was commanded to depart from his land and from his kindred and from his father's house[^198].

Then, first set free from the superstitions of the Chaldeans[^199], he worshiped the one true God by following him, and faithfully believed him as he made these promises.

This is the universal way, concerning which it is said in holy prophecy: *May God have mercy on us and bless us; may he make his face shine upon us, that we may know your way upon earth, your salvation among all nations*[^200]. Whence, so long afterward, having taken flesh from the seed of Abraham, the Savior himself says of himself: *I am the way, the truth, and the life*[^201].

This is the universal way, concerning which it was prophesied so long before: *In the last days the mountain of the Lord shall be manifest, established on the top of the mountains, and it shall be exalted above the hills; and all nations shall come to it, and many peoples shall enter in and shall say: Come, let us go up to the mountain of the Lord and to the house of the God of Jacob; and he will declare to us his way, and we will walk in it. For out of Zion shall go forth the law, and the word of the Lord from Jerusalem*[^202].

This way, therefore, is not of one nation, but of all nations; and the law and the word of the Lord did not remain in Zion and Jerusalem, but went forth from there to spread itself throughout the whole world. Whence the Mediator himself, after his resurrection, said to his trembling disciples: *These are the things which were written in the law and the Prophets and the psalms concerning me, that they must be fulfilled. Then he opened their minds to understand the Scriptures, and said to them that thus it was necessary for Christ to suffer and to rise from the dead on the third day, and that repentance and the forgiveness of sins should be preached in his name to all nations, beginning from Jerusalem*[^203].

This, then, is the universal way of the soul's liberation, which the holy angels and the holy Prophets first signified—among the few men who, where they could, found the grace of God, and especially in the Hebrew nation, whose very commonwealth was in a manner consecrated to be a prophecy and foreannouncement of the city of God that was to be gathered out of all nations—

and which they signified by the tabernacle, the temple, the priesthood, and the sacrifices, and foretold by certain plain utterances, and by very many mystical ones;

while the Mediator himself, present in the flesh, and his blessed apostles, now revealing the grace of the New Testament, indicated more openly the things which in earlier times were signified somewhat more obscurely, according to the distribution of the ages of the human race, as it pleased God in his wisdom to order it, with signs attesting them—signs of the wondrous works of God, of which I have already set down a few above.

For not only did angelic visions appear and the words of heavenly ministers alone resound, but also, while men of God acted in the Word of God with simple godliness, unclean spirits were driven from the bodies and senses of men, bodily defects and weaknesses were healed, the wild beasts of the lands and the birds of the air, trees, the elements, and the stars carried out the divine commands, the infernal regions gave way, and the dead came back to life[^204]—apart from the proper and singular miracles of the Savior himself, especially those of his birth and his resurrection, in the one of which he displayed only the mystery of his mother's virginity, while in the other he displayed also an example of those who are to rise again at the end.

This way cleanses the whole man and prepares him, mortal as he is, for immortality in all the parts of which he is composed. For, that no other purification might be sought for that part which Porphyry calls intellectual, another for that which he calls spiritual, and another for the body itself, the most truthful and most powerful Cleanser and Savior assumed the whole man.

Apart from this way, which has never been wanting to the human race—partly when these things were foretold as future, partly when they were announced as accomplished—no one has been set free, no one is set free, no one will be set free.

**32. 3.** But as for Porphyry's saying that the universal way of the soul's liberation had not yet been brought to his knowledge by historical inquiry:

what history can be found more illustrious than this, which has taken hold of the whole world with so lofty an authority, or more trustworthy, in which past events are so narrated that things to come are also foretold—many of which we see fulfilled, and from which we may without doubt hope that those which remain will be fulfilled?

For neither Porphyry nor any of the Platonists can, even in this way, despise the divination and foretelling of, as it were, earthly things and things pertaining to this mortal life—as they rightly do in the case of other soothsayers and in the divinations of whatever modes or arts.

For they deny that these things are to be reckoned the work of great men or of great value, and rightly. For either they come about through a foresensing of inferior causes, as in the art of medicine, where, by certain antecedent signs, many things that will befall one's health are foreseen;

or unclean demons foretell their own appointed deeds—deeds whose right they in a manner claim for themselves, both in leading the minds and desires of the wicked toward whatever deeds suit them, and in the lowest matter of human frailty.

Not such are the things which the holy men, walking in this universal way of the liberation of souls, took care to prophesy as though they were great—although these things too did not escape them and were often foretold by them, to produce faith in those things which could not be conveyed to mortal senses nor brought to experience with swift ease.

But there were other things, truly great and divine, which, as far as it was granted them to know the will of God, they announced as future.

For that Christ would come in the flesh, and the things which in him have been so clearly accomplished and fulfilled in his name—the repentance of men and the turning of their wills toward God, the forgiveness of sins, the grace of righteousness, the faith of the godly and the multitude throughout the whole world of those believing in the true Godhead, the overthrow of the worship of idols and demons and the testing of believers by temptations, the purification of those making progress and their liberation from all evil, the day of judgment, the resurrection of the dead, the eternal damnation of the fellowship of the impious, and the eternal kingdom of the most glorious city of God, immortally enjoying the sight of him—

these things were foretold and promised in the Scriptures of this way; and so many of them we behold fulfilled that with right godliness we are confident the rest will be. As for the rightness of this way, leading up to the seeing of God and to cleaving to him forever, those who do not believe it in the truth of the holy Scriptures, in which it is proclaimed and asserted—and who therefore do not understand it—may assail it, but cannot overthrow it.

**32. 4.** Wherefore, in these ten books, even if we have done less than the expectation of some concerning us looked for, yet, by the zeal of certain others, we have, as far as the true God and Lord has deigned to help us, given satisfaction by refuting the contradictions of the impious, who prefer their own gods to the founder of the holy city, about which we set out to dispute. Of these ten books, the first five were written against those who think the gods are to be worshiped for the sake of the goods of this life; the latter five against those who hold that the worship of the gods is to be maintained for the sake of the life that is to be after death. Next, therefore, as I promised in the first book, I shall set forth, as far as I shall be divinely helped, what I think ought to be said concerning the origin, the course, and the appointed ends of the two cities, which I have said are in this world intertwined and mutually mingled.
