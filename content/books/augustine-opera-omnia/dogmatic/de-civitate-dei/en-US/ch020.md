# Book XVIII: What Has Already Been Set Forth

**1.** I promised that I would write of the rise, the course, and the due ends of the two cities, of which one is God's, the other this world's, in which, as far as concerns the human race, that one too is a pilgrim; but first I refuted, as far as his grace helped me, the enemies of the city of God, who prefer their own gods to Christ its founder and, with a most pernicious envy to themselves, fiercely envy the Christians, which I did in the first ten volumes.

But concerning this threefold promise of mine, which I just mentioned, the rise of both was set forth in the four books following the tenth; then their course from the first man down to the flood in one book, which is the fifteenth of this work; and from there down to Abraham both cities ran on, as in time, so also in our writing. But from the father Abraham down to the time of the kings of the Israelites, where we ended the sixteenth volume, and from there down to the coming of the Savior himself in the flesh, as far as the seventeenth book extends, the city of God alone seems to have run on in my pen; although in this age it did not run alone, but both surely, in the human race, as from the beginning, varied the times together by their course.

But I did this for this reason, that first—from when the promises of God began to be more open, down to his birth from the virgin, in which were to be fulfilled the things that were promised at first—this one, which is God's, running on without the interruption of the other city on the contrary side, might appear more distinctly; although down to the revelation of the New Testament it ran not in light, but in shadow. Now, then, I see that what I had left aside must be done, so that I may touch on how that one too ran on from the times of Abraham, as far as seems enough, that both may be compared with each other by the consideration of the readers.

## What Are the Interests and Desires of the Earthly City

**2.** 1. The society of mortals, then, spread everywhere through the lands and bound, amid however great a diversity of places, by a certain community of one and the same nature, while each pursues its own interests and desires, since what is sought either suffices for no one or not for all, because it is not one and the same thing—this society is for the most part divided against itself, and part oppresses part, the part that prevails.

For the conquered yields to the conqueror, preferring any kind of peace and safety to domination or even to liberty[^1], so that those have been a great wonder who chose to perish rather than to serve[^2]. For in almost all nations the voice of nature in some way resounded this, that those to whom it fell to be conquered would rather be subjugated to their conquerors than be destroyed by warlike devastation of every kind. Hence it came about—not without the providence of God, in whose power it is that each be either subjugated or subjugate in war—that some were endowed with kingdoms, some subjected to the reigning.

But among the very many kingdoms of the earth, into which the society of earthly interest or desire is divided (which by a universal term we call "the city of this world"), we see two kingdoms that far surpassed the rest in renown—the Assyrians first, then the Romans—ordered and distinguished from each other, as in times, so in places.

For as the former was earlier, the latter later, so the former arose in the East, the latter in the West; and finally, at the end of the former the beginning of the latter immediately followed[^3]. The other kingdoms and other kings I would call, as it were, appendages of these.

## The Two Kingdoms of Babylon and Rome Are Preeminent

**2.** 2. Ninus, then, was already the second king of the Assyrians, who had succeeded his father Belus, the first king of that kingdom, when Abraham was born in the land of the Chaldeans[^4]. There was also at that time the kingdom of the Sicyonians, quite small, from which Marcus Varro, that man most learned in every respect, writing *On the Race of the Roman People*, began, as from an ancient time. For from these kings of the Sicyonians he came to the Athenians, from these to the Latins, thence to the Romans[^5].

But before the founding of Rome these things are recorded as very small in comparison with the kingdom of the Assyrians; although Sallust, the Roman historian, confesses that the Athenians flourished very greatly in Greece; yet more in fame than in the thing itself. For speaking of them he says, *the deeds of the Athenians, as I judge, were great enough and magnificent; but yet somewhat less than they are reported by fame. But because there arose there great talents of writers, throughout the world the deeds of the Athenians are celebrated as the greatest. Thus the virtue of those who did them is held to be as great as illustrious talents were able to extol it by words*[^6]. There accrues to this city no small glory too from letters and philosophers, in that there especially such studies flourished.

For as far as concerns empire, none was greater in the earliest times than that of the Assyrians, nor so far and wide diffused, since there king Ninus, the son of Belus, is reported to have subdued all Asia—which is said to be a third part of the whole world by the number of its parts, but is found to be half by its size—as far as the bounds of Libya[^7]. The Indians alone, in the parts of the East, he did not rule, whom yet, after his death, his wife Semiramis attacked by waging war[^8]. Thus it came about that whatever peoples or kings there were in those lands obeyed the kingdom and dominion of the Assyrians and did whatever was commanded.

Abraham, then, was born in that kingdom, among the Chaldeans, in the times of Ninus. But since Greek affairs are much better known to us than Assyrian, and those who have searched out the race of the Roman people in the antiquity of its origin have led down the series of times through the Greeks to the Latins and thence to the Romans, who themselves too are Latins: on this account we ought, where it is needful, to name the Assyrian kings, that it may appear how Babylon, as it were a first Rome, runs on together with the city of God a pilgrim in this world; but the matters which, for the comparison of the two cities—the earthly, namely, and the heavenly—it behooves us to insert into this work, we ought rather to take from the Greeks and Latins, where Rome itself is as it were a second Babylon[^9].

## Who Were the Kings among the Assyrians and the Sicyonians in the Time of Abraham

**2.** 3. When, then, Abraham was born, the second kings were, among the Assyrians, Ninus, among the Sicyonians, Europs; but the first there had been Belus, here Aegialeus. But when, Abraham having gone out from Babylon, God promised him that a great nation would come from him and a blessing of all nations in his seed, the Assyrians had a fourth king, the Sicyonians a fifth; for among them there reigned the son of Ninus after his mother Semiramis, who is reported to have been killed by him, since the mother dared to defile her son with incestuous union[^10].

Some think that she founded Babylon, which indeed she could have restored[^11]. But when or how it was founded, we said in the sixteenth book[^12]. But the son of Ninus and Semiramis, who succeeded his mother in the kingdom, some call also Ninus himself, but some Ninyas, by a name derived from his father[^13]. The kingdom of the Sicyonians was then held by Telxion. In whose reign the times there were so mild and glad that, when he died, they worshiped him as a god, sacrificing and celebrating games, which they report were first instituted for him.

**3.** In his times Isaac too was born, of the promise of God, the son of Abraham, a hundred years old, by his wife Sarah, who, being barren and now old, had lost the hope of offspring. Then the fifth king of the Assyrians was Arrius.

But to Isaac himself, when he was sixty, twins were born, Esau and Jacob, whom Rebecca his wife bore him, their grandfather Abraham still living and passing the hundred and sixtieth year of his age, who, when he had completed a hundred and seventy-five, died, while there reigned among the Assyrians that more ancient Xerxes, who was also called Baleus, and among the Sicyonians Thuriacus, whom some write Thurimachus, the seventh kings.

But the kingdom of the Argives arose together with Abraham's grandsons, where the first to reign was Inachus. To be sure—what was not to be passed over—Varro reports that the Sicyonians too were wont to sacrifice at the tomb of their seventh king Thuriacus[^14]. But when the eighth kings reigned—Armamitres of the Assyrians, Leucippus of the Sicyonians, and Inachus the first of the Argives—God spoke to Isaac[^15] and promised to him too those same two things which he had to his father—namely, to his seed the land of Canaan and in his seed the blessing of all nations.

These same things were promised also to his son, the grandson of Abraham, who was called first Jacob, afterward Israel, when now Belochus reigned as the ninth king of Assyria, and Phoroneus, the son of Inachus, as the second over the Argives, Leucippus still remaining among the Sicyonians.

In these times Greece, under Phoroneus the Argolic king, was made more renowned by certain institutions of laws and judgments. But when Phegous, the younger brother of this Phoroneus, had died, at his tomb a temple was set up, in which he was worshiped as a god and oxen were sacrificed to him. I believe they thought him worthy of so great an honor for this reason, that in his part of the kingdom (for their father had distributed to both the places in which, while he lived, they should reign) he had set up shrines for worshiping the gods and had taught the observance of times by months and years, and how far each of them should be measured and reckoned. These new things in him, men still untaught, wondering, when he had died, either supposed or willed him to have become a god.

For Io too is reported to have been the daughter of Inachus, who afterward, called Isis, was worshiped as a great goddess in Egypt; although others write that she came from Ethiopia into Egypt as a queen, and that, because she ruled widely and justly and instituted for them many useful things and letters, this divine honor was paid her after she died there, and so great an honor that anyone would be guilty of a capital crime if he said she had been a human being[^16].

**4.** When the tenth king of the Assyrians, Baleus, and the ninth of the Sicyonians, Messapus—who is also handed down by some as Cephisos (if, however, one man bore two names, and they did not rather suppose one man to have been another, who set down in their writings the other name)—were reigning, while Apis was the third king of the Argives, Isaac died at a hundred and eighty years and left his twins, a hundred and twenty years old; of whom the younger, Jacob, belonging to the city of God of which we write—the elder of course being rejected—had twelve sons, one of whom, who was called Joseph, his brothers had sold to merchants passing over into Egypt, while their grandfather Isaac was still living.

Now Joseph stood before Pharaoh when he was exalted out of the humiliation that he had endured, at the age of thirty; for, having by divine inspiration interpreted the king's dreams, he foretold that there would be seven years of plenty, whose surpassing abundance another seven barren years to follow were to consume, and on this account the king set him over Egypt, freeing him from the prison into which the integrity of his chastity had cast him—a chastity he kept so bravely that, when his mistress loved him wickedly and was ready to lie to her too-credulous master, he even left his garment in the hands of the woman who clutched at him and fled, refusing to consent to her seduction[^17]. Now in the second year of the seven barren years Jacob came into Egypt to his son with all his household, being one hundred and thirty years old, as he himself answered when the king asked[^18], while Joseph was thirty-nine—that is, thirty, the age he was when the king honored him, with seven years of plenty and two of famine added.

and for this reason the king had placed him over Egypt after freeing him from the prison into which his uncompromising chastity had cast him,

which he steadfastly maintained: even abandoning his garment and fleeing from the hands of his mistress as she pulled at him, he refused her illicit desire and would not consent to adultery, though she was going to lie to his all too credulous master[^19].

In the second of the seven years of famine, Jacob came to Egypt with all his people to join his son. He was 130 years old, as he himself answered when the king questioned him[^20], while Joseph was thirty-nine: to the thirty years of his age when the king honored him must be added seven years of abundance and two of famine.

**5.** In these times Apis, king of the Argives, was carried by ship into Egypt; and when he died there, he became Serapis, the greatest god of all the Egyptians. Varro gives a very simple explanation of why, after his death, he was called not Apis but Serapis. Because the chest in which a dead man is placed—what everyone now calls a sarcophagus—is in Greek called *soros*, and they had begun to venerate him there in his tomb before his temple was built, he was first called Sorapis, as it were from *soros* and "Apis," and then, with one letter changed, as commonly happens, he was called Serapis. And it was decreed concerning him that whoever said he had been a man should pay the penalty with his life. And because in nearly all the temples where Isis and Serapis were worshiped there was also a statue that seemed to enjoin silence by pressing a finger to its lips, the same Varro thinks this signified that men should keep quiet about their having been human.

But that bull which Egypt, deceived by an astonishing folly, fed with lavish delicacies in his honor was called Apis, not Serapis, because they venerated it alive and without a sarcophagus. When this bull died, since a calf of the same color was sought and found—that is, marked in the same way with certain white spots—they believed that something wondrous had been divinely provided for them[^21].

For it was no great matter for the demons, in order to deceive them, to display to a cow at the moment of conceiving and bearing young the phantasm of such a bull—which she alone perceived—so that the mother's desire might draw to itself what would then appear bodily in her offspring; just as Jacob, by means of the peeled rods, brought it about that mottled sheep and goats were born[^22]. For what men can do with real colors and bodies, the demons can do most easily for conceiving animals by means of feigned figures.

**6.** Apis, then, the king not of the Egyptians but of the Argives, died in Egypt. His son Argus succeeded him in the kingdom, and from his name both Argos and, from this, the Argives were so called; the earlier kings did not yet have this name, whether for the place or for the people. While he was reigning among the Argives, and Erato among the Sicyonians, and Baleus still remaining among the Assyrians, Jacob died in Egypt at the age of one hundred and forty-seven, having, when about to die, blessed his sons and his grandsons by Joseph and most plainly prophesied of Christ, saying in his blessing of Judah: *the scepter shall not depart from Judah, nor a ruler from his loins, until those things come that are laid up for him; and he is the expectation of the nations*[^23].

While Argus was reigning, Greece began to use its own grain crops and to have harvests from agriculture, the seeds having been brought from elsewhere. Argus too, after his death, came to be held a god, honored with a temple and sacrifices. This honor was conferred, while he was reigning and before his own death, upon a certain private man named Homogyrus, who had been struck by lightning, because he was the first to yoke oxen to the plow.

**7.** While Mamythus, the twelfth king of the Assyrians, and Plemmaeus, the eleventh of the Sicyonians, were reigning, and Argus still remained at Argos, Joseph died in Egypt at the age of one hundred and ten[^24]. After his death the people of God, increasing wondrously, remained in Egypt for one hundred and forty-five years—at first in peace, until those died who had known Joseph;

then, because their growth was envied and they were regarded with suspicion, they were oppressed—until they were delivered from there—by persecutions (amid which, however, they kept increasing through a divinely granted multiplication) and by the labors of an intolerable slavery[^25]. In Assyria and Greece, meanwhile, the same kingdoms continued during this same period.

## . . . and in the time of Moses.

**8.** When, therefore, Saphrus the fourteenth was reigning over the Assyrians, Orthopolis the twelfth over the Sicyonians, and Criasus the fifth over the Argives, Moses was born in Egypt, through whom the people of God were freed from Egyptian slavery, in which they had to be trained in this way so as to long for the help of their Creator. In the reign of the kings just mentioned, Prometheus is believed by some to have lived; they say that for this reason he is reported to have formed men out of clay, because he is said to have been the best teacher of wisdom; yet it is not shown who the wise men of his times were.

His brother Atlas is said to have been a great astronomer; from this the fable found occasion to invent that he carried the heavens, although a mountain is named after him, by whose height rather it would seem that the notion of his carrying the heavens entered the popular imagination[^26]. Many other fabulous things too began to be invented in Greece during those times; but down to Cecrops, king of the Athenians—in whose reign that city also received its present name[^27], and in whose reign God led his people out of Egypt through Moses—a number of dead men were enrolled among the gods by the blind and empty custom and superstition of the Greeks.

Among these were Melantomice, wife of King Criasus, and Phorbas their son, who after his father was the sixth king of the Argives; and Iasus, son of the seventh king, Triopas; and the ninth king, Sthenelas—or Stheneleus, or Sthenelus, for the name is found variously in different authors. In these times Mercury too is reported to have lived, grandson of Atlas by his daughter Maia, as even the more widely known writings proclaim[^28]. He became famous as one skilled in many arts, which he also handed on to men; for this merit they wished, or even believed, him to be a god after his death. Hercules is said to have lived later, yet still belonging to the times of the Argives, though some place him before Mercury in time—men whom I judge to be mistaken. But at whatever time they were born, it is agreed among the serious historians who committed these ancient matters to writing that both were men, and that, because they conferred many benefits on mortals to make this life more agreeable, they earned divine honors from them.

Minerva, however, was far more ancient than these; for in the times of Ogygus she is said to have appeared at the lake called Triton, in the form of a maiden, whence she was also named Tritonia. She was indeed the inventor of many works, and was believed a goddess all the more readily the less her origin was known. For her being sung as born from the head of Jupiter belongs to poets and fables, not to history and actual events[^29]. As to when Ogygus himself lived—in whose times also there occurred a great flood, not that greatest one in which no men escaped except those who could be in the ark[^30], which neither Greek nor Latin history of the nations knows of, but yet greater than the one afterward in the time of Deucalion—the writers of history do not agree.

For Varro begins from it the book I mentioned above[^31], and he proposes nothing more ancient, from which to arrive at Roman affairs, than the flood of Ogygus—that is, the one that occurred in the times of Ogygus[^32]. But our writers of chronicles—first Eusebius, then Jerome, who in this opinion certainly followed some earlier historians—record that the flood of Ogygus occurred more than three hundred years later, already in the reign of Phoroneus, the second king of the Argives. But at whatever time it occurred, Minerva was already being worshiped as a goddess in the reign of Cecrops over the Athenians, under which king they say the city itself was either restored or founded[^33].

## There was a contest between Minerva and Neptune over Athens . . .

**9.** For the reason why it was called Athens—a name that certainly comes from Minerva, who in Greek is called Athena—Varro gives as follows. When an olive tree had suddenly appeared there and water had burst forth in another place, these portents alarmed the king, and he sent to Delphic Apollo to inquire what they should be understood to mean and what should be done. Apollo answered that the olive signified Minerva and the water Neptune, and that it was in the citizens' power to decide after which of the two gods, whose signs these were, the city should rather be named. On receiving this oracle, Cecrops summoned all the citizens of both sexes (for the custom then in those places was that women too took part in public deliberations) to cast their votes. When the multitude had been consulted, the men cast their votes for Neptune, the women for Minerva; and because one more woman was found, Minerva won.

Then Neptune in anger laid waste the lands of the Athenians with the surging waves of the sea; for it is not difficult for demons to spread waters of any kind more widely. To appease his wrath, the same author says, the women were punished by the Athenians with a threefold penalty: that none of them should ever again cast a vote, that none of their newborn children should take the mother's name, and that no one should call them Athenians[^34].

Thus that city, mother or nurse of the liberal disciplines and of so many and so great philosophers—than which Greece had nothing more famous or more noble—was named Athens through the demons' mockery: out of a dispute between its gods, a male and a female, and out of a female's victory through females; and, wounded by the defeated god, it was compelled to punish the very victory of the victorious goddess, fearing Neptune's waters more than Minerva's arms.

For in the women who were thus punished, Minerva also, who had won, was defeated; nor did she stand by her supporters, so that—having thenceforth lost the power of voting and with their sons estranged from their mothers' names—they might at least be allowed to be called Athenians and to merit the name of that goddess whom they, by casting their votes, had made victorious over the male gods. What and how much might be said on this matter, were our discourse not hastening on to other things!

**10.** And yet Marcus Varro is unwilling to give credence to the fabulous fictions told against the gods, lest he think something unworthy of the dignity of their majesty. And therefore he is unwilling that even the Areopagus—where the apostle Paul disputed with the Athenians[^35], and from which place the senators of that city were called Areopagites—should have taken its name from this: that Mars, who in Greek is called Ares, when he was made a defendant on a charge of homicide, was acquitted by six of the votes of the twelve gods who were judging in that district; for when the votes were equal in number, acquittal was wont to be preferred to condemnation.

But against this opinion, which is far more widely current, he tries to construct another explanation of this name from the knowledge of obscure writings, so that the Athenians may not be believed to have named the Areopagus from the name of Mars and from "district," as it were "the district of Mars"—to the injury, that is, of the divinities, from whom he thinks quarrels and lawsuits are alien; affirming this account given of Mars to be no less false than the one told of three goddesses, namely Juno, Minerva, and Venus, who are said to have contended before the judge Paris over the prize of excellence in beauty, for the winning of a golden apple, and who, to appease the gods—who take delight in these charges against themselves, whether true or false—are sung and danced amid the applause of the theaters.

These things Varro does not believe, lest he believe what is unbecoming to the nature or character of the gods[^36]; and yet, while giving not a fabulous but a historical account of the name of Athens, he inserts into his own writings so great a dispute between Neptune and Minerva over which of them the city should rather be named after,

that, when they contended by the display of portents, not even Apollo, when consulted, dared to judge between them, but, in order to end the quarrel of the gods, he sent it—just as Jupiter sent the quarrel of the three goddesses to Paris—so this god sent it to men; where Minerva won by the votes, yet was defeated in the punishment of her own supporters, since she was able to obtain Athens among her adversaries the men, but could not have her friends the women called Athenians.

In these times—while, as Varro writes, Cranaus, successor of Cecrops, was reigning over the Athenians, but, as our writers Eusebius and Jerome have it, while the same Cecrops still remained—there occurred the flood that was called Deucalion's, because he was reigning in those parts of the world where it chiefly took place. This flood, however, by no means reached Egypt and the regions adjoining it[^37].

## What kingdoms there were from Moses to Joshua.

**11.** Moses, then, led the people of God out of Egypt in the last period of Cecrops, king of the Athenians, when Ascatades was reigning among the Assyrians, Marathus among the Sicyonians, and Triopas among the Argives. When the people had been led out, he delivered to them on Mount Sinai the law received by divine inspiration—what is called the Old Testament, because it has earthly promises, while the New Testament, by which the kingdom of heaven would be promised, was to come through Jesus Christ. For this order had to be observed, just as in each individual man who advances toward God there takes place what the Apostle says: *it is not the spiritual that is first but the animal, and then the spiritual*; for, as he also says and as is true, *the first man was from the earth, earthly; the second man is from heaven*[^38].

Moses ruled the people for forty years in the desert and died at the age of one hundred and twenty[^39], having himself also prophesied Christ through the figures of carnal observances in the tabernacle, the priesthood, the sacrifices, and other mystical and very numerous commandments. Joshua, son of Nun, succeeded Moses and settled the people he had brought in within the land of promise, by divine authority, having subdued in war the nations by whom those places were held. When he had ruled the people for twenty-seven years after the death of Moses, he too died, in the reign of Amyntas the eighteenth among the Assyrians, Corax the sixteenth among the Sicyonians, Danaus the tenth among the Argives, and Erichthonius the fourth among the Athenians.

**12.** During these times—that is, from the departure of Israel out of Egypt to the death of Joshua, son of Nun, through whom that people received the land of promise—rites were instituted to false gods by the kings of Greece, which by solemn celebration recalled the memory of the flood and of men's deliverance from it, and of the wretched life they then led as they migrated now to the heights and now to the plains. For the ascent and descent of the Luperci along the Sacred Way they interpret in this way, saying that by it are signified the men who, because of the flooding of the water, sought the tops of the mountains, and, when it subsided again, returned to the lowlands.

In these times they say that Dionysus, who was also called father Liber and was held a god after his death, showed the vine to his host in the land of Attica. Then musical contests were instituted for Delphic Apollo, to appease his anger, by which they thought the regions of Greece had been afflicted with barrenness, because they had not defended his temple, which King Danaus had burned when he invaded those lands in war.

They were admonished by his oracle to institute these contests. In Attica, however, King Erichthonius was the first to institute contests for him—and not only for him but also for Minerva, where oil was set as the prize for the victors, since they relate that Minerva was the discoverer of its fruit, as Liber was of wine.

In those years Europa is reported to have been carried off by Xanthus, king of the Cretans—whose name we find given otherwise in other writers—and from her were begotten Rhadamanthus, Sarpedon, and Minos, who in the common account are rather held to be sons of Jupiter by the same woman. But the worshipers of such gods reckon what we have said of the king of the Cretans to the truth of history, while what the poets sing of Jupiter—and the theaters resound with it and the people celebrate it—they reckon to the vanity of fables, so that there might be an occasion for holding games to appease their divinities even with their false crimes.

In these times Hercules was held famous in Syria; but doubtless another, not the one of whom we spoke above[^40]. For by a more secret history there are said to have been several both fathers Liber and Hercules. This Hercules, indeed, whose mighty deeds they number as twelve—among which they do not include the slaying of the African Antaeus, since that exploit belongs to the other Hercules—they relate in their writings to have burned himself to death on Mount Oeta, since with that strength by which he had subdued many things he yet could not endure the disease from which he languished.

Their writings report that this Hercules, whose twelve mighty deeds they enumerate—without including the killing of Antaeus the African, since that deed belongs to a different Hercules—burned himself on Mount Oeta. For although his strength had overcome many things, he could not endure the disease from which he was wasting away.

At that time Busiris, either king or rather tyrant, sacrificed his own guests to his gods; they report him to have been the son of Neptune by his mother Libya, daughter of Epaphus. But let it not be believed that Neptune committed this fornication, lest the gods be accused; rather, let such things be attributed to poets and theaters, that there may be an occasion for appeasing the gods.

Vulcan and Minerva are said to have been the parents of Erichthonius, king of the Athenians, in whose last years Joshua, son of Nun, is found to have died. But since they would have Minerva a virgin, they say that, when the two were struggling together, Vulcan in his agitation spilled his seed upon the earth, and that a man was born from it, and for that reason was given such a name. For in the Greek language *eris* means "strife" and *chthon* means "earth," and from these two the word Erichthonius is compounded.

But—and this must be admitted—the more learned refute these things and repel them from their gods, saying that this fabulous opinion arose from this: that in the temple of Vulcan and Minerva, which they both had as one at Athens, an exposed boy was found wrapped in a serpent, which signified that he would be great; and, because of the shared temple, since his parents were unknown, he was said to be the son of Vulcan and Minerva. Yet the origin of his name is designated rather by that fable than by this history. But what is that to us?

Let the one account, in truthful books, instruct religious men; let the other, in deceitful games, delight the impure demons—whom nevertheless those religious men worship as gods; and when they deny these things of them, they cannot clear them of every crime, since they put on games at the demons' demand, in which those things are shamefully enacted that are, as it were, wisely denied,

and the gods are appeased by these false and shameful things; for, even if the fable sings a false crime of the divinities, yet to take delight in a false crime is a true crime.

## . . . and in the time of the Judges.

**13.** After the death of Joshua, son of Nun, the people of God had judges, in whose times there alternated among them both the humiliations of hardships, on account of their sins, and the prosperities of consolations, on account of the mercy of God.

In these times fables were invented: about Triptolemus, that at Ceres's command, borne by winged serpents, he carried grain by flying to lands in need; about the Minotaur, that it was a beast shut up in the Labyrinth, into which when men had entered, they could not, by reason of its inextricable maze, find their way out; about the Centaurs, that they had the nature of horses and men joined together; about Cerberus, that he is the three-headed dog of the underworld; about Phrixus and his sister Helle, that they flew borne by a ram; about the Gorgon, that she had hair of serpents and turned those who looked at her into stone; about Bellerophon, that he was carried by a horse that flew with wings, which horse was called Pegasus; about Amphion, that by the sweetness of the lyre he soothed and drew the stones; about the craftsman Daedalus and his son Icarus, that they flew with wings fitted to themselves; about Oedipus, that he compelled a certain monster called the Sphinx—four-footed with a human face—to perish by hurling itself down, when the question it was wont to propose, as though insoluble, had been solved; about Antaeus, whom Hercules killed, that he was a son of the earth, on account of which, falling to the earth, he was wont to rise up stronger; and any others that I may perhaps have passed over.

These fables, down to the Trojan war, where Marcus Varro ended the second book *On the Race of the Roman People*, were so contrived by the talents of men, on the occasion of histories that contain things truly done, that they are not fastened upon as reproaches to the divinities[^41]. Moreover, whoever invented that the most beautiful boy Ganymede was carried off by Jupiter for his lust—a crime which King Tantalus committed and the fable attributed to Jupiter—or that he sought intercourse with Danae through a golden shower, by which it is understood that the woman's chastity was corrupted by gold:

these things, whether done or feigned in those times, or done by others and feigned of Jupiter—it cannot be said how much evil they presumed of men's hearts, that they could patiently bear these lies, which nevertheless they even gladly embraced.

And surely, the more devoutly they worshiped Jupiter, the more severely they ought to have punished those who dared to say such things of him. But as it is, they were not only not angry with those who invented these things, but they feared rather to have the gods themselves angry if they did not enact such fictions even in the theaters.

In these times Latona bore Apollo—not the one whose oracles we spoke above of being consulted, but the one who served Admetus along with Hercules; who nevertheless was so believed a god that very many, and almost all, think there was one and the same Apollo. Then father Liber too made war in India, having many women in his army, who were called Bacchae, distinguished not so much by valor as by frenzy.

Some indeed write that this Liber was both conquered and bound; some, that he was even slain in battle by Perseus, and they do not pass over in silence where he was buried; and yet under his name, as of a god, through the unclean demons the Bacchanalian rites—or rather sacrileges—were instituted, at whose raging shamefulness the senate, after so many years, was so ashamed that it forbade them to exist in the city of Rome[^42]. About that time, after Perseus and his wife Andromeda had died, they so believed them received into heaven that they were not ashamed, nor afraid, to mark their images with stars and to call them by their names.

**14.** During the same interval of time there arose poets who were also called theologians, because they composed songs about the gods—but about such gods as, though great men, were nevertheless men, or are the elements of this world, which the true God made, or were set among the principalities and powers according to the will of the Creator and their own merits. And if, amid many vain and false things, they sang anything about the one true God, yet by worshiping others along with him who are not gods, and by rendering to them the service due to the one God alone, they certainly did not duly serve him, nor could they themselves abstain from the fabulous disgrace of their gods—Orpheus, Musaeus, Linus.

But these theologians worshiped gods, they were not worshiped as gods—although the city of the impious is wont, I know not how, to set Orpheus over its infernal rites, or rather sacrileges. Now Ino, wife of King Athamas, and her son Melicertes perished in the sea by a voluntary plunge, and in the opinion of men were enrolled among the gods, like other men of those times, Castor and Pollux. That mother of Melicertes the Greeks indeed called Leucothea, the Latins Matuta, but both held her a goddess.

**15.** About these times the kingdom of the Argives came to an end, transferred to Mycenae, whence Agamemnon came, and the kingdom of the Laurentines arose, where Picus, son of Saturn, first received the kingdom, while a woman, Deborah, was judging among the Hebrews; but through her the Spirit of God was at work, for she was also a prophetess, whose prophecy is too little plain for us to be able, without lengthy exposition, to show that it was uttered of Christ[^43]. Already, then, the Laurentines were reigning, in Italy of course, from whom the Roman origin is more clearly derived, after the Greeks; and yet the Assyrian kingdom still continued, where Lampares was the twenty-third king, when Picus first began to be king of the Laurentines.

Concerning Saturn, the father of this Picus, let the worshipers of such gods see what they think—those who deny that he was a man; of whom others have written that he himself reigned in Italy before Picus his son, and Virgil, in his more familiar writings, says: *he gathered together a race untamed and scattered over the high mountains and gave them laws, and chose that it be called Latium, since he had lain hidden, safe, on these shores. The golden ages they tell of were under that king*[^44]. But let them suppose these to be poetic fictions, and assert rather that the father of Picus was Sterces, a most skilled farmer, by whom it is reported to have been discovered that fields are made fertile by the dung of animals, which from his name is called *stercus* (dung); some report that he was called Stercutius. But for whatever reason they wished to call him Saturn, certainly they made this Sterces, or Stercutius, a god, deservedly, on account of agriculture. Picus likewise, his son, they received into the number of such gods, asserting that he was a distinguished augur and warrior. Picus begot Faunus, the second king of the Laurentines; he too is, or was, a god to them. These men they conferred divine honors upon, after they were dead, before the Trojan war.

## What the fable was concerning Diomedes and his companions.

**16.** But when Troy had been overthrown by that destruction, sung everywhere and most familiar to boys—which both by its own greatness and by the surpassing tongues of its writers was notably spread abroad and made known, and which took place when Latinus, son of Faunus, was already reigning, from whom the kingdom began to be called that of the Latins, and that of the Laurentines ceased—the victorious Greeks, leaving Troy destroyed and returning to their own homes, were torn and crushed by various and horrible disasters; and yet even from among them they increased the number of their gods.

For they made Diomedes too a god, who, they report, by a punishment divinely inflicted, did not return to his own people; and they affirm—not by fabulous and poetic lying, but by historical attestation—that his companions were turned into birds. To these, even though he was, as they suppose, made a god, he was unable either himself to restore human nature or to obtain it from Jupiter his king, as a newcomer to heaven.

Moreover, they say that his temple is on the island of Diomedea, not far from Mount Garganus, which is in Apulia, and that these birds fly around this temple and dwell there with such wondrous reverence that they fill their beaks with water and sprinkle it; and that if Greeks come there, or those sprung from Greek stock, the birds are not only quiet but even fawn upon them; but if they see foreigners, they fly up to their heads and wound them with such heavy blows as even to kill them. For they are said to be sufficiently armed for these battles with hard and large beaks.

**17.** To establish this, Varro recalls other things no less incredible: about that most famous sorceress Circe, who also changed the companions of Ulysses into beasts[^45], and about the Arcadians, who, chosen by lot, swam across a certain pool and there were turned into wolves and lived with similar wild beasts through the deserts of that region. But if they did not feed on human flesh, then after nine years, by swimming back across the same pool, they were restored to men.

Finally, he even named expressly a certain Demaenetus who, when he had tasted of a sacrifice that the Arcadians were wont to make to their god Lycaeus by immolating a boy, was changed into a wolf and in the tenth year was restored to his own shape, and trained himself in boxing and won in the Olympic contest. And the same historian thinks that for no other reason was such a name given in Arcadia to Pan Lycaeus and to Jupiter Lycaeus than on account of this change of men into wolves, which they thought could not happen except by divine power. For "wolf" in Greek is called *lykos*, from which the name Lycaeus appears to be derived. He says that the Roman Luperci too arose, as it were, from the seed of those mysteries[^46].

**18.** **1.** But concerning this great mockery of the demons, those who read these things perhaps expect us to say something. And what shall we say, except that we must flee from the midst of Babylon[^47]? This prophetic precept is understood spiritually in this way: that we flee from the city of this world—which is assuredly a society of impious angels and men—by advancing toward the living God with the steps of faith, which works through love[^48]. For the greater the power of the demons that we see over these lowest things, the more tenaciously must we cling to the Mediator, through whom we ascend from the lowest to the highest. For if we should say that these things are not to be believed, there are not lacking even now those who affirm that they have heard, or even themselves experienced, certain things of this kind as most certain.

For we too, when we were in Italy, used to hear such things about a certain region of those parts, where they said that women who kept inns, steeped in these evil arts, were wont to give in cheese to travelers, whomever they wished or could, something whereby they were at once turned into beasts of burden and carried whatever was needed, and after the tasks were finished returned again to themselves; and yet their minds did not become bestial, but a rational and human mind was preserved—just as Apuleius, in the books he entitled *The Golden Ass*, either reported or feigned to have happened to himself: that, on taking poison, while his human mind remained, he became an ass.

**18.** **2.** These things are either false or so unusual that they are rightly not believed. Yet it must be most firmly believed that almighty God can do all that he wills, whether in punishing or in bestowing, and that the demons can effect nothing by the power of their own nature (for it too is an angelic creation, although by its own fault malign) except what he permits, whose judgments are many hidden, none unjust.

Nor indeed do the demons create natures, if they do anything of the kind about which this question turns; but they only change in appearance what has been created by the true God, so that things may seem to be what they are not. And so I would not believe that, by any reasoning, either the mind or even the body of a man can truly be turned by the demons' art or power into the limbs and features of a beast; but the phantasm of a man—which, even in thinking or dreaming, is varied through innumerable kinds of things and, though it is not a body, yet takes on with wondrous swiftness forms like those of bodies—can, when the bodily senses of the man are lulled or oppressed, be brought, in some ineffable way I know not how, in a bodily figure to the perception of others;

so that the men's own bodies lie somewhere, alive indeed, but with their senses locked far more heavily and forcibly than in sleep; while that phantasm, as though embodied in the likeness of some animal, appears to the senses of others, and the man seems even to himself to be such—just as he might seem to himself to be such in dreams—and to carry burdens; and if these burdens are real bodies, they are carried by the demons, that men may be deceived, who perceive partly the real bodies of the burdens and partly the false bodies of beasts of burden.

For a certain man named Praestantius reported that it had befallen his father to take that poison in cheese in his own house and to lie on his bed as though sleeping, and yet he could in no way be roused. After some days, he said, his father seemed to wake and to relate, as it were, the dreams he had suffered: namely, that he had been made a packhorse and, among other beasts of burden, had carried provisions for the soldiers, which provisions are called "Retica," because they are carried to Raetia.

And it was found that this had happened just as he related; yet to him these had seemed his own dreams. Another man also reported that in his own house, at night, before he went to rest, he had seen coming to him a certain philosopher very well known to him, who expounded to him certain points of Plato that, when asked before, he had refused to expound. And when this same philosopher was asked why he had done in that man's house what he had refused when asked in his own, he said: *I did not do it, but I dreamed that I did it*. And thus there was exhibited to one man, while awake, through a phantasmal image, what the other saw in dreams.

**18.** **3.** These things came to us not from just anyone, such as we would think it unworthy to believe, but from those reporting them whom we would not suppose to have lied to us. Accordingly, that men are said—and it has been committed to writing—to be wont to be changed into wolves by the gods, or rather the demons, of the Arcadians, and that *by her songs Circe changed the companions of Ulysses*[^49], it seems to me could have been done in the manner I have described, if indeed it was done at all.

But the Diomedean birds—since their kind is reported to endure through the succession of their offspring—I believe were not made from changed men, but were substituted when the men had been removed, just as a hind was substituted for Iphigenia, daughter of King Agamemnon.

For such tricks could not have been difficult for demons permitted by the judgment of God; but because that maiden was afterward found alive, it was easily recognized that a hind had been substituted for her. The companions of Diomedes, however, because they nowhere suddenly appeared and afterward appeared in no place, the avenging evil angels destroying them, are believed to have been changed into those birds which were secretly substituted for them, brought from other places where this kind of bird exists, and suddenly put in their stead.

But that they bring water with their beaks to Diomedes's temple and sprinkle it, and that they fawn upon those of Greek birth and persecute foreigners, is no wonder to happen at the instigation of demons, whose interest it is to persuade men that Diomedes was made a god, in order to deceive them, so that they may worship many false gods, to the injury of the true God, and may serve dead men—who not even while they lived truly lived—with temples, altars, sacrifices, and priests, all of which, when they are right, are owed to none but the one living and true God.

**19.** At that time, after Troy was captured and destroyed, Aeneas came into Italy with twenty ships, in which the remnants of the Trojans were carried, while Latinus was reigning there, and Menestheus among the Athenians, Polyphides among the Sicyonians, Tautanes among the Assyrians, and among the Hebrews the judge was Labdon. After Latinus died, Aeneas reigned three years, the same kings remaining in the places mentioned above, except that among the Sicyonians it was now Pelasgus, and the judge of the Hebrews was Samson; who, since he was wondrously strong, was thought to be Hercules.

But the Latins made Aeneas a god for themselves, because when he died he did not appear. The Sabines too enrolled among the gods their first king, Sancus—or, as some call him, Sanctus. About the same time Codrus, king of the Athenians, offered himself unrecognized to be killed by the Peloponnesians, the enemies of that same city; and it was done. In this way they proclaim that he freed his fatherland. For the Peloponnesians had received an answer that they would conquer only if they did not kill the king of the Athenians. So he deceived them by appearing in the dress of a poor man and provoking his own death by a quarrel. Whence Virgil says: *and the quarrels of Codrus*[^50]. And this man the Athenians worshiped as a god with the honor of sacrifices.

In the reign of the fourth king of the Latins, Silvius, son of Aeneas—not by Creusa, by whom was Ascanius, who reigned there third, but by Lavinia, daughter of Latinus, whom Aeneas is said to have had as a posthumous son—while Onneus was the twenty-ninth king of the Assyrians and Melanthus the sixteenth of the Athenians, and Eli the priest was judge of the Hebrews, the kingdom of the Sicyonians came to an end, which is recorded to have been prolonged for nine hundred and fifty-nine years.

## What befell from Saul to the founding of Alba.

**20.** Soon, while the same kings were reigning in the places mentioned, the kingdom of the Israelites, the time of the Judges being ended, took its beginning from King Saul, at which time Samuel was prophet[^51]. From that time, then, those kings of the Latins began to exist whom they surnamed the Silvii; for from him who, as the son of Aeneas, was first called Silvius, the rest who followed both had their own proper names imposed and did not lack this surname[^52]; just as, long afterward, those who succeeded Caesar Augustus were surnamed Caesars. But Saul having been rejected, so that none of his stock should reign, and he having died, David succeeded to the kingdom forty years after the rule of Saul began.

Then the Athenians, after the death of Codrus, ceased to have kings thereafter and began to have magistrates for the administration of the commonwealth. After David, who likewise reigned forty years[^53], his son Solomon was king of the Israelites, who built that most renowned temple of God at Jerusalem. In his time Alba was founded among the Latins, from which thenceforth the kings began to be called not kings of the Latins but kings of the Albans, in the same Latium nevertheless. Solomon was succeeded by his son Rehoboam, under whom that people was divided into two kingdoms, and each part began to have its own separate kings[^54].

## The end of the Assyrian kingdom and the beginning of the Roman occurred together.

**21.** Latium, after Aeneas, whom they made a god, had eleven kings, none of whom was made a god. But Aventinus, who follows Aeneas in the twelfth place, when he had been laid low in war and buried on that hill which even now is called by his name, was added to the number of such gods as they made for themselves. Some indeed were unwilling to write that he was killed in battle, but said that he did not appear; and that the hill was named not from his name, but called Aventine from the coming of birds. After him no god was made in Latium except Romulus, the founder of Rome. Between this one and that one two kings are found, the first of whom is—to express him in a Virgilian verse: *next to him is that Procas, glory of the Trojan race*[^55]. In his time, because Rome was already in some way being brought to birth, that greatest of all kingdoms, the Assyrian, received the end of so long a duration. For it was transferred to the Medes after nearly one thousand three hundred and five years, if even the times of Belus—who begot Ninus and, content there with a small empire, was the first king—are reckoned.

Now Procas reigned before Amulius. Further, Amulius had made his brother Numitor's daughter, named Rhea—who was also called Ilia—the mother of Romulus, a Vestal virgin, who, they would have it, conceived twins by Mars, in this way honoring or excusing her defilement, and adducing as an argument that a she-wolf nursed the exposed infants.

For they think that this kind of beast belongs to Mars, so that the she-wolf is believed to have offered its teats to the little ones, of course, because it recognized the sons of its lord Mars; although there are not lacking those who say that, when the exposed infants lay crying, they were first gathered up by some harlot or other and sucked her breasts first (now they called harlots "she-wolves," whence even now their shameful places are called "lupanaria," brothels), and that afterward they came to the shepherd Faustulus and were nursed by his wife Acca. Yet if, to convict the man, the king, who had cruelly ordered them to be thrown into the water, God willed to come to the aid of those infants—through whom so great a city was to be founded—after they had been divinely freed from the water, by means of a suckling beast, what wonder is it?

although some say that, when they lay exposed and crying, they were first picked up by some prostitute and first suckled at her breasts—for prostitutes were called “she-wolves,” whence even now their shameful establishments are called “wolf-dens”—and that they afterward came to the shepherd Faustulus and were reared by his wife Acca.

Yet if, to rebuke the human king who had cruelly ordered them to be thrown into the water, God wished to help these infants, through whom so great a city was to be founded, by means of a nursing wild beast after divinely delivering them from the water, what is surprising about that?

Amulius was succeeded in the kingdom of Latium by his brother Numitor, grandfather of Romulus, in whose—that is, Numitor's—first year Rome was founded; and so thereafter he reigned together with his grandson, that is, Romulus.

**22.** Not to delay over many things: the city of Rome was founded as another Babylon, and as it were the daughter of the former Babylon, through which it pleased God to subdue the whole world and, having brought it into one society of commonwealth and laws, to pacify it far and wide. For there were now peoples strong and brave, and nations trained in arms, who would not easily yield, and who needed to be overcome by enormous dangers and by no small devastation on both sides and by horrible toil. For when the kingdom of the Assyrians subjugated almost all of Asia, although it was done by warfare, yet it could not be done by very harsh and difficult wars, because the nations were as yet untrained to resist, and not so many or so great. Indeed, after that greatest and universal flood, when only eight men escaped in Noah's ark, not much more than a thousand years had passed when Ninus subjugated all of Asia except India. But Rome did not subdue so many nations of both the East and the West, which we see subject to the Roman empire, with that speed and ease, because, growing little by little, it found them strong and warlike, in whatever direction it spread.

At the time, then, when Rome was founded, the people of Israel had been in the land of promise seven hundred and eighteen years. Of these, twenty-seven belong to Joshua, son of Nun; then three hundred and twenty-nine to the time of the judges. And from the time when kings began to be there, the years were three hundred and sixty-two. And the king then in Judah was one whose name was Ahaz, or, as others reckon[^56], Hezekiah, who succeeded him—who, it is agreed, was a most excellent and most pious king—reigned in the times of Romulus. But in that part of the Hebrew people which was called Israel, Hoshea had begun to reign.

## What the song of the Erythraean Sibyl was.

**23.** **1.** At the same time some report that the Erythraean Sibyl prophesied. Varro relates that there were several Sibyls, not one[^57]. This Erythraean Sibyl, indeed, wrote certain plain things about Christ, which we too first read in the Latin tongue, in bad and halting verses, through the unskillfulness of some translator or other, as we afterward learned.

For the most illustrious man Flaccianus, who was also a proconsul, a man of the readiest eloquence and much learning, when we were conversing about Christ, brought out to us a Greek codex, saying that the verses were of the Erythraean Sibyl, where he showed in a certain place that the order of the letters at the heads of the verses was such that these words could be read in it: Iesous Chreistos Theou huios soter, which is in Latin: *Jesus Christ, Son of God, Savior*. Now these verses, whose first letters yield the meaning we have stated—as a certain person rendered them into Latin in regular verses—contain this:

Judgment's sign: the earth shall grow wet with sweat.

Ever shall come from heaven the King to reign through the ages,

So, present in the flesh, that he may judge the world.

Unbeliever and believer alike shall behold God,

Sublime among his saints, at the very end of the age.

Clothed with flesh shall the souls be present, whom he himself judges,

Here when the world lies untilled amid thick brambles.

Rejected by men shall be the idols, and all their treasure;

Enkindling fire shall burn up the lands, searching out the sea and the sky,

It shall break the gates of foul Avernus.

Saints, then, to all flesh shall the free light

To the guilty be given; the eternal flame shall consume them.

Opening then his hidden deeds, each one shall speak

Secrets, and God shall lay bare hearts to the light.

Then too there shall be wailing, and all shall gnash with their teeth.

Earth's sun is snatched away, and the choir of stars perishes.

Vaulting heaven shall be rolled up, the moon's splendor shall fade;

Down shall he cast the hills, and lift the valleys from below.

Nothing shall there be in human affairs lofty or high.

Instantly are the mountains leveled with the plains, and the blue depths of the sea;

Overthrown, all things shall cease, the earth shall be shattered and perish:

So likewise the springs and rivers are parched by fire.

Sound shall the trumpet then send down a mournful note from on high,

Oer the world, bewailing the wretched deed and its manifold woes;

The gaping earth shall show the chaos of Tartarus.

Every king shall be set, one and all, here before the Lord.

Raining down from heaven shall come a river of fire and brimstone[^58].

In these Latin verses, translated somehow from the Greek, that sense could not appear in those places—the sense that arises when the letters at the heads of the verses are joined—where the letter T was set in the Greek, because Latin words could not be found that began with the same letter and fit the meaning. These are three verses: the fifth, the eighteenth, and the nineteenth. Finally, if, in joining the letters at the heads of all the verses, we do not read those of these three that are written, but recall in their place the letter T, as though it stood in those same places, there is expressed in five words: *Jesus Christ, Son of God, Savior*; but it is said in Greek, not in Latin.

And there are twenty-seven verses, which number yields the solid cube of three. For three multiplied three times makes nine; and if these nine in turn are multiplied three times, so that the figure rises from breadth to height, they reach twenty-seven. Now of these five Greek words, which are Iesous Chreistos Theou huios soter—that is, in Latin, *Jesus Christ, Son of God, Savior*—if you join the first letters, the result will be *ichthys*, that is, "fish," in which name Christ is mystically understood, because he was able to be alive—that is, without sin—in the abyss of this mortality, as in the depth of the waters.

**23.** **2.** Now this Sibyl, whether the Erythraean or, as some rather believe, the Cumaean, has in her whole song—of which this is a tiny portion—nothing that pertains to the worship of false or fabricated gods; indeed, she even speaks against them and against their worshipers in such a way that she would seem to be reckoned in the number of those who belong to the city of God. There also inserts—

Lactantius into his own work certain prophecies of the Sibyl concerning Christ, though he does not specify which Sibyl. But what he set down one by one, I have thought should be set down joined together, as though it were one continuous passage, the several brief things he recounted.

*Afterward*, she says, *he will come into the wicked hands of unbelievers; and they will give God blows with unclean hands, and with defiled mouth will spit out poisoned spittle; but he will simply give his holy back to their lashes. And receiving buffets, he will keep silence, that no one may know what the Word is, or whence it comes, so that it may speak to the underworld and be crowned with a crown of thorns.*

*For food, moreover, they gave gall, and for thirst vinegar; this is the table of inhospitality they will display. For you, foolish one, did not understand your God, who plays with the minds of mortals, but crowned him with thorns and mixed dreadful gall. But the veil of the temple shall be rent; and at midday there shall be night, exceedingly dark, for three hours. And he shall die the death, sleep being undergone for three days; and then, returning from the underworld, he shall come into the light, first, the beginning of the resurrection being shown to those recalled*[^59].

These Sibylline testimonies Lactantius adduced piecemeal, through the intervals of his discussion, as the matters he intended to prove seemed to require them; we, interposing nothing but setting them connected in one series, have taken care to distinguish them by their heads only—if, that is, the scribes hereafter do not neglect to preserve them. Some indeed have written that the Erythraean Sibyl lived not in the time of Romulus, but in the time of the Trojan war.

## In the time of Romulus the kingdom of Israel was brought to an end.

**24.** While the same Romulus was reigning, Thales of Miletus is reported to have lived, one of the seven wise men who, after the theologian poets—among whom Orpheus most of all was renowned—were called *sophoi*[^60], which is in Latin "wise men." About the same time the ten tribes, which in the division of the people were called Israel, were conquered by the Chaldeans and led captive into those lands[^61], while those two tribes, which were called by the name of Judah and had the seat of the kingdom at Jerusalem, remained in the land of Judea. When Romulus died, since he too did not appear, the Romans enrolled him among the gods, as is most widely known[^62]; and this had so far ceased to be done, and afterward was done only by flattery, not by error, in the times of the Caesars, that Cicero counts it among the great praises of Romulus[^63] that he earned these honors not in rude and unlearned times, when men were easily deceived, but in times already refined and learned, although the subtle and keen loquacity of the philosophers had not yet boiled up and sprouted forth[^64].

But even if later times did not make gods of dead men, yet they did not cease to worship as gods, and to hold as gods, those instituted by the ancients; indeed, by statues, which the ancients did not have[^65], they increased the allurement of vain and impious superstition, the unclean demons in their hearts bringing this about and deceiving them through deceitful oracles as well, so that even the fabulous crimes of the gods—which in a now more cultivated age were no longer invented—were nevertheless shamefully enacted through games in homage to those same false divinities.

Numa then reigned after Romulus; and although he thought that city should assuredly be fortified by a multitude of false gods, yet he himself, when dead, did not merit to be enrolled in that same throng, as though he were thought to have so crowded heaven with a multitude of divinities that he could find no place there. While he was reigning at Rome, and among the Hebrews at the beginning of the reign of Manasseh—by which impious king the prophet Isaiah is reported to have been slain[^66]—they say there was the Samian Sibyl[^67].

**25.** When Zedekiah was reigning among the Hebrews, and Tarquinius Priscus, who had succeeded Ancus Marcius, among the Romans, the people of the Jews were led captive into Babylon, Jerusalem being overthrown and that temple built by Solomon[^68]. For the prophets, rebuking them for their iniquities and impieties, had foretold that these things would come upon them, especially Jeremiah, who even fixed the number of years[^69]. At that time Pittacus of Mitylene, another of the seven wise men, is reported to have lived. And Eusebius writes that the other five who are added to Thales, whom we mentioned above, and to this Pittacus, to make up the seven, lived at the time when the captive people of God were held in Babylon[^70]. These are they: Solon the Athenian, Chilon the Lacedaemonian, Periander the Corinthian, Cleobulus the Lindian, Bias of Priene. All these, called the seven wise men, became famous after the theologian poets, because they excelled other men in a certain praiseworthy manner of life and embraced some precepts of conduct in brief maxims. But they left no monuments, as far as letters are concerned, to posterity, except that Solon is reported to have given certain laws to the Athenians; Thales, however, was a natural philosopher and left books of his teachings. At that time of the Jewish captivity Anaximander, Anaximenes, and Xenophanes also flourished as natural philosophers. Then too Pythagoras, from whom they began to be called philosophers[^71].

For the prophets, rebuking them for their iniquities and impieties, had foretold that these things would happen to them, especially Jeremiah, who even specified the number of years[^72].

Pittacus of Mytilene, another of the seven sages, is said to have lived at that time. Eusebius writes that the other five, who are added to Thales, whom we mentioned above, and this Pittacus to make up the seven, lived when God’s people were held captive in Babylon[^73]. They are Solon of Athens, Chilon of Sparta, Periander of Corinth, Cleobulus of Lindus, and Bias of Priene. All these, called the seven sages, flourished after the theological poets, because they surpassed other men in a certain praiseworthy way of life and expressed some moral precepts in brief maxims.

They left no literary records to posterity, except that Solon is said to have given certain laws to the Athenians. Thales, however, was a natural philosopher and left books setting out his teachings. During that time of Jewish captivity, Anaximander, Anaximenes, and Xenophanes also flourished as natural philosophers, as did Pythagoras, with whom the name “philosophers” began[^74].

**26.** About the same time Cyrus, king of the Persians, who also ruled the Chaldeans and Assyrians, having somewhat relaxed the captivity of the Jews, caused fifty thousand of them to return to restore the temple[^75]. By these only the first foundations were begun and an altar built. But because enemies kept attacking, they were by no means able to proceed with the building, and the work was deferred until Darius. About the same time those things too were done that are recorded in the book of Judith, which indeed the Jews are said not to have received into the canon of the Scriptures.

Under Darius, then, king of the Persians, when the seventy years that the prophet Jeremiah had foretold were fulfilled, liberty was restored to the Jews, the captivity being ended, while Tarquin, the seventh king of the Romans, was reigning. When he was expelled, they too began to be free from the domination of their kings.

Down to this time the people of Israel had prophets; and although they were many, the canonical writings of only a few are retained, both among the Jews and among us. Of these I promised, when I was closing the previous book[^76], that I would set down some things in this book, which I now see must be done.

## At what time the writings of the prophets began.

**27.** In order, then, that we may discern their times, let us run back a little to earlier things. At the head of the book of *Hosea the prophet*, who is placed first among the twelve, it is thus written: *the word of the Lord that came to Hosea in the days of Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz, and Hezekiah, kings of Judah*[^77]. Amos too writes that he prophesied in the days of King Uzziah; he adds also Jeroboam, king of Israel, who lived in those same days[^78]. Isaiah likewise, son of Amos—whether of the aforesaid prophet or, as is more commonly held, of another who was not a prophet and was called by the same name—sets at the head of his book the same four kings whom Hosea set down, in whose days he says beforehand that he prophesied[^79]. Micah too recalls the same times of his prophecy, after the days of Uzziah. For he names the three kings who follow, whom Hosea also named: Jotham, Ahaz, and Hezekiah[^80]. These are they who are found from their writings to have prophesied together at the same time.

To these is joined Jonah, while the same Uzziah was reigning, and Joel, when Jotham, who succeeded Uzziah, was already reigning. But the times of these two prophets we have been able to find in the *Chronicles*, not in their books, since they are silent about their own days.

Now these days extend from Procas, king of the Latins, or his predecessor Aventinus, down to King Romulus, now a Roman, or even down to the beginnings of the reign of his successor Numa Pompilius; for Hezekiah, king of Judah, reigned up to that point[^81]. And so during those times these men, as it were the springs of prophecy, burst forth together, when the Assyrian kingdom failed and the Roman began; so that, just as in the first period of the kingdom of the Assyrians there arose Abraham, to whom the clearest promises were made that in his seed all nations would be blessed, so at the beginning of the western Babylon, under whose rule Christ was to come, in whom those promises would be fulfilled, the mouths of the prophets—not only speaking but also writing—would be loosed in testimony of so great a thing to come.

For although prophets had almost never been lacking to the people of Israel from the time when kings began to be there, they were for the use of those people only, not of the nations; but when prophetic Scripture was being more openly composed, which would one day profit the nations, then it had to begin when this city was being founded that would rule the nations.

**28.** Hosea the prophet, the more profoundly he speaks, the more laboriously is he penetrated. But something must be taken from him and set down here too, according to our promise. *And it shall come to pass*, he says, *that in the place where it was said to them, You are not my people, they too shall be called the sons of the living God*[^82]. This prophetic testimony about the calling of the people of the nations, who before did not pertain to God, the Apostles too understood[^83].

And because the people of the nations too is spiritually among the sons of Abraham, and therefore is rightly called Israel, on that account he goes on and says: *and the sons of Judah and the sons of Israel shall be gathered together into one, and shall set over themselves one head, and shall come up out of the land*[^84]. If we should still wish to expound this, the savor of the prophetic eloquence would be blunted. Yet let that cornerstone be recalled, and the two walls, one from the Jews, the other from the nations; the one under the name of the sons of Judah, the other under the name of the sons of Israel[^85]—leaning upon and coming up, into one, to that same single head of theirs, let them be recognized as coming up out of the land.

But that those carnal Israelites, who now refuse to believe in Christ, will afterward believe—that is, their sons (for these will surely pass to their own place by dying)—the same prophet testifies, saying: *for the sons of Israel shall sit many days without king, without prince, without sacrifice, without altar, without priesthood, without manifestations*. Who does not see that the Jews are now thus? But let us hear what he adds. *And afterward*, he says, *the sons of Israel shall return and shall seek the Lord their God and David their king, and shall be amazed at the Lord and at his good things in the last days*[^86]. Nothing is plainer than this prophecy, when by the name of David the king is understood to be signified Christ, who *was made*, as the Apostle says, *of the seed of David according to the flesh*[^87]. This prophet also foretold that Christ's resurrection would take place on the third day, as it had to be foretold with prophetic loftiness, where he says: *he will heal us after two days; on the third day we shall rise again*[^88]. For in accordance with this the Apostle says to us: *if you have risen with Christ, seek the things that are above*[^89].

Amos too prophesies about such things thus: *prepare yourself*, he says, *to call upon your God, O Israel; for behold, I am he who makes firm the thunder and creates the wind and announces among men his Christ*[^90]; and in another place: *in that day*, he says, *I will raise up the tabernacle of David that is fallen, and I will rebuild the things of it that have fallen, and I will raise up its ruins and rebuild them as in the days of old; so that the residue of men and all the nations upon whom my name is invoked may seek me, says the Lord who does these things*[^91].

**29.** **1.** Isaiah the prophet is not among the twelve prophets who are called "minor" because their discourses are short, in comparison with those who are called "major" because they composed lengthy volumes; among whom is this Isaiah, whom I join to the two aforesaid because of the same times of prophecy. Isaiah, then, among the things in which he rebukes the wicked and enjoins the just and foretells evils to come upon the sinful people, prophesied also of Christ and the Church—that is, of the King and the city he founded—far more than the rest, so that by some he was said to be an evangelist rather than a prophet.

But for the sake of bringing the work to an end, I will set down one passage out of many in this place. Speaking, indeed, in the person of God the Father: *behold*, he says, *my servant shall understand, and shall be exalted and glorified exceedingly. As many shall be amazed at you, so shall your appearance be without glory from men, and your glory without honor from men; so shall many nations marvel at him, and kings shall shut their mouths; for they to whom it was not told of him shall see, and they who have not heard shall understand. Lord, who has believed our report, and to whom is the arm of the Lord revealed? We declared before him, as a child, as a root in a thirsty land; there is no comeliness in him, nor glory. And we saw him, and he had no comeliness nor beauty; but his appearance was without honor, failing beyond all men.*

*A man set in suffering and knowing how to bear infirmity; for his face was turned away, he was dishonored and not esteemed. He bears our sins and grieves for us; and we thought him to be in grief and in suffering and in affliction. But he was wounded for our iniquities and weakened for our sins. The chastisement of our peace was upon him; by his bruise we are healed. We all wandered like sheep, each man wandered from his way; and the Lord delivered him up for our sins; and he, because he was afflicted, did not open his mouth.*

*As a sheep he was led to the slaughter, and as a lamb before its shearer is without voice, so he opened not his mouth. In his humiliation his judgment was taken away. Who shall declare his generation? For his life shall be taken from the earth. For the iniquities of my people he was led to death. And I will give the wicked for his burial and the rich for his death. For he did no iniquity, nor was there deceit in his mouth; and the Lord wills to cleanse him from his suffering. If you give your soul for sin, you shall see a long-lived seed; and the Lord wills to take away his soul from grief, to show him the light and to form his understanding, to justify the just one who serves many well; and he himself shall bear their sins. Therefore he shall inherit many and shall divide the spoils of the strong, because his soul was delivered to death, and he was reckoned among the wicked, and he himself bore the sins of many, and was delivered up for their sins*[^92]. These things concern Christ.

**29.** **2.** But now let us hear what follows concerning the Church: *rejoice*, he says, *O barren one, who do not bear; break forth and cry aloud, you who do not travail; for the children of the desolate are more than of her who has a husband. Enlarge the place of your tent and of your curtains; fix them, do not spare, lengthen your cords and strengthen your stakes, extend yet to the right and to the left. And your seed shall inherit the nations, and you shall inhabit the desolate cities.*

*Do not fear, because you were confounded, nor be ashamed, because you were reproached; for you shall forget the everlasting confusion, and you shall not remember the reproach of your widowhood. For the Lord who made you, the Lord of Sabaoth is his name*; *and he who delivered you, he himself, the God of Israel, shall be called the God of all the earth*[^93]; and the rest. But let these things be enough; and among them some require exposition; yet I think it sufficient that they are so plain that even enemies are compelled to understand them, unwilling though they be.

**30.** **1.** Micah the prophet, setting Christ under the figure of a certain great mountain, speaks thus: *in the last days the mountain of the Lord shall be manifest, prepared upon the tops of the mountains, and it shall be exalted above the hills. And peoples shall hasten to it, and many nations shall go and shall say: come, let us go up to the mountain of the Lord and to the house of the God of Jacob, and he will show us his way, and we will walk in his paths; for out of Zion shall go forth the law, and the word of the Lord out of Jerusalem. And he shall judge among many peoples, and shall rebuke mighty nations afar off*[^94].

This prophet, foretelling also the place in which Christ was born: *and you*, he says, *Bethlehem, house of Ephrathah, are little to be among the thousands of Judah; out of you shall come forth for me one to be ruler in Israel; and his going forth is from the beginning, from the days of eternity. Therefore he will give them up until the time of her who is in travail; she shall bring forth, and the rest of his brethren shall be converted to the sons of Israel. And he shall stand and shall see and shall feed his flock in the strength of the Lord, and they shall be in the honor of the name of the Lord their God; for now he shall be magnified to the ends of the earth*[^95].

**30.** **2.** Jonah the prophet, however, prophesied Christ not so much by speech as by a certain suffering of his own, indeed more plainly than if he had proclaimed his death and resurrection aloud. For why was he received into the belly of the sea-beast and given back on the third day, except to signify that Christ would return from the depth of the underworld on the third day[^96]?

**30.** **3.** Joel obliges all the things he prophesies to be expounded with many words, so that those things which pertain to Christ and the Church may become clear. Yet one thing, which the Apostles too recalled when the Holy Spirit, as had been promised by Christ, came from above upon the gathered believers, I will not pass over[^97]. *And it shall come to pass*, he says, *after these things, that I will pour out of my Spirit upon all flesh; and your sons and your daughters shall prophesy, and your elders shall dream dreams, and your young men shall see visions; and indeed upon my servants and handmaids in those days I will pour out of my Spirit*[^98].

## Obadiah speaks of the Church in Judea . . .

**31.** **1.** Three of the minor prophets—Obadiah, Nahum, Habakkuk—neither state their own times themselves, nor is it found in the Chronicles of Eusebius and Jerome when they prophesied. For Obadiah is indeed placed by them along with Micah[^99], but not in that place where the times are noted at which Micah is established from his writings to have prophesied; which I judge to have happened through the error of those who carelessly copy others' labors; the other two mentioned we could not find in the copies of the *Chronicles* that we had. Nevertheless, because they are contained in the canon, they too ought not to be passed over by us.

Obadiah, as far as his writing is concerned, the briefest of all the prophets, speaks against Idumea, that is, the nation of Esau, the elder of the two twin sons of Isaac, grandsons of Abraham, the rejected one. Further, if we take Idumea to be set, by that manner of speaking in which the whole is understood from a part, for the nations, we can recognize concerning Christ what he says among other things: *but on Mount Zion shall be salvation, and it shall be holy*[^100]; and a little later, at the end of his prophecy: *and*, he says, *the saved shall come up from Mount Zion, to defend the mount of Esau, and the kingdom shall be the Lord's*[^101].

It appears, indeed, that this was fulfilled when the saved from Mount Zion—that is, those of Judea believing in Christ, who are especially recognized as the Apostles—came up to defend the mount of Esau. How did they defend it, except by saving, through the preaching of the Gospel, those who believed, so that they might be rescued from the power of darkness and transferred into the kingdom of God?

Which he consequently expressed by adding: *and the kingdom shall be the Lord's*. For Mount Zion signifies Judea, where salvation was foretold to come, and the Holy One, who is Christ Jesus. But the mount of Esau is Idumea, by which was signified the Church of the nations, which the saved from Mount Zion defended, as I have expounded, so that the kingdom might be the Lord's. This was obscure before it came to pass; but who that is faithful does not recognize it now that it is done?

**31.** **2.** Nahum the prophet, or rather God through him: *I will destroy*, he says, *the graven and the molten images, I will make your grave; for behold, swift upon the mountains are the feet of him who brings good tidings and announces peace. Celebrate, O Judah, your feast days, pay your vows; for they shall no more pass over into oldness. It is finished, it is consumed, it is taken away. He has come up who breathes into your face, rescuing you from tribulation*[^102]. Who it was that came up from the underworld and breathed into the face of Judah—that is, of the Jewish disciples—the Holy Spirit, let him recall who remembers the Gospel. For they belong to the New Testament, whose feast days are so spiritually renewed that they cannot pass over into oldness. Moreover, we now see the graven and molten images—that is, the idols of false gods—destroyed by the Gospel and consigned, as it were, to a grave of oblivion, and we recognize that in this matter too the prophecy has been fulfilled.

**31.** **3.** Habakkuk—of whom other than the coming of Christ, which was to be, is he understood to speak: *and the Lord answered me and said: write the vision plainly upon boxwood, that he who reads it may follow on; for the vision is yet for a time, and it shall arise at the end and not in vain; if it tarry, wait for it, because it will surely come and will not delay*[^103]?

**32.** But in his prayer with a Canticle, to whom does he speak but to the Lord Christ: *Lord, I have heard your report, and I feared; Lord, I considered your works, and I was terrified?* For what is this but the ineffable wonder at the foreknown, new, and sudden salvation of men? *In the midst of two living creatures you shall be known*—what is this but either "in the midst of the two Testaments," or "in the midst of the two thieves," or "in the midst of Moses and Elijah conversing with him on the mountain"?

*As the years draw near, you shall be known; at the coming of the time you shall be shown*—this needs no exposition. *In it, when my soul shall be troubled, in wrath you will remember mercy*[^104]—what is this but that he transfigured into himself the Jews, of whose nation he was, who, troubled with great wrath, were crucifying Christ, while he, mindful of mercy, said: *Father, forgive them, for they know not what they do*[^105]?

*God will come from Teman, and the Holy One from the shady and dense mountain*[^106]. What is here said, *he will come from Teman*, others have translated *from the South* or *from the southwest*; by which is signified the midday, that is, the fervor of love and the splendor of truth. But the shady and dense mountain, although it can be understood in many ways, I would more gladly take to be the loftiness of the divine Scriptures, by which Christ was prophesied. For there are there many shady and dense things, which exercise the mind of the seeker. And from there he comes, when he finds there the one who understands. *His power covered the heavens, and the earth is full of his praise*[^107]—what is this but what is also said in the Psalm: *be exalted above the heavens, O God, and your glory above all the earth*[^108]? *His splendor shall be as the light*—what is this but: his fame will enlighten believers? *Horns are in his hands*—what is this but the trophy of the cross? *And he set firm the love of his strength*—this needs no exposition.

*Before his face shall go the word, and it shall go forth into the field after his feet*—what is this but that both before he came hither he was foretold, and after he returned hence he was announced? *He stood, and the earth was moved*—what is this but, *he stood* to bring help, *and the earth was moved* to believe?

*He looked, and the nations melted away*—that is, he had mercy and made the peoples repent. *The mountains were crushed by violence*—that is, by the miracles that did violence, the pride of the haughty was crushed. *The everlasting hills flowed down*—that is, they were humbled for a time, that they might be raised up for eternity. *I saw his everlasting ways in return for labors*—that is, I beheld the labor of love not without the reward of eternity. *The tents of the Ethiopians shall be terrified, and the tents of the land of Midian*—that is, the nations, suddenly terrified by the report of your wonders, even those that are not under Roman law, shall be among the Christian people.

*Were you angry at the rivers, O Lord, or was your fury against the rivers, or your onset against the sea?*[^109]—this was said because he came now not to judge the world, but that the world might be saved through him. *For you will mount upon your horses, and your riding is salvation*[^110]—that is, your Evangelists, who are governed by you, will carry you, and your Gospel will be salvation to those who believe in you. *Bending you will bend your bow upon the scepters, says the Lord*—that is, you will threaten your judgment even to the kings of the earth. *The earth shall be cleft with rivers*[^111]—that is, by the flowing words of those who preach you, the hearts of men shall be opened to confession, to whom it is said: *rend your hearts and not your garments*[^112].

What is: *the peoples shall see you and grieve*, but that by mourning they may be blessed? What is: *scattering the waters as you pass*, unless: by walking in those who announce you everywhere, you scatter the streams of doctrine this way and that? What is: *the abyss gave its voice*, unless the depth of the human heart expressed what seemed to it?

*The height of its imagining* is, as it were, the exposition of the previous verse; for *height* is "abyss." But when he says *of its imagining*, it must be understood, *it gave its voice*; that is, what we said, "it expressed what seemed to it." For *imagining* is a vision, which it did not keep, did not cover, but uttered forth by confessing. *The sun was raised up, and the moon stood in its order*—that is, Christ ascended into heaven, and the Church was ordered under her King. *Into the light your darts shall go*—that is, not into the hidden, but into the manifest your words shall be sent.

*Into the splendor of the flashing of your arms*[^113]—it must be understood, *your darts shall go*. For he had said to his own: *what I tell you in the darkness, speak in the light*[^114]. *In threatening you will lessen the earth*—that is, by threatening you will humble men. *And in fury you will cast down the nations*—because those who exalt themselves you will dash to pieces in punishing. *You went forth for the salvation of your people, to save your anointed; you sent death upon the heads of the wicked*—none of these things needs exposition.

*You raised up bonds even to the neck*. And here can be understood the good bonds of wisdom, that the feet may be put into her fetters and the neck into her collar[^115]. *You cut them off in amazement of mind*[^116]—we understand *the bonds*; for he raised up the good and cut off the evil, of which it is said to him: *you have broken my bonds*[^117], and this *in amazement of mind*, that is, wondrously.

*The heads of the mighty shall be moved in it*—in that amazement, namely. *They shall open their jaws, like a poor man eating in secret*[^118]. For certain mighty men of the Jews came to the Lord, marveling at his deeds and words, and, hungering, ate the bread of doctrine in secret, for fear of the Jews, as the Gospel disclosed them[^119].

*And you sent your horses into the sea, troubling many waters*, which are nothing else than many peoples; for some would not be converted by fear and others persecute in fury, unless all were troubled. *I watched, and my belly trembled at the voice of the prayer of my lips; and trembling entered into my bones, and my disposition was troubled beneath me*. He attended to the things he was saying, and was terrified by his own prayer, which he poured forth prophetically and in which he beheld the things to come; for, the many peoples being troubled, he saw the tribulations threatening the Church;

and at once he recognized himself a member of it and said: *I will rest in the day of tribulation*[^120], as belonging to those who are rejoicing in hope, patient in tribulation[^121]. *That I may go up*, he says, *to the people of my pilgrimage*—withdrawing, surely, from the wicked people of his own carnal kindred, who do not make pilgrimage on this earth nor seek the heavenly fatherland. *For the fig tree*, he says, *shall not bear fruit, and there shall be no produce in the vines; the labor of the olive shall fail, and the fields shall not yield food. The sheep have failed for food, and there are no oxen left in the stalls*[^122]. He saw that this nation, which was to slay Christ, would lose the abundance of spiritual riches, which he figured, after the prophetic manner, by earthly fertility.

And because that nation suffered such wrath of God for this reason, that, being ignorant of the righteousness of God, it wished to establish its own[^123], he at once: *but I*, he says, *will exult in the Lord, I will rejoice in God my Savior. The Lord my God is my strength, he will set my feet to perfection; upon the heights he will place me, that I may conquer in his song*[^124]—in that song, namely, of which certain similar things are said in the Psalm: *he set my feet upon a rock and directed my steps, and he put into my mouth a new song, a hymn to our God*[^125].

He, then, conquers in the Lord's song who is pleasing in his praise, not his own, so that *he who glories may glory in the Lord*[^126]. But it seems to me that certain copies have better: *I will rejoice in God my Jesus*, than those which, wishing to put it in Latin, did not put down the very name that is dearer and sweeter for us to name.

## Jeremiah foretold that Christ would call the nations.

**33.** **1.** Jeremiah the prophet is among the major, like Isaiah, not among the minor, like the rest, some of whose writings I have already set down. He prophesied while Josiah was reigning in Jerusalem and Ancus Marcius among the Romans, the captivity of the Jews now drawing near. He extended his prophecy, however, to the fifth month of the captivity, as we find in his writings[^127]. Zephaniah, one of the minor prophets, is joined to him. For he too says that he prophesied in the days of Josiah; but up to what point, he does not say[^128]. Jeremiah, then, prophesied not only in the times of Ancus Marcius, but also in those of Tarquinius Priscus, whom the Romans had as their fifth king. For he had already begun to reign when that captivity took place.

Prophesying, then, of Christ, Jeremiah says: *the spirit of our mouth, Christ the Lord, was taken captive in our sins*[^129], thus briefly showing both our Lord Christ and that he suffered for us. Likewise in another place: *this is my God*, he says, *and no other shall be reckoned beside him; who found the whole way of prudence and gave it to Jacob his servant and to Israel his beloved; after this he was seen on the earth and conversed with men*[^130]. This testimony some attribute not to Jeremiah but to his scribe, who was called Baruch; but it is more commonly held to be Jeremiah's.

Again the same prophet says of him: *behold*, he says, *the days are coming, says the Lord, and I will raise up for David a righteous branch, and a king shall reign and shall be wise and shall do judgment and justice in the land. In those days Judah shall be saved, and Israel shall dwell confidently; and this is the name they shall call him: the Lord our righteous one*[^131]. Of the calling of the nations, too, which was to come and which we now see fulfilled, he spoke thus: *O Lord my God and my refuge in the day of evils, to you the nations shall come from the ends of the earth and shall say: truly our fathers worshiped lies, idols, and there is no profit in them*[^132]. But because the Jews were not going to recognize him—by whom he also had to be slain—the same prophet signifies thus: *the heart is heavy in all, and he is a man, and who shall recognize him?*[^133] His also is that passage which I set down in the seventeenth book, concerning the New Testament, of which Christ is the mediator[^134]. For Jeremiah himself says: *behold, the days are coming, says the Lord, and I will complete over the house of Jacob a New Testament*, and the rest that is read there[^135].

**33.** **2.** But of Zephaniah the prophet, who prophesied with Jeremiah, I will for the present set down these things foretold concerning Christ: *wait for me, says the Lord, in the day of my resurrection in time to come; for my judgment is to gather the nations and to assemble the kingdoms*[^136]. And again: *terrible*, he says, *is the Lord over them, and he will destroy all the gods of the earth, and each man from his own place shall adore him, all the islands of the nations*[^137].

And a little later: *then*, he says, *I will turn upon the peoples a tongue, and its offspring, that all may call upon the name of the Lord and serve him under one yoke; from the ends of the rivers of Ethiopia they shall bring offerings to me. In that day you shall not be confounded by all your devices, which you wickedly did against me; for then I will take from you the depravities of your insolence; and you shall no more add to magnify yourself upon my holy mountain, and I will leave remaining in you a meek and humble people; and those who shall be left of Israel shall fear the name of the Lord*[^138]. These are the remnant, of which it is prophesied elsewhere, which the Apostle also recalls: *though the number of the sons of Israel be as the sand of the sea, a remnant shall be saved*[^139]. For this remnant of that nation believed in Christ.

**34.** **1.** Now in the captivity of Babylon itself, Daniel and Ezekiel prophesied earlier, two others, namely, of the major prophets[^140]. Of these, Daniel even fixed by the number of years the time at which Christ was to come and to suffer; which it is too long to show by reckoning, and which has been done by others before us. But of his power and the Church he spoke thus: *I beheld*, he says, *in a vision of the night, and behold, one coming with the clouds of heaven as a son of man, and he came even to the ancient of days, and he was brought into his sight; and to him was given dominion and honor and a kingdom, and all peoples, tribes, and tongues shall serve him. His power is a perpetual power, which shall not pass away, and his kingdom shall not be destroyed*[^141].

**34.** **2.** Ezekiel too, after the prophetic manner signifying Christ by David, because he took flesh from the seed of David[^142]—on account of which form of a servant, by which he was made man, the same Son of God is also called the servant of God—thus prophetically foretells him in the person of God the Father: *and I will raise up*, he says, *over my flocks one shepherd to feed them, my servant David; and he shall feed them, and he shall be to them a shepherd. But I the Lord will be to them their God, and my servant David a prince in the midst of them; I the Lord have spoken*[^143].

And in another place: *and one king*, he says, *shall rule over all; and there shall no more be two nations, nor shall they be divided any longer into two kingdoms; neither shall they be defiled any more with their idols and abominations and with all their iniquities. And I will save them from all their dwellings in which they sinned, and I will cleanse them; and they shall be to me a people, and I will be to them God; and my servant David shall be king over them, and there shall be one shepherd of them all*[^144].

## Haggai foresaw the One desired by the nations.

**35.** **1.** There remain three minor prophets who prophesied at the end of the captivity: Haggai, Zechariah, Malachi[^145]. Of these, Haggai prophesies Christ and the Church with this rather plain brevity: *thus says the Lord of hosts: yet one little while, and I will shake heaven and earth and the sea and the dry land, and I will move all the nations, and the desired of all the nations shall come*[^146].

This prophecy is seen partly already fulfilled, partly hoped to be fulfilled at the end. For he moved heaven by the testimony of angels and stars, when Christ was incarnate[^147]; he moved earth by the mighty miracle of the very birth from a virgin[^148]; he moved the sea and the dry land, when Christ is announced both in the islands and in the whole world: thus we see all the nations being moved to faith. But now what follows: *and the desired of all the nations shall come*, is awaited at his last coming. For, in order that he might be desired by those who awaited him, it was first necessary that he be loved by those who believed.

**35.** **2.** Zechariah of Christ and the Church: *rejoice*, he says, *greatly, O daughter of Zion, shout, O daughter of Jerusalem; behold, your king comes to you, just and a savior; he is poor and riding upon an ass and upon a colt the foal of an ass; and his power is from sea to sea and from the rivers to the ends of the earth*[^149]. When this came to pass—that the Lord Christ used on his journey a beast of burden of this kind—is read in the Gospel, where this prophecy too is recalled in part, as much as seemed sufficient for that place[^150]. In another place, speaking in the spirit of prophecy to Christ himself, of the remission of sins through his blood: *you too*, he says, *in the blood of your testament have sent forth your prisoners out of the pit in which there is no water*[^151]. What he wishes to be understood by this pit may be perceived in various ways, even according to the right faith. To me, however, it seems to be signified by nothing better than the depth of human misery, dry, as it were, and barren, where there are no streams of righteousness, but the mire of iniquity. For of this it is also said in the Psalm: *and he brought me out of the pit of misery and out of the miry clay*[^152].

**35.** **3.** Malachi, prophesying the Church, which we see propagated through Christ, says most plainly to the Jews in the person of God: *I have no pleasure in you, and I will not accept a gift from your hand. For from the rising of the sun even to its setting my name is great among the nations, and in every place sacrifice shall be made and a pure offering shall be offered to my name; for my name is great among the nations, says the Lord*[^153]. Since we now see this sacrifice offered to God in every place, from the rising of the sun even to its setting, through the priesthood of Christ *after the order of Melchizedek*, while the sacrifice of the Jews, to whom it was said, *I have no pleasure in you, nor will I accept a gift from your hands*, they cannot deny has ceased—why do they still await another Christ, since this, which they read prophesied and see fulfilled, could not have been fulfilled except through him?

For a little later he says of him in the person of God: *my testament with him was of life and peace, and I gave it to him, that he should fear me with fear, and reverence before the face of my name. The law of truth was in his mouth, walking with me in peace he directed his steps and turned many away from iniquity; for the lips of the priest shall keep knowledge, and they shall seek the law from his mouth; for he is the angel of the Lord almighty*[^154]. And it is no wonder that Christ Jesus is called the angel of the Lord almighty. For as he is a servant on account of the form of a servant in which he came to men, so he is an angel on account of the Gospel which he announced to men. For if we translate these Greek words, "gospel" is "good announcement" and "angel" is "messenger."

Of him, indeed, he says again: *behold, I will send my angel, and he shall look out the way before my face; and suddenly the Lord whom you seek shall come into his temple, and the angel of the testament whom you desire. Behold, he comes, says the Lord almighty; and who shall endure the day of his entrance? or who shall stand in his sight?*[^155] In this place he foretold both the first and the second coming of Christ; the first, namely, of which he says: *and suddenly he shall come into his temple*, that is, into his flesh, of which he said in the Gospel: *destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up*[^156]; but the second, where he says: *behold, he comes, says the Lord almighty, and who shall endure the day of his entrance? or who shall stand in his sight?* And in that he said: *the Lord whom you seek, and the angel of the testament whom you desire*, he signified, of course, that the Jews too, according to the Scriptures which they read, seek and desire Christ. But many of them did not recognize that the one they sought and desired had come, blinded in their hearts by their preceding deserts.

But that which he here names the testament—whether above, where he says: *my testament was with him*, or here, where he called him *the angel of the testament*—we ought without doubt to understand as the New Testament, where things eternal are promised, not the Old, where things temporal; which very many, being weak and serving the true God for the reward of such things, holding for a great matter, are troubled when they see the impious abound in them.

On account of which the same prophet, in order to distinguish the eternal blessedness of the New Testament, which will be given only to the good, from the earthly felicity of the Old, which is for the most part given even to the wicked: *you have made heavy*, he says, *your words against me, says the Lord, and you have said: in what have we spoken against you? You have said: vain is everyone who serves God, and what more, because we have kept his observances, and because we have walked as suppliants before the face of the Lord almighty? And now we call strangers blessed, and all who do wickedly are built up; and they have opposed God, and have been saved. These things they said in opposition who feared the Lord, each man to his neighbor; and the Lord took notice and heard; and he wrote a book of remembrance before himself for those who fear the Lord and reverence his name*[^157]. By this book is signified the New Testament.

Finally, let us hear what follows: *and they shall be mine, says the Lord almighty, in the day which I make for a possession, and I will choose them as a man chooses his son who serves him; and you shall be converted, and you shall see the difference between the just and the unjust, and between him who serves God and him who serves him not. For behold, the day comes burning like an oven, and it shall consume them, and all the strangers and all who do iniquity shall be stubble, and the day that is coming shall set them on fire, says the Lord almighty; and there shall not be left of them root nor branch. And to you who fear my name shall arise the sun of righteousness, and healing in his wings; and you shall go forth and exult like calves loosed from their bonds; and you shall trample upon the wicked, and they shall be ashes under your feet in the day which I make, says the Lord almighty*[^158]. This is what is called the day of judgment; of which, if God wills, we will speak more fully in its place[^159].

**36.** After these three prophets, Haggai, Zechariah, Malachi, during the same time of the people's liberation from the slavery of Babylon, Ezra too wrote, who is held to be a writer of deeds rather than a prophet; as is also the book called *Esther*, whose deed, to the praise of God, is found not far from these times; unless perhaps Ezra is to be understood to have prophesied Christ in this, that, when a question had arisen among certain young men as to what had greater power in things, since one had said kings, another wine, a third women—who most often rule kings—yet the same third one showed truth to be victorious over all[^160]. And on consulting the Gospel we recognize that Christ is the truth.

From this time among the Jews, the temple being restored, there were not kings but princes, down to Aristobulus[^161]; the reckoning of whose times is found not in the holy Scriptures, which are called canonical, but in others, among which are also the books of the *Maccabees*, which not the Jews but the Church holds as canonical, on account of the vehement and wondrous sufferings of certain martyrs who, before Christ had come in the flesh, struggled even to death for the law of God and endured most grievous and horrible evils[^162].

## The prophets arose before the philosophers.

**37.** In the time, then, of our prophets, whose writings have now come to the knowledge of almost all nations, and much more after them, lived the philosophers of the nations, who were called by this very name—which began with Pythagoras of Samos, who at the time when the captivity of the Jews was ended[^163] began to be eminent and known. Much more, then, are the rest of the philosophers found to have lived after the prophets. For Socrates the Athenian himself, the teacher of all who then most flourished, holding the first place in that part which is called moral or active, is found in the Chronicles after Ezra[^164]. Not long after, Plato too was born, who far surpassed the other disciples of Socrates.

And if to these we add even their predecessors, who were not yet called philosophers—namely the seven wise men and then the natural philosophers who succeeded Thales in the study of investigating the nature of things, imitating his pursuit: namely Anaximander, Anaximenes, Anaxagoras, and several others, before Pythagoras was the first to profess himself a philosopher—not even these precede all our prophets in antiquity of time, since indeed Thales, after whom the rest lived, is reported to have been eminent in the reign of Romulus, when from the springs of Israel the river of prophecy burst forth in those writings that would flow over the whole world. Therefore those theologian poets alone, Orpheus, Linus, Musaeus, and any other there was among the Greeks, are found to be earlier in years than these Hebrew prophets whose writings we hold in authority[^165].

But not even these preceded in time our true theologian Moses, who truly proclaimed the one true God, and whose writings are now first in the canon of authority; and so, as far as the Greeks are concerned, in whose language the literature of this world chiefly flourished, they have nothing wherein to boast of their wisdom, by which our religion, where true wisdom is, would seem, if not superior, at least more ancient. But—and this must be admitted—not indeed in Greece, but among barbarous nations, as in Egypt, there had already been before Moses some doctrine that might be called their wisdom; otherwise it would not be written in the holy Books that *Moses was learned in all the wisdom of the Egyptians*, at the time, surely, when, born there and adopted and nourished by Pharaoh's daughter, he was also liberally educated[^166]. But not even the wisdom of the Egyptians could precede in time the wisdom of our prophets, since indeed Abraham too was a prophet[^167]. And what wisdom could there be in Egypt before Isis—whom they thought, when dead, should be worshiped as a great goddess—handed letters down to them? Isis, moreover, is reported to have been the daughter of Inachus, who first began to reign over the Argives, when Abraham's grandsons are already found to have arisen[^168].

**38.** But now, if I should go back to far more ancient things, even before that great flood there was assuredly our patriarch Noah, whom I would not undeservedly call a prophet too; since indeed the very ark that he made, and in which he escaped with his own, was a prophecy of our times. What of Enoch, the seventh from Adam—is he not proclaimed to have prophesied even in the canonical *epistle* of the apostle *Jude*[^169]?

That the writings of these men should not be held in authority, both among the Jews and among us, their excessive antiquity brought about, on account of which they seemed they should be regarded as suspect, lest false things be brought forward for true. For there are even brought forward certain things said to be theirs by those who, according to their own opinion, believe at random what they wish. But the purity of the canon did not receive these, not because the authority of those men who pleased God is rejected, but because these things are not believed to be theirs.

Nor ought it to seem a wonder that those things are held suspect which are brought forward under the name of so great an antiquity; since indeed in the very history of the kings of Judah and the kings of Israel, which contains the deeds about which we believe the same canonical Scripture, very many things are mentioned which are not there explained and are said to be found in other books which the prophets wrote, and in some places the names of those prophets too are not passed over in silence[^170]; and yet they are not found in the canon which the people of God received.

The cause of this, I confess, is hidden from me, except that I think that even those very men to whom the Holy Spirit was surely revealing the things that ought to be in the authority of religion could write some things as men, by historical diligence, and others as prophets, by divine inspiration, and that these were so distinguished that the former were judged should be attributed, as it were, to themselves, but the latter, as it were, to God speaking through them,

and thus the former pertained to the abundance of knowledge, the latter to the authority of religion, in which authority the canon is guarded; beyond which, if now any writings even under the name of true prophets are brought forward, they have no value even for the store of knowledge, since it is uncertain whether they are theirs whose they are said to be; and for this reason no faith is given to them—especially to those in which certain things are read even against the faith of the canonical books, on account of which it plainly appears that they are not theirs at all.

**39.** It is therefore not to be believed, as some suppose, that only the Hebrew tongue was preserved through him who was called Heber—whence the word "Hebrews" comes—and thence reached Abraham, and that Hebrew letters began from the law that was given through Moses; but rather that through that succession of fathers the said tongue, with its letters, was guarded. Finally, Moses set up among the people men to be in charge of teaching letters, before they knew any letters of the divine law. These Scripture calls *grammatoeisagogous*, who may be called in Latin "inducers" or "introducers" of letters, because they induce them, that is, in a manner introduce them into the hearts of learners, or rather introduce the learners themselves into them.

No nation, therefore, may boast with any vanity of the antiquity of its wisdom above our patriarchs and prophets, in whom was divine wisdom, since not even Egypt—which is wont falsely and emptily to glory in the antiquity of its teachings—is found, by whatever wisdom of its own, to have preceded in time the wisdom of our patriarchs. For no one will dare to say that they were most skilled in wondrous disciplines before they knew letters—that is, before Isis had come there and taught them there. And that memorable doctrine of theirs, which was called wisdom, what was it but chiefly astronomy and whatever else of such disciplines is wont to avail more for exercising the wits than for enlightening minds with true wisdom?

For as concerns philosophy, which professes to teach something whereby men become blessed, studies of this kind flourished in those lands about the times of Mercury, whom they called Trismegistus—long indeed before the wise men or philosophers of Greece, but yet after Abraham and Isaac and Jacob and Joseph, and indeed after Moses himself[^171]. For at the time when Moses was born, that great Atlas, the astronomer, brother of Prometheus and maternal grandfather of the elder Mercury, is found to have lived, whose grandson was this Mercury Trismegistus[^172].

**40.** Vainly, therefore, do certain men prate with most empty presumption, saying that from the time when Egypt grasped the reckoning of the stars more than a hundred thousand years are counted. In what books did they gather this number, who learned letters not much more than two thousand years before, with Isis as their teacher? For Varro is no small authority in history, who reported this, which is not at variance with the truth of the divine letters too[^173]. For since from the very first man, who was called Adam, not yet six thousand years are completed, how are these men not rather to be laughed at than refuted, who try to persuade us of things so divergent, concerning the span of times, and so contrary to this established truth?

For whom do we better believe, as one narrating things past, than him who also foretold things future, which we now see present? For the very discordance of the historians among themselves affords us occasion that we ought rather to believe him who is not at odds with the divine history, which we hold. Moreover, the citizens of the impious city, scattered everywhere through the lands, when they read most learned men, the authority of none of whom would seem to be despised, disagreeing among themselves about deeds done most remotely from the memory of our age, do not find whom they ought rather to believe.

But we, supported in the history of our religion by divine authority, do not doubt that whatever resists it is most false, however the other matters in secular literature stand—which, whether true or false, bring nothing of moment whereby we may live rightly and blessedly.

## In the schools of the philosophers, disputation prevailed.

**41.** **1.** But, to set aside now the knowledge of history, the philosophers themselves, from whom we have advanced to these matters, who seem to have labored in their studies for no other end than to find how life should be ordered for attaining blessedness—why did they disagree, both disciples from their masters and fellow disciples among themselves, except because, as men, they sought these things by human senses and human reasonings?

Now although there could here have been also the zeal for boasting, by which each desires to seem wiser and keener than another and not, as it were, bound to another's opinion, but the discoverer of his own teaching and view—yet, even granting that there were some, or even very many, of them whom love of truth tore away from their teachers or fellow students, so that they might contend for that which they thought to be truth, whether it was so or not: what does human unhappiness do, or whither, or by what way does it stretch itself out so as to attain blessedness, if divine authority does not lead it?

Indeed, far be it that our authors, in whom the canon of the sacred Letters is fixed and bounded not in vain, should disagree among themselves in any way. Whence not undeservedly, when they wrote those things, that God spoke to them, or through them, so many and so great peoples believed—not a few prattlers in schools and gymnasiums with contentious disputations, but in fields and cities, the learned and the unlearned.

They themselves, indeed, had to be few, lest by multitude that should grow cheap which by religion ought to be precious; yet not so few that their agreement is not to be marveled at. For in the multitude of philosophers who, by literary labor too, left monuments of their teachings, one would not easily find any among whom all that they thought agrees; which it is too long to show in this work[^174].

For among the multitude of philosophers who committed the record of their teachings to writing, one would scarcely find any who agree in everything they thought; to demonstrate this would take too long in this work[^175].

**41.** **2.** But what founder of any sect whatever was so approved in this demon-worshiping city that the rest were disapproved who thought differently and contrarily? Were not the Epicureans famous at Athens, asserting that human affairs do not pertain to the care of the gods, and the Stoics, who, thinking the contrary, argued that they are ruled and guarded by gods as helpers and protectors? Whence I wonder why Anaxagoras was made a defendant because he said the sun was a burning stone, denying it surely to be a god[^176], while in the same city Epicurus flourished in glory and lived secure, not only not believing the sun or any of the stars to be a god, but contending that neither Jupiter nor any of the gods dwells at all in the world, to whom the prayers and supplications of men may come[^177].

Did not Aristippus there, placing the highest good in bodily pleasure, and Antisthenes there, asserting rather that a man becomes blessed by virtue of the mind—two noble philosophers and both Socratics, locating the highest end of life in ends so diverse and contrary to each other, of whom the one said that the commonwealth should be shunned by the wise man, the other that it should be administered by him[^178]—each gather disciples to follow his own sect?

Surely, openly in the conspicuous and most well-known portico, in the gymnasiums, in the little gardens, in public and private places, they contended in throngs, each for his own opinion: some asserting one world, others innumerable worlds; and of the one world itself, some that it had a beginning, others that it had no beginning; some that it would perish, others that it would always be; some that it is driven by a divine mind, others by chance and accidents;

some that souls are immortal, others that they are mortal; and of those who held them immortal, some that they pass over into beasts, others by no means; but of those who held them mortal, some that they perish soon after the body, others that they live even afterward, either a little while or longer, yet not forever; some placing the end of good in the body, others in the mind, others in both, others adding to mind and body even goods placed outside; some thinking that the bodily senses are always to be believed, others not always, others never.

some locating the supreme good in the body, others in the soul, others in both, and others adding external goods to those of soul and body;

some thinking that the bodily senses should always be trusted, others that they should not always be trusted, and others that they should never be trusted.

These and other almost innumerable disagreements of the philosophers—what people, what senate, what public power or dignity of the impious city ever took care to judge them, approving and receiving some, disapproving and rejecting others, instead of holding indiscriminately, without any judgment and confusedly, in its bosom so many controversies of men disagreeing, not about fields and houses or any monetary matter, but about those things by which one lives either wretchedly or blessedly?

Where, even if some true things were said, with the same license false things too were said, so that not in vain did such a city receive the mystical name of "Babylon." For Babylon is interpreted "confusion," which we remember we have already said[^179]. Nor does it matter to the devil, its king, by what errors contrary to one another they wrangle, whom he deservedly possesses alike in their manifold and varied impiety.

**41.** **3.** But that nation, that people, that city, that commonwealth, those Israelites to whom were entrusted the oracles of God, in no way confounded false prophets with true prophets by an equal license, but, in agreement among themselves and disagreeing in nothing, recognized and held the truthful authors of the sacred Letters. These were for them their philosophers—that is, lovers of wisdom—their wise men, their theologians, their prophets, their teachers of uprightness and piety. Whoever was wise and lived according to them was wise and lived not according to men but according to God, who spoke through them. There, if sacrilege was forbidden, God forbade it. If it was said: *honor your father and your mother*, God commanded it. If it was said: *you shall not commit adultery, you shall not commit murder, you shall not steal*[^180], and the rest of this kind, not human mouths but divine oracles poured these forth.

Whatever certain philosophers, amid the false things they held, were able to see as true, and strove by laborious disputations to persuade men of—that God made this world and himself most providently governs it; concerning the honorableness of the virtues, the love of country, the fidelity of friendship, good works, and all matters pertaining to upright conduct, though not knowing to what end and in what manner all these things were to be referred[^181]—was commended to the people in that city by prophetic, that is, divine, voices, though through men, not driven home by the contentions of argumentations, so that whoever came to know it might be afraid to despise not the wit of man, but the eloquence of God.

## The Seventy translated the Scriptures.

**42.** These sacred Letters even one of the Ptolemies, kings of Egypt, was eager to know and to have. For after the most magnificent and least long-lasting power of Alexander of Macedon, who was also surnamed "the Great," by which he had subdued all Asia—indeed almost the whole world—partly by force and arms, partly by terror, when among other parts of the East he also entered and held Judea[^182]; when he died and his companions, having divided that most ample kingdom not to possess it peacefully among themselves but rather having broken it up, laying everything waste with wars, Egypt began to have the Ptolemies as kings; the first of whom, son of Lagus, transferred many captives from Judea into Egypt[^183]. But another Ptolemy succeeding him, who was called Philadelphus, permitted all whom that one had brought subjugated to return free; moreover, he sent royal gifts to the temple of God and asked from Eleazar, then high priest, that the Scriptures be given to him, which assuredly he had heard, by report's proclaiming, to be divine, and therefore had desired to have in the library, which he had made most renowned[^184]. When the same high priest had sent them to him in Hebrew, afterward he also requested translators; and there were given seventy-two, from each of the twelve tribes six men, most learned in both languages, namely Hebrew and Greek, whose translation custom has now obtained should be called the Septuagint.

It is indeed handed down that there was in their words an agreement so wonderful and astounding and plainly divine that, although they sat separately, each one, at this work (for thus it pleased Ptolemy to test their fidelity), in no word that meant the same and was of equal value, nor in the order of words, did one differ from another; but, as though there were one translator, so what they all translated was one; since in truth the Spirit was one in them all.

And therefore they had received so wonderful a gift of God, that the authority of those Scriptures might be commended in this way too, not as human but, as they were, as divine—to be of profit one day to the nations who would believe, which we now see accomplished.

**43.** For although there have been other translators too who rendered those sacred oracles from the Hebrew tongue into Greek, such as Aquila, Symmachus, Theodotion; as is also that translation whose author does not appear and which on this account, without the name of a translator, is called the "fifth" edition: yet this one, which is the Septuagint, the Church received as though it were the only one, and the Greek Christian peoples use it, most of whom do not know whether there is any other. From this Septuagint translation a rendering was made into the Latin tongue too, which the Latin Churches hold; although in our times there was not lacking the presbyter Jerome, a most learned man and skilled in all three languages, who translated those same Scriptures into Latin speech not from the Greek but from the Hebrew.

But although the Jews admit that his so learned labor is truthful, and contend that the Seventy translators erred in many things: yet the Churches of Christ judge that no one is to be preferred to the authority of so many men, chosen by Eleazar, then high priest, for this great work alone;

because, even if there had not appeared in them one Spirit without doubt divine, but the seventy learned men had, after the manner of men, compared among themselves the words of their translation, so that what pleased all might stand, no one translator ought to have been set before them; but since so great a sign of divinity appeared in them, surely whatever other truthful translator of those Scriptures there is from the Hebrew into any other language whatsoever, either agrees with those Seventy translators, or, if he does not seem to agree, a prophetic loftiness is to be believed there.

For the Spirit who was in the prophets when they said those things was the very same who was also in the seventy men when they translated them; who surely, by divine authority, could say something else as well, as though that prophet had said both, because the same Spirit said both, and could say this very thing in another way, so that, if not the same words, yet the same sense might shine forth to those who understand well, and could pass over something and add something, so that even from this it might be shown that there was in that work not a human servitude, which the translator owed to the words, but rather a divine power, which filled and governed the translator's mind.

But some have thought that the Greek copies of the Septuagint translation should be corrected from the Hebrew copies; yet they did not dare to remove what the Hebrews did not have and the Septuagint set down, but only added the things found in the Hebrew that were not in the Septuagint, and noted them, by certain marks made in the manner of stars, at the heads of those same verses, which marks they call asterisks. But those things which the Hebrews do not have, and the Septuagint do have, they similarly marked at the heads of the verses with little dashes lying flat, as ounces are written. And many copies having these marks are diffused everywhere, the Latin ones too.

But those things which are neither omitted nor added, but said differently—whether they make another sense not itself abhorrent, or are shown to explain the same sense in another way—cannot be found unless both copies are inspected. If, then, as we ought, we look in those Scriptures at nothing else but what the Spirit of God said through men, whatever is in the Hebrew copies and is not in the Septuagint translators, the Spirit of God did not will to say through these but through those prophets.

But whatever is in the Septuagint and is not in the Hebrew copies, the same Spirit preferred to say through these rather than through those, thus showing that both were prophets.

For in this way he said some things through Isaiah, some through Jeremiah, some through one and another prophet, or the same things differently through this one and that one, as he willed.

Moreover, whatever is found in both, one and the same Spirit willed to say through both; but in such a way that the former went before by prophesying, the latter followed by prophetically translating them; because, just as in the former, who said true and concordant things, there was one Spirit of peace, so also in the latter, who did not confer among themselves and yet translated everything as though with one mouth, the same one Spirit appeared.

**44.** But someone says: "How shall I know what Jonah the prophet said to the Ninevites, whether: *three days, and Nineveh shall be overthrown*, or: *forty days*[^185]? For who does not see that both could not then have been said by the prophet, who had been sent to terrify the city by the threat of imminent destruction? For if its destruction was to be on the third day, then surely not on the fortieth day; but if on the fortieth, then surely not on the third."

If, then, I am asked which of these Jonah said, I think it rather to be what is read in the Hebrew: *forty days, and Nineveh shall be overthrown*. For the Septuagint, translating much later, could say something else, which nevertheless pertained to the matter and converged into one and the same sense, though under another signification, and would admonish the reader, not despising either authority, to raise himself above the history to seek out those things for whose signifying the history itself was written.

For those things were indeed done in the city of Nineveh, but they also signified something that exceeds the measure of that city; just as it was done that the prophet himself was in the belly of the sea-beast for three days[^186], and yet he signified another who would be in the depth of the underworld for three days, who is the Lord of all the prophets[^187].

Wherefore, if by that city is rightly understood the Church of the nations prophetically figured—overthrown, namely, through repentance, so that what it had been it now was not, since this was done through Christ in the Church of the nations, of which that Nineveh bore the figure—whether by forty days or by three days the same Christ was signified; by forty, namely, because he spent so many days with his disciples after the resurrection and ascended into heaven; but by three, because he rose again on the third day;

as though the Septuagint translators, themselves also prophets, had roused from sleep the reader desiring to cling to nothing else than the history of the deeds done, to scrutinize the loftiness of prophecy, and had said, as it were: "In forty days seek him in whom you can also find the three days; the former you will find in the ascension, the latter in his resurrection." On account of which it could most fittingly be signified by either number, one of which one and the same Spirit said through Jonah the prophet, the other through the prophecy of the Septuagint translators.

I avoid length, so as not to demonstrate this through the many cases in which the Septuagint translators are thought to differ from the Hebrew truth and, well understood, are found to be in agreement. Whence I too, in my own small measure, following the footsteps of the Apostles—because they too set down prophetic testimonies from both, that is, from the Hebrew and from the Septuagint—have thought that both authorities should be used, since both are one and divine. But now let us pursue what remains, as we can.

## The Church is the temple of the Lord.

**45.** **1.** After the Jewish nation began to have no prophets, without doubt it became worse, at that very time, namely, when it hoped that, the temple being restored after the captivity that was in Babylon, it would be better. For so that carnal people understood what was foretold through Haggai the prophet, saying: *great shall be the glory of this latest house, more than of the first*[^188]; which he showed a little above was said of the New Testament, where he says plainly, promising Christ: *and I will move all the nations, and the desired of all the nations shall come*[^189].

In which place the Septuagint translators, by prophetic authority, gave another sense more fitting to the body than to the head—that is, more to the Church than to Christ: *the things that are chosen of the Lord from all the nations shall come*, that is, the men of whom Jesus himself says in the Gospel: *many*, he says, *are called, but few are chosen*[^190]. For of such chosen ones of the nations the house of God is built up through the New Testament with living stones, far more glorious than that temple was which was constructed by King Solomon and restored after the captivity. On this account, then, that nation had no prophets from that time, and was afflicted with many disasters by foreign kings and by the Romans themselves, lest it should suppose this prophecy of Haggai to have been fulfilled in that restoration of the temple.

**45.** **2.** For not long after, when Alexander came, it was subjugated, when, even though no devastation was made—because they did not dare to resist him and therefore, being subdued, received him most easily appeased—yet there was not so great a glory of that house as there was under the free power of their own kings.

Alexander, indeed, offered victims in the temple of God, not turned to his worship by true piety, but, by impious vanity, thinking he should be worshiped along with the false gods[^191]. Then Ptolemy, son of Lagus, as I mentioned above, after the death of Alexander transferred captives from there into Egypt, whom his successor Ptolemy Philadelphus most benevolently released from there; through whom it came about, as I narrated a little before, that we have the Scriptures of the Septuagint translators.

Then they were crushed by wars, which are unfolded in the *books of the Maccabees*. After these things they were captured by Ptolemy, king of Alexandria, who was called Epiphanes; then by Antiochus, king of Syria, they were compelled by many and most grievous evils to worship idols[^192], and the temple itself was filled with the sacrilegious superstitions of the nations, which, however, their most vigorous leader Judas, who was also called Maccabeus, having driven out the generals of Antiochus, cleansed from all that contamination of idolatry[^193].

**45.** **3.** But not long after, a certain Alcimus, through ambition, although he was a stranger to the priestly family—which was unlawful—was made high priest[^194]. From this point, after about fifty years, in which, however, there was no peace for them, although they accomplished some things even prosperously, Aristobulus first among them, having assumed the diadem, was made both king and high priest. For before, from the time when they returned from the captivity of Babylon and the temple was restored, they had not kings but leaders or princes; although one who is a king can also be called a prince, from the headship of command, and a leader, because he is the leader of an army; but not for that reason can all who are princes or leaders also be called kings, which this Aristobulus was.

He was succeeded by Alexander, himself too king and high priest, who is reported to have reigned cruelly over his own people. After him his wife Alexandra was queen of the Jews, from whose time thereafter graver evils followed them. For the sons of this Alexandra, Aristobulus and Hyrcanus, contending between themselves over the rule, called forth the Roman forces against the Israelite nation. For Hyrcanus asked from them aid against his brother.

By then Rome had already subjugated Africa, had subjugated Greece, and, ruling widely over other parts of the world too, as though unable to bear itself, had in a manner broken itself by its own greatness. For it had come to grave domestic seditions and thence to the Social Wars and soon the civil wars, and had so diminished and worn itself away that the changing of the form of the commonwealth, whereby it should be ruled by kings, threatened it.

Pompey, then, the most illustrious leader of the Roman people, entering Judea with an army, captures the city, opens the temple—not by the devotion of a suppliant, but by the right of a victor—and to the holy of holies, where none but the high priest was permitted to enter, he approaches not as a worshiper but as a profaner; and, having confirmed the pontificate of Hyrcanus and imposed upon the subjugated nation a guardian, Antipater—such as they then called procurators—he leads Aristobulus with him in bonds.

From that time the Jews too began to be tributaries of the Romans. Afterward Cassius also despoiled the temple. Then after a few years they even merited to have as king a foreigner, Herod, in whose reign Christ was born. For now the fullness of time had come[^195], signified by the prophetic spirit through the mouth of the patriarch Jacob, where he says: *the scepter shall not depart from Judah, nor a ruler from his loins, until he come for whom it is laid up, and he is the expectation of the nations*[^196]. There was, then, no lack of a prince of the Jews from among the Jews down to this Herod, whom they first received as a foreign king. The time, therefore, had now come that he should come for whom it was laid up, which was promised in the New Testament, that he himself should be the expectation of the nations. But it could not come to pass that the nations should await him as one to come—as we see him awaited, that he may come to render judgment in the brightness of power—unless they first believed in him, when he came to suffer judgment in the humility of patience.

## What the testimony of the dispersed Hebrews is.

**46.** While Herod, then, was reigning in Judea, but among the Romans, the form of the commonwealth being now changed, Caesar Augustus was ruling and through him the world was pacified, Christ was born, according to the preceding prophecy, in Bethlehem of Judah[^197], a man manifest from the human virgin, God hidden from God the Father. For thus the prophet had foretold: *behold, a virgin shall conceive in her womb and shall bear a son, and they shall call his name Emmanuel, which is interpreted: God with us*[^198]. And in order to commend God in himself, he did many miracles, of which the evangelical Scripture contains some, as much as seemed sufficient to proclaim him. The first of these is that he was born so wondrously; but the last, that he ascended into heaven with his body raised from the dead.

But the Jews, who killed him and refused to believe in him, because it was necessary that he die and rise again, laid waste more unhappily by the Romans and utterly uprooted from their kingdom, where foreigners already lorded it over them, and scattered through the lands (since indeed they are nowhere lacking), are by their own Scriptures a testimony to us that we have not invented the prophecies concerning Christ;

which prophecies very many of them, considering both before the passion and especially after his resurrection, believed in him, of whom it was foretold: *though the number of the sons of Israel be as the sand of the sea, a remnant shall be saved*[^199]. But the rest were blinded, of whom it was foretold: *let their table become a snare before them and a recompense and a stumbling block. Let their eyes be darkened, that they may not see; and bow down their back always*[^200]. Accordingly, since they do not believe our Scriptures, their own, which they read blind, are fulfilled in them.

Unless perhaps someone should say that the Christians invented those prophecies about Christ which are brought forward under the name of the Sibyl, or of others, if there are any, that do not pertain to the people of the Jews. For us, indeed, those suffice which are brought forward from the books of our enemies, whom we recognize, on account of this testimony which they unwillingly bear us by having and preserving those same books, to be themselves dispersed too among all the nations, wherever the Church of Christ is spread.

For a prophecy concerning this matter was set down beforehand in the Psalms, which they too read, where it is written: *my God, his mercy shall go before me; my God has shown me concerning my enemies: slay them not, lest at any time they forget your law; scatter them by your power*[^201]. God has shown, then, to the Church, in its enemies the Jews, the grace of his mercy, since, as the Apostle says, *their offense is the salvation of the nations*[^202], and therefore he did not slay them—that is, he did not destroy in them what they are as Jews—although they were conquered and oppressed by the Romans, lest, having forgotten the law of God, they should be of no avail for this testimony of which we are treating.

Therefore it would have been too little for him to say: *slay them not, lest at any time they forget your law*, unless he also added: *scatter them*; since, if with this testimony of the Scriptures they were only in their own land and not everywhere, surely the Church, which is everywhere, could not have them as witnesses, among all the nations, of the prophecies that were set down beforehand concerning Christ.

**47.** Wherefore whatever foreigner—that is, one not begotten of Israel nor received by that people into the canon of the sacred Letters—is read to have prophesied something about Christ, if it has come or shall come to our notice, can be recalled by us for good measure; not as though it were necessary, even if it were lacking, but because it is not unfitting to believe that there were, even among other nations, men to whom this mystery was revealed, and who were impelled to foretell these things too, whether they were partakers of the same grace or without it, but were taught by evil angels—whom we know to have confessed even Christ present, whom the Jews did not recognize[^203].

Nor do I think that the Jews themselves dare to contend that no one pertained to God except the Israelites, from the time when the line of Israel began to be, his elder brother being rejected. For in truth no other people was that which would properly be called the people of God; but they cannot deny that there were even among other nations certain men pertaining, not by an earthly but by a heavenly fellowship, to the true Israelites, citizens of the heavenly fatherland;

because, if they deny it, they are most easily refuted by the holy and wondrous man Job, who was neither a native nor a proselyte—that is, a newcomer—of the people of Israel, but, drawing his lineage from the Idumean nation, was born there and there died; who is so praised by the divine eloquence that, as concerns righteousness and piety, no man of his times is made equal to him[^204]. Although we do not find his times in the *Chronicles*, yet we gather from his book—which the Israelites, on account of its merit, received into canonical authority—that he was three generations later than Israel.

But I do not doubt that it was divinely provided, that from this one man we might know that even among other nations there could be those who lived according to God and pleased him, pertaining to the spiritual Jerusalem. Which is to be believed to have been granted to no one except him to whom was divinely revealed the *one Mediator of God and men, the man Christ Jesus*[^205], who, as one to come in the flesh, was thus foretold to the ancient saints, just as he was announced to us as having come, so that one and the same faith through him might lead to God all who are predestined to the city of God, the house of God, the temple of God.

But whatever prophecies of others are brought forward concerning the grace of God through Jesus Christ can be thought to have been fabricated by the Christians. Therefore nothing is more firm for convincing any outsiders whatever, if they should contend about this matter, and for making them ours, if they should be rightly minded, than that those divine predictions about Christ be brought forward which are written in the books of the Jews; who, torn from their own seats and on account of this testimony dispersed throughout the whole world, the Church of Christ has everywhere grown.

**48.** This house of God is of greater glory than was that first one, constructed of wood and stones and other precious things and metals. Not therefore was the prophecy of Haggai fulfilled in the restoration of that temple. For from the time it was restored, it is nowhere shown to have had so great a glory as it had in the time of Solomon; rather, on the contrary, it is shown that the glory of that house was first diminished by the cessation of prophecy, then by such great disasters of the nation itself, even to the final destruction which was wrought by the Romans, as the things recalled above testify[^206]. But this house, pertaining to the New Testament, is surely of so much greater glory as the *living stones*[^207], of which, believing and renewed, it is built, are better. But it was signified by the restoration of that temple because the very renewal of that edifice signifies, in prophetic eloquence, the other Testament, which is called New. So that what God said through the aforesaid prophet: *and I will give peace in this place*[^208], the one signified by the signifying place is to be understood; so that, because by that restored place was signified the Church, which was to be built through Christ, nothing else is taken to be said in *I will give peace in this place* than: "I will give peace in the place which this place signifies." For all signifying things seem in a manner to sustain the persons of the things they signify; as it was said by the Apostle: *the rock was Christ*[^209], since that rock of which this was said surely signified Christ. Greater, therefore, is the glory of this house of the New Testament than of the former house of the Old Testament, and then it will appear greater, when it shall be dedicated.

For then *the desired of all the nations shall come*[^210], as is read in the Hebrew. For his first coming was not yet desired by all the nations. For they did not know whom they ought to desire, in whom they had not believed. Then too, according to the Septuagint translators (because that sense too is prophetic), *the things that are chosen of the Lord from all the nations shall come*. For then truly none shall come but the chosen, of whom the Apostle says: *as he chose us in him before the foundation of the world*[^211]. For the very architect, who said: *many are called, but few are chosen*[^212], will show the house built not of those who, being called, came in such a way as to be cast out of the banquet, but of the chosen—a house which will thenceforth dread no ruin. But now, when these too fill the churches, whom the winnowing, as on a threshing floor, will separate, there does not appear so great a glory of this house as will then appear, when whoever shall be there will be there forever.

**49.** In this malignant world, then, in these evil days, where through present lowliness the Church prepares for future loftiness and is trained by the goads of fears, the torments of griefs, the troubles of labors, the perils of temptations, rejoicing in hope alone, when it rejoices soundly, many reprobates are mingled with the good, and both are gathered together as into the evangelical dragnet, and in this world, as in a sea, both enclosed in the nets swim about indiscriminately, until the shore is reached, where the evil may be separated from the good[^213], and in the good, as in his own temple, *God may be all in all*[^214].

Accordingly, we now recognize the voice of him being fulfilled who spoke in the Psalm and said: *I have announced and spoken, they are multiplied above number*[^215]. This happens now, from the time when first through the mouth of his forerunner John, then through his own mouth, he announced and spoke, saying: *do penance, for the kingdom of heaven is at hand*[^216].

He chose disciples, whom he also named Apostles[^217], humbly born, unhonored, unlettered, so that whatever great thing they should be and do, he himself might be and do in them. He had among them one whom, using the evil one well, he might both fulfill the appointed plan of his passion and provide his Church an example of evils to be endured. The holy Gospel having been sown, as much as it was fitting through his bodily presence, he suffered, died, rose again, showing by his passion what we ought to endure for the truth, by his resurrection what we ought to hope for in eternity—setting aside the loftiness of the sacrament, by which his blood was poured out for the remission of sins. He conversed on earth forty days with his disciples and, while they were looking on, ascended into heaven[^218], and after ten days sent the promised Holy Spirit[^219]; of whose coming upon those who had believed the greatest and most necessary sign was then that each one of them spoke in the tongues of all nations; thus signifying the unity of the Catholic Church that would be among all nations, and that would thus speak in all tongues.

## The Church spread itself from Jerusalem.

**50.** Then, according to that prophecy: *out of Zion shall go forth the law, and the word of the Lord out of Jerusalem*[^220], and according to the predictions of the Lord Christ himself, where after the resurrection, while his disciples were amazed at him, *he opened their understanding, that they might understand the Scriptures, and said to them that thus it is written, and thus it behooved Christ to suffer and to rise from the dead on the third day, and that penance and remission of sins should be preached in his name among all nations, beginning at Jerusalem*[^221], and where again, when they asked him about his last coming, he answered and said: *it is not yours to know the times which the Father has put in his own power; but you shall receive the power of the Holy Spirit coming upon you, and you shall be witnesses to me in Jerusalem and in all Judea and Samaria and even to the ends of the earth*[^222],

the Church first spread itself from Jerusalem, and when very many had believed in Judea and Samaria, there was a going forth to other nations too, as they announced the Gospel whom he himself, like luminaries, had both fitted by his word and kindled by the Holy Spirit. For he had said to them: *do not fear those who kill the body but cannot kill the soul*[^223]. That they might not be cold with fear, they burned with the fire of love.

Finally, not only through those who had seen and heard him both before the passion and after the resurrection, but also after their death, through their successors, amid horrible persecutions and various tortures and the deaths of martyrs, the Gospel was preached throughout the whole world, God bearing witness by signs and wonders and various powers and the gifts of the Holy Spirit[^224]; so that the peoples of the nations, believing in him who was crucified for their redemption, venerated with Christian love the blood of the martyrs which they had shed with diabolical fury, and the very kings, by whose laws the Church was being laid waste, submitted with saving effect to that name which they had cruelly tried to remove from the earth, and began to persecute the false gods, for whose sake they had before persecuted the worshipers of the true God.

**51.** **1.** But the devil, seeing the temples of the demons deserted and the human race running to the name of the liberating Mediator, stirred up heretics who, under the Christian name, would resist Christian doctrine, as though they could be held indifferently, without any correction, in the city of God, just as the city of confusion held indifferently philosophers thinking diverse and contrary things.

Those, then, in the Church of Christ who relish something diseased and perverse, if, when corrected so that they may relish what is sound and right, they obstinately resist and refuse to amend their pestilent and deadly teachings, but persist in defending them, become heretics, and, going outside, are reckoned among the enemies who exercise it. For even thus, by their own evil, they profit those true Catholic members of Christ, while God uses even the evil well and *works all things together for good for those who love him*[^225].

For all the enemies of the Church, by whatever error they are blinded or by whatever malice they are depraved, if they receive power to afflict it bodily, exercise its patience; if they oppose it only by thinking evil, they exercise its wisdom; but so that even enemies may be loved, they exercise its benevolence, or even its beneficence, whether it deals with them by persuasive teaching or by terrifying discipline.

And on this account the devil, the prince of the impious city, by stirring up his own vessels against the city of God on pilgrimage in this world, is permitted to do it no harm, for which, without doubt, both consolation in prosperity, that it be not broken by adversity, and exercise in adversity, that it be not corrupted by prosperity, are procured by divine providence; and so each is tempered by the other, so that we recognize that voice in the Psalm to have arisen from no other source: *according to the multitude of my sorrows in my heart, your consolations have given joy to my soul*[^226]. Hence is also that saying of the Apostle: *rejoicing in hope, patient in tribulation*[^227].

**51.** **2.** For that too which the same teacher says: *all who wish to live piously in Christ suffer persecution*[^228], is to be thought lacking to no times. For even when from those who are outside, not raging, there seems to be tranquillity—and in truth there is, and it brings very much consolation, especially to the weak—yet there are not lacking, indeed there are many within, who torment the hearts of those living piously by their lost morals; since through them the name "Christian" and "Catholic" is blasphemed[^229]; which, the dearer it is to those who wish to live piously in Christ, the more they grieve that, through the evil placed within, it comes to pass that it is loved less than the minds of the pious desire.

The heretics themselves too, when they are considered to have the Christian name and sacraments and Scriptures and profession, cause great grief in the hearts of the pious; because both many who wish to be Christians are compelled to waver on account of their dissensions, and many slanderers also find in these a subject for blaspheming the Christian name, because they too are in some way called Christians. By these and the like perverse morals and errors of men, those who wish to live piously in Christ suffer persecution, even though no one is attacking or harassing their bodies. For they suffer this persecution not in their bodies but in their hearts. Whence is that voice: *according to the multitude of my sorrows in my heart*. For he does not say: "in my body."

But again, since the divine promises are considered immutable, and what the Apostle says: *the Lord knows who are his*[^230]; *for whom he foreknew, he also predestined to be conformed to the image of his Son*[^231]; of these none can perish; therefore it follows in that Psalm: *your consolations have given joy to my soul*[^232]. But the very grief that arises in the hearts of the pious, whom the morals of evil or false Christians persecute, profits the grievers, since it descends from love, by which they do not wish them to perish nor to hinder the salvation of others.

Finally, great consolations arise even from their corrections, which suffuse the souls of the pious with as much joy as the griefs with which they had tormented them by their own perdition. Thus in this world, in these evil days, not only from the time of the bodily presence of Christ and his Apostles, but from Abel himself, whom, as the first just man, his impious brother slew, and thenceforth even to the end of this world, the Church runs its course on pilgrimage amid the persecutions of the world and the consolations of God.

## There are ten persecutions from the Roman emperors . . .

**52.** **1.** Accordingly, I do not think it should even be rashly said or believed, as it has seemed and seems to some, that the Church will suffer no more persecutions, down to the time of Antichrist, than it has already suffered—that is, ten—so that the eleventh and last will be from Antichrist. For they count the first as that which was made by Nero, the second by Domitian, the third by Trajan, the fourth by Antoninus, the fifth by Severus, the sixth by Maximinus, the seventh by Decius, the eighth by Valerian, the ninth by Aurelian, the tenth by Diocletian and Maximian.

For the plagues of the Egyptians, since they were ten before the people of God began to go forth from there, they think are to be referred to this understanding, so that the last persecution of Antichrist may seem like the eleventh plague, by which the Egyptians, while pursuing the Hebrews as enemies, perished in the Red Sea, the people of God passing over on dry ground[^233]. But I do not judge that by that deed done in Egypt these persecutions were prophetically signified; although by those who think this each of those particulars seems to be compared with each of these elaborately and ingeniously, not by the prophetic Spirit, but by the conjecture of the human mind, which sometimes arrives at the truth and sometimes is deceived.

**52.** **2.** For what will those who hold this say of the persecution by which the Lord himself was crucified? In what number will they place it? But if they think it should be reckoned with this one excepted, as though those are to be numbered which pertain to the body, not the one by which the head itself was attacked and slain: what will they do with that one which, after Christ ascended into heaven, was made at Jerusalem,

where blessed Stephen was stoned[^234], where James the brother of John was slain with the sword, where the apostle Peter was shut up to be killed and freed by an angel, where the brethren were put to flight and dispersed from Jerusalem[^235], where Saul, who afterward became the apostle Paul, was laying waste the Church, where he too, now preaching the faith which he had persecuted, suffered such things as he had done, whether through Judea or through other nations, wherever, most fervently, he preached Christ?

Why, then, does it seem to them that the beginning should be made from Nero, when the Church, by growing, reached the times of Nero amid most atrocious persecutions, of which it is too long to tell all? But if they think that persecutions made by kings ought to be in the number: Herod was a king, who made a most grievous one even after the ascension of the Lord.

Then what do they answer also about Julian, whom they do not number among the ten? Did he not persecute the Church, who forbade Christians to teach and learn the liberal letters? Under whom the elder Valentinian, who after him was the third emperor, stood forth a confessor of the Christian faith and was deprived of his military service;

to omit what he had begun to do at Antioch, except that he shuddered at the freedom and cheerfulness of one most faithful and most steadfast young man, who, when many had been seized to be tortured, was the first tortured the whole day long, singing amid the iron claws and the torments, and feared to blush more disgracefully in the case of the rest.

who, when many had been arrested for torture, was the first to be tortured throughout the entire day, might have been awestruck at the freedom and cheerfulness with which he sang psalms amid the iron claws and torments, and might have feared still more shameful humiliation in the case of the others.

Finally, in our own memory, did not Valens, the brother of the aforesaid Valentinian, an Arian, lay waste the Catholic Church with a great persecution through the parts of the East? But how strange it is not to consider that the Church, bearing fruit and growing throughout the whole world, can in some nations suffer persecution from kings even when in others it does not suffer it?

Unless perhaps it is not to be reckoned a persecution when the king of the Goths, in Gothia itself, persecuted the Christians with wondrous cruelty, when there were none there but Catholics, very many of whom were crowned with martyrdom, as we have heard from certain brethren who had then been boys there and recalled without hesitation that they had seen these things? What of late in Persia? Did not persecution there so blaze up against the Christians (if indeed it has now subsided) that some, fleeing thence, reached even the towns of the Romans?

When I consider these things and the like, it does not seem to me that a number should be defined of the persecutions by which the Church must be exercised. But again, to affirm that there will be certain ones to come from kings, besides that last one about which no Christian doubts, is of no less rashness. And so we leave this in the middle, neither establishing nor demolishing either side of this question, but only recalling men from the bold presumption of affirming whichever of these.

**53.** **1.** That last persecution, indeed, which is to come from Antichrist, Jesus himself will extinguish by his own presence. For thus it is written, that he *will slay him with the spirit of his mouth and will destroy him with the illumination of his presence*[^236]. Here it is wont to be asked: When will this be? Most untimely. For if it profited us to know this, by whom could it better be told to the asking disciples than by God himself, the master? For they were not silent about it with him, but asked him while present, saying: *Lord, will you at this time restore the kingdom to Israel?* But he: *it is not*, he says, *yours to know the times which the Father has put in his own power*[^237].

They surely had asked him not about the hour or day or year, but about the time, when they received this answer. In vain, therefore, do we try to compute and define the years that remain to this world, since we hear from the mouth of Truth that it is not ours to know this; which, however, some have said could be completed in four hundred years, others five hundred, others even a thousand, from the ascension of the Lord to his last coming.

But how each of them constructs his own opinion, it is long to demonstrate and not necessary. For they use human conjectures, and nothing certain is brought forward by them from the authority of canonical Scripture. But the fingers of all who calculate about this matter are loosed, and they are bidden to be at rest, by him who says: *it is not yours to know the times which the Father has put in his own power*.

## They say that Peter worked sorceries for the worship of Christ's name.

**53.** **2.** But because this is an evangelical statement, it is no wonder that the worshipers of the many and false gods were not restrained by it from feigning that it had been defined, by the responses of the demons whom they worship as gods, how long the Christian religion would last. For when they saw that it could not be consumed even by so many and so great persecutions, but had rather taken wondrous increase from them, they devised certain Greek verses, as though poured forth to someone consulting a divine oracle, where they make Christ indeed innocent of this charge of sacrilege, as it were, but add that Peter worked sorceries, so that the name of Christ might be worshiped for three hundred and sixty-five years, and then, the said number of years being completed, it would without delay come to an end.

O hearts of learned men! O lettered wits, worthy to believe these things about Christ, which you are unwilling to believe in Christ: that his disciple Peter did not learn magic arts from him, but, while he was innocent, was nevertheless his sorcerer, and chose by his magic arts that the name of Christ rather than his own be worshiped—by his great labors and perils, and finally even by the shedding of his own blood!

If Peter the sorcerer brought it about that the world should so love Christ, what did the innocent Christ do, that Peter should so love him? Let them, then, answer themselves, and, if they can, let them understand that it was brought about by that supernal grace, that the world should love Christ for the sake of eternal life, by which grace it was brought about that Peter too should love Christ for the sake of eternal life to be received from him, and even of a temporal death to be suffered for him.

And then, what gods are these who can foretell these things and cannot avert them, thus succumbing to one sorcerer and one magic crime—by which, as they say, a year-old boy was slain and torn to pieces and buried with an unspeakable rite—so that they permitted a sect adverse to themselves to grow strong for so long a time, to overcome the horrible cruelties of so many and so great persecutions not by resisting but by suffering, and to arrive at the overthrow of their own images, temples, and sacred oracles?

Finally, what god is it—not ours, of course, but theirs—who was either enticed by so great a crime or impelled to grant these things? For those verses say that Peter defined these things by magic art not to some demon, but to a god. Such a god they have, who do not have Christ.

**54.** **1.** These and many such things I would collect, if the very year had not yet passed which the feigned divination promised and the deceived vanity believed. But since, from the time when the worship of Christ's name was instituted through his presence in the flesh and through the Apostles, three hundred and sixty-five years were completed some years ago, what else do we seek, whereby this falsehood may be refuted? For, to set the beginning of this matter not at Christ's nativity—because as an infant and boy he had no disciples—yet when he began to have them, without doubt then the Christian doctrine and religion became known through his bodily presence, that is, after he was baptized in the river Jordan by the ministry of John. For on this account that prophecy concerning him had gone before: *he shall rule from sea to sea and from the river even to the ends of the earth*[^238].

But since, before he suffered and rose from the dead, faith had not yet been defined to all (for it was defined in the resurrection of Christ, for thus the apostle Paul speaks to the Athenians, saying: *now he announces to men that all everywhere should do penance, because he has appointed a day to judge the world in equity by a man in whom he has defined faith to all, raising him from the dead*[^239]): we better take the beginning, in solving this question, from there; especially because then too the Holy Spirit was given, as he was to be given after Christ's resurrection in that city from which the second law—that is, the New Testament—had to begin. For the first was from Mount Sinai through Moses, which Testament is called Old. But of this one, which was to be given through Christ, it was foretold: *out of Zion shall go forth the law, and the word of the Lord out of Jerusalem*[^240]. Whence he himself too said that penance ought to be preached among all nations in his name, but yet beginning at Jerusalem[^241].

There, then, the worship of this name began, that there might be belief in Jesus Christ, who had been crucified and had risen again. There this faith glowed with such notable beginnings that several thousand men, converted to the name of Christ with wondrous eagerness[^242], having sold their goods to be distributed to the needy[^243], by a holy purpose and most ardent love arrived at voluntary poverty and prepared themselves, amid the raging and blood-thirsty Jews, to contend for the truth even to death, not by armed power, but by a more powerful patience. If this was done by no magic arts, why do they hesitate to believe that the same thing could be done throughout the whole world by the same divine power by which this was done?

But if, in order that at Jerusalem so great a multitude of men should be kindled to the worship of Christ's name—men who had either seized and fixed him on the cross or laughed at him fixed there—Peter had already worked that sorcery, the question must be raised from that very year, when the three hundred and sixty-five were completed. Christ, then, died in the consulship of the two Gemini, on the twenty-fifth of March. He rose again on the third day, as the Apostles proved even by their own senses[^244]. Then after forty days he ascended into heaven[^245]; after ten days—that is, on the fiftieth day after his resurrection—he sent the Holy Spirit[^246]. Then three thousand men believed when the Apostles preached him[^247].

Then, accordingly, the worship of that name began—as we believe, and as the truth holds, by the efficacy of the Holy Spirit; but as impious vanity feigned or supposed, by the magic arts of Peter. A little later, a wondrous sign too having been done, when at the very word of Peter a certain beggar, so lame from his mother's womb that he was carried by others and set at the gate of the temple, where he might beg alms, in the name of Jesus Christ leaped up sound[^248], five thousand men believed[^249]; and thereafter, by one access after another of believers, the Church grew.

And on this account is gathered also the day from which the year itself took its beginning, namely when the Holy Spirit was sent, that is, on the ides of May. Reckoning, accordingly, by the consuls, three hundred and sixty-five years are found to have been completed on those same ides, in the consulship of Honorius and Eutychianus. Now in the following year, in the consulship of Mallius Theodorus, when, according to that oracle of the demons or fiction of men, there ought to have been no Christian religion, what was perhaps done through other parts of the world it was not necessary to inquire; meanwhile, what we know is that in the most well-known and most eminent city of Carthage in Africa, Gaudentius and Jovius, counts of the emperor Honorius, on the nineteenth of March overthrew the temples of the false gods and broke the images.

From that time to this, through almost thirty years, who does not see how greatly the worship of Christ's name has grown, especially after many of those became Christians who, as though by that true divination, were being called back from the faith and, the same number of years being completed, saw it to be empty and laughable? We, then, who are and are called Christians, do not believe in Peter, but in him in whom Peter believed; built up by Peter's sermons about Christ, not poisoned by his songs; nor deceived by his sorceries, but aided by his benefits. That Christ, Peter's master in the doctrine that leads to eternal life, is himself our master too.

**54.** **2.** But let us now at length conclude this book, having discoursed thus far, and demonstrated as much as seemed sufficient, what the mortal course is of the two cities, the heavenly and the earthly, intermingled from the beginning even to the end; of which the one that is earthly made for itself, from wherever it wished or even from men, false gods whom it might serve by sacrificing; but the one that is heavenly and on pilgrimage on earth does not make false gods, but is itself made by the true God, whose true sacrifice it itself is. Both, however, alike use temporal goods or are alike afflicted by temporal evils, with a different faith, a different hope, a different love, until they are separated by the last judgment, and each receives its own end, of which there is no end; concerning the ends of both of which we must discourse next.
