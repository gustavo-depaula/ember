# Book VII: The natural philosophers' account of the select gods contains nothing that pertains to true happiness

## Preface

As I strive more diligently to root up and extirpate the perverse and ancient opinions, hostile to the truth of piety, which the long-standing error of the human race has fixed more deeply and more tenaciously in darkened minds, and as, according to my small measure, I cooperate with the help of his grace who, as the true God, can do this, the quicker and better minds—for whom the earlier books are amply sufficient for this matter—will have to bear with me patiently and with equanimity, and not think superfluous, on account of others, what they already feel to be unnecessary for themselves.

A great matter is greatly at stake, when the true and truly holy divinity—although it provides us with the supports necessary even for this frailty which we now bear—is nevertheless proclaimed as to be sought and worshiped not on account of the transitory vapor of mortal life, but on account of the blessed life, which is none other than eternal.

**1.** That this divinity, or, so to speak, this deity (for our writers now do not shrink from using this word too, that they may more expressly translate from the Greek what those people call θεότης)—that this divinity or deity, then, is not in that theology which they call civil, which was set forth by Marcus Varro in sixteen volumes; that is, that one does not arrive at the happiness of eternal life through the worship of such gods as the cities established to be worshiped, and in such manner—whoever has not yet been persuaded of this by the sixth book, which we have just finished, when perhaps he has read this one, will have nothing further to desire toward settling this question.

For it can happen that someone supposes that at least the select and principal gods, whom Varro embraced in his last volume, and about whom we have said too little, are to be worshiped on account of the blessed life, which is none other than eternal.

On this matter I do not say what Tertullian says, perhaps more wittily than truly: *If gods are chosen like onions, then surely the rest are judged worthless*[^1].

I do not say this; for I see that even from the select some are chosen for something greater and more excellent—just as in military service, when recruits have been chosen, from these too some are chosen for some greater work of arms; and when men are chosen in the Church to be made overseers, the rest are surely not thereby disapproved, since all good believers are rightly called the chosen.

In a building the cornerstones are chosen[^2], without the rest being disapproved, which are assigned to other parts of the structure. Grapes are chosen for eating, and the others, which we leave for drinking, are not disapproved.

There is no need to run through many examples, since the matter is plain. For this reason, it is not on the ground that certain gods have been selected from many that either the man who wrote it, or their worshipers, or the gods themselves are to be blamed,

but rather it must be observed who these gods are and for what purpose they seem to have been selected.

**2.** These select gods, at any rate, Varro commends in the compass of a single book: Janus, Jupiter, Saturn, Genius, Mercury, Apollo, Mars, Vulcan, Neptune, Sol, Orcus, Father Liber, Tellus, Ceres, Juno, Luna, Diana, Minerva, Venus, Vesta; among all of whom, nearly twenty in number, twelve are male and eight are female[^3].

Are these divinities said to be select on account of greater administrations in the world, or because they became better known to the peoples and a greater worship was rendered to them? If because greater works are administered by them in the world, we ought not to have found them among that, as it were, plebeian multitude of divinities assigned to petty little tasks. For Janus himself first, when a child is conceived—whence all those works take their beginning, distributed minutely among minute divinities—opens the entrance for the reception of the seed. There too is Saturn, on account of the seed itself; there Liber, who frees the male by the emission of seed; there Libera, whom they also will to be Venus, who confers this same benefit on the female, that she too may be freed by the emission of seed[^4].

All these are among those who are called select. But there too is the goddess Mena, who presides over the menstrual flows, although a daughter of Jupiter, yet ignoble. And this province of the menstrual flows the same author assigns, in the book of the select gods, to Juno herself, who among the select gods is even queen, and here, as Juno Lucina, together with Mena her stepdaughter, presides over the same blood[^5]. There too are two utterly obscure deities, I know not who, Vitumnus and Sentinus, of whom the one bestows life, the other the senses, on the child being born[^6]. And no doubt they furnish much more, though they are most ignoble, than all those nobles and select ones. For surely, without life and sense, what is that whole thing borne in a woman's womb but some most abject thing, to be compared with mud and dust?

**3. 1.** What cause, then, compelled so many select gods to these least tasks, where they are surpassed in the apportioning of this munificence by Vitumnus and Sentinus, whom *obscure repute keeps hidden*[^7]? For the select Janus furnishes the entrance and, as it were, the gate for the seed; the select Saturn furnishes the seed itself; the select Liber furnishes the emission of this same seed for males; Libera, who is Ceres or Venus, furnishes this same thing for females; the select Juno furnishes—and this not alone, but with Mena, the daughter of Jupiter—the menstrual flows for the increase of what has been conceived:

and the obscure and ignoble Vitumnus furnishes life; the obscure and ignoble Sentinus furnishes sense; which two are so much more excellent than those other things, by as much as they themselves are surpassed by understanding and reason. For just as the things that reason and understand are surely superior to those which, without understanding and reason, live and feel like beasts, so too the things that are endowed with life and sense are rightly preferred to those which neither live nor feel.

And so, among the select gods, Vitumnus the life-giver and Sentinus the sense-giver ought rather to have been reckoned than Janus the admitter of seed, and Saturn the giver or sower of seed, and Liber and Libera the stirrers up or emitters of seeds; which seeds it is unworthy to think about, unless they have arrived at life and sense. These select gifts are given not by the select gods, but by certain unknown ones, neglected in comparison with the dignity of the others.

But if it is answered that Janus has power over all beginnings, and therefore that what is opened for conception is not undeservedly attributed to him; that Saturn has power over all seeds, and therefore the sowing of a human being too cannot be separated from his operation; that Liber and Libera have power over all the seeds to be emitted, and therefore preside over these things too that pertain to the propagation of human beings; that Juno has power over all purgings and bearings, and therefore is not absent from the purgings of women and the births of human beings:

let them seek what they will answer about Vitumnus and Sentinus—whether they wish these too to have power over all things that live and feel.

And if they grant this, let them notice how much more loftily they will have to place them. For to be born from seeds is in the earth and from the earth; but to live and feel they think belongs even to the gods of the stars.

But if they say that to Vitumnus and Sentinus only those things are attributed which come to life in the flesh and are aided by the senses, why does that God who makes all things live and feel not also furnish life and sense to the flesh, granting this gift even to births by his universal work? And what need is there of Vitumnus and Sentinus?

But if, by him who universally presides over life and the senses, these carnal matters have been committed, as the lowest and basest, to these as it were servants,

are those select gods so destitute of a household that they could find none to whom they too might commit these matters, but were compelled, with all their nobility—for which they seemed worthy of being selected—to do the work alongside the ignoble?

Juno, select and queen, *both sister and wife of Jupiter*[^8]; yet she is Iterduca for children and does the work alongside the most ignoble goddesses Abeona and Adeona.

There too they have placed the goddess Mens, who is to give children a good mind, and she is not placed among the select—as though anything greater could be furnished to a human being; but Juno is placed there, because she is Iterduca and Domiduca,

as though it were of any profit to take a journey and to be led home, if the mind is not good[^9]—the goddess of which gift these selectors did not at all place among the select divinities. She surely ought to have been preferred even to Minerva, to whom, by these petty tasks, they attributed the memory of children.

For who would doubt that it is far better to have a good mind than a memory, however vast? For no one is wicked who has a good mind; but some of the worst men have a marvelous memory, all the worse the less they are able to forget what they think wickedly. And yet Minerva is among the select gods, while the goddess Mens was buried by the cheap crowd.

What shall I say of Virtue? What of Happiness? Of whom we have already said much in the fourth book[^10]; whom, though they held them to be goddesses, they were unwilling to give any place among the select gods, where they gave a place to Mars and Orcus, the one a maker of deaths, the other a receiver of them.

**3. 2.** Since, then, in these petty tasks, which have been minutely distributed among many gods, we see even the select ones working, like a senate, on equal terms with the commons, and find that by certain gods who were judged in no way worthy of selection things far greater and better are administered than by those who are called select—it remains to suppose that they were named select and principal not on account of more excellent administrations in the world, but because it came about that they became better known to the peoples.

Hence Varro himself says that to certain father gods and mother goddesses, as to human beings, ignobility had befallen[^11]. If, then, Happiness perhaps ought not to have been among the select gods, on the ground that they arrived at that nobility not by merit but by chance, at least Fortune should have been placed among them, or rather before them—Fortune, whom they call a goddess who confers her gifts on each not by a rational arrangement, but as it has happened by chance.

She ought to have held the topmost place among the select gods, since among them she showed most of all what she could do; for we see them selected not by any outstanding virtue, not by any rational happiness, but, as their worshipers think of her, by the random power of Fortune.

For the most eloquent Sallust too perhaps had the gods themselves in view, when he said: *But surely Fortune rules in everything; it is she who makes all things famous or obscure according to caprice rather than according to truth*[^12].

For they cannot find a cause why Venus has been made famous and Virtue obscured, when the divinities of both have been consecrated by them, and their merits are not to be compared.

Or if she deserved to be ennobled because more people seek her—for more seek Venus than Virtue—why was the goddess Minerva made famous and the goddess Pecunia obscured?

For in the human race avarice allures more than skill, and even among those who are skilled you will rarely find a man who does not have his art for sale at a money price, and that for the sake of which something is done is always valued more than that which is done for the sake of something else.

If, then, this selection of gods was made by the judgment of the foolish multitude, why was the goddess Pecunia not preferred to Minerva, since on account of money there are many craftsmen? But if this distinction belongs to the few wise, why was Virtue not preferred to Venus, since reason far prefers her?

At least, surely, as I have said, Fortune herself—who, as those think who attribute most to her[^13], rules in everything and makes all things famous or obscure according to caprice rather than according to truth—if she had such power even over the gods, that by her random judgment she made famous whom she would and obscured whom she would, ought to have held a principal place among the select, she who is of such principal power even over the gods themselves.

Or are we to suppose that, in order that she could not be there, Fortune herself had nothing but adverse fortune? She was, then, adverse to herself, who, while making others noble, was not herself ennobled.

**4.** But someone covetous of nobility and renown might congratulate these select gods and call them fortunate, if he did not see that they were selected rather for injuries than for honors.

For that lowest crowd was covered by its very ignobility, lest it be overwhelmed with reproaches. We laugh, indeed, when we see them, by the fictions of human opinions, distributed with tasks parceled out among themselves, like petty tax-farmers, or like workmen in the silversmiths' street, where a single little vessel, in order to come out finished, passes through many craftsmen, though it could be finished by one finished craftsman.

But it was thought that there was no other way to provide for the multitude of workers than that each should learn quickly and easily his single part of the craft, lest all be compelled to be slowly and with difficulty finished masters in one craft. Yet hardly anyone is found among the non-select gods who has drawn an infamous reputation by some crime; but hardly any of the select ones who has not received upon himself the mark of some notable disgrace. The former descended to the lowly tasks of the latter; the latter did not come into the lofty crimes of the former. About Janus, indeed, nothing readily occurs to me that pertains to reproach.

And perhaps he was such a one as lived more innocently and more removed from misdeeds and disgraces.

He kindly received the fleeing Saturn; he shared the kingdom with his guest, so that they founded separate cities as well, the one Janiculum, the other Saturnia.

But those people, in the worship of the gods seekers of every disgrace, the one whose life they found less shameful they disfigured with the monstrous deformity of his image, making him now two-faced, now even four-faced, as it were twin. Or did they perhaps wish that, since very many of the select gods had, by perpetrating shameful things, lost their faces, the more innocent this one was, the more faces he should appear to have?

**5.** But let us rather hear their own physical interpretations, by which they try to color the shamefulness of a most wretched error with the appearance, as it were, of a deeper learning.

First Varro commends these interpretations thus: he says that the ancients invented the images of the gods, and their emblems and adornments, so that those who had approached the mysteries of the doctrine, when they observed them with their eyes, might see with the mind the soul of the world and its parts, that is, the true gods; and that those who made the images in the shape of a man seem to have followed this, namely, that the mortal mind, which is in the human body, is most like the immortal mind;

as if vessels were set up for the sake of marking the gods, and in the temple of Liber a wine jar were placed, which would signify wine, by that which contains signifying that which is contained; so by an image that had a human form the rational soul is signified, because by that as by a vessel this nature is wont to be contained, of which nature they will the god, or the gods, to be[^14].

These are the mysteries of the doctrine into which this most learned man had penetrated, that he might bring them forth into the light.

But, O most acute man, did you, in these mysteries of doctrine, lose that prudence by which it soberly seemed to you that those who first set up images for the peoples both took away fear from their fellow citizens and added error, and that the ancient Romans worshiped the gods more chastely without images[^15]?

For these were your authorities, that you should dare to say these things against the later Romans.

For if those most ancient men too had worshiped images, perhaps you would have suppressed in the silence of fear this whole opinion about not setting up images—true, for the moment—

and you would have proclaimed more garrulously and more loftily these mysteries of doctrine amid such pernicious and empty fictions.

Yet your soul, so learned and gifted (wherein we much grieve for you), through these mysteries of doctrine could in no way arrive at its God—that is, at the God *by whom* it was made, not the one *with* whom it was made; not the one whose portion it is, but whose creation it is; not the one who is the soul of all things, but who made every soul, by whose enlightening alone the soul becomes blessed, if it is not ungrateful to his grace.

But what these mysteries of doctrine are, and at how much they are to be valued, what follows will show. Meanwhile this most learned man confesses that the soul of the world and its parts are the true gods;

from which it is understood that his whole theology—that very natural one, namely, to which he attributes most—could extend itself as far as the nature of the rational soul. For he says very little by way of preface about the natural theology in this book, which he wrote last, about the select gods; in which we shall see whether, by physiological interpretations, he can refer the civil theology back to this natural one.

And if he can, the whole will be natural; and what need was there to join the civil to it with such great care of distinction? But if it is separated by a correct distinction—since not even that one is true which pleases him as natural (for it reaches only as far as the soul, not as far as the true God who made the soul as well)—how much more abject and more false is this civil one, which is occupied chiefly with the nature of bodies, as its very interpretations, certain of which I must necessarily recall, will show, sought out and worked out by them with so much diligence?

**6.** The same Varro, then, still speaking by way of preface about the natural theology, says that he judges God to be the soul of the world, which the Greeks call κόσμος, and that this world itself is God; but that, just as a wise man, though he is made of body and mind, is yet called wise from his mind, so the world is called God from its mind, though it is made of mind and body.

Here he seems in some way to confess one God; but, in order to introduce many as well, he adds that the world is divided into two parts, heaven and earth;

and heaven in two ways, into ether and air; and earth into water and land; of which the highest is ether, the second air, the third water, the lowest land;

and that all these four parts are full of souls—in ether and air of immortal souls, in water and land of mortal ones. And that from the highest circuit of heaven to the circle of the moon there are ethereal souls, the stars and the heavenly bodies, and that these heavenly gods are not only understood to be, but even seen;

but that between the orbit of the moon and the tops of the rain clouds and winds there are aerial souls, but that these are seen with the mind, not with the eyes, and are called heroes, lares, and genii[^16]. This, you see, is the natural theology briefly set forth in that preface, which pleased not only this man but many philosophers; about which we must then discuss more diligently, when, with the help of the true God, I have completed what remains concerning the civil theology, as far as the select gods are concerned.

**7.** Janus, then, from whom he took his start, I ask who he may be. The answer is: He is the world. This answer is plainly brief and open. Why, then, are the beginnings of things said to belong to him, but the ends to another, whom they call Terminus[^17]?

For on account of beginnings and ends they say that two months are dedicated to these two gods, besides those ten of which March is the head, down to December: January to Janus, February to Terminus.

For this reason they say the Terminalia are celebrated in the same month of February, when the purgatorial rite is performed which they call Februm, from which the month received its name[^18].

Do the beginnings of things, then, belong to the world, which is Janus, and the ends not belong to it, so that another god had to be set over them? Do they not confess that all the things they say come to pass in this world are also terminated in this world? What is this vanity—to give him half power in his work, a double face in his image?

Would they not interpret this two-faced god far more elegantly if they said that the same one is both Janus and Terminus, and gave one face to beginnings, the other to ends?

For one who acts ought to attend to both; for in every motion of his action, he who does not look back to the beginning does not look forward to the end.

Hence the forward-looking intention must necessarily be connected with the backward-looking memory; for one from whom what he has begun has slipped away will not find how to finish it. But if they thought that the blessed life is begun in this world and perfected outside the world, and therefore attributed to Janus, that is, to the world, only the power of beginnings, they would surely set Terminus before him and would not make him a stranger to the select gods.

And yet even now, when in these two gods the beginnings and ends of temporal things are treated, more honor ought to have been given to Terminus.

For greater is the joy when any matter is brought to completion; but things begun are full of anxiety until they are brought to their end,

which end he who begins anything most of all seeks, intends, awaits, and longs for, nor does he exult in a thing begun unless it be terminated.

**8.** But now let the interpretation of the two-faced image be brought forth. They say he has two faces, before and behind, because the opening of our mouth, when we open it, seems like the world;

whence the Greeks call the palate οὐρανός[^19], and some Latin poets too, he says, have called the palate heaven, from which opening of the mouth there is a passage both outward toward the teeth and inward toward the throat[^20].

See to what the world has been brought, on account of the term for our palate, whether Greek or poetic. But what has this to do with the soul, what with eternal life? For the sake of saliva alone is this god worshiped, for the partly swallowing and partly spitting out of which both doors are opened beneath the heaven of the palate.

What, moreover, is more absurd than, in the world itself, not to find two doors set opposite each other, through which it might either admit something inward to itself or send something outward from itself, and to wish to compose from our mouth and throat—of which the world has no likeness—an image of the world in Janus, on account of the palate alone, of which Janus has no likeness?

But when they make him four-faced and call him "twin Janus," they interpret this with reference to the four parts of the world, as though the world looked outward by all its faces, as Janus does.

Then, if Janus is the world and the world consists of four parts, the image of the two-faced Janus is false; or if it is true on the ground that the whole world is wont to be understood even by the name of "East and West," would anyone, when we name the two other parts of North and South, say "twin world," just as they call the four-faced one "twin Janus"?

They have no way at all to interpret four doors, open to those entering and leaving, after the likeness of the world, as, regarding the two-faced one, they at least found something to say in the human mouth,

unless perhaps Neptune comes to the rescue and holds out a fish, which, besides the opening of the mouth and throat, has gills open on the right and the left as well. And yet no soul escapes this vanity by any of so many doors, except one that hears the Truth saying: *I am the door*[^21].

**9. 1.** But let them explain whom they would have understood by Jupiter, who is also called Juppiter. *He is a god*, they say, *having power over the causes by which something comes to be in the world*[^22]. How great this is, that most noble verse of Virgil testifies:

*Happy is he who could know the causes of things*[^23]. But why is Janus set before him? Let that most acute and most learned man answer us this.

*Because with Janus*, he says, *are the first things, with Jupiter the highest. Rightly, then, is Jupiter held to be king of all. For the first things are surpassed by the highest, since, although the first precede in time, the highest surpass in dignity*[^24].

But this would be rightly said if the first and the highest of things done were being distinguished;

as the beginning of an act is to set out, the highest point to arrive; the beginning of an act is the inception of learning, the highest point the grasp of the doctrine;

and so in all things the first are the beginnings and the highest are the ends. But this matter has already been discussed between Janus and Terminus.

But the causes which are given to Jupiter are efficient, not effected; nor can it in any way come to pass that they be anticipated, even in time, by the beginnings of things done, or by the things done.

For always the thing that makes is prior to that which is made. Wherefore, if the beginnings of things done belong to Janus, they are not on that account prior to the efficient causes which they attribute to Jupiter. For just as nothing comes to be, so nothing is begun in order to come to be, which the making cause has not preceded.

This god, indeed, with whom are all the causes of all the natures that have been made and of all natural things, if the peoples call him Jupiter and worship him with such great insults and such wicked accusations, they bind themselves with a fouler sacrilege than if they thought there were no god at all.

Hence it would be better for them to call by the name of Jupiter some other being, worthy of shameful and disgraceful honors,

substituting an empty fiction which they might rather blaspheme (just as a stone is said to have been substituted for Saturn, for him to devour in place of his son)[^25], than to call this god both a thunderer and an adulterer, both ruling the whole world and flowing apart in so many debaucheries, both holding the highest causes of all natures and natural things and not holding good causes of his own.

**9. 2.** Next I ask what place among the gods they now assign to this Jupiter, if Janus is the world. For he defined the true gods as the soul of the world and its parts; and on this account, whatever is not this is surely, according to these men, not the true God.

Will they then say that Jupiter is the soul of the world in such a way that Janus is its body, that is, this visible world? If they say this, there will be no way for them to call Janus a god, since the body of the world is not a god even according to themselves, but the soul of the world and its parts.

Hence the same author says most openly that he judges God to be the soul of the world and that this world itself is God; but that, just as a wise man, though he is made of mind and body, is yet called wise from his mind, so the world is called God from its mind, though it is made of mind and body[^26].

And so the body of the world alone is not a god, but either its soul alone, or body and mind together, yet in such a way that it is a god not from the body but from the mind.

If, then, Janus is the world and Janus is a god, will they say that Jupiter, in order to be able to be a god, is some part of Janus? For they are rather wont to attribute the universe to Jupiter; whence comes the line: *All things are full of Jove*[^27].

Therefore Jupiter too, that he may be a god, and indeed the king of the gods, they can hold to be no other than the world, so that he reigns over the other gods, who, according to these men, are his parts. In this sense the same Varro also expounds certain verses of Valerius Soranus, in that book which he wrote separately from these, about the worship of the gods; which verses are these: *Almighty Jupiter, begetter of kings and of things and of the gods, and mother of the gods, one god and all*[^28].

They are expounded in the same book thus: since they reckoned that to be male which emits seed, that to be female which receives it, and that Jupiter is the world, and that he emits all seeds from himself and receives them into himself:

*With cause*, he says, *did Soranus write Jupiter "begetter" and "mother," and no less with cause that he is "one and all the same"; for the world is one, and in that one are all things*[^29].

**10.** Since, then, both Janus is the world and Jupiter is the world, and the world is one, why are Janus and Jupiter two gods?

Why do they have temples apart, altars apart, different rites, dissimilar images? If it is because the force of beginnings is one thing, the force of causes another, and the former has received the name of Janus, the latter that of Jupiter:

is it the case that, if one man has in different matters two powers or two skills, because the force of each is different, he is therefore said to be two judges or two craftsmen?

So then one God too, since he himself has the power over beginnings and himself over causes, must he on that account be thought to be two gods, because beginnings and causes are matters that are his own? But if they think this just, let them say that Jupiter himself is as many gods as the surnames they have given him on account of his many powers, since all the things from which those surnames have been drawn are many and diverse; of which I recall a few.

**11.** They have called him Victor, Invictus, Opitulus, Impulsor, Stator, Centumpeda, Supinal, Tigillus, Almus, Ruminus, and others which it would be long to pursue[^30].

These surnames they imposed on one god on account of diverse causes and powers, yet they did not compel him, on account of so many matters, to be also so many gods:

namely, that he conquered all things, that he was conquered by no one,

that he brought help (ops) to the needy,

that he had the power of impelling, of establishing, of making firm, of laying on its back,

that, like a beam (tigillus), he held together and upheld the world, that he nourished all things, that with the teat (ruma)—that is, the breast—he nourished living creatures.

Among these, as we notice, some are great, some are slight; and yet one and the same god is said to do both.

I think the causes of things and their beginnings—on account of which they wished one world to be two gods, Jupiter and Janus—are more closely related to each other than holding the world together and giving the breast to living creatures;

and yet, on account of these two works, so far apart from each other in force and dignity, they were not compelled to be two gods;

but one Jupiter was called Tigillus on account of the one, Ruminus on account of the other.

I will not say that to furnish the breast to suckling animals might more fittingly have befitted Juno than Jupiter, especially since there was also the goddess Rumina, who might furnish him help or service in this work.

For I reflect that it can be answered that Juno herself is nothing other than Jupiter, according to those verses of Valerius Soranus, where it is said: *Almighty Jupiter, begetter of kings and of things and of the gods, and mother of the gods*. Why, then, was he also called Ruminus, when perhaps, to those who inquire more diligently, he himself is found to be also that goddess Rumina?

For if it rightly seemed unworthy of the majesty of the gods that, in one ear of grain, one god should attend to the care of the joint, another to that of the husk: how much more unworthy is it that one lowest matter—namely, that animals be nourished at the breast—should be cared for by the power of two gods, of whom one is Jupiter, the very king of all,

and that he should do this not even with his wife, but with some ignoble Rumina—unless he himself is also Rumina herself; Ruminus perhaps for the suckling males, Rumina for the females.

I would indeed say that they were unwilling to impose a feminine name on Jupiter, were it not that in those verses too he is called *begetter and mother*, and that I read among his other surnames that he was also called Pecunia—a goddess whom we found among those petty divinities and recalled in the fourth book[^31].

But since both males and females have money, why he was not called both Pecunia and Pecunius, as Rumina and Ruminus, let them see to it.

**12.** But how elegantly they gave the reason for this name! *And he is called Pecunia*, he says, *because all things are his*[^32]. O great reason for a divine name! Nay rather, he whose all things are is most basely and insultingly named Pecunia. For in relation to all things that are contained by heaven and earth, what is money, among all the things whatsoever that are possessed by men under the name of money? But no doubt it was avarice that imposed this name on Jupiter, so that whoever loves money might seem to himself to love not just any god, but the very king of all. But it would be far otherwise if he were called Riches.

For riches are one thing, money another. For we call wise, just, good men rich, who have either no money or little; for they are rather rich in virtues, through which, even amid the very necessities of bodily things, what they have is enough for them:

but the avaricious we call poor, always gaping and in want; for they can have ever so great sums of money, but in however great an abundance of it they cannot be without want.

And the true God himself we rightly call rich, yet not in money, but in omnipotence. And so the moneyed are called rich;

but inwardly needy, if they are covetous; likewise those lacking money are called poor, but inwardly rich, if they are wise.

What kind of theology, then, ought this to be for a wise man, where the king of the gods received the name of that thing *which no wise man has coveted*[^33]? For how much more easily, if anything pertaining to eternal life were wholesomely learned by this teaching, would the God who rules the world be called by them not Pecunia but Wisdom, the love of which cleanses from the filth of avarice, that is, from the love of money!

**13.** But why say more about this Jupiter, to whom perhaps the rest are to be referred, so that the opinion of very many gods may remain empty, since these are all he himself—whether when they are thought to be his parts or powers, or when the force of the soul, which they think to be diffused through all things, has received, as it were, the names of many gods, from the parts of this mass into which this visible world rises up, and from the manifold administration of nature?

For what too is Saturn? *One*, he says, *of the chief gods, with whom is the dominion over all sowings*[^34]. Does not the exposition of those verses of Valerius Soranus run thus, that Jupiter is the world and that he emits all seeds from himself and receives them into himself? He, then, is the one with whom is the dominion over all sowings. What is Genius?

*A god*, he says, *who is set over and has power over all things that are to be begotten*. Whom else do they believe to have this power but the world, to whom it was said: *Jupiter, begetter and mother?*

And when in another place he says that the Genius is the rational mind of each person, and therefore that there are individual genii for individual persons, but that such a mind of the world is God: he certainly brings it back to this same point, that the soul of the world itself be believed to be, as it were, a universal genius[^35]. This, then, is the one they call Jupiter.

For if every genius is a god, and every man's mind is a genius, it follows that every man's mind is a god; and if the very absurdity compels even them to shrink from this, it remains that they call him, singularly and pre-eminently, the god Genius, whom they call the soul of the world, and on this account Jupiter.

**14.** But Mercury and Mars they did not find how to refer to any parts of the world and works of God which are in the elements, and therefore they at least set them over the works of men, as ministers of speaking and of waging war. Of these, if Mercury holds the power even over the speech of the gods, he is master even of the king of the gods himself, if Jupiter speaks according to his decision, or has received from him the faculty of speaking; which is certainly absurd.

But if the power over human speech only is said to be attributed to him, it is not credible that Jupiter was willing to come down to suckle at the breast not only children but even cattle—whence he was surnamed Ruminus—and yet was unwilling that the care of our speech, by which we excel the cattle, should belong to himself; and on this account Jupiter himself is the same as Mercury.

But if speech itself is said to be Mercury, as the things they interpret about him show (for Mercury is said to be so named as it were "running in the middle" (medius currens), because speech runs in the middle between men;

and therefore Ἑρμῆς in Greek, because speech, or interpretation, which certainly pertains to speech, is called ἑρμηνεία; and therefore he presides over merchandise too, because between sellers and buyers speech is made "in the middle";

that his wings on his head and on his feet signify that speech is borne winged through the air; that he is called the messenger, since by speech all that is thought is announced)[^36]—if, then, Mercury is speech itself, then, even by their own confession, he is not a god. But when they make themselves gods of those who are not even demons, by making supplication to unclean spirits they are possessed by those who are not gods but demons.

Likewise, because for Mars too they could find no element or part of the world where he might do any works whatever of nature, they said he was the god of war[^37], which is a work of men, and not the more desirable one. If, then, Happiness gave perpetual peace, Mars would have nothing to do. But if war itself is Mars, as speech is Mercury: would that, as it is manifest that he is not a god, so too there were no war, which might even falsely be called a god.

**15.** Unless perhaps these gods are those stars which they have called by their names. For one star they call Mercury, another likewise Mars. But there too is the one they call Jupiter, and yet to them the world is Jupiter; there the one they call Saturn, and yet they give him besides no small substance, namely, that of all seeds; there too is that brightest of all, which is called by them Venus, and yet they will the same Venus to be also the Moon[^38]; although about that most shining star Juno and Venus contend among them as over a golden apple[^39]. For some say the Morning Star is Venus's, some Juno's; but, as usual, Venus wins. For far more attribute that star to Venus, so that scarcely any of them is found who thinks otherwise[^40].

But who would not laugh, when they call Jupiter king of all, that his star is surpassed in brightness by the star of Venus by so much? For it ought to have been as much more shining than the rest as he himself is more powerful. They answer that it seems so for this reason: that the one which is thought dimmer is higher and far more remote from the lands.

If, then, a greater dignity deserved the higher place, why is Saturn there higher than Jupiter? Or could the vanity of the fable, which makes Jupiter king, not reach all the way to the stars, and what Saturn could not maintain in his kingdom nor in the Capitol, was he at least permitted to obtain in heaven[^41]? But why did Janus receive no star? If because he is the world and all the stars are in him: Jupiter's too is the world, and yet he has one. Or did this one arrange his own case as he could, and, for the one star which he does not have among the stars, receive so many faces on earth?

Then, if it is on account of the stars alone that they think Mercury and Mars parts of the world, that they may be able to hold them gods—since certainly speech and war are not parts of the world but acts of men—why for Aries and Taurus and Cancer and Scorpio and the rest of this kind,

which they count as celestial signs and which consist not of single stars but each of several, and which they hold to be placed above these in the highest heaven[^42], where a more constant motion furnishes the stars an unerring course—why did they make no altars, no rites, no temples for these, nor hold them as gods, I do not say among these select ones, but not even among those, as it were, plebeian ones?

**16.** Although they would have Apollo to be a diviner and a physician, yet, in order to set him in some part of the world, they said he was also the sun, and Diana his sister likewise the moon and the guardian of roads (whence they also will her to be a virgin, because a road bears nothing), and therefore that both have arrows, because those two heavenly bodies stretch their rays from heaven all the way to the lands[^43]. Vulcan they would have to be the fire of the world, Neptune the waters of the world, Father Dis—that is, Orcus—the earthly and lowest part of the world[^44].

Liber and Ceres they set over the seeds—either him over the male seeds, her over the female; or him over the liquid, her over the dryness of the seeds[^45]. And all this is certainly referred to the world, that is, to Jupiter, who was for this reason called *begetter and mother*, because he emitted all seeds from himself and received them into himself. Since indeed they would have the Great Mother to be the same Ceres, whom they say to be nothing other than the earth, and they hold her to be Juno also, and therefore attribute to her the secondary causes of things[^46],

although it was to Jupiter that it was said, *begetter and mother of the gods*, because, according to them, the whole world itself is Jupiter's. Minerva too, because they set her over human arts, and found neither a star where they might place her, they said to be either the highest ether or even the moon[^47]. Vesta too they thought the greatest of goddesses, for this reason: that she is the earth—although they believed that the lighter fire of the world, which pertains to the easy uses of men, not the more violent fire such as Vulcan's, was to be assigned to her[^48].

And on this account they would have all these select gods to be this world—in some the whole, in some its parts; the whole, like Jupiter; its parts, like Genius, like the Great Mother, like the Sun and Moon, or rather Apollo and Diana. And sometimes they make one god several things, sometimes one thing several gods. For one god is several things, like Jupiter himself; for the whole world is held and called Jupiter, and the heaven alone Jupiter, and a star alone Jupiter;

and likewise Juno, mistress of the secondary causes, and Juno the air, and Juno the earth, and, if she should win against Venus, Juno the star. Similarly Minerva the highest ether, and Minerva likewise the moon, which they reckon to be at the lowest limit of the ether. But they make one thing several gods thus: Janus is both the world and Jupiter; so too Juno is both the earth and the Great Mother and Ceres.

**17.** And as with these things, which I have recalled by way of example, so with the rest: they do not explicate them but rather implicate them; as the impulse of wandering opinion has driven them, so they leap and recoil this way and that, hither and thither, so that Varro himself preferred to doubt about everything rather than to affirm anything.

For of the three last books, when he had finished the first, about the certain gods, in the second, beginning to speak about the uncertain gods, he says: *Since in this little book I shall set down doubtful opinions about the gods, I ought not to be rebuked.*

*For he who thinks judgment ought to and can be passed, when he has heard, will pass it himself. I can more readily be brought to recall into doubt the things I said in the first book, than to bring into some sum total all the things I shall write through in this one*[^49].

Thus he made uncertain not only that book about the uncertain gods, but also that one about the certain.

Further, in this third book, about the select gods, after he had said by way of preface what he thought should be prefaced from the natural theology, when about to enter upon the vanities and lying madnesses of this civil theology—where not only did the truth of things not lead him, but the authority of his elders even pressed upon him—he says:

*About the public gods of the Roman people, to whom they have dedicated temples and whom they have marked out, adorned with many emblems, I shall write in this book,*

*but, as Xenophanes of Colophon writes, I shall set down what I think, not what I would maintain. For it belongs to man to hold these opinions, to God to know them*[^50].

Trembling, then, he promises a discourse about things not comprehended nor most firmly believed, but merely opined and to be doubted, about to speak of things that were established by human beings.

For just as he knew that there is a world, that there are heaven and earth—the heaven shining with stars, the earth fertile with seeds—and the like, and as he believed with a fixed steadiness of mind that this whole mass and nature is ruled and administered by a certain invisible and most powerful force, so he could not affirm about Janus that he was the world itself, nor find out about Saturn how he was both the father of Jupiter and made subject to the reigning Jupiter, and other such things.

**18.** About these gods a more credible account is given when they are held to have been men, and to have had, for each of them, rites and solemnities established by those who, in flattering them, wished them to be gods—established according to each one's character, morals, deeds, and fortunes—and when these, gradually creeping in through the souls of men who are like demons and greedy for sportive things, were spread far and wide, the lies of the poets adorning them and deceitful spirits leading men astray toward them.

For it could more easily have come about that an impious youth, either fearing to be killed by an impious father and greedy for the kingdom, drove his father from the kingdom, than that which this man interprets—that Saturn the father was overcome by Jupiter his son, because the cause that pertains to Jupiter is prior to the seed that pertains to Saturn[^51]. For if this were so, Saturn would never have been prior, nor would he be the father of Jupiter. For the cause always precedes the seed, and is never begotten from the seed. But when they try to honor most empty fables, or the deeds of men, with as it were natural interpretations, even the most acute men suffer such straits that we are compelled to grieve over their vanity too.

**19.** *They said*, he says, *that Saturn was accustomed to devour what was born from him, because the seeds returned thither, whence they were born.*

*And that a clod was thrown to him to devour in place of Jupiter signifies*, he says, *that, before the usefulness of plowing was discovered, the fruits sown began to be covered over by human hands*[^52].

The earth itself, then, ought to have been called Saturn, not the seeds; for it, in a certain way, devours what it has begotten, when the seeds born from it return to be received into it again.

And that he is said to have received a clod in place of Jupiter—what does this have to do with the seed's being covered over by a clod by the hands of men?

Is it then, like the rest, not devoured because it is covered over by a clod? For this is so stated as though one who set a clod against it took the seed away, just as they hold that, when the clod was offered to Saturn, Jupiter was taken away,

and not rather that the clod, by covering the seed, made it the more diligently devoured. Then, in this way, Jupiter is the seed, not the cause of the seed, as was said a little before. But what are men to do who, when they interpret foolish things, do not find anything that may be said wisely?

*He has a sickle*, he says, *on account of agriculture*. Certainly, while he reigned, there was not yet any agriculture, and therefore his times are held to be earlier, as the same man himself interprets the little fables, because the first men lived on those seeds which the earth begot of its own accord.

Or did he receive the sickle after losing the scepter, so that he who in the first times had been an idle king became, when his son reigned, a toiling laborer?

Then he says that by some, boys were accustomed to be sacrificed to him, as by the Carthaginians, and by some even grown men, as by the Gauls[^53], because of all seeds the best kind is the human race. About this most cruel vanity, what need is there to say more? Let us rather notice and hold this: that these interpretations are not referred to the true God, the living, incorporeal, and unchangeable nature, from whom eternally blessed life is to be sought; but that their ends lie in things corporeal, temporal, changeable, and mortal.

*That Saturn is said in the fables to have castrated his father Caelum signifies*, he says, *that the divine seed is with Saturn, not with Caelum*[^54].

This, as far as can be understood, is because nothing is born in heaven from seeds. But behold, if Saturn is the son of Caelum, he is the son of Jupiter. For that Caelum is Jupiter they affirm innumerable times and diligently.

Thus these things, which do not come from the truth, for the most part overturn themselves even with no one pushing them. He says that he was called Chronos, which by the Greek term signifies a space of time, without which, he says, seed cannot be fruitful. These and many other things are said about Saturn, and all are referred to seed.

But at least Saturn, with such great power as this, would suffice for the seeds; why for these are other gods required, especially Liber and Libera—that is, Ceres? About whom again, as far as concerns the seed, he says as much as if he had said nothing about Saturn.

**20.** But in the rites of Ceres those Eleusinian mysteries are renowned, which among the Athenians were most celebrated. Of these this man interprets nothing except what concerns grain, which Ceres discovered, and Proserpina, whom she lost when Orcus carried her off;

and he says that this very Proserpina signifies the fruitfulness of seeds; and that, when this had failed at a certain time, and the earth mourned with that same barrenness, the opinion arose that the daughter of Ceres—that is, fruitfulness itself, which from "creeping forth" (proserpere) was called Proserpina—Orcus had carried off and detained among those below; and that, when this matter had been observed with public mourning, since that same fruitfulness returned again, gladness arose at Proserpina's restoration, and from this the solemnities were established. He then says that many things are handed down in her mysteries which pertain to nothing but the discovery of crops[^55].

**21.** Now, indeed, the rites of Liber, whom they set over the liquid seeds—and on this account not only over the liquids of fruits, of which wine in a certain way holds the chief place, but also over the seeds of living creatures—to what shamefulness they came, it irks me to tell, on account of the length of the discourse;

but on account of these men's proud dullness it does not irk me. Among the other things which, since they are many, I am compelled to pass over, he says that at the crossroads of Italy certain

rites of Liber were celebrated with such great license of shamefulness that in his honor the male private parts were worshiped, and that not with at least a somewhat more modest secrecy, but in the open, with exulting wickedness.

For this shameful member, during the festal days of Liber, was placed with great honor upon little carts and carried about, first in the countryside at the crossroads, and afterward all the way into the city[^56]. But in the town of Lavinium one whole month was assigned to Liber, during whose days all used the most disgraceful words, until that member had been carried across the forum and came to rest in its own place.

Upon this dishonorable member it was necessary that a most honorable mother of a family publicly place a crown[^57].

Thus, you see, the god Liber had to be placated for the outcome of the seeds; thus bewitchment had to be driven away from the fields—so that a matron was compelled to do in public what not even a harlot, if matrons were watching, ought to have been permitted to do in the theater.

On account of these things Saturn alone was not believed able to suffice for the seeds, so that the unclean soul might find occasions for multiplying gods, and, rightly forsaken by the one true God on account of its uncleanness, and prostituted through many false gods by a greed for greater uncleanness, might call these sacrileges sacred rites, and offer itself to be violated and polluted by the crowds of foul demons.

**22.** Now Neptune already had Salacia for his wife, whom they said to be the lower water of the sea: for what purpose was Venilia also joined to him, except that, without any cause of necessary rites, by the mere lust of a prostituted soul, the invitation of demons might be multiplied? But let the interpretation of this excellent theology be brought forth, which by the account it renders may restrain us from this rebuke.

*Venilia*, he says, *is the wave that comes to the shore; Salacia, the one that returns into the deep*[^58]. Why, then, are two goddesses made, when there is one wave that comes and returns?

Surely it is madness itself, the lust boiling over into many divinities. For although the water that goes and returns is not doubled, yet, by the occasion of this vanity, with two demons invited, the soul is the more defiled, which goes and does not return.

I ask you, Varro—or you who have read such writings of such learned men and boast that you have learned something great—interpret this for me; I will not say according to that eternal and unchangeable nature which alone is God, but at least according to the soul of the world and its parts, which you reckon to be the true gods.

That you made for yourselves a god, Neptune, of the part of the soul of the world which pervades the sea, is, somehow or other, an error of a more tolerable kind. Is the wave coming to the shore and returning into the deep, then, two parts of the world, or two parts of the soul of the world?

Who among you is so senseless as to think this wise? Why, then, did they make two goddesses for you, except that it was provided by your wise elders, not that more gods should rule you, but that more demons—which rejoice in these vanities and falsehoods—should possess you?

But why did that Salacia, through this interpretation, lose the lower part of the sea, by which she was subject to her husband? For just now, when you hold her to be the refluent wave, you placed her on the surface. Or, because he took Venilia as a concubine, did she, in anger, shut her husband out from the upper parts of the sea?

**23. 1.** Surely there is one earth, which we see full indeed of its living creatures, yet itself a great body among the elements and the lowest part of the world. Why do they will it to be a goddess? Is it because it is fruitful?

Why, then, are men not rather gods, who make it more fruitful by cultivating it—but when they plow, not when they adore? But, they say, the part of the soul of the world which pervades it makes it a goddess[^59].

As if the soul were not more evident in men, of which there is no question whether it exists; and yet men are not held to be gods, and—what is to be grievously lamented—by a marvelous and pitiable error they are made subject to worshiping and adoring those who are not gods and than whom they themselves are better.

And certainly the same Varro, in the same book about the select gods, affirms that there are three grades of soul in the whole of universal nature: one which passes through all the parts of the body that live, and has no sense, but only the vigor for living;

this force, he says, permeates in our body into the bones, nails, and hair; just as in the world trees are nourished and grow without sense, and live in a manner of their own: the second grade of soul, in which there is sense; this force reaches into the eyes, ears, nostrils, mouth, and touch: the third grade is the highest soul, which is called the mind (animus), in which intelligence is pre-eminent; of this all mortals are devoid except man.

This part of the soul of the world he calls God, but in us he calls it a genius. And he says that in the world the stones and the earth which we see, into which sense does not permeate, are as it were the bones and nails of God; but that the sun, moon, and stars, which we perceive and by which he himself perceives, are his senses; and that the ether is his mind; the force of which, reaching into the stars, makes these too gods; and through them, that which permeates into the earth is the goddess Tellus; but that which permeates from there into the sea and ocean is the god Neptune[^60].

**23. 2.** Let him return, then, from this which he thinks the natural theology, into which he had gone out as if for the sake of resting, wearied by these windings and turnings; let him return, I say, let him return to the civil; here I still hold him, for so long I treat of this one.

I do not yet say that, if the earth and the stones are like our bones and nails, they similarly have no intelligence, just as they lack sense; or that, if our bones and nails are said to have intelligence on the ground that they are in a man who has intelligence, then he who calls these things in the world gods is as foolish as the fool who calls bones and nails in us men. But these matters are perhaps to be dealt with among the philosophers; now, however, I still want this man as a political theologian.

For it can happen that, although he seems to have wished to lift his head a little into that, as it were, freedom of the natural theology, yet, still turning over this book and thinking that he was engaged in it, he looked back from there to it too, and said this for this reason: lest his elders, or other cities, be believed to have worshiped Tellus and Neptune in vain.

But this I say: the part of the world's mind which permeates through the earth, since the earth is one, why did it not also make one goddess, whom he says to be Tellus[^61]? And if it did so, where will Orcus be, the brother of Jupiter and Neptune, whom they call Father Dis? Where his consort Proserpina, who, according to another opinion set down in the same books, is held to be not the fruitfulness of the earth, but its lower part?

But if they say that the part of the world's mind, when it permeates the upper part of the earth, makes the god Father Dis; but when it permeates the lower part, the goddess Proserpina[^62]: what will that Tellus be?

For thus the whole that she was has been divided into these two parts and gods, so that one cannot find what she herself, as a third, is, or where she is; unless someone should say that those gods Orcus and Proserpina together are the one goddess Tellus, and that there are now not three, but either one or two;

and yet they are called three, are held to be three, are worshiped as three, with their own altars, their own shrines, rites, images, and priests, and through these also with their own deceitful demons, who debauch the prostituted soul.

Let it still be answered: what part of the earth does the part of the world's mind permeate, that it may make the god Tellumo? Not so, he says; but one and the same earth has a twofold force, both masculine, in that it produces seeds, and feminine, in that it receives and nourishes them; hence from the force of the female it was called Tellus, from that of the male Tellumo. Why, then, do the pontiffs—as he himself indicates—with two others also added, perform the divine rite to four gods, Tellus, Tellumo, Altor, and Rusor? About Tellus and Tellumo it has already been said. Why Altor? Because, he says, all things that are born are nourished from the earth. Why Rusor? Because, he says, all things roll back again (rursus) to the same place.

**24. 1.** The one earth, then, on account of this fourfold force, ought to have had four surnames, not to have made four gods; just as one Jupiter has so many surnames and one Juno has so many surnames, in all of which a manifold force is said to pertain to one god or one goddess, the multitude of surnames not making also a multitude of gods.

But surely, just as sometimes even the basest women grow weary and repent of the crowds of those whom they have sought out in lust, so the soul, made base and prostituted to unclean spirits, sometimes also grew irked at multiplying gods for itself, by which, to be defiled, it was laid prostrate—as much as it sometimes pleased it, so much did it sometimes irk it. For Varro himself too, as if ashamed of the very crowd, wishes Tellus to be one goddess.

*The same one*, he says, *they call the Great Mother; that she has a tambourine signifies that she is the orb of the earth; that she has towers on her head, the towns;*

*that she is fashioned sitting signifies that, while all things around her move, she herself does not move. That they made Galli serve this goddess signifies that those who lack seed ought to follow the earth; for in it all things are found.*

*That they fling themselves about before her teaches*, he says, *that those who cultivate the earth ought not to sit still; for there is always something to be done. The sound of cymbals signifies the clatter of the iron tools to be wielded and of the hands and of that work which is done in cultivating the field; and that it is of bronze, because the ancients cultivated it with bronze, before iron had been discovered.*

*They join to her*, he says, *a lion, unbound and tame, to show that there is no kind of land so remote and exceedingly wild that it is not fitting it be subdued and cultivated*[^63]. Then he adds and says that, because they called the mother Tellus by several names and surnames, she was reckoned to be several gods.

*They think Tellus to be Ops*, he says, *because she is made better by work (opus); the Mother, because she bears very many things; the Great one, because she bears food; Proserpina, because the crops creep forth (proserpere) from her; Vesta, because she is clothed (vestiri) with herbs.*

*Thus, he says, they not absurdly bring other goddesses back to this one*[^64]. If, then, she is one goddess—who indeed, when the truth is consulted, is not even that—why, in the meantime, is recourse had to many?

Let these many be the divinities of one—not so much many goddesses as names. But the authority of his erring elders presses him down and compels the same Varro, after this opinion, to waver.

For he adds and says: *With these the opinion of our elders about these goddesses, that they thought them to be several, does not conflict.* How does it not conflict, when it is a very different thing for one goddess to have many names, and for there to be many goddesses?

*But it can happen*, he says, *that the same thing is both one and that in it there are several things*[^65]. I grant that in one man there are several things; are there therefore also several men?

So in one goddess there are several things; are there therefore also several goddesses? But let them, as they will, divide, combine, multiply, fold back, and entangle.

**24. 2.** These are the renowned mysteries of Tellus and the Great Mother, in which everything is referred to mortal seeds and the practice of agriculture. Do, then, the tambourine, the towers, the Galli, the insane flinging about of limbs, the clatter of cymbals, the fashioning of lions—referred to these things and having this as their end—promise anyone eternal life? Do the castrated Galli serve this Great goddess for this reason: to signify that those who lack seed ought to follow the earth—as though that very servitude did not rather make them lack seed?

For do they, by following this goddess, when they lack it, acquire seed, or do they rather, by following this goddess, when they have it, lose seed? Is this to interpret, or to detest? Nor is it considered how greatly the malign demons have prevailed, who, although they dared not promise anything great by these rites, were able to exact things so cruel.

If the earth were not a goddess, men would lay hands on it by working, that they might obtain seeds through it, not also by raging against themselves, that they might lose seeds on account of it;

if it were not a goddess, it would become fruitful by the hands of others in such a way that it would not compel a man to become barren by his own hands.

Now, that in the rites of Liber an honorable matron crowned the male private parts while a multitude looked on—where, blushing and sweating, if there is any shame in human beings, the husband too perhaps stood by; and that, in the celebration of weddings, the new bride was bidden to sit upon the shaft of Priapus[^66]:

these are far more contemptible and trifling in comparison with this most cruel shamefulness, or most shameful cruelty, in which by demoniac rites both sexes are so mocked that neither is killed by its own wound.

There the bewitchment of the fields is feared; here the amputation of the limbs is not feared. There the modesty of the new bride is so dishonored that not even her fruitfulness, let alone her virginity, is taken away; here the virility is so amputated that the man is neither turned into a woman nor left a man.

**25.** And that Attis was not mentioned, nor was an interpretation of him sought by this man—in memory of the love of whom the Gallus is castrated.

But the learned and wise Greeks by no means kept silent about so holy and renowned an account. For on account of the springtime face of the earth, which is more beautiful than at other seasons, Porphyry, a noble philosopher, held that "Attis" signifies the flowers, and was castrated for this reason: because the flower falls before the fruit.

They compared, then, not the man himself, or as it were the man, who was called Attis, but his virile parts, to the flower[^67]. For those parts fell while he was living; nay rather, they did not fall, nor were they plucked, but were plainly torn off;

and when that flower was lost, no fruit afterward followed, but rather barrenness. What, then, is signified, they say, by what remained of him, by whatever remained after the castration? To what is it referred? What interpretation is brought forth from it?

Do they, by laboring in vain at this and finding nothing, persuade us that we ought rather to believe what report has spread and what has been committed to writing about the castrated man? Rightly did our Varro turn away from this and was unwilling to say it; for it was not hidden from a most learned man.

**26.** Likewise, about the effeminates consecrated to this same Great Mother against all the modesty of men and women,

who, down to yesterday, with dripping hair, faces whitened with paint, flowing limbs, and a womanly gait, went through the streets and alleys of Carthage, exacting even from the shopkeepers the means by which they might live shamefully,

Varro was unwilling to say anything, nor do I recall having read it anywhere. The interpretation failed, the account blushed, the discourse fell silent.

The Great Mother surpassed all her sons, the gods, not by the greatness of her divinity, but by that of her crime. Not even the monstrosity of Janus is compared with this monster.

He had in his images mere deformity, she in her rites a deformed cruelty; he had limbs added in stone, she limbs lost in men.

This disgrace not even all the many and so great debaucheries of Jupiter himself surpass. He, amid female seductions, defamed heaven with one Ganymede alone; she, with so many professed and public effeminates, both defiled the earth and did injury to heaven.

Saturn perhaps we could, in this kind of most shameful cruelty, either compare or even prefer to her, who is held to have castrated his father; but in the rites of Saturn men could be killed by the hands of others rather than castrate themselves by their own.

He devoured his sons, as the poets relate, and the natural philosophers interpret from this what they will; but, as history records, he killed them; yet the Romans did not adopt the custom by which the Carthaginians sacrificed their sons to him. But this Great Mother of the gods brought even castrated men into Roman temples and preserved this savagery and custom, being believed to aid the strength of the Romans by the cutting off of the virility of men.

What are, beside this evil, the thefts of Mercury, the wantonness of Venus, the debaucheries and shameful deeds of the rest, which we might bring forth from the books, were they not daily sung and danced in the theaters?

But what are these beside so great an evil, whose magnitude befitted the Great Mother alone? Especially since those things are said by the poets to have been invented, as though the poets had also invented this: that they are pleasing and acceptable to the gods.

That they should be sung or written, then, let it be the audacity or wantonness of the poets; but that they should be added to divine matters and honors, with the very divinities commanding and extorting them—what is this but a crime of the gods, nay rather a confession of the demons and a deception of the wretched?

But that very thing, that the Mother of the gods deserved to be worshiped by the consecration of castrated men, the poets did not invent, but chose rather to shudder at than to sing.

Is anyone, then, to be consecrated to these select gods, that after death he may live blessedly, to whom, having been consecrated, he cannot before death live honorably, subjected to such foul superstitions and bound to unclean demons?

But all these things, he says, are referred to the world. Let him see whether they are not rather referred to the unclean. But what cannot be referred to the world, which is shown to be in the world?

But we seek a mind which, trusting in the true religion, does not adore the world as its god, but praises the world as the work of God for God's sake, and, cleansed from worldly filth, arrives clean at the God who founded the world.

**27. 1.** These select gods we see, indeed, to have become more clearly known than the rest, yet not so that their merits might be made illustrious, but so that their reproaches might not be hidden; whence it is the more credible that they were men, as not only the writings of the poets but also those of the historians have handed down.

For what Virgil says—*First Saturn came from ethereal Olympus, fleeing the arms of Jupiter and an exile from his lost kingdom*[^68]—and what follows pertaining to this matter, the whole history of it Euhemerus lays open, which Ennius turned into Latin speech[^69]; whence, because those who wrote before us against errors of this kind, whether in Greek or in Latin, set down very many things, I did not see fit to dwell on it.

**27. 2.** When I consider the very physiologies by which learned and acute men try to turn these human matters into divine ones, I see nothing that could be brought back to anything but temporal and earthly works and a corporeal nature, or, even if invisible, yet a changeable one; which is in no way the true God.

But if this were at least done with significations befitting religiousness, it would indeed be a thing to be lamented that the true God was not announced and proclaimed by these things,

yet in some way to be borne, that such foul and shameful things were not done or commanded;

but as it is, since, in place of the true God, by whose indwelling alone the soul becomes happy[^70], it is wicked to worship either body or soul, how much more wicked is it so to worship these things, that neither the salvation nor the dignity of the worshiper's human body or soul is obtained!

Therefore, if by temple, priest, and sacrifice—which is owed to the true God—some element of the world or some created spirit is worshiped, even one that is not unclean and evil, it is not evil because the things by which it is worshiped are evil, but because such things ought to be offered only to him to whom such worship and service is owed.

But if by the stupidity or monstrousness of idols, by sacrifices of murdered men, by the crowning of the male genitals, by payment for fornication, by the cutting of limbs, by the severing of the genitals, by the consecration of effeminate men, by the festivals of impure and obscene games, anyone should contend that he is worshiping the one true God, that is, the Creator of every soul and body,

he does not sin because the one he worships ought not to be worshiped, but because he worships one who ought to be worshiped in a way in which he ought not to be worshiped.

But he who worships both by such things—that is, base and wicked things—and worships not the true God, that is, the maker of soul and body, but a creature, however free of fault, whether that be soul or body or soul and body together, sins twice against God:

in that he worships in place of him something that is not himself, and worships by such things as he himself ought not to be worshiped by, nor anything that is not himself.

But the manner in which these men worshiped—that is, how basely and wickedly—is plain to see;

what, however, or whom they worshiped would be obscure, were it not that their own history bears witness that those very things which they confess to be foul and base were rendered to the divinities at their terrifying demand;

whence it stands established, with all evasions set aside, that wicked demons and most unclean spirits were invited by this whole civil theology to take up their abode in senseless images, and through these images to take possession of foolish hearts as well.

**28.** What good is it, then, that the most learned and acute man Varro tries, by what looks like a subtle argument, to reduce and refer all these gods to heaven and earth?

He cannot do it; they slip out of his hands, they spring back, they glide away and fall down. For when he is about to speak of the females, that is, the goddesses, he says: *since, as I said in the first book about places, two principles of the gods have been observed, drawn from heaven and earth—from which the gods are called partly heavenly, partly earthly—just as in the earlier books we made our beginning from heaven when we spoke of Janus, whom some have said to be the heaven and others the world, so concerning the females we make the beginning of our writing from Tellus*[^71].

I sense how great a difficulty such a great mind suffers. For he is led by a kind of verisimilar reasoning, namely that heaven is what acts and earth is what is acted upon,

and so he attributes the masculine power to the one and the feminine to the other, and does not consider that it is rather he who does these things, who made them both.

Hence in the earlier book he interprets in this way the famous mysteries of the Samothracians, and he promises, as though most religiously, that by writing he will set forth, and send to them, things that are not known even to their own initiates.

For he says that there, from many indications, he has gathered that in the images one thing signifies heaven, another earth, and another the patterns of things which Plato calls ideas; he would have heaven understood as Jupiter, earth as Juno, and the ideas as Minerva—heaven as that by which something comes to be, earth as that from which it comes to be, the pattern as that according to which it comes to be.

On this matter I forbear to mention that Plato says these ideas have such power that it is not according to them that heaven made anything, but rather that even heaven itself was made according to them[^72].

This I do say: that in this book on the select gods he has lost that account of the three gods by which he had, as it were, embraced all things.

For he assigns the male gods to heaven and the females to earth; and among the females he placed Minerva, whom above he had set higher than heaven itself.

Then the male god Neptune is in the sea, which belongs to the earth rather than to heaven. Finally Dis Pater, who in Greek is called Πλούτων, though he too is male and the brother of both, is said to be an earthly god, holding the upper earth and having Proserpine as his wife in the lower. How, then, do they try to refer the gods to heaven and the goddesses to earth? What that is solid, what that is constant, what that is sober, what that is definite does this argument have?

But Tellus is the beginning of the goddesses, namely the Great Mother, in whose presence the insane vileness of effeminate and castrated men, gashing and throwing themselves about, raises its uproar. What is it, then, that is said—that Janus is the head of the gods and Tellus the head of the goddesses? Error does not make one head in the former case, nor does frenzy make a sound one in the latter.

Why do they strive in vain to refer these things to the world? Even if they could, no devout person worships the world in place of the true God; and yet the open truth proves that they cannot do even this. Let them rather refer these things to dead men and to the worst of demons, and no question will remain.

**29.** For all the things that are referred by them to the world, by what they call physical reasonings, out of the theology of these gods, might without any scruple of sacrilegious opinion be attributed rather to the true God, who made the world, the founder of every soul and every body. Let us observe this in the following way:

we worship God, not heaven and earth, the two parts of which this world consists; nor a soul or souls diffused through whatever living things, but God, who made heaven and earth and all that is in them; who made every soul, whether living in some way and lacking sense and reason, or also sentient, or also intelligent.

**30.** And to begin now to run through those works of the one and true God, on account of which these men, while trying as it were to give an honorable interpretation to their most base and most wicked rites, have made for themselves many false gods:

we worship that God who has established for the natures created by him the beginnings and ends of subsisting and of moving; who holds, knows, and orders the causes of things;

who founded the power of seeds; who gave the rational soul, which is called the mind, to whatever living beings he willed;

who bestowed the faculty and use of speech; who imparted the gift of foretelling the future to whatever spirits he pleased, and through whomever he pleases himself foretells the future, and through whomever he pleases drives away ill health;

who governs the beginnings, progress, and ends of wars themselves, when the human race must thus be corrected and chastened; who both created and rules the most violent and vehement fire of this world, in proportion to the temper of its immense nature;

who is the creator and governor of all waters; who made the sun, the brightest of corporeal lights, and gave it fitting power and motion; who does not withdraw his dominion and power even from the regions below; who supplies the seeds and nourishment of mortal creatures, whether dry or liquid, assigning them to their suitable natures; who establishes and makes the earth fruitful; who lavishes its fruits upon animals and human beings; who knows and orders not only the principal causes but also those that follow upon them; who fixed for the moon its own course; who provides the celestial and terrestrial pathways for changes of place; who granted to human minds, which he created, even the knowledge of various arts for the help of life and nature; who instituted the union of male and female as a help for the propagation of offspring; who, for human gatherings, indulged them with the gift of earthly fire for their readiest uses, which they might apply to hearths and lamps.

These are certainly the things which the most acute and learned man Varro labored to distribute among the select gods through I know not what physical interpretations—whether things he received from elsewhere or things he himself conjectured.

But all this the one true God does and performs, yet as God, that is, wholly everywhere, enclosed in no places,

bound by no chains, divisible into no parts, in no part changeable, filling heaven and earth with his present power, not with a needy nature.

And so he administers all the things he created in such a way that he also lets them put forth and carry out their own proper motions. For although they can be nothing without him, they are not what he is.

He does many things also through angels; but he does not make the angels blessed except from himself. So, although for certain causes he sends angels to human beings, he does not make human beings blessed from the angels, but from himself, just as he does the angels. From this one and true God we hope for eternal life.

**31.** For besides benefits of this kind, which he lavishes upon good and bad alike out of this administration of nature, of which we have said something, we have from him a great token of his great love, proper to the good.

For although for the fact that we are, that we live, that we behold heaven and earth, that we have mind and reason by which to seek out him who founded all these things, we can in no way render sufficient thanks: yet for this—that he did not utterly abandon us, who were burdened and overwhelmed with sins and turned away from the contemplation of his light and blinded by the love of darkness, that is, of iniquity—

and that he sent us his Word, who is his only Son, so that by his being born for us, having taken on flesh, and by his suffering, we might know how highly God valued man, and by that singular sacrifice might be cleansed from all our sins, and, with his Spirit pouring love into our hearts, might overcome all difficulties and come to eternal rest and the ineffable sweetness of contemplating him—what hearts, how many tongues, could claim to be enough to give him thanks?

**32.** This mystery of eternal life, from the very beginning of the human race, was proclaimed through angels by means of certain signs and sacraments suited to the times, as was fitting.

Then the Hebrew people was gathered into a single commonwealth, as it were, that might enact this sacrament, where, through some who knew and some who did not, it was foretold that what is being done from the coming of Christ until now and henceforth would come to pass; and afterward this same people was scattered among the nations for the sake of the testimony of the Scriptures, in which eternal salvation in Christ was foretold as to come.

For all things—not only the prophecies that are in words, nor only the precepts of life that form character and piety and are contained in those writings, but also the sacred rites, the priesthoods, the tabernacle or temple, the altars, the sacrifices, the ceremonies, the feast days, and whatever else pertains to that service which is owed to God and is in Greek properly called λατρεία—signified and foretold those things which, for the sake of the eternal life of the faithful in Christ, we believe to have been fulfilled, perceive to be in the course of fulfillment, and trust will be fulfilled.

**33.** Through this one and true religion, then, it could be made plain that the gods of the nations are most unclean demons, who, taking the occasions of departed souls or the appearance of worldly creatures, desire to be thought gods and rejoice with proud impurity in honors that are as it were divine but in fact wicked and base, and who begrudge to human souls their turning toward the true God. From their most monstrous and most impious domination man is set free when he believes in him who set before us, that we might rise up again, an example of as great a humility as that pride by which they fell.

To this class belong not only those of whom we have already said much, and others like them among the rest of the nations and lands, but also these of whom we are now treating, as though chosen into a senate of the gods;

but plainly chosen for the eminence of their crimes, not for the worth of their virtues.

While Varro tries to refer their rites, as it were, to natural reasonings, seeking to make base things honorable, he cannot find how they may fit and accord with these, since the real causes of those rites are not the ones he supposes, or rather wishes to be supposed.

For if not only these very causes, but any others whatever of this kind, were the case—although they would have nothing to do with the true God and eternal life,

which is what must be sought in religion—yet by some account or other rendered concerning the nature of things, they would somewhat soften the offense

which some seeming baseness or absurdity in the rites, when not understood, had caused;

as he tried to do in the case of certain plays of the theaters or mysteries of the shrines, where he did not absolve the theaters by likening them to the shrines, but rather condemned the shrines by likening them to the theaters;

yet he tried, in whatever way he could, that an account rendered, as it were, of natural causes might soothe the sense offended by horrible things.

**34.** But against this we find, as that most learned man himself has reported, that the causes of the sacred rites set down in the books of Numa Pompilius could in no way be tolerated, nor were they thought worthy either to be read and become known to the religious or even, once written, to be stored away in darkness.

Now I shall say what I had promised, in the third book of this work, that I would say in its proper place. For, as we read in the same Varro, in his book *On the Worship of the Gods*, *a certain Terentius had a farm at the Janiculum, and when his plowman, driving the plow near the tomb of Numa Pompilius, had dug up out of the ground the books of Numa, in which were written the causes of the institution of the sacred rites, he carried them into the City to the praetor. But the praetor, when he had looked over the openings of them, brought so great a matter to the senate.*

*And there, when the leading men had read certain of the causes why each thing had been instituted in the sacred rites, the senate agreed with the dead Numa, and the conscript fathers, religious men as they were, decreed that the praetor should burn those books.*[^73]

Let each believe what he thinks; indeed, let any distinguished defender of so great an impiety say whatever frenzied contentiousness may prompt him to say. For me it is enough to point out that the causes of the sacred rites, written down by King Pompilius, the founder of the Roman rites, ought to have become known neither to the people nor to the senate nor even to the priests themselves, and that Numa Pompilius himself, by an illicit curiosity, arrived at those secrets of the demons which he indeed wrote down, that he might have something from which, by reading, he could be reminded;

yet, king though he was and one who feared no one at all, he did not dare either to teach anyone these things or to destroy them by erasing or in any way consuming them. Thus what he wished no one to know, lest he teach men wicked things, yet feared to violate, lest he have the demons angry,

he buried where he thought it safe, not believing that a plow could come near his tomb. But the senate, since it feared to condemn the religious observances of their forefathers and was therefore compelled to agree with Numa, nevertheless judged those books to be so pernicious that it did not order them to be buried again, lest human curiosity seek out far more eagerly a matter now brought to light,

but ordered the nefarious records to be done away with by flames, so that—since they now reckoned it necessary to perform those rites—

men might err more tolerably with the causes of those rites unknown than be thrown into confusion as a city with them known.

**35.** For Numa himself too, to whom no prophet of God and no holy angel was sent, was driven to practice hydromancy, so that in water he might see images of the gods, or rather the mockeries of demons, from whom he might hear what he ought to establish and observe in the sacred rites. This kind of divination the same Varro says was brought from the Persians, and he records that both Numa himself and afterward the philosopher Pythagoras made use of it; and he reports that, with blood applied, the dead in the lower world are also questioned, and he says it is called in Greek νεκυομαντεία[^74],

which—whether it be called hydromancy or necromancy—is the very same thing, where the dead seem to divine.

By what arts these things are done, let those who practice them see to it. For I do not wish to say that these arts were wont, even before the coming of our Savior, to be forbidden in the very cities of the nations by their laws and to be punished with the severest penalty.

I do not wish, I say, to say this; for perhaps such things were then permitted.

Yet it was by these arts that Pompilius learned those sacred rites,

whose performance he made known but whose causes he buried—so much did even he fear what he had learned—and the books that betrayed those causes the senate burned.

Why, then, does Varro interpret for me certain other, I know not what, supposedly natural causes of those rites?

For if those books had contained such things, they would certainly not have been burned; or else the conscript fathers would likewise have burned these books of Varro, written and published to Caesar the pontiff.

Now because Numa Pompilius drew off water, that is, carried it away, from which he might perform hydromancy, he is therefore said to have had the nymph Egeria as his wife, as is set forth in the book of Varro mentioned above[^75]. For thus are deeds wont to be turned into fables by a sprinkling of falsehoods. In that hydromancy, then, that most curious Roman king learned both the sacred rites, which the pontiffs were to keep in their books, and their causes, which he wished no one but himself to know.

And so he made those causes, written down separately, die in a manner with him, when he took care that they should thus be withdrawn from men's knowledge and buried. Either, therefore, the foul and harmful desires of the demons were there written down, so that from them the whole of that civil theology might appear execrable even to such men as had taken up so many shameful things in those very rites; or else all those whom, by so long an antiquity of time, nearly all the peoples of the nations had believed to be immortal gods were betrayed as nothing other than dead men, while those same demons took delight in such rites—demons who, by certain attestations of deceitful miracles, were substituting themselves to be worshiped in place of the dead men whom they had caused to be thought gods.

But by the hidden providence of the true God it came about that, having been won over to Pompilius his friend by those arts whereby hydromancy could be performed, they were permitted to confess all those things, and yet—that he might, being about to die, burn rather than bury them—they were not permitted to advise him; while, that they might not become known,

they could neither resist the plow by which they were dug up, nor the pen of Varro, by which the things that were done concerning this matter have come down to our remembrance.

For they cannot do what they are not permitted to accomplish; and they are permitted, by the deep and just judgment of the most high God, according to the deserts of those whom it is just that they should either merely afflict, or even subject and deceive.

But how pernicious those writings were judged to be, or how alien to the worship of the true divinity, can be understood from this: that the senate preferred to burn what Pompilius had hidden rather than to fear what he had feared, who could not dare to do this.

He, therefore, who does not wish to have even now a godly life, let him seek the eternal one by such rites; but he who does not wish to have fellowship with the malignant demons, let him not dread the harmful superstition by which they are worshiped, but acknowledge the true religion by which they are exposed and overcome.
