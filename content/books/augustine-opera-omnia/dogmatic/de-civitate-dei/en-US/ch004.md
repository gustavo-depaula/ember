# Book II: How greatly the worship of the gods produces depraved morals

**1**. If the weak sense of human custom did not dare to resist the clear reason of truth, but submitted its languor to wholesome teaching as to a medicine, until it were healed by divine help with the faith of piety obtaining it, there would be no need of much discourse to refute the error of any empty opinion in those who think rightly and explain their thoughts in sufficient words. But now, since that is the greater and fouler sickness of foolish minds, by which they defend their irrational impulses—even after reason has been fully rendered, as much as is owed by man to man—as if it were reason and truth itself, whether through excessive blindness, by which not even open things are discerned, or through the most obstinate stubbornness, by which even the things that are discerned are not endured, there arises a necessity of speaking more copiously, for the most part, on clear matters, presenting them as it were not to be looked upon by those gazing, but in a certain way to be touched by those groping and shutting their eyes.

And yet what end of disputing and measure of speaking will there be, if we should think we must always reply to those who reply? For those who either cannot understand what is said, or are so hard with adversity of mind that, even if they have understood, they do not obey, reply, as it is written, and *speak iniquity*[^1], and are untiringly vain. If we should wish to refute their contrary sayings as often as they have determined, with an obstinate brow, not to consider what they say, while in some way or other they contradict our disputations, you see how endless and toilsome and fruitless it would be. Wherefore I would not wish either you yourself, my son Marcellinus, or others whom this labor of ours usefully and freely serves in the love of Christ, to be such judges of my writings as always to desire a reply when they have heard something contradicted in what is read, lest they become like those *little women* whom the Apostle mentions, *always learning and never arriving at the knowledge of the truth*[^2].

## What was said in the first book.

**2**. In the preceding book, then, when I had set out to speak about the city of God, from which this whole work, with his help, was taken in hand, it occurred to me that I must first resist those who attribute to the Christian religion these wars by which this world is worn down, and especially the recent devastation of the city of Rome by the barbarians,

by which they are forbidden to serve demons with unspeakable sacrifices—when rather they ought to attribute this to Christ: that for the sake of his name, contrary to the established practice and custom of wars, the barbarians offered them free, as places to which they might flee, the religious and most spacious places; and in many things they so honored the service rendered to Christ—not only true service, but even that feigned out of fear—that what it would have been permitted to do against them by the law of war they judged to be unlawful for themselves.

Thence arose the question, why these divine benefits reached even the impious and ungrateful, and why likewise those hard things which were done in a hostile manner afflicted the pious together with the impious?

This question, spread over many points (for in all the daily gifts of God or disasters of men, both of which often befall those who live well and those who live ill mingled and without distinction, it is wont to move many), I dwelt on for a while, in order to resolve it according to the necessity of the work undertaken: most of all to console the holy and piously chaste women, against whom something was perpetrated by the enemy that brought the pain of shame, even if it did not take away the firmness of chastity, lest they should regret their life, who have no ground on which they can regret any wickedness.

Then I said a few things against those who, with most shameless effrontery, harass the Christians afflicted by those adverse things, and especially the modesty of the women who were humbled, though chaste and holy—men who are most worthless and most irreverent, far degenerate from those very Romans whose many illustrious deeds are praised and celebrated in the memory of literature, indeed strongly opposed to the glory of those men.

For they had made Rome—begotten and increased by the labors of the ancients—fouler while it stood than when it fell, since indeed in its ruin stones and timbers fell, but in the life of these men there fell all the defenses and ornaments not of walls but of morals, when their hearts burned with more deadly desires than the roofs of that city did with fires.

With these things said, I first brought the book to an end. Next, then, I set out to say what evils that city suffered from its origin, whether within itself or in the provinces already subject to it, all of which they would attribute to the Christian religion, if even then the gospel teaching had resounded most freely with its testimony against their false and deceitful gods.

## The learned and the unlearned alike oppose us.

**3**. But remember that, in recalling these things, I am still dealing with the unskilled, from whose lack of skill that common proverb too arose: "rain is lacking, the Christians are the cause."[^3] For those of them who, trained in liberal studies, love history, know these things most easily; but, in order to make the crowds of the uneducated most hostile to us, they pretend not to know, and strive to confirm this among the crowd: that the disasters by which the human race must be afflicted at fixed intervals of places and times happen by reason of the Christian name, which against their gods is spread through all the world with immense fame and most illustrious renown.

Let them, then, recall with us, before Christ had come in the flesh, before his name became known to the peoples with that glory which they envy in vain, by what calamities Roman affairs were worn down in manifold and various ways, and in these let them defend their gods, if they can, if they are worshiped for this purpose, that their worshipers may not suffer these evils; of which, if they now suffer any, they contend that it is to be imputed to us. For why did they allow those things which I am about to relate to befall their worshipers, before the proclaimed name of Christ offended them and forbade their sacrifices?

## Dishonorable mysteries of the goddess Caelestis are celebrated at Carthage.

**4**. First, why were their gods unwilling to take care that they should not have the worst morals? For the true God deservedly neglected those by whom he was not worshiped; but why did those gods, from whose worship the most ungrateful men complain that they are forbidden, aid their worshipers by no laws to live well? Surely it was fitting that, just as these took care of those gods' rites, so those should take care of these men's deeds.

But it is answered that everyone is wicked by his own will. Who would deny this? Nevertheless it pertained to the gods, as counselors, not to hide from their peoples and worshipers the precepts of a good life, but to offer them by clear proclamation, even to confront and reprove sinners through seers, openly to threaten penalties to evildoers, to promise rewards to those who live rightly. What such thing ever rang out with a ready and prominent voice in the temples of those gods?

We too once came, as young men, to the spectacles and mockeries of the sacrileges; we watched the possessed, we listened to the musicians, we were delighted by the most shameful games which were exhibited to the gods and goddesses, to the virgin Caelestis and to Berecynthia, the mother of all[^4], before whose litter, on the solemn day of her washing, such things were sung in public by the most worthless stage-players as it would befit—I do not say the mother of the gods, but the mother of any senators whatever or of any honorable men, indeed such things as it would not befit even the mother of the stage-players themselves to hear. For human modesty has something toward parents which not even wickedness itself can take away. That baseness of obscene words and deeds, then, the stage-players themselves would be ashamed to perform at home, for the sake of rehearsal, before their own mothers, which they performed in public before the mother of the gods, with a most crowded multitude of both sexes watching and listening. If she could be present there, drawn by enticing curiosity and surrounded by the crowd, she ought at least to have gone away confounded, her chastity offended.

What are sacrileges, if those are sacred rites? Or what is defilement, if that is a washing? And these were called *courses*, as if a banquet were being celebrated, at which the unclean demons might be fed as it were with their own feasts. For who would not perceive what kind of spirits are delighted by such obscenities—unless he either does not know whether there are any unclean spirits at all deceiving under the name of gods, or leads such a life in which he both wishes these rather than the true God to be propitious and dreads them when angry?

**5**. I would by no means wish to have as a judge in this matter those who study to be delighted by the vices of a most shameful custom rather than to struggle against them, but that very Nasica Scipio, who was chosen by the senate as the best man, by whose hands the image of that same demon was received and carried into the City[^5]. He would tell us whether he would wish his own mother to deserve so well of the commonwealth that divine honors should be decreed to her; just as it is established that both Greeks and Romans and other nations decreed such things to certain mortals whose benefits toward themselves they had highly esteemed, and believed them to have been made immortal and received into the number of the gods[^6]. Surely he would wish so great a happiness for his mother, if it could be done.

Further, if we then asked him whether he would wish those base things to be celebrated among her divine honors: would he not cry out that he preferred his mother to lie dead without any sensation rather than to live as a goddess for this, that she should gladly hear those things? Far be it that a senator of the Roman people, endowed with that mind by which he forbade a theater to be built in the city of brave men, should so wish his mother to be worshiped that she should be propitiated by such rites as a matron would be offended by such words. Nor would he in any way believe that the modesty of a praiseworthy woman could be so changed into its contrary by divinity that her worshipers should invoke her with such honors as the insults which, if hurled at anyone while she lived among men, unless she closed her ears and withdrew herself, both her kinsmen and her husband and her children would blush for her.

Accordingly such a mother of the gods—such as it would shame even the worst man to have as a mother—when she was about to occupy the minds of the Romans, sought the best man, not one whom she might make so by admonishing and helping, but one whom she might delude by deceiving, like her of whom it is written: *but a woman hunts the precious souls of men*[^7], so that that soul of great natural gifts, exalted as it were by this divine testimony and truly thinking itself best, should not seek true piety and religion, without which every talent, however praiseworthy, fades and falls through pride. How, then, did that goddess seek the best man except treacherously, when she seeks in her own rites such things as the best men abhor to apply to their banquets?

**6**. Hence it is that they did not take care about the life and morals of the cities and peoples by whom those divinities were worshiped, so that they permitted them to be filled with such horrible and detestable evils, and to become most wicked, without any terrible prohibition of theirs—not in the field and the vines, not in the house and money, not, finally, in the body itself, which is subject to the mind, but in the very mind, in the very soul that rules the flesh.

Or if they did forbid it, let this rather be shown, let this be proved. And let them not boast to us of some whispers or other, breathed into the ears of a very few and handed down as if by a secret religion, by which uprightness and chastity of life are learned[^8];

but let there be pointed out or recalled places at some time consecrated to such assemblies—not where games were performed with the obscene voices and motions of actors, nor where the Fugalia were celebrated with all license of baseness poured out (and truly Fugalia, but of modesty and honor);

but where the peoples might hear what the gods commanded about restraining avarice, breaking ambition, reining in luxury, where the wretched might learn what Persius rebukes them for needing to learn, saying: *learn, O wretched ones, and know the causes of things: what we are and for what end we are born to live, what order has been given, and where and from what point the turn around the goal is gentle, what limit there is to wealth, what it is lawful to wish for, what use harsh coin has, how much it is fitting to bestow on one's fatherland and dear kinsmen, what God has commanded you to be, and in what part of human affairs you have been placed*[^9].

Let it be told in what places the precepts of these gods who teach such things were wont to be recited and frequently heard by their worshiping peoples, just as we have shown that churches were instituted for this purpose wherever the Christian religion is spread.

**7**. Or will they perhaps remind us of the schools and disputations of the philosophers? First, these are not Roman but Greek; or if they are now Roman for this reason, that Greece too became a Roman province, they are not the precepts of gods but the inventions of men, who, endowed with the keenest minds, tried as best they could, by reasoning, to track down what lay hidden in the nature of things, what in morals was to be sought and what shunned, what in the very rules of reasoning was drawn by a fixed connection, or what was not consequent or even contradictory. And some of them found certain great things, insofar as they were divinely aided; but insofar as they were hindered in a human way, they erred—most of all when divine providence justly resisted their pride, so that it might show, even by comparison with these men, the way of piety rising from humility to things above; whence there will later be a place for us, in the will of God the true Lord, to inquire and discuss.

But nevertheless, if the philosophers found anything that could be enough for leading a good life and attaining a blessed one: how much more justly would divine honors be decreed to such men!

how much better and more honorably would his books be read in a temple of Plato than, in the temples of demons, the Galli be castrated, the effeminate consecrated, the madmen gashed, and whatever else either cruel or base, either basely cruel or cruelly base, is wont to be celebrated in the rites of such gods!

how much more satisfactory it was, for instructing the youth in justice, that the laws of the gods be publicly recited than that the laws and institutions of the ancestors be vainly praised!

For all the worshipers of such gods, as soon as lust has driven them, *steeped*, as Persius says, *in burning poison*[^10], look more to what Jupiter did than to what Plato taught or Cato judged.

Hence in Terence a shameful youth looks at a certain picture painted on a wall, *where this picture was: how Jupiter, they say, once sent a golden shower into Danae's lap*[^11], and from this so great an authority he furnishes a defense for his own baseness, when he boasts that in it he imitates a god. *But what a god!* he says; *one who shakes the temples of heaven with his topmost thunder. Should I, a mere little man, not do that? I did indeed do it, and gladly*[^12].

## The mystical rites of the gods are as base as the theatrical ones.

**8**. But, it is said, these things are not handed down in the rites of the gods, but in the fables of the poets. I do not wish to say that those mystical things are baser than these theatrical ones; this I say, which history convicts those who deny it: that those very games, in which the fictions of the poets reign, the Romans introduced into the rites of their gods not through unskilled compliance, but the gods themselves brought it about, by harshly commanding and in a certain way extorting, that they be solemnly performed for them and consecrated to their honor; which in the first book I touched on with a brief mention[^13].

For when a pestilence was growing worse, stage-shows were first instituted at Rome by the authority of the pontiffs[^14]. Who, then, in leading his life, would not think that those things are rather to be followed by him which are acted in games instituted by divine authority, than those which are written in laws promulgated by human counsel? If the poets falsely reported Jupiter an adulterer, surely the chaste gods ought to have been angry and to have taken vengeance because so great a wickedness was fabricated through human games—not because it was neglected.

And these are the more tolerable of the stage-shows, namely comedies and tragedies—that is, the fables of the poets to be acted in the spectacles—composed with much baseness of matter, but at least with no obscenity of words, as many other things are; which boys are even compelled by old men to read and learn among the studies that are called honorable and liberal.

## The Greeks were permitted to bring infamy upon another in the theater.

**9**. But what the ancient Romans thought about this, Cicero testifies in the books which he wrote *On the Republic*, where Scipio, disputing, says: *comedies could never have proven their shameful deeds acceptable in the theaters, unless the custom of life allowed it*[^15]. And the more ancient Greeks indeed preserved a certain consistency with their faulty opinion, among whom it was even permitted by law that comedy might say whatever it wished, about whomever it wished, by name.

And so, as Africanus speaks in those same books, *whom did it not touch—or rather, whom did it not harass? whom did it spare? Granted, it injured base popular men, seditious in the commonwealth, Cleon, Cleophon, Hyperbolus. Let us bear with it*, he says, *although it is better for such citizens to be branded by a censor than by a poet. But that Pericles, when with the greatest authority he had for very many years governed his city at home and in war, should be assailed in verses and that these be acted on the stage, was no more fitting than if our Plautus*, he says, *or Naevius had wished to speak ill of Publius and Gnaeus Scipio, or Caecilius of Marcus Cato.*

Then a little after: *our Twelve Tables*, he says, *on the contrary, though they had made very few things punishable by death, thought this also was to be made so among them: if anyone had chanted or composed a song which brought infamy or disgrace upon another. Excellently. For our life ought to be exposed to the judgments of magistrates and to lawful disputes, not to the talents of poets, nor ought we to hear reproach except on the condition that it be permitted to reply and to defend ourselves in a trial*[^16].

These things I have thought ought to be excerpted word for word from Cicero's fourth book *On the Republic*, some things being either omitted or slightly changed for the sake of easier understanding. For it pertains much to the matter which I am endeavoring to explain, if I shall be able. He then says other things and so concludes this passage, in order to show that it displeased the ancient Romans either that any living man should be praised on the stage or that he should be reviled.

But, as I said, the Greeks wished this to be permitted—more shamelessly indeed, yet more consistently—since they saw that reproaches were acceptable and pleasing to their gods, not only those of men but even of the gods themselves, in the stage-fables, whether those things were fabricated by the poets or their real shameful deeds were recalled and acted in the theaters; and would that these had seemed to their worshipers worthy only of laughter, and not also of imitation. For it would have been too proud to spare the reputation of the leaders of the city and of the citizens, where the divinities were unwilling that their own reputation be spared.

**10**. For as for what is brought forward in defense—that those things are not said truly against the gods, but falsely and fictitiously—that very thing is more wicked, if you consult the piety of religion; but if you consider the malice of the demons, what is more cunning for deceiving, what more crafty? For when a reproach is hurled at a good and useful leader of the fatherland, is it not the more unworthy the more remote it is from the truth and the more foreign to his life? What punishments, then, suffice, when so unspeakable, so signal an injury is done to God?

But the malign spirits, whom these people think gods, wish even the shameful deeds which they did not commit to be told of themselves, provided that they enmesh human minds in these opinions as in nets and drag them with themselves to the predestined punishment,

whether men committed those things whom they rejoice to have held as gods—men who rejoice in human errors, on whose behalf they even interpose themselves to be worshiped by a thousand arts of harming and deceiving[^17]; or whether those crimes are not even the true crimes of any men, which nevertheless the most deceitful spirits gladly accept to be invented about the divinities,

so that an authority sufficiently suited, as it were brought down from heaven itself to the earth, may seem to exist for perpetrating wicked and base deeds.

Since, therefore, the Greeks felt themselves to be the servants of such divinities, they thought that in no way ought they to be spared by the poets amid so many and so great theatrical reproaches of them,

either desiring to be made even thus similar to their gods, or fearing lest, by seeking a more honorable reputation for themselves and in this way preferring themselves to them, they should provoke them to wrath.

**11**. To this consistency it pertains that they judged even the stage-actors of those same fables worthy of no small honor of the city, since indeed—which is also recalled in that book *On the Republic*—Aeschines the Athenian, a most eloquent man, when as a young man he had acted tragedies, took up public affairs, and the Athenians often sent Aristodemus, likewise a tragic actor, as an envoy to Philip on the greatest matters of peace and war[^18]. For it was not thought consistent, when they saw the same arts and the same stage-games acceptable even to their gods, to reckon those through whom they were performed in the place and number of the infamous.

These things the Greeks did basely indeed, but certainly altogether in keeping with their gods, since they did not dare to withdraw the life of citizens from being torn by the tongues of poets and actors, by whom they saw the life of the gods carped at, with those very gods willing and glad; and the very men through whom those things were performed in the theaters—things which they had come to know were pleasing to the divinities to whom they were subject—they thought ought not only not to be despised in the city, but even to be most highly honored. For what cause could they find why they should honor priests, because through them they offered victims acceptable to the gods, and hold the stage-players reproachful, through whom they had learned, by the gods' own admonition, that that pleasure or honor was exhibited to the gods who demanded it and who, if it were not done, grew angry?

Especially since Labeo, whom they proclaim most skilled in matters of this kind[^19], distinguishes good divinities from evil divinities even by this diversity of worship, asserting that the evil gods are propitiated by slaughters and mournful supplications, but the good ones by glad and pleasant observances, such as are, as he himself says, games, banquets, lectisternia. What sort of thing all this is, we will later, if God help, discuss more carefully[^20]. Now, as far as concerns the present matter, whether all things are attributed to all of them as good in a mixed way (for it is not fitting that there be evil gods, since rather these, because they are unclean spirits, are all evil), or whether, by a fixed distinction, as it seemed to Labeo, those observances are distributed to the one kind and these to the other, the Greeks most fittingly hold both in honor—both the priests, through whom the victims are administered, and the stage-players, through whom the games are exhibited—lest they be convicted of doing an injury either to all their gods, if the games too are pleasing to all, or, what is more unworthy, to those whom they think good, if the games are loved by them alone.

**12**. But the Romans, as Scipio boasts in that disputation *On the Republic*, were unwilling to have their life and reputation subjected to the reproaches and injuries of the poets, even sanctioning it with death if anyone dared to compose such a song. This indeed they established honorably enough as regards themselves, but as regards their gods proudly and irreligiously; for though they knew that they were torn not only patiently but even gladly by the reproaches and curses of the poets, they judged themselves rather than them to be unworthy of injuries of this kind, and protected themselves from them even by law, but mingled those things into the very solemn rites of the gods.

Do you then, after all, Scipio, praise that this license was denied to the Roman poets, that they might not inflict a reproach on any of the Romans, when you see that they spared none of your gods? Did the estimation of your senate-house seem to you to be held of more worth than the Capitol—indeed, of Rome alone than of the whole heaven—so that poets were forbidden even by law to exercise a slanderous tongue against your citizens, and against your gods they might hurl such great insults in safety, with no senator, no censor, no leader, no pontiff forbidding it? It was unworthy, you see, that Plautus or Naevius should speak ill of Publius and Gnaeus Scipio, or Caecilius of Marcus Cato, and it was worthy that your Terence should incite the wickedness of young men by the shameful deed of Jupiter best and greatest?

**13**. But he would perhaps answer me, if he were alive: "How could we wish those things to be unpunished which the gods themselves wished to be sacred, since they both brought stage-shows—where such things are celebrated, recited, and acted—into Roman customs, and commanded them to be dedicated and exhibited for their own honors?" Why, then, were they not rather from this understood to be not true gods, nor at all worthy that that commonwealth should offer them divine honors?

For those whom it would in no way befit and in no way be fitting to worship, if they demanded games to be performed with insults to the Romans, how, I ask, were they thought worthy of worship—how were they not understood to be detestable spirits—who, from a desire of deceiving, demanded that their own crimes be celebrated among their honors?

And likewise the Romans, although they were now pressed by a harmful superstition, so as to worship those gods whom they saw had wished stage-basenesses to be consecrated to them, nevertheless, mindful of their own dignity and modesty, by no means honored the actors of such fables after the manner of the Greeks, but, as the same Scipio speaks in Cicero: *since they held the art of acting and the whole stage in reproach, they wished that kind of men not only to lack the honor of the rest of the citizens, but even to be removed from their tribe by the censor's mark*[^21]. A prudence indeed illustrious and to be numbered among Roman praises; but I would wish that it followed itself, that it imitated itself. For behold, rightly, whoever of the Roman citizens had chosen to be a stage-player, not only was no place given him for honor, but he was even by the censor's mark not at all allowed to keep his own tribe.

O spirit of a city greedy for praise and genuinely Roman! But let it be answered to me: by what consistent reasoning are stage-men repelled from all honor, and stage-games mingled with the honors of the gods? Roman virtue had long not known those theatrical arts, which, if they were sought for the gratification of human pleasure, crept in by the vice of human morals.

The gods demanded that they be exhibited to them: how, then, is the stage-player cast out, through whom a god is worshiped? And with what face is the actor of that theatrical baseness branded, if the exactor of it is adored?

In this controversy let the Greeks and Romans contend. The Greeks think they rightly honor stage-men, because they worship gods who demand stage-games; but the Romans do not allow even the plebeian tribe—how much less the senatorial chamber—to be dishonored by stage-men. In this dispute a reasoning of this kind resolves the sum of the question. The Greeks propose: if such gods are to be worshiped, surely such men too are to be honored. The Romans assume: but in no way are such men to be honored. The Christians conclude: in no way, then, are such gods to be worshiped.

## Plato rejects the theatrical works of the poets; his precepts come close to the law of the Romans.

**14**. Then we ask, the poets themselves, the composers of such fables, who are forbidden by the law of the Twelve Tables to harm the reputation of citizens, why, hurling such reproachful insults at the gods, they are not held dishonorable like the stage-players. By what reasoning is it right that the actors of the poetic fictions and of ignominious gods be made infamous, while the authors are honored? Or is the palm perhaps rather to be given to the Greek Plato, who, when by reason he was forming what a city ought to be, judged that the poets, as adversaries of truth, ought to be driven from the city? He indeed both took ill the injuries of the gods and was unwilling that the minds of citizens be tinted and corrupted by fictions[^22].

Compare now the humanity of Plato, driving the poets from the city to keep the citizens from being deceived, with the divinity of the gods, demanding stage-games for their honor.

He, that such things might not even be written, though he did not persuade by disputing, yet counseled the levity and lasciviousness of the Greeks; these, that such things might even be acted, extorted by commanding from the gravity and modesty of the Romans. And they wished not only that these things be acted, but that they be dedicated to themselves, consecrated to themselves, solemnly exhibited to themselves.

To which, after all, would a city more honorably decree divine honors? To Plato, forbidding these base and unspeakable things, or to the demons rejoicing in this deception of men, whom he could not persuade of the truth?

This Plato Labeo thought ought to be reckoned among the demigods, like Hercules, like Romulus. But he sets demigods before heroes; yet he places both among the divinities. Nevertheless, I do not doubt that this man, whom he calls a demigod, ought to be preferred not only to heroes but even to the gods themselves.

But the laws of the Romans come close to the disputations of Plato, since he condemns all poetic fictions, while these take from the poets at least the license of speaking ill of men; he removes the poets from the dwelling of the city itself, these remove at least the actors of poetic fables from the society of the city; and if they dared anything against the gods who demand stage-games, perhaps they would remove them entirely. In no way, then, could the Romans receive or hope to receive from their gods laws for instituting good morals or correcting evil ones, gods whom they overcome and convict by their own laws.

For those gods demand stage-games for their honor; these men repel stage-men from all honors. Those command the reproaches of the gods to be celebrated for themselves by poetic fictions; these deter the shamelessness of the poets from the reproaches of men. But that demigod Plato both resisted the lust of such gods, and showed, from the disposition of the Romans, what ought to be accomplished, who was altogether unwilling that the poets themselves—either lying at their own pleasure or setting before wretched men the worst deeds of the gods as things to be imitated—should live in a well-ordered city.

We indeed proclaim Plato neither a god nor a demigod, nor do we compare him to any holy angel of the supreme God, nor to a truthful prophet, nor to any apostle, nor to any martyr of Christ, nor to any Christian man; the reason of this opinion of ours will be explained, God prospering it, in its place[^23]. But nevertheless we judge that he—since they themselves wish him to have been a demigod—ought to be preferred, if not to Romulus and Hercules (although no historian or poet has said or invented that this man either killed his brother or perpetrated any shameful deed), at least certainly to Priapus, or to some Dog-headed one, or finally to Fever, divinities which the Romans received partly as foreign, partly consecrated as their own.

How, then, could such gods forbid, by good precepts and laws, the great evils of soul and morals as they threatened, or take care to root them out when implanted—gods who even took care that shameful deeds be sown and increased, desiring that such deeds, either their own or as if their own, become known to the peoples through theatrical celebrations, so that, as if by divine authority, the most worthless human lust might of its own accord be kindled, Cicero crying out against this in vain, who, when he treated of the poets, says: *when the shout and approval of the people, as of some great and wise master, has come to them, what darkness do they spread, what fears do they bring in, what desires do they inflame!*[^24]

**15**. But what reasoning—or rather, what flattery—is there in the choosing even of those false gods themselves? When that Plato, whom they wish to be a demigod, laboring with such great disputations lest human morals be corrupted by the evils of the soul, which are above all to be guarded against, they thought worthy of no sacred shrine, and preferred their own Romulus to many gods, although their more secret doctrine too commends him as a demigod rather than a god[^25].

For they even instituted a flamen for him, which kind of priesthood so excelled among Roman rites, as the apex testifies, that they had only three flamens instituted for three divinities[^26]: the Dial flamen for Jupiter, the Martial for Mars, the Quirinal for Romulus. For by the goodwill of the citizens, Romulus, as if received into heaven, was afterward named Quirinus.

And through this Romulus was preferred in this honor even to Neptune and Pluto, Jupiter's brothers, and to Saturn himself, their father, so that, as something great, the priesthood which they had assigned to Jupiter they assigned also to him, and to Mars as if his father, perhaps on his own account.

**16**. But if the Romans could have received laws of living from their gods, they would not, some years after Rome was founded, have borrowed from the Athenians the laws of Solon[^27], which, however, they did not keep as they received them, but tried to make better and more correct—although Lycurgus pretended that he had instituted laws for the Lacedaemonians by the authority of Apollo[^28], which the Romans prudently were unwilling to believe, and therefore did not receive them from there. Numa Pompilius, who succeeded Romulus in the kingship, is said to have established certain laws which indeed would in no way suffice for governing a city[^29], and who established for them many sacred rites as well; yet he is not reported to have received those same laws from the divinities.

The evils of soul, then, the evils of life, the evils of morals, which are so great that by these their most learned men affirm that commonwealths perish even while their cities stand, their gods took no care that they should not befall their worshipers; on the contrary, that they might be increased, as was disputed above, they took care in every way.

## The Romans were unjust to the Sabines, to Collatinus, to Camillus.

**17**. Or were laws perhaps not established by the divinities for the Roman people for this reason, that, as Sallust says, *right and good among them prevailed not by laws more than by nature*[^30]? From this *right and good*, I suppose, the Sabine women were carried off. For what is more just and better than that the daughters of others, drawn in by the trick of a spectacle, should not be received from their parents, but carried off by force, as each was able?

For if the Sabines acted unjustly in refusing them when asked, how much more unjust it was to seize them when not given! But more justly could a war be waged with that nation which had refused its daughters to its neighbors and borderers when sought in marriage, than with that which sought back those carried off. The former, then, should rather have been done; there Mars would aid his son in fighting, that he might avenge by arms the injury of the marriages refused, and in that way arrive at the women he had wished for.

For by some right of war, perhaps, the victor might justly carry off those unjustly refused; but by no right of peace did he seize those not given, and he waged an unjust war with their parents who were justly angry.

This indeed turned out more usefully and happily, that, although for the memory of that trick the spectacle of the circus games remained, yet the example of the deed did not please in that city and empire, and the Romans more easily erred in this—in consecrating Romulus as a god to themselves after that injustice—than in permitting, by any law or custom, his deed in carrying off the women to be imitated.

From this right and good, after King Tarquin had been expelled with his children—whose son had violently oppressed Lucretia by rape—the consul Junius Brutus compelled Lucius Tarquinius Collatinus, the husband of that same Lucretia, his own colleague, a good and innocent man, on account of the name and kinship of the Tarquins, to resign his magistracy, nor did he permit him to live in the city. This crime he committed with the people favoring or allowing it—the people from whom that same Collatinus, like Brutus himself, had received the consulship.

From this right and good, Marcus Camillus, a distinguished man of that time, who most easily overcame the Veientes, the gravest enemies of the Roman people, after a ten-year war in which the Roman army, so often fighting badly, was gravely afflicted, when Rome itself was now doubting and trembling about its safety, and took their most opulent city, was made a defendant by the envy of those who disparaged his virtue and by the insolence of the tribunes of the plebs, and felt the city which he had freed to be so ungrateful that, most certain of his condemnation, he departed of his own accord into exile and, though absent, was even fined ten thousand asses—soon afterward to be again the avenger of his ungrateful fatherland against the Gauls.

It now irks me to recall many foul and unjust things by which that city was agitated, when the powerful tried to subject the plebs to themselves and the plebs refused to be subjected to them, and the defenders of each party acted more from partisan zeal in the love of conquering than thought of anything fair and good.

## Sallust testifies that after the destruction of Carthage the morals were depraved, because there was lacking a reason for leading life.

**18**. And so I will keep a measure, and will rather bring forward Sallust himself as a witness, who, when he had said in praise of the Romans—whence this discourse of ours arose—*right and good among them prevailed not by laws more than by nature*[^31], proclaiming that time in which, with the kings expelled, the city incredibly grew very much in a short span of time, nevertheless himself in the first book of his history, and in its very beginning, confesses that even then, when the commonwealth had been transferred from kings to consuls, after a small interval there were injuries of the stronger and, on account of them, a secession of the plebs from the fathers and other dissensions in the City.

For when he had recalled that the Roman people acted with the best morals and the greatest concord between the second and the last Carthaginian war, and had said that the cause of this good was not the love of justice but, while Carthage stood, the fear of an unreliable peace (whence that Nasica too, to repress wickedness and preserve those best morals, that vices might be checked by fear, was unwilling that Carthage be overthrown):

immediately the same Sallust subjoined and said: *but discord and avarice and ambition and the other evils wont to arise in prosperity were most of all increased after the destruction of Carthage*[^32], so that we might understand that even before they were wont to arise and increase.

Hence, adding why he said this: *for the injuries*, he says, *of the stronger and, on account of them, the secession of the plebs from the fathers and other dissensions at home existed from the very beginning, nor was it governed by fair and moderate right any longer than while the kings were expelled, until the fear of Tarquin and the grave war with Etruria was laid aside*[^33]. You see how, even in that short time, that after the kings were expelled—that is, cast out—things were governed for a while by somewhat fair and moderate right, he said the cause was fear, since the war was feared which King Tarquin, expelled from his kingdom and city and allied with the Etruscans, was waging against the Romans.

Attend, then, to what he next weaves in: *then*, he says, *the fathers exercised the plebs with servile command, decided about their life and back in the manner of kings, drove them from their land, and, with the rest dispossessed, held power over the soil alone. Oppressed by these cruelties and most of all by usury, the plebs, while in continual wars it endured tribute and military service at once, took up arms and occupied the Sacred Mount and the Aventine, and then procured for itself tribunes of the plebs and other rights. The end of discords and of the struggle on both sides was the second Punic War.*

You discern from what time—that is, a short interval after the kings were expelled—what sort the Romans were, of whom he says: *right and good among them prevailed not by laws more than by nature*[^34].

Further, if those times are found to be such, in which the Roman commonwealth is proclaimed to have been most beautiful and best, what now must we judge ought to be said or thought about the following age, when, *gradually changed*, to use the same historian's words, *from most beautiful and best it became most wicked and most shameful*[^35]—after the destruction of Carthage, namely, as he recalled? How Sallust himself briefly recalls and describes those times can be read in his *Histories*; with what great evils of morals, which arose from prosperity, he shows that things came even to civil wars. *From which time*, as he says, *the morals of the ancestors were precipitated not gradually as before, but in the manner of a torrent, the youth being so corrupted by luxury and avarice that it may deservedly be said that men were born who could neither themselves keep family estates nor allow others to keep them*[^36]. Sallust then says more about the vices of Sulla and the rest of the foulness of the commonwealth, and other writers agree on these things, although with eloquence much inferior.

You discern, however, as I suppose—and whoever takes note perceives most easily—into what filth of the worst morals that city slid before the coming of our heavenly King. For these things were done not only before Christ, present in the flesh, had begun to teach, but even before he was born of the Virgin.

Since, then, they do not dare to impute to their gods so many and so great evils of those times—either the more tolerable ones above, or, after Carthage was overthrown, the intolerable and horrible ones—when malign cunning was inserting into human minds the opinions from which such vices grew wild:

why do they impute present evils to Christ, who by his most wholesome teaching both forbids the worship of false and deceitful gods, and, detesting and condemning by divine authority these harmful and shameful desires of men, everywhere gradually withdraws his household from a world wasting and slipping with these evils, that he may found from it an eternal city, most glorious not by the applause of vanity but by the judgment of truth?

**19**. Behold, the Roman commonwealth (which I am not the first to say, but their own authors, from whom we learned these things for a fee, said so long ago, before the coming of Christ) *gradually changed from most beautiful and best to most wicked and most shameful*. Behold, before the coming of Christ, after Carthage was destroyed, *the morals of the ancestors were precipitated not gradually, as before, but in the manner of a torrent, the youth being so corrupted by luxury and avarice*[^37].

Let them read to us the precepts of their gods given to the Roman people against luxury and avarice; would that these had only kept silent about chaste and modest things to it, and not also demanded from it reproachful and ignominious things, by which, through a false divinity, they procured a pernicious authority. Let them read our writings—through the prophets, and through the holy Gospel, and through the Acts of the Apostles, and through the Epistles—so many things against avarice and luxury everywhere, how excellently, how divinely they resound to the peoples gathered for this, not as from the contests of philosophers, but as if thundering from oracles and the clouds of God.

And yet they do not impute to their gods that the commonwealth was made most wicked and most shameful by luxury and avarice and savage and base morals before the coming of Christ; but whatever affliction of it their pride and pleasures have suffered in this time, they reproach to the Christian religion. If together *the kings of the earth and all peoples, princes and all judges of the earth, young men and maidens, old men with younger*[^38]—every age capable of it and both sexes, and those whom John the Baptist addresses, the tax collectors themselves and the soldiers[^39]—heard and heeded its precepts about just and upright morals: then both the commonwealth would adorn the lands of the present life with its own happiness, and, about to reign most blessedly, would mount to the summit of eternal life. But because one hears, another despises, and more are friendlier to the vices that wickedly flatter than to the useful harshness of the virtues, the servants of Christ are commanded to tolerate—whether they be kings or princes or judges, whether soldiers or provincials, whether rich or poor, whether free or slave, of either sex—even a most wicked and most shameful commonwealth, if it is so necessary, and by this very toleration to procure for themselves a most illustrious place in that most holy and most august court of the angels and the heavenly commonwealth, where the will of God is law.

**20**. But such worshipers and lovers of those gods, whose imitators in crimes and shameful deeds they rejoice to be, in no way care that the commonwealth be most wicked and most shameful.

"Only let it stand," they say, "only let it flourish, packed with resources, glorious with victories, or—what is happier—let it be secure in peace. And what is that to us? Indeed, this rather pertains to us, that each man always increase his riches, which may supply daily extravagances, by which each more powerful man may also subject the weaker to himself. Let the poor serve the rich for the sake of being filled, and that under their patronage they may enjoy a quiet idleness; let the rich abuse the poor for clienteles and for the service of their own arrogance.

Let the peoples applaud not the counselors of their own interests, but the bestowers of pleasures. Let no hard things be commanded, let no impure things be forbidden. Let kings care not how good but how submissive a people they reign over. Let the provinces serve their kings not as rulers of morals, but as masters of their affairs and providers of their pleasures, and let them honor them not sincerely, but fear them wickedly and slavishly. Let the laws attend to what harm anyone does to another's vineyard rather than to his own life. Let no one be brought to the judges except one who has been troublesome or harmful to another's property, house, safety, or to anyone against his will; but as for his own, let each do what he pleases with his own, or with his own people, or with any who are willing. Let public harlots abound, either for the sake of all who please to enjoy them, or especially for the sake of those who cannot have private ones. Let most spacious and most adorned houses be built, let sumptuous banquets be frequented, where, when and as each pleases and is able, day and night let there be playing, drinking, vomiting, dissipation. Let dances resound everywhere, let the theaters seethe with the voices of dishonorable mirth and with every kind of pleasure, whether most cruel or most base."

"Let him be a public enemy to whom this happiness displeases; whoever shall attempt to change or take it away, let the free multitude turn him away from their ears, overthrow him from his seat, take him away from the living. Let those be held the true gods who have procured this for the peoples to attain and have preserved it when attained. Let them be worshiped as they wish, let them demand such games as they wish, which they may have with their worshipers or from their worshipers: only let them bring it about that nothing be feared, against such happiness, from an enemy, from a pestilence, from any disaster."

What sane man would compare this commonwealth—I will not say to the Roman empire, but to the house of Sardanapalus? That king was once so given over to pleasures that he had it written on his tomb that he had, dead, only those things which his lust, even while he lived, had consumed by draining[^40]. If these people had this king indulging them in such things and opposing no one in them with any severity, they would more gladly consecrate to him a temple and a flamen than the ancient Romans did to Romulus.

## By Cicero's testimony the Roman commonwealth was not just nor allied by right, nor was it the affair of a people, since it had departed from the morals of the ancestors; therefore its evils are not to be imputed to the name of Christ.

**21**. But if he is despised who called the Roman commonwealth most wicked and most shameful, and these people do not care with how great a blight and disgrace of the worst and most shameful morals it is filled, but only that it subsist and remain: let them hear that it was made not, as Sallust narrates, *most wicked and most shameful*, but, as Cicero argues, that even then it had utterly perished and no commonwealth at all remained. For he brings in Scipio—that very man who had extinguished Carthage—disputing about the commonwealth, when it was foreseen that, by that corruption which Sallust describes, it was now on the very point of perishing. For the disputation takes place at that time when one of the Gracchi had already been killed, from whom Sallust writes that grave seditions began[^41]. For mention is made of his death in those same books.

But when Scipio had said at the end of the second book: *as in lyres or flutes and in singing itself and in voices a certain harmony must be maintained out of distinct sounds, which, if altered or discordant, trained ears cannot bear, and that harmony, out of the regulation of most dissimilar voices, is nevertheless made concordant and congruous: so out of the highest and lowest and middle interposed orders, as out of sounds, by a measured reasoning, a city sounds in harmony by the consent of the most dissimilar, and that which is called harmony by musicians in song, that in a city is concord, the tightest and best bond of safety in every commonwealth, and that this can in no way exist without justice*[^42],

and then, when he had discussed somewhat more broadly and abundantly how much justice profited a city and how much it harmed it if it were absent, Philus, one of those present at the disputation, took up the discussion and demanded that this very question be treated more carefully and more be said about justice, on account of what was now commonly reported, that a commonwealth could not be governed without injustice. Scipio assented that this question ought accordingly to be discussed and disentangled, and replied *that there was nothing yet said about the commonwealth, as he thought, by which they could go further, unless it were established not only that that saying was false—that it cannot be without injustice—but that this was most true: that a commonwealth cannot be governed without the highest justice*[^43].

When the unfolding of this question had been deferred to the following day, in the third book the matter was carried on with great conflict. For Philus himself took up the disputation of those who held that a commonwealth could not be conducted without injustice, especially clearing himself, lest he be believed to hold this, and argued diligently for injustice against justice, trying as it were to show, by verisimilar reasonings and examples, that this was useful to the commonwealth, but that justice was useless. Then Laelius, at the request of all, undertook to defend justice, and asserted as much as he could that nothing was so hostile to a city as injustice, and that a commonwealth could not at all be conducted or stand except by great justice[^44].

When this question had been treated as much as seemed enough, Scipio returns to the interrupted matters, and recalls and commends his own brief definition of the commonwealth, by which he had said that it was the affair of a people[^45]. But he determines that a people is not every gathering of a multitude, but a gathering allied by the consent of right and by a community of interest. He then teaches how great is the usefulness of a definition in disputing, and from those definitions of his he gathers that there is then a commonwealth—that is, an affair of a people—when it is conducted well and justly, whether by one king or by a few aristocrats or by the whole people.

But when the king is unjust, whom he called a tyrant in the Greek manner, or the aristocrats unjust, whose consent he said was a faction, or the people itself unjust, for which he found no usual name, except that he called it too a tyrant: then the commonwealth is not now faulty, as had been argued the day before, but, as the reasoning connected from those definitions had taught, is altogether none, since it would not be the affair of a people, when a tyrant seized it by faction, nor would the people itself be now a people, if it were unjust, since it would not be a multitude allied by the consent of right and by a community of interest, as a people had been defined[^46].

When, therefore, the Roman commonwealth was such as Sallust describes it, it was now not most wicked and most shameful, as he himself says, but altogether none, according to that reasoning which the disputation about the commonwealth, held among its great leaders of that time, laid open.

As also Cicero himself, speaking not in the words of Scipio nor of any other, but in his own, at the beginning of the fifth book, having first recalled that verse of the poet Ennius, in which he had said: *on ancient morals and men the Roman state stands*. *That verse indeed*, he says, *seems to me, either by its brevity or its truth, to have been uttered as if from some oracle. For neither would the men, unless the city had been so charactered, nor the morals, unless these men had presided, have been able either to found or so long to hold so great a commonwealth, ruling so vastly far and wide. And so before our memory both the ancestral custom itself supplied outstanding men, and excellent men retained the old custom and the institutions of the ancestors.*

*But our age, when it had received the commonwealth like a distinguished painting, but fading with age, not only neglected to renew it with the same colors it had had, but did not even take care to preserve at least its form and, as it were, its outermost lineaments. For what remains of the ancient morals, on which he said the Roman state stands, which we see so obsolete by oblivion that they are not only not cultivated, but now not even known? For what shall I say about the men? For the morals themselves have perished through a dearth of men, of which so great an evil not only is an account to be rendered by us, but the case is, as it were, to be pleaded by us as defendants on a capital charge. For by our vices, not by any chance, we retain the commonwealth in word, but in fact have long since lost it*.

These things Cicero confessed—long indeed after the death of Africanus, whom he made to dispute in his books *On the Republic*, yet still before the coming of Christ; and if these things were thought and said with the Christian religion spread abroad and prevailing, which of these people would not judge that they ought to be imputed to the Christians? Wherefore why did their gods not take care that that commonwealth should not then perish and be lost, which Cicero, long before Christ had come in the flesh, so mournfully laments as lost?

Let its praisers see what sort it was even with those *ancient men and morals*—whether true justice flourished in it, or whether perhaps not even then was it alive in its morals, but painted in colors; which Cicero himself, unknowingly, when he set it forth, expressed.

But at another time, if God will, we will see this[^47]. For I will strive in its place to show, according to Cicero's own definitions, by which, with Scipio speaking, he briefly set down what a commonwealth is and what a people is (with many opinions also attesting, whether his own or of those whom he made to speak in the same disputation), that it never was a commonwealth, because there never was in it true justice. But according to more probable definitions it was, in a manner of its own, a commonwealth, and was better administered by the more ancient Romans than by the later ones; but true justice is not except in that commonwealth whose founder and ruler is Christ, if it pleases to call it too a commonwealth, since we cannot deny that it is the affair of a people. But if this name, which is elsewhere and otherwise spread abroad, is perhaps too remote from the use of our speech, certainly in that city there is true justice, of which the holy Scripture says: *glorious things are spoken of you, O city of God*[^48].

## The shameful deeds of the commonwealth show that the gods did not at all come to Rome's aid.

**22**. But as for what pertains to the present question, however praiseworthy they say that commonwealth was or is, according to their own most learned authors it had already, long before the coming of Christ, been made "most wicked and most shameful"; indeed it was none and had altogether perished by its most ruined morals. That it might not perish, then, its guardian gods ought above all to have given precepts of life and morals to their worshiping people, by whom they were worshiped with so many temples, so many priests and kinds of sacrifices, with rites so manifold and various, so many festal solemnities, the celebrations of so many and so great games—where the demons did nothing but their own business, not caring how those people lived, indeed caring that they might even live ruinously, provided only that, subjected by fear, they ministered all those things to their honor.

Or if they did give them, let it be brought forward, shown, read—what laws of the gods, given to that city, the Gracchi despised, so as to throw everything into confusion by seditions,

what laws Marius and Cinna and Carbo despised, so as to proceed even to civil wars, undertaken from the most unjust causes and waged cruelly and ended more cruelly[^49],

what laws, finally, Sulla himself[^50], at whose life, morals, and deeds, as Sallust and other writers of history describe them, who would not shudder?

Who would not confess that that commonwealth then perished?

Or will they perhaps dare, on account of morals of citizens of this kind, to set up in defense of their gods that Virgilian sentence, as they are wont: *all the gods, by whom this empire had stood, departed, the shrines and altars left behind*[^51]? First, if it is so, they have no reason to complain about the Christian religion, that their gods, offended by it, deserted them, since indeed their ancestors long ago, by their own morals, drove so many petty gods, like flies, from the altars of the City. But yet where was this crowd of divinities, when long before the ancient morals were corrupted, Rome was taken and burned by the Gauls? Were they present, perhaps, but asleep? For then, with the whole City reduced to the power of the enemy, the Capitoline hill alone remained, which would itself too have been taken, had not at least the geese kept watch while the gods slept[^52]. Whence Rome had almost fallen into the superstition of the Egyptians, who worship beasts and birds, when they celebrated solemn rites for the goose.

But about these adventitious evils, of the body rather than of the soul, which happen either from enemies or from another disaster, I do not yet for the present dispute: now I treat of the blight of morals, by which—first gradually discolored, then precipitated in the manner of a torrent—so great a ruin of the commonwealth was made, though the roofs and walls were intact, that their great authors do not hesitate to say it was then lost.

But rightly had *all the gods departed, the shrines and altars left behind*, that it might be lost, if the city had despised their precepts about a good life and justice. But now what sort of gods, I ask, were they, if they were unwilling to live with their worshiping people, whom, living ill, they had not taught to live well?

## The prosperous or adverse circumstances of the times are not from the demons but from God.

**23**. What of the fact that they even seem to have been present for the fulfilling of their desires, and are shown not to have presided over the reining in of them—they who aided Marius, a new and ignoble man, the most bloody author and conductor of civil wars, to become consul seven times, and that he might die in his seventh consulship, an old man, lest he fall into the hands of Sulla, soon to be victor[^53]?

For if their gods did not aid him to these things, it is no small matter that they confess that even without their gods being propitious, that great temporal happiness which they love too much can befall a man, and that men, as Marius was, can be heaped with and enjoy safety, strength, wealth, honors, dignity, long life, with the gods angry; that men too, as Regulus was, can be tortured by captivity, slavery, want, sleeplessness, pains, and die, with the gods friendly.

But if they grant that it is so, they confess in brief that the gods profit them nothing and are worshiped superfluously. For if they have rather pressed the people to learn things contrary to the virtues of the soul and to uprightness of life, whose rewards are to be hoped for after death; if even in these transient and temporal goods they neither harm those whom they hate nor profit those whom they love, why are they worshiped, why are they sought with such great zeal to be worshiped? Why, in laborious and sad times, is it murmured that they have departed as if offended, and on their account is the Christian religion injured with most unworthy insults?

But if they have in these matters the power either of benefit or of harm, why were they present in them for the worst man, Marius, and absent for the best man, Regulus? Or are they from this very fact understood to be most unjust and most evil?

But if for this reason they are thought the more to be feared and worshiped: let not even this be thought; for Regulus is found to have worshiped them no less than Marius.

Nor does a most wicked life therefore seem to be chosen, because the gods are thought to have favored Marius more than Regulus. For Metellus, the most praised of the Romans, who had five consular sons, was also happy in temporal things, and the most wicked Catiline, oppressed by want and laid low in the war of his own crime, was unhappy; and good men worshiping God are preeminent in the truest and most certain happiness, which can be conferred by him alone.

When, therefore, that commonwealth was perishing by evil morals, their gods did nothing for directing or correcting the morals, that it might not perish; on the contrary, they added to the depraving and corrupting of the morals, that it might perish. Nor let them feign themselves good, in that they departed as if offended by the iniquity of the citizens. They were entirely there; they are betrayed, they are convicted; they could neither come to aid by teaching nor lie hidden by keeping silent.

I pass over that Marius was commended by the pitying people of Minturnae to the goddess Marica in her grove, that she might prosper all things for him[^54], and that, returning unharmed from the depths of despair into the City, the cruel man led a cruel army; where how bloody, how uncivil and more monstrous than an enemy's his victory was, let those who wish read those who wrote it.

But this, as I said, I pass over, nor do I attribute Marius's bloody happiness to some Marica or other, but rather to the hidden providence of God, for closing the mouths of these people and freeing from error those who attend to these things prudently, not from partisan zeal; because, even if the demons can do something in these matters, they can do only as much as is permitted by the secret judgment of the Almighty—lest we make much of earthly happiness, which, as to Marius, is for the most part granted even to the wicked, nor again think it as if evil, when we see that many even pious and good worshipers of the one true God have been preeminent in it with the demons unwilling, nor think that those most unclean spirits are to be propitiated or feared even on account of these earthly goods or evils; because, just as the wicked men themselves on earth, so also those spirits cannot do all the things they wish, except so far as is allowed by the ordering of him whose judgments no one fully comprehends, no one justly reproves.

## Sulla's prosperous circumstances show that the demons incite to shameful deeds.

**24**. Sulla himself, certainly, whose times were such that the earlier ones, of which he seemed to be the avenger, were longed for in comparison with his—when he had first moved his camp toward the City against Marius, the entrails were so favorable to him as he sacrificed, Livy writes[^55], that Postumius the soothsayer wished to be kept under guard, ready to undergo capital punishment, unless Sulla, with the gods aiding, accomplished what he had in mind. Behold, *the gods had not departed, the shrines and altars left behind*, when they foretold the outcome of events and cared nothing about Sulla's own correction. By foretelling they promised great happiness, and did not break his evil desire by threatening.

Then, when he was in Asia waging the Mithridatic war, it was sent to him by Jupiter, through Lucius Titius, that he would overcome Mithridates, and it was done. And afterward, when he was striving to return to the City and avenge his own and his friends' injuries with civil blood, it was again sent to him by the same Jupiter, through a certain soldier of the sixth legion, that he had first foretold victory over Mithridates, and now promised that he would give him the power by which he might recover the commonwealth from his enemies, not without much blood. Then Sulla, having asked what the form was that had appeared to the soldier, when the man had described it, recognized it as the one he had earlier heard of from him who had brought the messages sent by the same Jupiter about the Mithridatic victory.

What can be answered here, as to why the gods took care to announce these as it were happy things, and none of them took care to correct Sulla by admonishing him, about to do such great evils with wicked civil arms—such as would not foul but altogether take away the commonwealth?

Surely the demons are understood—as I have often said, and as is known to us in the sacred writings, and as the facts themselves sufficiently indicate—to be doing their own business: that they may be held for gods and worshiped, that those things may be exhibited to them by which those who exhibit them, allied with them, may have one most wicked cause with them in the judgment of God.

Then, when Sulla had come to Tarentum and had sacrificed there, he saw on the top of the calf's liver the likeness of a golden crown. Then that soothsayer Postumius answered that it signified an illustrious victory for him, and bade that he alone eat of those entrails.

Afterward, a short interval later, the slave of a certain Lucius Pontius, prophesying, cried out: *I come as a messenger from Bellona; the victory is yours, Sulla*. Then he added that the Capitol would burn. When he had said this, immediately going out from the camp, the next day he returned more agitated and cried out that the Capitol had burned. And in fact the Capitol had burned. Which indeed was both easy for a demon to foresee and most swift to announce.

But attend, certainly, to this, which pertains most to the matter: under what sort of gods those wish to be who blaspheme the Savior, who frees the wills of the faithful from the dominion of demons. The man cried out, prophesying, *the victory is yours, Sulla*, and, that it might be believed that he cried it by a divine spirit, he announced also something both near at hand and soon to come, from which the one through whom that spirit spoke was far away; yet he did not cry out, *spare yourself from crimes, Sulla*, the so horrible crimes which Sulla there, as victor, committed—he to whom the golden crown, the most illustrious sign of that very victory, appeared in the calf's liver,

such signs as, if just gods were wont to give and not impious demons, surely they would have shown by those entrails the wicked evils to come, gravely harmful to Sulla himself. For that victory did not so much profit his dignity as it harmed his desire; by which it came about that, gaping after immoderate things and lifted up and cast headlong by prosperity, he himself perished more in his morals than he destroyed his enemies in their bodies.

These truly sad and truly lamentable things those gods did not foretell to him, not by entrails, not by auguries, not by anyone's dream or prophecy. For they were more afraid that he would be corrected than that he would be conquered. Indeed they took pains that the victor over his fellow citizens, glorious, conquered and captive to unspeakable vices, should through these be subjected much more closely even to the demons themselves.

## The gods drive men to cruelty and crime; Christ to the best morals.

**25**. But from this, who would not understand, who would not see—except one who has chosen rather to imitate such gods than to be separated by divine grace from their society—how much these malign spirits strive, by their own example, to lend as it were a divine authority to crimes? Which they did even on a certain broad plain of Campania, where not long after the civil battle lines clashed in a wicked conflict; there they themselves were first seen to fight among themselves. For there great crashes were first heard, and soon many announced that they had seen, for several days, two battle lines fighting. When this battle ceased, they found also footprints, as it were of men and horses, as many as could be impressed from that conflict[^56].

If, then, the divinities truly fought among themselves, then human civil wars are excused; yet let it be considered what is the malice or the misery of such gods: but if they feigned that they fought, what else did they do but bring it about that the Romans, in waging civil war, should seem to commit no wickedness, as if by the example of the gods?

For the civil wars had already begun, and the execrable slaughter of several wicked battles had preceded. Already it had moved many that a certain soldier, while he was stripping the spoils from a slain man, recognized his brother by the stripped corpse, and, detesting civil wars, killing himself there, joined himself to his brother's body[^57].

So that there might be no weariness of this so great evil, but rather that the ardor of wicked arms might grow more and more, the harmful demons, whom those people, thinking them gods, judged to be worshiped and venerated, wished to appear to men fighting among themselves, lest civic affection should tremble to imitate such battles, but rather that human crime should be excused by the divine example.

By this cunning the malign spirits commanded that even stage-shows, of which I have already said much, be dedicated and consecrated to them, where so great shameful deeds of the gods, celebrated by theatrical songs and the acting of fables, both whoever believed that they had done them, and whoever did not believe it but yet saw that they most gladly had such things exhibited to them, might securely imitate.

Lest, therefore, anyone should think that the poets had written insults against the gods rather than something worthy of them, wherever the poets recalled that they fought among themselves, the gods themselves, to deceive men, confirmed the songs of the poets, exhibiting their battles to human eyes, namely, not only through the stage-players in the theater, but even through themselves on the field.

We have been compelled to say these things, since their own authors did not at all hesitate to say and write that the Roman commonwealth had already before been ruined by the worst morals of the citizens, and that none remained, before the coming of Christ Jesus our Lord. This ruin they do not impute to their gods, who impute to our Christ the transitory evils by which the good, whether they live or die, cannot perish: although our Christ frequents so many precepts for the best morals against ruined morals; but their gods did nothing by any such precepts with their worshiping people for that commonwealth, that it might not perish; on the contrary, by corrupting those very morals as if by their own examples, with a harmful authority, they rather brought it about that it should perish.

No one, I think, will now dare to say that it then perished because *all the gods departed, the shrines and altars left behind*, as if friends of the virtues, when they were offended by the vices of men; since by so many signs of entrails, auguries, prophecies—by which they were eager to boast of and commend themselves as foreknowers of the future and helpers in battles—they are convicted of having been present; and if they had truly departed, the Romans would have flared up into civil wars more mildly by their own desires than by their instigations.

## The gods incite to base things, as in the rites of the goddess Caelestis.

**26**. Since these things are so—since openly and plainly basenesses mingled with cruelties, the reproaches and crimes of the divinities, whether disclosed or fabricated, at their own demand and with them growing angry unless it were done, have shone forth as consecrated and dedicated to them even by fixed and appointed solemnities, and have come forth to the eyes of all to be set before them as things to be imitated: what is it, then, that those same demons, who by pleasures of this kind confess themselves to be unclean spirits, who by their own shameful deeds and crimes—whether disclosed or feigned—and by the celebration of them demanded for themselves, sought from the shameless, extorted from the modest, testify that they are authors of a wicked and unclean life, are nevertheless reported, in their sanctuaries and secret inner chambers, to give certain good precepts about morals to certain of their, as it were, chosen consecrated ones[^58]? But if it is so, by this very fact the more crafty malice of the harmful spirits is to be noticed and convicted.

For so great is the force of uprightness and chastity that all, or nearly all, human nature is moved by the praise of it, and is not so far vicious in baseness as to have lost the whole sense of honor. Accordingly the malignity of the demons, unless somewhere it *transfigures itself into an angel of light*[^59], as we know is written in our writings, does not accomplish the business of deception.

And so outside, among the peoples, impure impiety resounds round about with most crowded uproar, and within, to a few, feigned chastity scarcely sounds; the open spaces are offered to shameful things and the secret places to praiseworthy ones; honor lies hidden and dishonor lies open; the evil that is done calls together all spectators, the good that is spoken scarcely finds any hearers, as if honorable things were to be blushed at and dishonorable ones to be gloried in.

But where is this, except in the temples of the demons? Where, except in the lodgings of falsehood? For the one is done that the more honorable, who are few, may be captured; but the other, lest the more, who are most base, be corrected.

Where and when the consecrated of Caelestis heard the precepts of chastity, we do not know; yet before her very shrine, where we beheld that image set up, we, all flowing together from everywhere and standing where each could, most attentively watched the games that were being performed, gazing with alternating view, on the one side at the harlot procession, on the other at the virgin goddess; we saw her adored suppliantly, and before her base things celebrated; we saw there no bashful mimes, no more modest actress; all the offices of obscenity were performed.

It was known what pleased the virginal divinity, and there was exhibited what the more learned matron might carry home from the temple. Some of the more modest turned their faces away from the impure motions of the stage-players and learned the art of shame by a furtive attention. For they were ashamed before men to dare to look upon shameless gestures with a free face; but much less did they dare to condemn with a chaste heart the rites of her whom they venerated.

This, nevertheless, was offered openly to be learned in the temple, which to perpetrate at least secrecy was sought at home—the modesty of mortals, if there was any there, wondering exceedingly that men did not freely commit the human shameful deeds which among the gods they even religiously learned, intending to have them angry unless they took care also to exhibit them.

For what other spirit, by a hidden instinct stirring up the most worthless minds, both urges adulteries to be committed and is fed by them when done, except the one who is also delighted by such rites—setting up in the temples the images of demons, loving in the games the images of vices, whispering in secret the words of justice to deceive even the few good, frequenting in the open the enticements of wickedness to possess the innumerable wicked?

**27**. A grave man and a would-be philosopher, Tullius, about to be aedile, cries out in the ears of the city that, among the other duties of his magistracy, the mother Flora must be placated by him with the celebration of games[^60]; which games are wont to be celebrated the more devoutly the more basely.

He says in another place, now as consul, in the extreme perils of the city, that games were held for ten days, and that nothing which pertained to placating the gods was omitted[^61]; as if it were not better to provoke such gods by temperance than to placate them by luxury, and to call them even to enmity by honor rather than to soothe them by such great deformity.

For the men on whose account they were placated would not have been about to harm them, by however most cruel a monstrousness, more gravely than the gods themselves harmed, when they were placated by the foulest viciousness. Since indeed, that what was feared from the enemy in bodies might be averted, the gods were conciliated in such a way that virtue might be subdued in minds—gods who would not be set as defenders against the assailants of the walls, unless they first became stormers of good morals.

This most petulant, most impure, most shameless, most worthless, most unclean placation of such divinities, whose actors the praiseworthy disposition of Roman virtue deprived of honor, removed from the tribe, marked, made base, declared infamous—this, I say, shameful placation of such divinities, to be turned from and detested by true religion: the fables about the gods, enticing and criminal, these ignominious deeds of the gods, either wickedly and basely done or more wickedly and basely fabricated, the whole city learned with public eyes and ears; it saw these committed things to please the divinities, and therefore believed that they were not only to be exhibited to them, but also to be imitated by itself—not that something, I know not what, as if good and honorable, which was said to so few and so secretly (if indeed it was said), so that it was feared rather lest it become known than lest it not be done[^62].

this, I say, shameful appeasement of such deities, which true religion must reject and detest—the alluring and criminal stories about the gods, these disgraceful deeds of the gods, either wickedly and shamefully done or still more wickedly and shamefully invented—the whole city learned through public displays and performances,

it saw that the perpetration of these things pleased the deities, and therefore believed that they should not only be presented to them but also imitated by itself, rather than that something supposedly good and honorable which was taught to so few and so secretly—if indeed it was taught—that there was more fear of its becoming known than of its not being practiced[^63].

## The Christian mysteries are of most wholesome piety.

**28**. That men are rescued through the name of Christ from the tartarean yoke and penal society of these most unclean powers, and transferred into the light of most wholesome piety from that night of most pernicious impiety, the unjust and ungrateful, and those more deeply and closely possessed by that wicked spirit, complain and murmur—because the peoples flow together to the Church in chaste assembly, with an honorable separation of both sexes, where they hear how well they ought to live here for a time, so that after this life they may deserve to live blessedly and forever, where holy Scripture and the teaching of justice resound from a higher place in the sight of all, and both those who do it hear it unto reward, and those who do not do it hear it unto judgment. To which place, even if certain mockers of such precepts come, all their petulance is either laid aside by a sudden change, or repressed by fear or shame. For nothing base and shameful is set before them to be watched and imitated, where either the precepts of the true God are instilled, or his miracles narrated, or his gifts praised, or his benefits sought.

## The Romans are invited to Christ, that they may reject the false gods.

**29**. Desire these things rather, O praiseworthy Roman disposition, O offspring of the Reguli, the Scaevolae, the Scipios, the Fabricii; desire these things rather, distinguish these from that most base vanity and most deceitful malignity of the demons. If anything praiseworthy stands out in you naturally, it is purified and perfected only by true piety, but by impiety it is destroyed and punished. Now then choose what you will follow, that you may be praised, not in yourself, but in the true God without any error. For then popular glory was present to you, but by the hidden judgment of divine providence the true religion which you might choose was lacking. Awake, it is day, as you have awakened in certain men, in whose perfect virtue and for the true faith even in their sufferings we glory, who, everywhere fighting against the most hostile powers and conquering them bravely by dying, *begot for us this fatherland with their blood*[^64].

Now choose whom to follow, so that you may be praised without error, not in yourself but in the true God. At that time popular glory was yours, but by the hidden judgment of divine providence you lacked the true religion to choose. Awake: it is day! You have already awakened in some of your people, whose perfected virtue and sufferings for the true faith are our glory. Contending everywhere against the most hostile powers and conquering them by bravely dying, they *won this homeland for us with their own blood*.[^65]

To which fatherland we invite and exhort you, that you may be added to the number of its citizens, of which in a certain way the sanctuary is the true remission of sins. Do not listen to your degenerate ones disparaging Christ and Christians and accusing the times as if evil, when they seek times in which there may be not a quiet life, but rather a secure wickedness. These things never pleased you, not even for the earthly fatherland. Now then seize the heavenly one, for which you will labor very little, and in it you will reign truly and forever. For there you will have neither a Vestal hearth nor the Capitoline stone[^66], but the one and true God *sets neither bounds nor times to things, he will give empire without end*[^67].

Now take possession of the heavenly country, for which your labor will be very slight, and in it you will reign truly and forever. There neither Vesta’s hearth nor the Capitoline stone[^68], but the one true God *sets neither boundaries to your dominion nor limits to its duration; he will grant an empire without end*[^69].

Do not seek the false and deceitful gods; cast them off rather and despise them, flashing out into true liberty. They are not gods; they are malign spirits, to whom your eternal happiness is a punishment. Not so much does Juno seem to have envied the Romans' citadels to the Trojans, from whom you draw your carnal origin, as these demons, whom you still think gods, envy to every kind of men the everlasting seats.

And you yourself, in no small part, have judged about such spirits, when you placated them by games, and willed that those men through whom you held those same games should be infamous. Suffer your liberty to be asserted against the unclean spirits, who had imposed on your neck their own ignominy to be consecrated and celebrated for them. You removed the actors of the divine crimes from your honors: pray to the true God that he remove from you those gods who are delighted by their own crimes, whether true—which is most ignominious—or false—which is most malicious.

It is well that of your own accord you were unwilling that the society of the city be open to actors and stage-players; awake more fully! In no way is the divine majesty placated by those arts by which human dignity is defiled. By what reasoning, then, do you think that gods who are delighted by such observances are held in the number of the holy heavenly powers, when you have not thought that men, through whom those same observances are performed, should be held in the number of even any kind of Roman citizens? Incomparably more illustrious is the heavenly city, where the victory is truth, where the dignity is holiness, where the peace is happiness, where the life is eternity. Much less does it have such gods in its society, if you blushed to have such men in yours. Accordingly, if you desire to arrive at the blessed city, shun the society of demons. Unworthily are those worshiped by the honorable who are placated by the base. So let these be removed from your piety by Christian purification, as those were removed from your dignity by the censorial mark.

But about carnal goods, which alone the wicked wish to enjoy, and about carnal evils, which alone they refuse to suffer—that these demons do not have in these things even the power they are thought to have (although, if they did have it, we ought rather to despise even these things than to worship them on their account, and, by worshiping them, to be unable to arrive at those things which they envy us)—nevertheless that they do not avail even in these things as much as those think who contend that they ought to be worshiped on their account, we will see next, so that here may be the limit of this volume.
