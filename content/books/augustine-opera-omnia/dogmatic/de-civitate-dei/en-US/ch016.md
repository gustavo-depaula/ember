# Book XIV: The two cities shown forth from the things that were done

**1.** We have already said in the earlier books[^1] that God willed to establish the human race from one man, both that the human race might be associated not only by likeness of nature but also bound together by a certain kinship-tie into a concord of unity by the bond of peace, and that this race would not have died in each individual unless the first two—of whom one was created from nothing, the other from him—had merited this by disobedience, by whom so great a sin was committed that human nature was changed thereby for the worse, with the bondage of sin and the necessity of death transmitted even to posterity.

But the reign of death so dominated over human beings that it would drive all headlong by the due penalty into the second death also, of which there is no end, were it not that the undue grace of God freed some of them from it. And by this it came about that, although so many and so great nations living throughout the world by diverse rites and customs are distinguished by a manifold variety of languages, arms, and dress, there nonetheless arose no more than two kinds of human society, which according to our Scriptures we might rightly call two cities. The one, namely, is of human beings willing to live according to the flesh, the other of those willing to live according to the spirit, each in the peace of its own kind, and, when they attain what they seek, living each in the peace of its own kind.

## What it is to live according to the flesh.

**2. 1.** First, then, we must see what it is to live according to the flesh, and what according to the spirit. For whoever looks at what we have said only on the surface, either not recollecting or paying too little attention to how the holy Scriptures speak, can suppose that the Epicurean philosophers indeed live according to the flesh, because they placed man's highest good in the pleasure of the body, and any others who in whatever way held the good of the body to be man's highest good, and all that common crowd who philosophize by no doctrine or in any such manner, but, prone to lust, do not know how to rejoice except from the pleasures they take by the bodily senses;

but that the Stoics, who place man's highest good in the mind, live according to the spirit—because what is man's mind but spirit? But, as divine Scripture speaks, both are shown to live according to the flesh.

For he calls "flesh" not only the body of an earthly and mortal living creature (as when he says, *not all flesh is the same flesh; one indeed is the flesh of man, another the flesh of beasts, another of birds, another of fishes*[^2]), but he uses the signification of this name in many other ways too, among which various modes of speech he often calls the human being himself, that is, the nature of man, *flesh*,

by the mode of speech that takes the whole from the part, as is, *by the works of the law no flesh shall be justified*[^3]. For what did he wish to be understood but "every human being"? This he says a little later more plainly, *in the law no one is justified*[^4], and to the Galatians, *but knowing that a human being is not justified by the works of the law*[^5].

According to this is understood, *and the Word was made flesh*[^6], that is, a human being; which some, not taking it rightly, supposed that Christ lacked a human soul. For just as the part is taken from the whole, where the words of Mary Magdalene are read in the Gospel saying, *they have taken away my Lord, and I know not where they have laid him*[^7]—when she was speaking only of the flesh of Christ, which she thought, once buried, had been taken away from the tomb—so too the whole is understood from the part when, the flesh being named, a human being is meant, as in those passages we recalled above.

For just as a part is understood from the whole when we read Mary Magdalene’s words in the Gospel, *they have taken away my Lord, and I do not know where they have laid him*[^8]—although she was speaking only of Christ’s flesh, which she thought had been taken from the tomb where it had been buried—so also the whole is understood from a part when a human being is meant by the word “flesh,” as in the passages recalled above.

**2. 2.** Since, therefore, divine Scripture calls things "flesh" in many ways, which it would be long to search out and gather, in order that we may be able to trace what it is to live according to the flesh (which is surely an evil, although the nature of the flesh itself is not an evil), let us look carefully at that passage in the letter the apostle Paul wrote to the Galatians, where he says, *now the works of the flesh are manifest, which are fornications, uncleannesses, lust, the service of idols, sorceries, enmities, contentions, jealousies, animosities, dissensions, heresies, envies, drunkennesses, carousings, and the like; of which I forewarn you, as I have forewarned, that those who do such things shall not possess the kingdom of God*[^9].

This whole passage of the apostolic letter, considered as far as will seem sufficient for the matter at hand, will be able to resolve this question, what it is to live according to the flesh. For among the works of the flesh, which he said are manifest and, having recalled them, condemned, we find not only those that pertain to the pleasure of the flesh, such as fornications, uncleannesses, lust, drunkennesses, carousings, but also those by which vices of the mind are shown forth, alien to the pleasure of the flesh. For who would not understand the service rendered to idols, sorceries, enmities, contentions, jealousies, animosities, dissensions, heresies, envies to be vices of the mind rather than of the flesh? Since indeed it can happen that for the sake of idolatry or the error of some heresy a person abstains from the pleasures of the body; and yet even then a human being, although he seems to restrain and check the lusts of the flesh, is convicted by this apostolic authority of living according to the flesh, and in that very abstaining from the pleasures of the flesh is shown to be doing the damnable works of the flesh.

Who would not have enmities in the mind? Or who would so speak as to say to his enemy, or to one he thinks an enemy, "you have evil flesh," rather than "you have an evil mind against me"? Finally, just as, if anyone heard of "carnalities," so to speak, he would not hesitate to attribute them to the flesh, so no one hesitates that animosities pertain to the mind. Why, then, does the *teacher of the nations in faith and truth*[^10] call all these and the like the *works of the flesh*, except because, by that mode of speech in which the whole is signified by the part, he wishes the human being himself to be understood by the name *flesh*?

## The body can be clothed with immortality.

**3. 1.** But if anyone says that the flesh is the cause, in evil morals, of whatever vices there may be, on the ground that the soul, affected by the flesh, lives thus, he surely has not attended carefully to the whole nature of man. For indeed *the corruptible body weighs down the soul*. Hence the same Apostle too, treating of this corruptible body, of which he had a little before said, *although our outer man is corrupted*[^11], says, *we know that, if our earthly house of habitation be dissolved, we have a building from God, a house not made with hands, eternal in the heavens. For in this we groan, desiring to be clothed over with our dwelling which is from heaven; if so be that, being clothed, we be not found naked. For we who are in this habitation groan, being burdened, in that we would not be unclothed, but clothed over, that what is mortal may be swallowed up by life*[^12].

We are weighed down, then, by the corruptible body, and, knowing that the cause of this weighing-down is not the nature and substance of the body but its corruption, we do not wish to be unclothed of the body, but to be clothed with its immortality. For then the body will still be, but, because it will not be corruptible, it will not weigh us down. So now *the corruptible body weighs down the soul, and the earthly dwelling presses down the mind that ponders many things*[^13]. Nevertheless, those who think that all the soul's evils have come from the body are in error.

**3. 2.** For although Virgil seems to set forth the Platonic opinion in brilliant verses, saying, *fiery is the vigor of those seeds and heavenly their origin, so far as harmful bodies do not slow them and earthly limbs and dying members dull them*, and, wishing those four most familiar disturbances of the mind—desire, fear, gladness, sadness, as if the origins of all sins and vices—to be understood as befalling from the body, adds and says, *hence they fear and desire, grieve and rejoice, nor look up to the airs, shut in by darkness and a blind prison*[^14]; nevertheless our faith holds otherwise. For the corruption of the body, which weighs down the soul[^15], is not the cause of the first sin, but its punishment; nor did corruptible flesh make the soul a sinner, but the sinning soul made the flesh corruptible.

From which corruption of the flesh, although certain incitements of vices and the vicious desires themselves arise, nevertheless not all the vices of an iniquitous life are to be attributed to the flesh, lest we acquit the devil of all these, who has no flesh.

For although the devil cannot be called a fornicator or a drunkard, or anything of this kind of evil that pertains to the pleasures of the flesh—though he is even the persuader and hidden instigator of such sins—yet he is exceedingly proud and envious. This viciousness so took hold of him that on account of it he was destined to eternal punishment in the prisons of this murky air.

But these vices, which hold the chief place in the devil, the Apostle attributes to the flesh, which it is certain the devil does not have. For he says that enmities, contentions, jealousies, animosities, envies are works of the flesh[^16]; of all which evils the head and origin is pride, which reigns without flesh in the devil. And who is more an enemy to the saints than he? Who is found more contentious, more animated, more jealous and envious against them? But since he has all these without flesh, how are they works of the flesh, except because they are the works of the human being, whom, as I said, he calls by the name *flesh*?

For it was not by having flesh, which the devil does not have, but by living according to himself, that is, according to man, that the human being became like the devil; because he too willed to live according to himself, when he did not stand in the truth, so that he spoke a lie not from God's but from his own—he who is not only a liar but also the father of lies[^17]. For he lied first, and from him by whom sin began, the lie too began to be.

## Every sin is a lie.

**4. 1.** When, therefore, a human being lives according to man, not according to God, he is like the devil; because not even an angel ought to have lived according to an angel, but according to God, so as to stand in the truth and speak the truth from him, not a lie from himself. For of man, in another place, the same Apostle says, *but if the truth of God has abounded in my lie*[^18]. He called the lie ours, the truth God's.

When, therefore, a human being lives according to the truth, he does not live according to himself, but according to God. For it is God who said, *I am the truth*[^19]. But when he lives according to himself, that is, according to man, not according to God, he surely lives according to a lie; not because man himself is a lie, since his author and creator is God, who is certainly not the author and creator of a lie, but because man was so made upright that he should live not according to himself, but according to him by whom he was made—that is, should do his will rather than his own: not to live as he was made to live, this is a lie.

For he wishes to be happy even by not living in such a way that he can be so. What is more a lie than this wish? Whence not without reason can it be said that every sin is a lie. For sin is committed only by that will by which we wish that it may be well with us or do not wish that it may be ill with us.

It is a lie, then, that, while it is done that it may be well with us, hence rather it is ill with us, or, while it is done that it may be better with us, hence rather it is worse with us. Whence is this, except because it can be well with a human being from God, whom by transgressing he forsakes, not from himself, according to whom by living he transgresses?

**4. 2.** What we have therefore said—that two cities have arisen, diverse and contrary to each other, because some live according to the flesh, others according to the spirit[^20]—can also be said in this way: that some live according to man, others according to God. For Paul most plainly says to the Corinthians, *for since there are jealousy and contention among you, are you not carnal and do you not walk according to man?*[^21] So then, to walk according to man is to be carnal, since by the flesh, that is, by a part of man, the human being is understood.

For he called those very same ones *natural* above whom he afterward called *carnal*, speaking thus, *for who among men knows the things of a man, except the spirit of the man which is in him? So too the things of God no one knows except the Spirit of God. But we*, he says, *have not received the spirit of this world, but the Spirit which is from God, that we may know the things that have been given us by God; which things we also speak, not in the learned words of human wisdom, but in those taught by the Spirit, comparing spiritual things with spiritual. But the natural man does not perceive the things that are of the Spirit of God; for it is foolishness to him*[^22]. To such, then, that is, to natural men, he says a little later, *and I, brethren, could not speak to you as to spiritual men, but as to carnal*[^23];

both that and this by the same mode of speech, that is, the whole from the part. For from the soul too and from the flesh, which are parts of man, the whole, which is man, can be signified; and thus the natural man is not one thing and the carnal another, but both are the very same, that is, a human being living according to man; just as nothing other than human beings is signified, whether where it is read, *by the works of the law no flesh shall be justified*[^24], or what is written, *seventy-five souls went down with Jacob into Egypt*[^25]. For there by *all flesh* "every human being," and there by *seventy-five souls* "seventy-five human beings" are understood.

And what was said, *not in the learned words of human wisdom*, could have been said, "not in those of carnal wisdom"; just as what was said, *you walk according to man*, could have been said, "according to the flesh." But this appeared more clearly in what he added, *for when one says, I indeed am of Paul, but another, I of Apollos, are you not men?*[^26] What he was saying, *you are natural*, and *you are carnal*, he said more expressly, *you are men*, which is, "you live according to man, not according to God, according to whom if you were to live, you would be gods."

## What the motions are in returning souls according to the Platonists.

**5.** There is therefore no need, in our sins and vices, to accuse the nature of the flesh, to the injury of the Creator, for it is good in its kind and order; but, having forsaken the good Creator, to live according to a created good is not good, whether one chooses to live according to the flesh, or according to the soul, or according to the whole man, who consists of soul and flesh (whence he can be signified both by the name of "soul" alone and by the name of "flesh" alone). For whoever praises the nature of the soul as the highest good and accuses the nature of the flesh as an evil, surely both desires the soul carnally and flees the flesh carnally, since he feels this by human vanity, not by divine truth.

The Platonists indeed do not rave like the Manichees, so as to detest earthly bodies as though an evil nature, since they attribute all the elements of which this visible and tangible world is composed, and their qualities, to God the artificer; yet they suppose that souls are so affected by earthly limbs and dying members that hence they have the diseases of desires and fears and of gladness or sadness—by which four either disturbances, as Cicero calls them[^27], or passions, as most render the Greek word word-for-word, all the viciousness of human morals is comprised.

But if it is so, what is it that Aeneas in Virgil, when he had heard from his father in the underworld that souls would return again to bodies, wonders at this opinion, exclaiming, *O father, must it be thought that any sublime souls go from here to heaven and again return to sluggish bodies? What desire of light, so dire, is in the wretched ones?*[^28] Is this desire, so dire, still present in that most highly praised purity of souls, from earthly limbs and dying members? Does he not assert that they have been purged of all corporeal plagues of this kind, as he says, when they begin again to wish to return into bodies?

From which it is gathered that, even if it were so—which is altogether most vain—that there is an unceasing alternating cleansing and defiling of souls going and returning by turns, it could not have been truly said that all the blameworthy and vicious motions of souls grow in them from earthly bodies; since indeed, according to these very men, that dire desire, as the noble speaker says, is so far from being from the body that it forces the soul, purged of every corporeal plague and established outside every body, to be itself in a body.

Whence even by their own admission the soul is affected not only by the flesh, so as to desire, fear, rejoice, sicken, but can also be agitated by these motions from itself.

## The will is compared with the motions.

**6.** But it matters of what sort a man's will is; for if it is perverse, it will have these motions perverse; but if it is right, they will be not only blameless but even praiseworthy. For the will is in all of them; indeed all of them are nothing other than acts of will. For what is desire and gladness but the will in agreement with the things we will? And what is fear and sadness but the will in disagreement with the things we do not will? But when we agree by seeking the things we will, it is called desire; when we agree by enjoying the things we will, it is called gladness. Likewise, when we disagree with that which we do not wish to happen, such a will is fear; but when we disagree with that which happens against our will, such a will is sadness. And in general, according to the variety of the things that are sought and fled, just as the will of a man is allured or offended, so it is changed and turned into these or those affections.

Wherefore the human being who lives according to God, not according to man, must be a lover of good; whence it follows that he hates evil. And since no one is evil by nature, but whoever is evil is evil by vice, he who lives according to God owes a perfect hatred to the evil[^29], so that he neither hates the human being on account of the vice nor loves the vice on account of the human being, but hates the vice and loves the human being. For the vice being healed, all that he ought to love will remain, but nothing that he ought to hate.

**7. 1.** For whoever has the purpose to love God, and to love his neighbor not according to man but according to God, just as also himself: he is without doubt, on account of this love, said to be of good will, which is more usually called in the holy Scriptures "charity"; but it is also called "love" according to those same sacred writings. For the Apostle says that one who is to be chosen for ruling the people ought to be a lover of good[^30], and the Lord himself, questioning the apostle Peter, when he had said, *do you love me more than these?*, the other answered, *Lord, you know that I love you*; and again the Lord asked, not whether Peter loved him with one word, but with another; and the other answered again, *Lord, you know that I love you*; but at the third questioning Jesus himself too said not "do you esteem me?" but *do you love me?*

where the Evangelist goes on to say, *Peter was grieved because he said to him the third time, do you love me?* although the Lord had said not three times but once, *do you love me?*, and twice *do you esteem me?*; whence we understand that even when the Lord said *do you esteem me?* he said nothing other than *do you love me?* But Peter did not change the word for this one thing, but even the third time said, *Lord, you know all things; you know that I love you*[^31].

**7. 2.** I thought this should be recalled because some suppose that "esteem," or "charity," is one thing, and "love" another. For they say that "esteem" is to be taken in a good sense, "love" in a bad one. But it is most certain that not even the authors of secular letters spoke thus. Yet let the philosophers see whether, or by what reason, they distinguish these; their books say clearly enough that they too rate love highly in good things and toward God himself. But it had to be conveyed that the Scriptures of our religion, whose authority we set before all other writings, do not say that love is one thing and esteem or charity another. For we have already shown that love too is spoken of in a good sense.

But lest anyone suppose that "love" indeed is to be said both in a bad and in a good sense, but "esteem" only in a good one, let him attend to what is written in the Psalm, *but he who loves iniquity hates his own soul*[^32], and that of the apostle John, *if anyone esteems the world, the esteem of the Father is not in him*[^33]. Behold, in one place esteem both in a good and in a bad sense. But lest anyone demand love in a bad sense (since we have already shown it in a good one), let him read what is written, *for men will be lovers of themselves, lovers of money*[^34].

A right will, then, is good love, and a perverse will is evil love. Love, therefore, longing to have what is loved, is desire; but having and enjoying it, gladness; fleeing what is contrary to it, fear; and, if this befall, feeling it, sadness. Accordingly, these are evil if the love is evil, good if it is good.

Let us prove what we say from the Scriptures. The Apostle desires to be dissolved and to be with Christ[^35], and, *my soul has desired to long for your judgments*[^36], or if it is said more fittingly, *my soul has longed to desire your judgments*; and, *the desire of wisdom leads to the kingdom*[^37]. Yet the custom of speech has so prevailed that, if "desire" (cupiditas) or "concupiscence" be said and it is not added of what thing it is, it can be understood only in a bad sense. Gladness is in a good sense, *be glad in the Lord and rejoice, you just*[^38]; and, *you have given gladness in my heart*[^39]; and, *you will fill me with gladness with your countenance*[^40]. Fear is in a good sense in the Apostle, where he says, *work out your own salvation with fear and trembling*[^41]; and, *do not be high-minded, but fear*[^42]; and, *but I fear lest, as the serpent seduced Eve by his cunning, so your minds too be corrupted from the chastity which is in Christ*[^43]. But concerning sadness, which Cicero rather calls "sickness"[^44], and Virgil *grief*, where he says, *they grieve and rejoice*[^45] (but I preferred to say "sadness" because "sickness" or "grief" is more usually said of bodies), there is a more scrupulous question, whether it can be found in a good sense.

**8. 1.** For what the Greeks call εὐπάθειαι[^46], and Cicero in Latin named "constancies"[^47], the Stoics held to be three, corresponding to three disturbances in the mind of the wise man: for desire, will; for gladness, joy; for fear, caution; but corresponding to sickness or grief, which we have preferred to call "sadness" for the sake of avoiding ambiguity, they denied that there could be anything in the mind of the wise man.

For will, they say, seeks the good, which the wise man does; joy is over the good attained, which the wise man everywhere attains; caution avoids evil, which the wise man ought to avoid; but sadness, since it is over an evil that has already befallen, and they think no evil can befall the wise man, they said there can be nothing in his mind corresponding to it. Thus, then, they speak so as to deny that anyone wills, rejoices, or is cautious except the wise man; but that the foolish man only desires, is glad, fears, is saddened; and that those three are constancies, but these four disturbances according to Cicero, and according to most, passions. In Greek those three, as I said, are called εὐπάθειαι, but these four πάθη.

When I inquired as carefully as I could whether this manner of speech agrees with the holy Scriptures, I found what the prophet says, *there is no rejoicing for the impious, says the Lord*[^48]; as though the impious can be glad rather than rejoice over evils, because rejoicing belongs properly to the good and devout. Likewise that in the Gospel, *whatever you will that men do to you, do you also to them*[^49], seems to be so said as though no one can will anything evil or shameful, but only desire it.

Finally, on account of the custom of speech, some translators added *good* and so rendered it, *whatever good you will that men do to you*. For they thought caution must be taken lest anyone will dishonorable things to be done to him by men—to say nothing of the more shameful—certainly luxurious banquets, in which, if he too provided them for others, he might suppose he was fulfilling this precept. But in the Greek Gospel, from which it was translated into Latin, *good* is not read, but, *whatever you will that men do to you, do you also to them*; I believe for this reason, that in saying *you will* he already wished *good* to be understood. For he did not say "you desire."

**8. 2.** Yet our speech is not always to be reined in by these properties, but sometimes they must be used; and when we read those whose authority it is not lawful to resist, they are to be understood in that sense where a right meaning can find no other way out, as are those passages we recalled, partly from the prophet, partly from the Gospel, by way of example. For who does not know that the impious exult with gladness? And yet, *there is no rejoicing for the impious, says the Lord*[^50]. Whence, except because *to rejoice* is something else, when this word is put properly and precisely?

Likewise, who would deny that men are rightly commanded to do to others whatever they desire to be done to themselves by others, lest they delight one another in the shamefulness of unlawful pleasure? And yet it is a most wholesome and most true precept, *whatever you will that men do to you, do you also the same to them*[^51]. And whence is this, except because in this place *will* is put in a certain proper sense, which cannot be taken in a bad sense?

But in the more usual mode of speech, which the custom of language especially frequents, it would surely not be said, *do not will to tell any lie*[^52], unless there were also an evil will, from whose depravity that will is distinguished which the angels proclaimed, saying, *peace on earth to men of good will*[^53]. For *good* was added superfluously, if it could not be other than good. And what great thing would the Apostle have said in the praises of charity, that it does not rejoice over iniquity[^54], except because malice so rejoices?

And among the authors of secular letters such an indifference of these words is found. For Cicero, that most distinguished orator, says, *I desire (cupio), conscript fathers, to be merciful*[^55]. Because he put that word in a good sense, who would be so perversely learned as to contend that he ought to have said not *I desire* (cupio) but rather "I will" (volo)? Again, in Terence the shameful young man, burning with insane desire, says, *I will nothing else but Philumena*. That this will was lust the reply sufficiently indicates which is there put in the mouth of his more sober slave. For he says to his master, *how much better it would be for you to take pains to remove this love from your mind, than to say things by which your lust is the more vainly inflamed?*[^56] But that they put "joy" too in a bad sense, that very Virgilian verse is witness, where he embraced these four disturbances with the utmost brevity, *hence they fear and desire, grieve and rejoice*[^57]. The same author also said, *the evil joys of the mind*[^58].

**8. 3.** Accordingly, both the good and the evil will, are cautious, rejoice; and, to express the same things in other words, both the good and the evil desire, fear, are glad; but the former well, the latter ill, according as men have a right or a perverse will. Sadness itself, too, in place of which the Stoics thought nothing could be found in the mind of the wise man, is found in a good sense, especially among our own. For the Apostle praises the Corinthians because they were saddened according to God. But perhaps someone will say that the Apostle congratulated them because they were saddened by repenting, which kind of sadness can belong only to those who have sinned. For he says thus, *I see that that letter, though for an hour, made you sad; now I rejoice, not because you were made sad, but because you were made sad unto repentance. For you were made sad according to God, that in nothing you might suffer loss from us. For the sadness that is according to God works repentance unto a salvation not to be repented of; but the sadness of the world works death. For behold, this very thing, to be saddened according to God, how great an earnestness it has wrought in you*[^59].

And on this score the Stoics can answer for their part that sadness seems useful for this, that one may repent of having sinned; but that in the mind of the wise man it cannot be, because neither sin befalls him, of whose repentance he would be saddened, nor any other evil, by suffering and feeling which he would be sad. For they relate that Alcibiades too (if memory does not deceive me about the man's name), when he seemed happy to himself, wept while Socrates argued and showed him how wretched he was, since he was foolish[^60]. For him, then, folly was the cause even of this useful and to-be-wished-for sadness, by which a man grieves that he is what he ought not to be. But the Stoics say that not the foolish, but the wise man, cannot be sad.

## What the affections are in the saints . . .

**9. 1.** But to these philosophers, as far as pertains to this question of the disturbances of the mind, we have already replied in the ninth book of this work[^61], showing that they are more eager for contention about words than about things, and more for contention than for truth. But among us, according to the holy Scriptures and sound doctrine, the citizens of the holy city of God, living according to God in the pilgrimage of this life, fear and desire, grieve and rejoice; and because their love is right, they have all these affections right.

They fear eternal punishment, they desire eternal life; they grieve in the present, because they themselves still groan within themselves, awaiting the adoption, the redemption of their body[^62]; they rejoice in hope, because there will come to pass *the word that is written: death is swallowed up in victory*[^63]. Likewise they fear to sin, they desire to persevere; they grieve over sins, they rejoice in good works. For that they may fear to sin, they hear, *because iniquity will abound, the charity of many will grow cold*[^64]; that they may desire to persevere, they hear what is written, *he who shall persevere to the end, he shall be saved*[^65]; that they may grieve over sins, they hear, *if we say that we have no sin, we deceive ourselves, and the truth is not in us*[^66]; that they may rejoice in good works, they hear, *God loves a cheerful giver*[^67].

Likewise, according as their weakness or strength shall be, they fear to be tempted, they desire to be tempted; they grieve in temptations, they rejoice in temptations. For that they may fear to be tempted, they hear, *if anyone be overtaken in any fault, you who are spiritual instruct such a one in the spirit of meekness, considering yourself, lest you also be tempted*[^68]; but that they may desire to be tempted, they hear a certain strong man of the city of God saying, *prove me, Lord, and tempt me; burn my reins and my heart*[^69]; that they may grieve in temptations, they see Peter weeping[^70]; that they may rejoice in temptations, they hear James saying, *count it all joy, my brethren, when you fall into various temptations*[^71].

**9. 2.** But they are moved by these affections not only on their own account, but also on account of those whom they desire to be freed and fear lest they perish, and grieve if they perish and rejoice if they are freed. For that best and most courageous man who glories in his weaknesses[^72]—to recall him especially, we who have come into the Church of Christ from the nations—the teacher of the nations in faith and truth, who both labored more than all his fellow apostles[^73] and instructed the peoples of God by many letters, not only those who were seen present by him, but also those who were foreseen as to come;

that man, I say, the athlete of Christ, taught by him, anointed by him[^74], crucified with him[^75], glorious in him, in the theater of this world, to which he was made a spectacle both to angels and to men[^76], contending lawfully in the great contest and pressing on toward the palm of the heavenly calling[^77]—they most gladly behold with the eyes of faith rejoicing with those who rejoice, weeping with those who weep[^78], having fightings without, fears within[^79], desiring to be dissolved and to be with Christ[^80], longing to see the Romans, that he might have some fruit among them too, as among the other nations[^81], jealous over the Corinthians and by that very jealousy fearing lest their minds be seduced from the chastity which is in Christ[^82], having great sadness and continual grief of heart over the Israelites[^83], because, ignorant of God's righteousness and wishing to establish their own, they were not subject to the righteousness of God[^84]; and not only declaring his grief but also his mourning over certain ones who had sinned before and had not done penance over their uncleanness and fornications[^85].

**9. 3.** If these motions, these affections, coming from the love of good and from holy charity, are to be called vices, let us allow that things which are truly vices be called virtues. But since these affections follow right reason when they are applied where they ought to be, who would then dare to call them diseases or vicious passions? Wherefore even the Lord himself, in the form of a servant deigning to lead a human life[^86], but having no sin at all, applied them where he judged they should be applied. For in him, in whom there was a true body of a man and a true mind of a man, human affection was not false. When, therefore, these things are related of him in the Gospel—that he was saddened with anger over the hardness of heart of the Jews[^87], that he said, *I rejoice for your sakes, that you may believe*[^88], that, about to raise Lazarus, he even shed tears[^89], that he desired to eat the Passover with his disciples[^90], that, with his passion drawing near, his soul was sorrowful[^91]—they are surely not related falsely. But he took up these motions, by the grace of a certain dispensation, in his human mind when he willed, just as he became man when he willed.

## What is to be thought of the impassibility of the Stoics.

**9. 4.** Accordingly—as must be confessed—even when we have these affections right and according to God, they belong to this life, not to that which we hope is to come, and often we yield to them even against our will. And so sometimes, although moved not by blameworthy desire but by praiseworthy charity, we weep even while we do not wish to. We have them, then, from the weakness of the human condition; but not so the Lord Jesus, whose weakness too was from power. But while we bear the weakness of this life, if we have none of them at all, then rather we do not live rightly. For the Apostle censured and detested certain ones whom he even said were without affection[^92]. The sacred Psalm too blamed those of whom it says, *I waited for one who would be saddened together with me, and there was none*[^93]. For to grieve not at all, while we are in this place of misery, surely—as one even among the learned of this world perceived and said—*does not happen without a great cost: savagery in the mind, stupor in the body*[^94].

Wherefore that which is called in Greek ἀπάθεια (which, if it could be rendered in Latin, would be called "impassibility"), if it is to be so understood (it is taken of the mind, indeed, not of the body) that one lives without those affections which befall contrary to reason and disturb the mind, is plainly good and most to be wished; but it too does not belong to this life. For it is not the voice of just any sort of men, but especially of the devout and of those who are much just and holy, *if we say that we have no sin, we deceive ourselves, and the truth is not in us*[^95]. That ἀπάθεια, then, will be when there will be no sin in man. But now one lives well enough if without crime; but he who supposes that he lives without sin does not bring it about that he has no sin, but that he receives no pardon.

Furthermore, if that is to be called ἀπάθεια, when no affection at all can touch the mind, who would not judge this stupor to be worse than all vices? It can therefore be said, not absurdly, that perfect blessedness will be without the goad of fear and without any sadness; but who would say that there will not be love and joy there, unless he were in every way cut off from the truth? But if that is ἀπάθεια where no fear terrifies and no grief torments, it is to be shunned in this life, if we wish to live rightly, that is, according to God; but in that blessed life which is promised as everlasting, it is plainly to be hoped for.

**9. 5.** For that fear of which the apostle John says, *fear is not in charity, but perfect charity casts out fear, because fear has punishment; but he who fears is not perfected in charity*[^96], is not of the kind of fear by which the apostle Paul feared lest the Corinthians be seduced by the serpent's cunning[^97]; for this fear charity has—indeed only charity has it—but it is of that kind of fear which is not in charity, of which the apostle Paul himself says, *for you have not received the spirit of bondage again in fear*[^98]. But that *chaste fear that abides forever and ever*[^99], if it will be also in the age to come (for in what other way can it be understood to abide forever and ever?), is not a fear that terrifies away from an evil that can befall, but one that holds fast in a good that cannot be lost.

For where the love of the good attained is unchangeable, surely, if it can be so said, the fear of avoiding evil is free of care. For by the name of chaste fear is signified that will by which it will be necessary for us to be unwilling to sin, and to beware of sin not by anxiety of weakness, lest perchance we sin, but by the tranquillity of charity. Or if there can be no kind of fear at all in that most sure security of perpetual and happy joys, then it was said, *the chaste fear of the Lord abiding forever and ever*[^100], in the same manner as it was said, *the patience of the poor will not perish forever*[^101]. For neither will patience itself be eternal, since it is not necessary except where evils must be endured; but that will be eternal at which one arrives through patience. So perhaps chaste fear was said to abide forever and ever because that will abide to which fear itself leads.

**9. 6.** Since these things are so, because a right life must be led by which one is to arrive at the blessed life, the right life has all these affections right, the perverse life has them perverse. But the blessed and likewise eternal life will have love and joy not only right but also sure; but no fear or grief. Whence it now appears, in some measure, what the citizens of the city of God ought to be in this pilgrimage, living according to the spirit, not according to the flesh, that is, according to God, not according to man, and what they will be in that immortality toward which they are tending.

But the city, that is, the society, of the impious—of those living not according to God but according to man, and following, in the very worship of false divinity and contempt of the true, the doctrines of men or of demons—is shaken by these depraved affections as by diseases and disturbances. And if it has any citizens who seem to moderate such motions and, as it were, to temper them, they are so proud and elated in their impiety that, by this very thing, their swellings are the greater in them, the smaller their griefs. And if some, by a vanity the more monstrous the rarer, have so loved this in themselves that they are roused and stirred by no affection at all, bent and inclined by none: they lose all humanity rather than attain true tranquillity. For not because a thing is hard is it therefore right, nor because it is stupefied is it therefore sound.

## The first human beings were blessed in paradise.

**10.** But whether the first man, or the first human beings (for it was a marriage of two), had these affections in the natural body before sin, such as we shall not have in the spiritual body when all sin has been purged and ended, is not unreasonably asked. For if they had them, how were they blessed in that memorable place of blessedness, that is, paradise? For who, after all, can be called absolutely blessed who is affected by fear or grief? And what could those human beings fear or grieve over in so great an affluence of so great goods, where neither was death feared nor any ill health of the body, nor was anything lacking that a good will would attain, nor was anything present that would offend the flesh or mind of a man living happily?

There was an undisturbed love toward God and between the spouses living in a faithful and sincere fellowship, and from this love a great joy, since what was loved did not cease to be there for enjoyment. There was a tranquil avoidance of sin, and while this remained, no evil at all rushed in from any source to sadden them. Or were they perhaps desiring to touch the forbidden tree to eat of it, but fearing to die, and thus did both desire and fear even then disturb those human beings, even in that place?

Far be it that we should suppose this to have been, where there was no sin at all. For it is no small sin to covet the things that the law of God forbids and to abstain from them out of fear of punishment, not out of love of righteousness. Far be it, I say, that before all sin there should already have been there such a sin that they should commit, with regard to the tree, what the Lord says concerning a woman, *if anyone looks at a woman to desire her, he has already committed adultery with her in his heart*[^102].

As happy, then, as they were, and disturbed by no perturbations of mind, harmed by no discomforts of body: so happy would the whole human society be, if neither they had committed the evil which they passed on also to their posterity, nor anyone of their stock had committed by iniquity anything that he would receive by damnation;

and, this happiness remaining until, through that blessing by which it was said, *increase and multiply*[^103], the number of the predestined saints was completed, another and greater happiness would be given, which has been given to the most blessed angels, where there would now be sure security that no one would sin and no one would die, and the life of the saints would be such, after no experience of labor, grief, or death, as it will be after all these things, in the incorruption of bodies, the resurrection of the dead being rendered.

## God, foreknowing that man would sin, resolved to save him.

**11. 1.** But because God foreknew all things, and therefore could not be ignorant that man too would sin, we ought to assert the holy city according to that which he foreknew and disposed, not according to that which could not come into our knowledge, because it was not in God's disposition. For man could not by his sin disturb the divine counsel, as though he compelled God to change what he had resolved; since God by foreknowing anticipated both—that is, both how evil the man whom he himself created good would be, and what good he himself would even so make out of him.

For although God is said to change what he has resolved (whence by a figurative mode of speech God is even read in the Scriptures to have repented[^104]), this is said according to that which man had hoped, or which the order of natural causes was bringing about, not according to that which the Almighty had foreknown he himself would do. God therefore made man, as it is written, *upright*[^105], and on this account of good will. For he would not be upright if he did not have a good will. A good will, then, is the work of God; for with it man was made by him.

But the first evil will, since it preceded all evil works in man, was rather a certain falling away from the work of God to its own works than any work, and therefore the works were evil, because they were according to itself, not according to God; so that the will itself was, as it were, the evil tree[^106] of those works as of evil fruits, or rather the man himself insofar as he was of evil will.

Furthermore, the evil will, although it is not according to nature but contrary to nature, because it is a vice, nevertheless belongs to that nature of which it is the vice, since it cannot be except in a nature—but in that nature which the Creator created from nothing, not that which he begot from himself, as he begot the Word through whom all things were made[^107]; for, although God fashioned man from the dust of the earth[^108], that same earth and all earthly matter is altogether from nothing, and he gave to the body a soul made from nothing, when man was made.

But evils are so far overcome by goods that, although they are allowed to be, in order to demonstrate how the most provident justice of the Creator can use even them well, goods can nevertheless exist without evils, as does God himself, the true and supreme, as does every heavenly creature, invisible and visible, above this murky air; but evils cannot exist without goods, since the natures in which they are, insofar as they are natures, are certainly good. Moreover, evil is removed not by some nature that had been added being taken away, or any part of it, but by that which had been vitiated and depraved being healed and corrected.

The choice of the will, then, is truly free only when it does not serve vices and sins. Such it was given by God; and, lost through its own vice, it cannot be restored except by him by whom it could be given. Whence the Truth says, *if the Son frees you, then you will be truly free*[^109]. And this is the same as if he said, "if the Son saves you, then you will be truly saved." For he is the one who frees from the same source from which he is the one who saves.

## The first human beings sinned otherwise.

**11. 2.** Man therefore lived according to God in a paradise both corporeal and spiritual. For neither was the paradise corporeal on account of the goods of the body and not spiritual on account of those of the mind; nor was it spiritual that man might enjoy it through the inner senses and not corporeal that he might enjoy it through the outer. It was plainly both, for the sake of both.

But after that proud, and therefore envious, angel, by that same pride turned from God to himself and, by a certain tyrannical haughtiness as it were, choosing to rejoice over subjects rather than to be subject, fell from the spiritual paradise

(concerning whose fall, and that of his companions, who from angels of God were made his angels, I have argued sufficiently, as far as I could, in the eleventh and twelfth books of this work[^110]), seeking by ill-advising craftiness to creep into the perception of man—whom, since he himself had fallen, he envied for standing—he chose the serpent in the corporeal paradise, where, with those two human beings, male and female, the other land animals too dwelt subject and harmless, as the one through whom he might speak,

a slippery animal, namely, and moving in tortuous coils, suited to his work; and, having subjected it to himself by his angelic presence and more excellent nature through spiritual wickedness, and abusing it as an instrument, he spoke with deceit to the woman, beginning, namely, from the inferior part of that human couple, that he might come gradually to the whole, not supposing the man to be easily credulous or able to be deceived by erring himself, but while yielding to another's error.

For just as Aaron did not consent, persuaded, to the erring people in making the idol, but yielded, constrained[^111], and it is not credible that Solomon supposed by error that idols should be served, but was driven to those sacrileges by womanly blandishments[^112]:

so it must be believed that that man believed his woman—one believing one, a human being a human being, a spouse a spouse—in transgressing God's law, seduced not as though believing one speaking the truth, but yielding by the tie of fellowship. For not in vain did the Apostle say, *and Adam was not seduced, but the woman was seduced*[^113], except because she accepted what the serpent spoke to her as though it were true, while he was unwilling to be parted from his sole companion even in the fellowship of sin; nor was he therefore less guilty, since he sinned knowingly and deliberately.

Whence the Apostle too says not "he did not sin," but, *he was not seduced*; for he certainly shows him to have sinned where he says, *by one man sin entered into the world*, and a little later more plainly, *in the likeness*, he says, *of the transgression of Adam*[^114].

But he wished those to be understood as "seduced" who do not think that what they do is a sin; but the man knew. Otherwise how will it be true, *Adam was not seduced*? But, inexperienced in the divine severity, he could be deceived in this, that he believed what was committed to be venial. And on this account, in that respect in which the woman was seduced, he was not seduced, but it deceived him how that was to be judged which he was going to say, *the woman whom you gave to be with me, she gave to me, and I ate*[^115]. What more, then? Even if both were not deceived by believing, yet both were captured by sinning and entangled in the snares of the devil.

**12.** But if anyone is moved as to why human nature is not so changed by other sins as it was changed by that transgression of the first two human beings, so that it lies subject, through this, to such great corruption as we see and feel, and to death, and is disturbed and tossed by so many and so great affections so contrary to one another, such as it certainly was not in paradise before sin, although it was in a natural body:

if anyone is moved by this, as I said, he ought not therefore to suppose that thing committed to have been slight and small because it was done with food—not indeed evil or harmful, except that it was forbidden; for God would not create or plant anything evil in that place of such great happiness. But obedience was commended in the precept, which virtue, in a rational creature, is in a certain way the mother and guardian of all virtues; since it was so made that it is useful for it to be subject, but ruinous to do its own will, not that of him by whom it was created. This precept, then, about not eating one kind of food, where so great an abundance of others lay at hand—so slight to observe, so brief to retain in memory, where especially desire did not yet resist the will, as it did afterward as a consequence of the punishment of transgression—was violated with the greater injustice the easier it could have been kept by observance.

## Each city is distinguished by pride and humility.

**13. 1.** But in secret they began to be evil, so as to slip into open disobedience. For one would not come to an evil deed unless an evil will had preceded. Now what could be the beginning of an evil will but pride? For *pride is the beginning of all sin*[^116].

But what is pride but an appetite for a perverse height? For it is a perverse height to forsake the principle to which the mind ought to cleave, and in a certain way to become and to be a principle to oneself. This happens when one is too pleased with oneself. But one is thus pleased with oneself when one falls away from that unchangeable good which ought to have pleased one more than oneself.

But this falling away is voluntary; since, if the will had remained stable in the love of the higher unchangeable good, by which it was illumined that it might see and kindled that it might love, it would not have turned from there to being pleased with itself, and from this grown dark and cold, so that the woman believed the serpent had spoken the truth, or the man set the will of his wife before the command of God and supposed himself to be venially a transgressor of the precept, if he did not forsake the companion of his life even in the fellowship of sin.

The evil deed, then—that is, that transgression, that they ate of the forbidden food—was not done except by those who were already evil. For that evil fruit could not be produced except by an evil tree[^117]. But that the tree was evil was done contrary to nature, because it would certainly not have come about except by a vice of the will, which is contrary to nature. But a nature could not be depraved by a vice unless it had been made from nothing. And on this account, that it is a nature, it has from the fact that it was made by God; but that it falls away from that which it is, from the fact that it was made from nothing.

Nor did man so fall away as to become altogether nothing, but, inclined toward himself, to become less than he was when he cleaved to him who supremely is. Therefore, having forsaken God, to be in oneself, that is, to please oneself, is not now to be nothing, but to draw near to nothing. Whence, according to the holy Scriptures, the proud are called by another name *self-pleasers*[^118]. For it is good to have the heart on high; yet not toward oneself, which is of pride, but toward the Lord[^119], which is of obedience, which can belong only to the humble.

## [Humility and pride]

There is therefore, in a wondrous way, something of humility that makes the heart high, and there is something of elation that makes the heart low. This indeed seems as it were contrary, that elation should be downward and humility upward. But devout humility makes one subject to a superior; but nothing is superior to God; and therefore humility exalts, because it makes one subject to God.

But elation, which is in vice, by that very thing rejects subjection and falls from him than whom nothing is higher, and from this it will be lower, and there comes to pass what is written, *you cast them down while they were being lifted up*[^120]. For he does not say "after they had been lifted up," so that first they were lifted up and afterward cast down; but *while they were being lifted up*, then they were cast down. For the very being lifted up is already to be cast down.

Wherefore, that now in the city of God, and to the city of God on pilgrimage in this age, humility is especially commended, and is especially proclaimed in its king, who is Christ[^121], while the contrary vice of elation is taught by the sacred writings especially to dominate in his adversary, who is the devil[^122]: this surely is the great difference by which the two cities of which we speak are distinguished—one, namely, the society of devout human beings, the other of the impious, each with the angels belonging to it, in which in the one love of God has gone before, in the other love of self.

**13. 2.** By a manifest and open sin, then, where there was done what God had forbidden to be done, the devil would not have captured man, had he not already begun to be pleased with himself. For from this there also delighted him what was said, *you will be as gods*[^123]. Which they could better be by cleaving to the supreme and true principle through obedience, than by becoming their own principle to themselves through pride.

For created gods are gods not by their own truth, but by participation in the true God[^124]. But by seeking more, he becomes less, who, while he chooses to suffice for himself, falls away from him who truly suffices for him.

That evil, then, by which, when man is pleased with himself as though he too were light, he is turned away from that light by which, if he were pleased with it, he too would become light: that evil, I say, preceded in secret, so that there should follow this evil which was perpetrated in the open. For it is true what is written, *before ruin the heart is exalted, and before glory it is humbled*[^125]. That ruin, indeed, which happens in secret, precedes the ruin which happens in the open, while the former is not thought to be a ruin. For who thinks exaltation to be a ruin, when there is already there the falling away by which the Most High has been forsaken? But who does not see it to be a ruin, when there comes about an evident and undoubted transgression of the commandment?

For this reason God forbade that which, once it had been committed, could be defended by no pretense of justice. And I dare to say that it is useful for the proud to fall into some open and manifest sin, that they may be displeased with themselves, who had already fallen by being pleased with themselves.

For Peter was more wholesomely displeased with himself when he wept than he was pleased with himself when he presumed[^126]. This the sacred Psalm too says, *fill their faces with shame, and they will seek your name, Lord*[^127], that is, that you may please them seeking your name, who had pleased themselves seeking their own.

**14.** But there is a worse and more damnable pride, by which a refuge of excuse is sought even in manifest sins; as did those first human beings, of whom the woman said, *the serpent seduced me, and I ate*, and the man said, *the woman whom you gave to be with me, she gave to me of the tree, and I ate*[^128]. Nowhere here is there the sound of a request for pardon, nowhere an imploring of medicine. For although these do not, like Cain[^129], deny what they committed, yet pride still seeks to refer to something else what it did wrongly: the woman's pride to the serpent, the man's pride to the woman.

But it is rather an accusation than a true excuse, where there is an open transgression of the divine command. For they did not do this for the reason that the woman committed it at the serpent's persuasion, or the man at the woman's prompting, as though anything was to be set before God, to whom either belief or deference should have been given.

## A most grievous disobedience was justly condemned . . .

**15.** 1. Because, then, God was despised when he commanded—he who had created man, who had made him to his own image, who had set him above the other animals, who had established him in paradise, who had furnished an abundance of all things and of salvation, who had burdened him with precepts neither many nor great nor difficult, but had supported him with one most brief and most light precept toward the wholesomeness of obedience,

by which he reminded that creature, for whom a free servitude was profitable, that he was its Lord: a just condemnation followed, and such a condemnation,

that the man, who by keeping the command was to have been spiritual even in flesh, became carnal even in mind; and he who by his own pride had been pleased with himself was given over to himself by God's justice;

and not so as to be in every way in his own power, but, dissenting even from himself, he would lead under him, to whom by sinning he consented, a hard and wretched servitude in place of the liberty he had desired—dead in spirit willingly, and about to die in body unwillingly, a deserter of eternal life and condemned to eternal death too, unless grace should free him.

Whoever thinks such a condemnation either excessive or unjust surely does not know how to measure how great the iniquity was in sinning, where there was so great an ease of not sinning. For just as Abraham's great obedience is not undeservedly proclaimed, because a most difficult thing was commanded, that he should kill his son[^130]: so in paradise the disobedience was the greater, the greater the ease, since that which was commanded was of no difficulty. And just as the obedience of the second man is the more to be proclaimed, because *he became obedient unto death*[^131]: so the disobedience of the first man is the more to be detested, because he became disobedient unto death. For where a great punishment of disobedience was set forth, and an easy thing commanded by the Creator, who can sufficiently explain how great an evil it is not to obey in an easy matter, at the command of so great a power, and with so terrifying a punishment?

**15.** 2. In short, to say it briefly, in the punishment of that sin what was rendered for disobedience but disobedience? For what other misery of man is there but his own disobedience against himself, so that, because he would not what he could, he wills what he cannot[^132]? For in paradise, although before sin he could not do all things, yet whatever he could not do, he did not will, and therefore he could do all that he willed; but now, as we recognize in his stock, and as divine Scripture testifies, *man has been made like to vanity*[^133]. For who can count how many things he wills which he cannot, while he himself—that is, his own mind, and his flesh, which is lower than it—does not obey himself, that is, his will? For even against his will the mind is often disturbed, and the flesh grieves and grows old and dies, and whatever else we suffer that we would not suffer unwillingly, if our nature in every way and in all its parts obeyed our will.

But the flesh suffers something, whereby it is not allowed to serve. What does it matter whence, provided that by the justice of the dominating God, whom, being subject, we would not serve, our flesh, which had been subject to us, is troublesome to us by not serving—although we, by not serving God, could be troublesome to ourselves, not to him?

For he does not need our service as we need the service of the body, and therefore what we receive is our own punishment, not his, for what we did. But the pains that are called pains of the flesh are pains of the soul in the flesh and from the flesh. For what does the flesh, by itself without the soul, either grieve over or desire?

But when the flesh is said to desire or to grieve, it is either the man himself, as we have discussed[^134], or something of the soul which the affection of the flesh touches—either harsh, so as to make pain, or gentle, so as to make pleasure. But pain of the flesh is only an offense of the soul from the flesh and a certain disagreement with its affection, just as pain of the mind, which is called sadness, is a disagreement with those things which have happened to us against our will. But sadness is usually preceded by fear, which is itself also in the soul, not in the flesh. But pain of the flesh is not preceded by any fear of the flesh, as it were, which would be felt in the flesh before the pain. But pleasure is preceded by a certain appetite, which is felt in the flesh as its desire, such as hunger and thirst and that which in the genitals is more usually named lust, although this is the general term of every desire.

For the ancients defined anger itself to be nothing other than the lust of avenging[^135]; although sometimes a man, where there is no sense of revenge, is angry even at inanimate things, and, angered, dashes down the pen that writes badly or breaks the reed. But this too, although more irrational, is nevertheless a certain lust of avenging, and—if I may so speak—a sort of shadow of retribution, that those who do ill may suffer ill. There is, then, a lust of avenging, which is called anger; there is a lust of having money, which is avarice; there is a lust of conquering in any way whatever, which is obstinacy; there is a lust of boasting, which is named vainglory. There are many and various lusts, of which some even have their own proper names, but some do not. For who could easily say what is to be called the lust of dominating, which yet civil wars too bear witness avails very greatly in the minds of tyrants?

## The heat of lust stirs the will.

**16.** Since, therefore, there are lusts of many things, yet when "lust" is said, and it is not added of what thing the lust is, scarcely anything is wont to occur to the mind but that by which the obscene parts of the body are excited. But this lust claims for itself not only the whole body, and not only outwardly, but also inwardly, and stirs up the whole man, the affection of the mind being joined and mingled at once with the appetite of the flesh, so that there follows that pleasure than which there is no greater among the pleasures of the body; so that at the very moment of time at which it is brought to its consummation, almost all the keenness and, as it were, watchfulness of thought is overwhelmed. But what friend of wisdom and of holy joys, leading a married life but, as the Apostle warned, *knowing how to possess his vessel in sanctification and honor, not in the disease of desire, as also the nations that know not God*[^136], would not prefer, if he could, to beget children without this lust, so that even in this office of sowing offspring those members which were created for this work might serve his mind, just as the other members each distributed to their own works, set in motion by the bidding of the will, not incited by the heat of lust?

But not even the very lovers of this pleasure are stirred when they will, whether to conjugal intercourse or to the uncleannesses of debaucheries; but sometimes that motion is importunate when no one asks for it, and sometimes it deserts the one who longs for it, and while desire boils in the mind, it grows cold in the body; and thus, in a wondrous way, lust does not serve not only the will to generate but even the lust to wanton, and although for the most part it opposes the mind that restrains it, sometimes it is even divided against itself, and, the mind being stirred, in stirring the body it does not follow itself.

Rightly is one most ashamed of this lust; rightly are those very members, which it moves or does not move by a certain right of its own, so to speak, not in every way at our choice, called shameful, which they were not before the sin of man. For as it is written, *they were naked, and were not confounded*[^137], not because their nakedness was unknown to them, but because there was not yet a shameful nakedness, since lust did not yet move those members apart from their choice, nor did the flesh, by its own disobedience, in a manner bear witness to convict the disobedience of man.

For they were not created blind, as the ignorant crowd supposes: since indeed he saw the animals to which he gave names[^138], and of her it is read, *the woman saw that the tree was good for food and that it pleased the eyes to behold*[^139].

Their eyes, then, were open, but they were not opened to this—that is, not attentive—so as to recognize what was furnished them by the garment of grace, when their members did not know how to resist their will. This grace being removed, that disobedience might be punished by a reciprocal punishment, there arose in the motion of the body a certain shameless novelty, whence nakedness became indecent, and it made them attentive and rendered them confounded.

Hence it is that, after they violated the command of God by open transgression, it is written of them, *and the eyes of both were opened, and they knew that they were naked, and they sewed fig leaves and made for themselves aprons*[^140]. *The eyes of both were opened*, it says, not to seeing, for they saw even before, but to discerning between the good which they had lost and the evil into which they had fallen. Whence the tree itself, because it would make this discernment if it were touched for eating against the prohibition, received its name from that very thing, so that it was called the tree of knowing good and evil. For when the trouble of disease has been experienced, the pleasantness of health too becomes more evident.

They knew, then, *that they were naked*—stripped, namely, of that grace by which it came about that the nakedness of the body did not confound them, no law of sin resisting their mind. This, then, they knew, which they would more happily not have known, if, believing and obedient to God, they had not committed what would force them to experience what unbelief and disobedience harm.

Accordingly, confounded by the disobedience of their flesh, as by a witness to the punishment of their own disobedience, *they sewed fig leaves and made for themselves aprons*, that is, girdles for the genitals. For some translators put *girdles*. Now *campestria* is indeed a Latin word, but said from this, that young men who exercised naked in the field (campus) covered their shameful parts; whence those who are so girded the common people call "campestrati."

What, then, lust moved disobediently against the will condemned by the guilt of disobedience, modesty covered shamefacedly. From this all the nations, since they were begotten from that stock, so hold it ingrained to veil the shameful parts that some barbarians do not have those parts of the body naked even in the baths, but bathe with coverings on them. Through the shady wildernesses of India too, where certain men philosophize naked, whence they are named gymnosophists, they nevertheless apply coverings to the genitals, which they lack over the rest of their members.

But the very act which is carried out by such lust—not only in any debaucheries, where hiding places are sought to escape human judgments, but even in the use of harlots, which the earthly city has made a permitted shamefulness—although there is done what no law of that city punishes, yet even permitted and unpunished lust shuns the public gaze, and by a natural shamefacedness the very brothels have provided secrecy; and unchastity could more easily go without the bonds of prohibition than shamelessness remove the hiding places of that foulness. But even shameful men themselves call this shameful, and although they are lovers of it, they do not dare to be displayers of it.

What of conjugal intercourse, which, according to the prescriptions of the marriage tablets, is done for the sake of begetting children—does not even it, although it is licit and honorable, seek out a chamber removed from witnesses? Does it not send out of doors all the servants, and even the bridesmen themselves, and whomever any kind of relationship had permitted to enter, before the spouse even begins to caress the spouse? And since, as even a certain *greatest author of Roman eloquence*[^141] says, *all rightly done things wish to be set in the light*[^142], that is, seek to be known: this rightly done thing so seeks to be known that it nevertheless blushes to be seen.

For who does not know what spouses do between themselves that children may be begotten? Since indeed, that this may be done, wives are led home with so great a celebration; and yet, when that is done whence children are born, not even the children themselves, if any are now born from it, are permitted to be witnesses.

For this rightly done thing so seeks the light of minds for the knowledge of itself that it nevertheless flees the light of eyes. Whence is this, except because that which is fitting by nature is so carried out that what is shameful by punishment also accompanies it?

**19.** Hence it is that even those philosophers who came nearer to the truth confessed anger and lust to be vicious parts of the soul, because they were moved in a turbid and disordered way even to those things which wisdom forbids to be perpetrated, and on this account have need of the mind and reason as their governor. This third part of the soul they hold to be set, as in a certain citadel, to rule these, so that, with it commanding and these serving, justice can be preserved in man from every part of the soul.

These parts, then, which they confess to be vicious even in a wise and temperate man, so that the mind, by checking and restraining them, may rein in and recall them from those things to which they are unjustly moved and permit them to those things which are granted by the law of wisdom (as anger to the exercise of a just chastisement, as lust to the office of propagating offspring): these parts, I say, were not vicious in paradise before sin. For they were not moved to anything against the right will, so that it would be necessary to hold them back by reason as if by reins governing them.

For that they are now so moved, and by those who live temperately and justly and devoutly are moderated—sometimes more easily, sometimes more difficultly—by restraining and resisting, is surely not soundness from nature, but languor from guilt.

But that modesty does not so hide the works of anger and of the other affections, in any words and deeds, as it hides the works of lust, which are done with the genital members—what is the reason, except because in the other members it is not the affections themselves, but the will, that moves the members of the body, when it has consented to them, which altogether dominates in their use? For whoever utters a word in anger, or even strikes someone, could not do this unless the tongue and hand were moved at the command, in a manner, of the will; which members, even when there is no anger, are moved by the same will.

But lust has so subjected the genital parts of the body to its own right, in a manner, that they are unable to be moved if it is lacking, and unless it has arisen, either of its own accord or excited. This is what is shameful, this is what, blushing, it shuns the eyes of beholders; and a man more easily bears a multitude of spectators when he unjustly grows angry at a man than the gaze even of one when he justly unites with his wife.

**20.** This those canine philosophers—that is, the Cynics—did not see, putting forth against human shamefacedness what else but a canine, that is, an unclean and shameless opinion? namely, that, since what is done with one's wife is just, one should not be ashamed to do it openly, nor avoid conjugal intercourse in any street or square whatever. Yet natural shame overcame the opinion of this error. For although they relate that Diogenes once did this, boastfully, supposing his sect would thus become more noble if his shamelessness were fixed more notably in the memory of men, yet afterward it ceased to be done by the Cynics, and shame prevailed more, so that men blushed before men, than the error, so that men should aspire to be like dogs.

Whence I rather think that he, or those, who are reported to have done this, gave to the eyes of men, who did not know what was being done under the cloak, the motions of those lying together, rather than that that pleasure could be carried out with a human gaze pressing upon them.

For there the philosophers were not ashamed to seem to wish to lie together, where lust itself would have been ashamed to arise.

And now we see that there are still Cynic philosophers; for these are they who not only are clothed with a cloak but even carry a club; yet none of them dares to do this, and if any did dare, they would be overwhelmed—not to say by the blows of those stoning them—certainly by the spittle of those spitting on them.

Human nature, then, without any doubt, is ashamed of this lust, and rightly is ashamed. For in its disobedience, which subjected the genital members of the body to its own motions alone and snatched them from the power of the will, there is sufficiently shown what was rendered to man for that first disobedience; which it was fitting should appear most of all in that part by which is generated the very nature which was changed for the worse by that first and great sin; from whose bond no one is rescued unless that which, when all were in one, was perpetrated unto the common ruin and avenged by God's justice, be expiated in each by God's grace.

## Before sin there was the power of begetting without lust.

**21.** Far be it, then, that we should believe that those spouses established in paradise would have fulfilled, through this lust of which, blushing, they covered those same members, what God said in his blessing, *increase and multiply and fill the earth*[^143]. For this lust arose after sin; after sin nature, not shameless, having lost the power to which the body served in every part, felt it, noticed it, blushed, covered it. But that blessing of marriage, that the married should increase and multiply and fill the earth, although it remained even in those who sinned, was nevertheless given before they sinned, that it might be known that the begetting of children pertains to the glory of marriage, not to the punishment of sin.

But now men, surely ignorant of that happiness which was in paradise, suppose that children could not have been begotten except through that which they have experienced, that is, through lust, of which we see even the very honorableness of marriage blush—some not accepting the divine Scriptures at all, where it is read that after sin they were ashamed of their nakedness and the shameful parts were covered, but unbelievingly mocking them;

but others, although they accept and honor them, nevertheless do not wish that which was said, *increase and multiply*, to be understood according to carnal fecundity, because something of the kind is read said also according to the soul, *you will multiply me in my soul in strength*[^144], so that what follows in Genesis, *and fill the earth and have dominion over it*[^145], they understand the *earth* to be the flesh, which the soul fills by its presence and especially dominates when it is multiplied in strength;

but that carnal offspring, without the lust which arose after sin, was looked upon, was confounded, was veiled, could not then be born, just as neither can it now, nor would there have been any in paradise, but outside, as also it was done. For after they were dismissed from there, they came together to beget children and begot them.

**22.** But we in no way doubt that to increase and multiply and fill the earth according to the blessing of God is a gift of marriage, which God instituted from the beginning before the sin of man, by creating male and female, which sex is certainly evident in the flesh. For to this work of God the blessing itself too was joined. For when Scripture had said, *male and female he made them*, it immediately added, *and God blessed them, saying: increase and multiply and fill the earth and have dominion over it*[^146], and the rest.

All of which things, although they can not unfittingly be referred also to a spiritual understanding, yet *male and female* cannot be understood as something similar even in one man—namely, because in him there is one thing that rules, another that is ruled; but, as appears most evidently in bodies of different sex, that male and female were so created that, by generating offspring, they would increase and multiply and fill the earth, it is of great absurdity to resist.

For it was not concerning the spirit that commands and the flesh that obeys, nor concerning the rational mind that rules and the irrational desire that is ruled, nor concerning the contemplative virtue that excels and the active that is subject, nor concerning the understanding of the mind and the sense of the body, but plainly concerning the conjugal bond by which each sex is bound to the other, that the Lord, when asked whether it was lawful to dismiss a wife for any cause whatever—since on account of the hardness of heart of the Israelites Moses permitted a bill of divorce to be given—

answered and said, *have you not read that he who made them from the beginning made them male and female, and said: for this cause a man shall leave father and mother and shall cleave to his wife, and the two shall be in one flesh? Therefore now they are not two, but one flesh. What therefore God has joined, let not man separate*[^147].

It is certain, then, that male and female were instituted at the first just as now we see and know two human beings of different sex, but that they are called one, either on account of the conjunction or on account of the origin of the woman, who was created from the side of the man. For the Apostle too, through this first example which preceded by God's institution, admonishes each individual that husbands should love their wives[^148].

## They could have begotten even without sin . . .

**23.** 1. But whoever says that they would not have had intercourse or begotten unless they had sinned, what does he say but that sin was necessary for the sake of the multitude of the saints? For if by not sinning they would have remained alone, because, as they think, they could not generate unless they had sinned: then surely sin was necessary, so that there could be not only two just human beings, but many.

But if it is absurd to believe this, rather is that to be believed, that the number of saints which suffices to complete that most blessed city would have come to be, even if no one had sinned, as great as is now gathered by God's grace from the multitude of sinners, so long as the children of this age generate and are generated[^149].

**23.** 2. And therefore that marriage, worthy of the happiness of paradise, had there been no sin, would both beget offspring to be loved and have no lust to be ashamed of. But how that could come about there is now no example by which to demonstrate it. Nor for that reason ought it to seem incredible that even that one member could serve the will without this lust, when so many members now serve it.

For do we not move hands and feet, when we will, to the things that are to be done with these members, without any resistance, with as great an ease as we see both in ourselves and in others, especially in the craftsmen of every kind of bodily work, where a more agile industry has come to exercise a weaker and slower nature? And do we not believe that, for the work of generating children, had there been no lust, which was rendered for the sin of disobedience, those members could similarly have served human beings, like the rest, at the bidding of the will?

Did not Cicero, in the books On the Republic, when he was discussing the difference of governments and took the likeness of this thing from the nature of man, say that the members of the body are commanded like sons, on account of the ease of obeying; but that the vicious parts of the soul are restrained like slaves, with a harsher rule[^150]? And surely by the order of nature the soul is set before the body, and yet the soul itself commands the body more easily than itself. But nevertheless this lust, of which we now treat, came to be the more to be blushed at because in it the soul neither effectively commands itself, so as not to lust at all, nor altogether the body, so that the will rather than lust may move the shameful members; for if it were so, they would not be shameful.

But now the soul is ashamed that it is resisted by the body, which by nature is subject to it as its inferior. For in the other affections, when it resists itself, it is therefore less ashamed, because, when it is conquered by itself, it itself conquers itself; even if inordinately and viciously, because it is conquered by those parts which ought to be subject to reason—yet by its own parts, and thus, as has been said, by itself.

For when the soul conquers itself in an orderly way, so that its irrational motions are subjected to the mind and reason, provided that reason too is subject to God, it is a matter of praise and virtue. Yet it is less shameful when the soul does not obey itself from its vicious parts than when the body, which is other than it and beneath it and whose nature does not live without it, does not yield to it willing and commanding.

**23.** 3. But when the other members are held under the command of the will, without which those that are aroused by lust against the will cannot fulfill what they crave, chastity is preserved, the pleasure of sin not being lost, but not permitted.

This resistance, this opposition, this quarrel of will and lust, or at any rate the insufficiency of lust for the will's purpose, marriage in paradise would without doubt not have had, unless the blameworthy disobedience were punished by a penal disobedience; but the members would all serve the will, like the rest.

Thus the vessel created for this work would sow the *generative field* as now the hand tills the earth[^151]; and—though now, when we wish to discuss this matter more carefully, shamefacedness resists and compels us to ask pardon of chaste ears, with honor first paid—there would then be no cause for that, but on all things that touched the perception of one pondering such members, free speech would be carried without any fear of obscenity, nor would the very words be such as are called obscene, but whatever was said of them would be as honorable as when we speak of the other parts of the body.

Whoever, then, comes to these pages with an unchaste mind, let him flee the guilt, not the nature; let him brand the deeds of his own foulness, not the words of our necessity; in which a chaste and religious reader or hearer most easily pardons me, while I refute unbelief, which argues not from the experience of things untried, but from the perception of things tried. For he reads these things without offense who does not shudder at the Apostle reproving the dreadful debaucheries of women, who *changed the natural use into that use which is against nature*[^152]; especially because we are not now recalling and reproving a damnable obscenity, as he was, but, in explaining as far as we can the workings of human generation, we nevertheless avoid obscene words, as he did.

**24.** 1. The man, then, would sow offspring, the woman would receive it, with the genital members moved by the will when there was need and as much as there was need, not aroused by lust. For we move at our bidding not only those members which are articulated with compacted bones, such as the hands and feet and fingers, but also those which are relaxed with soft sinews: when we will, we move them by agitating, and by stretching we extend them, and by twisting we bend them, and by tightening we harden them, such as are the things which, in the mouth and face, the will moves as much as it can.

The lungs themselves, finally, the softest of all the inner organs except the marrow, and for this reason protected by the cavity of the chest, serve the will, like the bellows of smiths or of organs, for drawing in and letting out the breath and for emitting or modulating the voice, in one who breathes out, breathes in, speaks, shouts, sings.

I pass over what has been naturally given to certain animals, that, if they have felt anything to be driven off at any place on the covering with which the whole body is clothed, they move it only there, where they feel it, and shake off by a trembling of the skin not only flies that settle but even spears that stick fast.

Is it, because man cannot do this, that therefore the Creator could not grant it to whatever living things he willed? So, then, man too could have had obedience even of the lower members, which he lost by his own disobedience. For it was not difficult for God so to create him that even that in his flesh should be moved only by his will, which now is not moved except by lust.

**24.** 2. For we know the natures of certain human beings to be much unlike the rest, and, by that very rarity, marvelous, doing certain things with their body as they will, which others can in no way do and, when they hear of them, scarcely believe. For there are those who can move their ears, either singly or both together. There are those who, with the head unmoved, bring down to the forehead the whole head of hair, as far as the hair reaches, and draw it back when they will.

There are those who, of the things they have swallowed—incredibly many and various—bring forth, with the upper belly a little handled, as if from a bag, whatever they please, quite whole. Some so imitate and reproduce the voices of birds and beasts and of any other human beings that, unless they are seen, they can in no way be distinguished. Some produce at will so many sounds from below, without any stench, that they seem to be singing even from that part. I myself have known a man who was wont to sweat when he willed. It is well known that certain men weep when they will, and pour out tears abundantly.

But that other thing is far more incredible, which very many of the brethren have experienced in most recent memory. There was a certain presbyter named Restitutus in the parish of the church of Calama. Whenever it pleased him (and he was asked to do this by those who desired to know the marvelous thing in their presence), at imitated voices, as it were, of someone lamenting, he so withdrew himself from his senses and lay most like a dead man, that not only did he by no means feel those who pinched and pricked him, but sometimes he was even burned by fire applied to him without any sense of pain except afterward from the wound; and that he did not move his body, not by resisting but by not feeling, was proved by this, that, as in a dead man, no breathing was found; yet the voices of men, if they spoke more clearly, he afterward reported that he had heard, as it were, from afar.

Since, therefore, the body even now serves certain persons—although leading this toilsome life in corruptible flesh—marvelously in very many motions and affections beyond the usual measure of nature: what reason is there that we should not believe that before the sin of disobedience and the punishment of corruption the human members could serve the human will for propagating offspring without any lust?

Man, then, was given over to himself, because he forsook God by being pleased with himself, and, not obeying God, he could not obey even himself. Hence the more evident misery, by which man does not live as he wills. For if he lived as he willed, he would think himself happy; yet not even so would he be, if he lived shamefully.

## Who can live as he wills if he wills what he can.

**25.** Although, if we attend more carefully, no one lives as he wills but the happy man, and no one is happy but the just man. But even the just man himself will not live as he wills, unless he has arrived where he can in no way die, be deceived, be offended, and where it is certain to him that it will always be so. For this nature seeks, and it will not be fully and perfectly happy unless it has attained what it seeks. But now what man can live as he wills, when to live itself is not in his power? For he wills to live, he is forced to die. How, then, does he live as he wills, who does not live as long as he wills? But if he should will to die, how can he live as he wills, who does not will to live? And if he should will to die, not because he does not will to live, but that after death he may live better: then he does not yet live as he wills, but will when, by dying, he has arrived at that which he wills.

But now, what human being can live as he wishes, when living itself is not within his power? He wishes to live and is forced to die. How, then, does someone live as he wishes if he does not live as long as he wishes? But if he wishes to die, how can he live as he wishes when he does not wish to live? And if he wishes to die, not because he does not wish to live, but in order to live better after death, then he does not yet live as he wishes; he will do so when, by dying, he reaches what he wishes.

But behold, let him live as he wills, since he has wrung from himself and commanded himself not to will what he cannot, and to will what he can; as Terence says, *since that cannot be done which you wish, wish that which you can*[^153]: is he therefore happy, because he is patiently wretched? For the happy life, if it is not loved, is not possessed. Moreover, if it is loved and possessed, it must be loved more excellently than all other things, since on account of it whatever else is loved is to be loved. Moreover, if it is loved as much as it is worthy to be loved (for he is not happy by whom the happy life itself is not loved as it is worthy), it cannot be that one who so loves it should not will it eternal. Then, therefore, it will be happy, when it will be eternal.

**26.** Man, then, lived in paradise as he willed, as long as he willed what God had commanded; he lived enjoying God, from which good he was good; he lived without any want, having it in his power so to live always. Food was at hand lest he hunger, drink lest he thirst, the tree of life lest old age dissolve him. There was nothing of corruption in the body, or from the body, to bring any troubles to any of his senses. No disease within, no blow from without was feared. In the flesh the highest health, in the mind complete tranquillity.

As in paradise there was no heat or cold, so in its inhabitant there befell no offense of good will from desire or fear. There was nothing sad at all, nothing emptily glad. True joy was perpetuated from God, toward whom blazed love *from a pure heart and a good conscience and unfeigned faith*[^154]; and between the spouses a faithful fellowship from honorable love, a harmonious vigilance of mind and body, and a keeping of the command without labor. No weariness wearied them at leisure, no sleep pressed upon them unwilling.

In so great an ease of things and happiness of human beings, far be it that we should suspect that offspring could not be sown without the disease of lust; but those members would be moved by the bidding of the will, like the rest, and, without the alluring goad of heat, with tranquillity of mind and body, with no corruption of integrity, the husband would be poured into the bosom of the wife[^155].

For it is not to be disbelieved because it cannot be proved by experience, that, when a turbid heat did not drive those parts of the body, but a spontaneous power applied them as there was need, the male seed could then be sent into the womb of the wife with the integrity of the female genitals preserved, just as now, with the same integrity preserved, the menstrual flow of blood can be sent out from the womb of a virgin. For by the same way that this can be expelled, that could be inserted. For as, for giving birth, not the groans of pain but the impulse of maturity would relax the female organs, so, for impregnating and conceiving, not the appetite of lust but the voluntary use would join the two natures.

We are now speaking of shameful things, and therefore, although, before they were shameful, we conjecture as we can what they could have been, yet it is necessary that our discussion be reined in more by that shamefacedness which calls us back than aided by an eloquence which little supplies us. For since even those who could have experienced what I say did not experience it (because, sin coming first, they earned exile from paradise before they came together in the work of sowing offspring by a tranquil choice), how does it now occur to human perception, when these things are recalled, except by the experience of turbid lust, not by the conjecture of a placid will? This is why shame impedes the speaker, even if reason does not fail the thinker.

## [The sin of man and the foreknowledge of God]

Nevertheless, to almighty God, the supreme and supremely good creator of all natures, but the helper and rewarder of good wills, and the abandoner and condemner of evil ones, and the orderer of both, there was certainly not lacking a counsel by which to fill the fixed number of citizens predestined in his wisdom for his city, even from the condemned human race—now discerning them not by their merits, since the whole mass was condemned as in a vitiated root, but by grace, and showing, to those who are freed, not only from their own case but also from that of those who are not freed, what he bestows upon them.

For it is not by debt, but by gratuitous goodness, that each then recognizes himself rescued from evils, when he becomes free from the fellowship of those men with whom he would justly have a common punishment. Why, then, should God not create those whom he foreknew would sin, since indeed in them and from them he could show both what their guilt deserved and what his grace bestowed, nor under him as creator and disposer would the perverse disorder of those who transgress pervert the right order of things?

**27.** Accordingly, sinners, both angels and human beings, do nothing whereby *the great works of the Lord, sought out in all his wills*[^156], are hindered, since he who providently and omnipotently distributes to each his own knows how to use well not only the good but also the evil. And on this account, with the evil angel so condemned and hardened, for the deserving of his first evil will, that he would no longer have a good will, why should God, using him well, not permit the first man to be tempted by him—the man who had been created upright, that is, of good will? Since indeed he was so constituted that, if the good man trusted in God's help, he would conquer the evil angel; but if, proudly pleasing himself, he forsook God his creator and helper, he would be conquered: having a good merit in a right will divinely aided, but an evil one in a perverse will forsaking God.

For even that very trusting in God's help he could not indeed do without God's help, nor yet did he therefore not have it in his power to withdraw from these benefits of divine grace by pleasing himself. For just as to live in this flesh without the aids of food is not in our power, but not to live in it is in our power, which those do who kill themselves: so to live well without God's help, even in paradise, was not in his power; but to live ill was in his power, though with a happiness not to abide and a most just punishment to follow. Since, then, God was not ignorant of this future fall of man, why should he not allow him to be tempted by the malice of the envious angel? Not at all uncertain, indeed, that he would be conquered, but nonetheless foreknowing that by his seed, aided by his grace, that same devil was to be conquered, to the greater glory of the saints.

Thus it came about that neither did anything of the future lie hidden from God, nor did he by foreknowing compel anyone to sin, and that he demonstrated, by the experience that followed, to the angelic and human rational creature, what the difference is between each one's own presumption and his protection. For who would dare to believe or say that it was not in God's power that neither angel nor man should fall? But he preferred not to take this from their power, and so to show both how great an evil their pride could work and how great a good his grace.

## What the two cities are.

**28.** Two loves, then, made two cities: the earthly, namely, love of self even to the contempt of God; but the heavenly, love of God even to the contempt of self. In short, the former glories in itself, the latter in the Lord. For the former seeks glory from men; but for the latter God, the witness of conscience, is the greatest glory. The former lifts up its head in its own glory; the latter says to its God, *my glory, and the lifter up of my head*[^157]. In the former the lust of dominating dominates, in its princes or in the nations it subjugates; in the latter they serve one another in love, the rulers by counseling and the subjects by obeying. The former loves its own strength in its powerful men; the latter says to its God, *I will love you, Lord, my strength*[^158].

And therefore in the former its wise men, living according to man, pursued either the goods of the body or of their own mind or of both; or those who could know God *did not honor him as God or give thanks, but became vain in their thoughts, and their foolish heart was darkened; saying that they were wise*—that is, exalting themselves in their own wisdom, pride dominating them—*they became fools and changed the glory of the incorruptible God into the likeness of an image of corruptible man and of birds and of fourfooted beasts and of serpents*—for to the worship of idols of this kind they were either leaders or followers of the peoples—*and worshiped and served the creature rather than the Creator, who is blessed forever*[^159]. But in the latter there is no wisdom of man but piety, by which the true God is rightly worshiped, awaiting as its reward, in the society of the saints—not only of men but also of angels—*that God may be all in all*[^160].
