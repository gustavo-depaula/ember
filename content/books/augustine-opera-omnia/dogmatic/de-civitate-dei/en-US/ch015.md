# Book XIII: From sin death arose

**1.** Now that the most difficult questions concerning the origin of our age and the beginning of the human race have been dealt with, the order of things demands from us the discussion we have undertaken concerning the fall of the first man—nay, of the first men—and concerning the origin and propagation of human death. For God had not founded men in the way he founded the angels, so that even if they sinned they could in no way die[^1]; but in such a way that, having performed the duty of obedience, angelic immortality and a blessed eternity should follow without the intervention of death; but the disobedient death should chastise by a most just damnation; which we have already said also in the preceding book[^2].

## There is a death of the body and of the soul.

**2.** But concerning the very kind of death, I see that I must discuss it a little more diligently. For although the human soul is truly said to be immortal, yet it too has a certain death of its own. For it is called immortal because in some manner, however slight, it does not cease to live and to perceive; but the body is called mortal because it can be forsaken by all life and does not at all live of itself. The death of the soul, therefore, comes about when God forsakes it, as that of the body when the soul forsakes it. Therefore the death of both, that is, of the whole man, is when the soul, forsaken by God, forsakes the body. For thus neither does it itself live from God, nor the body from it.

But this death of the whole man is followed by that which the authority of the divine words calls the second death[^3]. This the Savior signified, where he says: *fear him who has power to destroy both body and soul in hell*[^4]. Since this does not come about before the soul has been so joined to the body that they are separated by no severance, it can seem strange how the body is said to be slain by that death by which the soul is not forsaken, but, ensouled and sentient, is tormented.

For in that last and everlasting punishment, of which we must discuss more diligently in its proper place[^5], the death of the soul is rightly so called, because it does not live from God; but the death of the body—in what way, since it lives from the soul? For it cannot otherwise feel those bodily torments which are to be after the resurrection. Or is it because life of any kind is some good, but pain is an evil, that the body is therefore not to be said even to live, in which the soul is the cause not of living but of suffering pain?

The soul, therefore, lives from God when it lives well; for it cannot live well unless God works in it what is good; but the body lives from the soul when the soul lives in the body, whether the soul itself lives or does not live from God. For the life in the bodies of the impious is not the life of their souls, but of their bodies; which life their souls, even when dead—that is, forsaken by God—can confer on them, their own life, however slight, by which they are also immortal, not ceasing.

But in the final damnation, although man does not cease to feel, yet, because that feeling itself is neither sweet with pleasure nor wholesome with rest, but penal with pain, it is not undeservedly called death rather than life. And it is called second because it is after that first, by which there comes about the severance of cohering natures, whether of God and the soul or of the soul and the body. Of the first death of the body, then, it can be said that to the good it is good, to the evil evil; but the second, without doubt, just as it belongs to none of the good, so is good to none[^6].

## From sin the offspring too experiences death.

**3.** But there arises a question not to be dissembled, whether in very truth death, by which soul and body are separated, is good to the good; for if it is so, how can it be maintained that it too is a punishment of sin? For the first men, had they not sinned, would surely not have suffered it. How, then, can that be good to the good which could not happen except to the evil[^7]? But again, if it could happen only to the evil, it ought not to be good to the good, but no good at all. For why should there be any punishment in those in whom there was nothing to be punished?

Yet, again, if it could happen only to the evil, it ought not to be good for the good, but ought not to exist for them at all. For why should there be any punishment in those in whom there is nothing to punish?

Wherefore it must be confessed that the first men indeed were so instituted that, if they had not sinned, they would have experienced no kind of death; but that these same first sinners were so punished with death that whatever sprang from their stock was held liable to the same punishment. For nothing else would be born from them than what they themselves had been.

For according to the greatness of that fault, the damnation changed their nature for the worse, so that what penally went before in the first sinning men should follow even naturally in the rest who are born.

For man does not come from man as man comes from dust. For dust was the material for making man, but man is the parent for begetting man. Accordingly, what the earth is, this the flesh is not, although the flesh was made from earth; but what the parent man is, this the offspring man is also. In the first man, therefore, the whole human race was, which was to pass through the woman into the offspring, when that married couple received the divine sentence of their damnation; and what man became—not when he was created, but when he sinned and was punished—this he begot, as far at least as pertains to the origin of sin and death.

For he was not reduced by sin or punishment to that infantile dullness and weakness of mind and body which we see in little ones (which God willed to be, as it were, the beginnings of the young of beasts, whose parents he had cast down into a bestial life and death; for as it is written, *man, when he was in honor, did not understand; he was compared to the beasts that do not understand, and was made like to them*[^8]; except that we see infants even weaker in the use and movement of their members and in the sense of seeking and avoiding than are the tenderest young of other animals; as though the power of man raised itself above the other living beings the more excellently, the more it had drawn back and deferred its own impetus, like an arrow when the bow is drawn back);

the first man, therefore, was not, by an unlawful presumption and a just damnation, slipped or driven down to these infantile beginnings[^9]; but human nature was in him vitiated and changed to this extent, that he suffered in his members a rebellious disobedience of desire and was bound by the necessity of dying, and so begot offspring liable to sin and death, even as he had become by vice and punishment. And if infants are loosed from this bond of sin by the grace of Christ the Mediator, they can suffer only this death, which separates the soul from the body; but, freed from the obligation of sin, they do not pass into that second, endlessly penal death.

**4.** But if anyone is moved as to why they suffer even this death, if it too is a punishment of sin, those whose guilt is abolished by grace: this question has already been treated and resolved in another of our works, which we wrote *On the Baptism of Little Ones*; where it was said that the soul is left to experience the separation from the body for this reason, that, although the bond of crime has already been removed, if immortality of the body immediately followed the sacrament of regeneration, faith itself would be enervated, which is then faith when there is awaited in hope what is not yet seen in reality[^10].

But by the strength and struggle of faith—at least in those of greater age—even the fear of death had to be overcome, which shone forth especially in the holy martyrs; of which struggle there would surely be no victory, no glory (because it could not be a struggle at all), if, after the washing of regeneration[^11], the saints could no longer suffer bodily death. But who, with little ones to be baptized, would not rather run to the grace of Christ for the very reason that he might not be loosed from the body? And thus faith would be proved not by an invisible reward, but would not even be faith any longer, seeking and taking the wage of its own work at once.

But now, by a greater and more marvelous grace of the Savior, the punishment of sin has been turned to the uses of righteousness. For then it was said to man: "you will die, if you sin"[^12]; now it is said to the martyr: "die, lest you sin." Then it was said: "if you transgress the commandment, you will die the death"; now it is said: "if you refuse death, you will transgress the commandment." What then had to be feared, that one might not sin, now has to be undertaken, that one may not sin. Thus, through the ineffable mercy of God, even the punishment of vices passes into the arms of virtue, and the very penalty of the sinner becomes the merit of the righteous.

For then death was acquired by sinning, now righteousness is fulfilled by dying. But this is in the holy martyrs, to whom one of two things is set before them by the persecutor, that they should either forsake the faith or suffer death. For the righteous prefer, by believing, to suffer what the first unrighteous suffered by not believing. For unless those had sinned, they would not have died; but these will sin, unless they die. Those, then, died because they sinned; these do not sin, because they die. It came about through the fault of those that one should come into punishment; it comes about through the punishment of these that one may not come into fault; not because death was made some good thing, which before was an evil; but God bestowed so great a grace upon faith that death, which is agreed to be contrary to life, became an instrument through which one might pass over to life.

**5.** When the Apostle wished to show how greatly sin, without grace coming to aid, availed to do harm, he did not hesitate to say that even the very law, by which sin is forbidden, is the power of sin. *The sting of death*, he says, *is sin, but the power of sin is the law*[^13]. Most truly indeed. For prohibition increases the desire of the unlawful work, when righteousness is not so loved that the craving for sinning is overcome by the delight in it. But that true righteousness be loved and give delight, only the divine grace comes to aid. But lest the law should on that account be thought an evil, because it is called the power of sin: therefore he himself, treating a question of this kind in another place, says: *and so the law indeed is holy, and the commandment holy and just and good. Did, then, that which is good*, he says, *become death to me? Far from it. But sin, that it might appear sin, wrought death in me through that which is good, that the sinner, or sin, might become beyond measure sinful through the commandment*[^14]. *Beyond measure* he said, because transgression too is added, when, with the lust of sinning increased, even the law itself is despised.

Why have we thought this should be mentioned? Because, namely, just as the law is not an evil when it increases the concupiscence of those sinning, so neither is death a good when it increases the glory of those suffering it—since either the former is forsaken for the sake of iniquity and makes transgressors, or the latter is undertaken for the sake of truth and makes martyrs.

And through this the law indeed is good, because it is the prohibition of sin; but death is evil, because it is the wages of sin[^15]; but just as unrighteousness makes ill use not only of evil things, but even of good, so righteousness makes good use not only of good things, but even of evil. Hence it comes about that the evil use the law ill, although the law is a good, and the good die well, although death is an evil.

**6.** Wherefore, as far as pertains to the death of the body, that is, the separation of the soul from the body, when those suffer it who are called dying, it is good to none[^16]. For that very force by which both are torn apart, which had been joined and interwoven in the living being, has, while it lingers, a harsh feeling and one contrary to nature, until all feeling is taken away which was present from that very embrace of soul and flesh. This whole trouble sometimes one blow of the body, or a snatching-away of the soul, cuts short and, by its preceding swiftness, does not allow it to be felt.

Yet whatever it is in the dying which, with a grievous feeling, takes away feeling, by being borne godly and faithfully it increases the merit of patience, but does not take away the name of "punishment." Thus, since death, by the perpetuated propagation from the first man, is without doubt the punishment of one being born, yet if it be paid for godliness and righteousness, it becomes the glory of one being reborn; and although death is the retribution of sin, it sometimes obtains that nothing be retributed to sin.

## Sin is remitted by the washing and by martyrdom.

**7.** For whoever, even without having received the washing of regeneration, die for the confession of Christ, it avails them as much for the remitting of sins as if they were washed in the sacred font of baptism. For he who said, *unless one is born again of water and the Spirit, he will not enter into the kingdom of heaven*[^17], made these men excepted by another sentence, where he says, no less generally: *whoever confesses me before men, I too will confess him before my Father who is in heaven*[^18]; and in another place: *whoever loses his life for my sake will find it*[^19].

Hence it is that it is written: *precious in the sight of the Lord is the death of his saints*[^20]. For what is more precious than the death through which it comes about that both all transgressions are remitted and merits are more abundantly increased? For those are not of so great merit who, since they could not defer death, were baptized and, with all their sins blotted out, departed from this life, as are those who, since they could have deferred death, did not defer it for this reason, that they preferred to end life by confessing Christ rather than, by denying him, to come to his baptism.

And surely, if they had done this, even this would be remitted them in that washing, that they had denied Christ through fear of death—in which washing was remitted even to those that so monstrous a crime, who had killed Christ. But how, without the abundance of the grace of that Spirit who *breathes where he wills*[^21], could they so love Christ that they could not deny him in so great a peril of life, under so great a hope of pardon?

The precious death, therefore, of the saints, to whom the death of Christ was sent ahead and granted beforehand with such great grace that, to gain him, they did not hesitate to spend their own, has shown that what had before been appointed for the punishment of sin has been turned to these uses, that from it a more abundant fruit of righteousness might be born.

Death, therefore, ought not on that account to seem a good, because it has been turned, not by its own force, but by divine aid, to so great a use—that, which then was set before men to be feared, lest sin be committed, is now set before them to be undertaken, that sin be not committed, and, having been committed, be blotted out, and the palm of righteousness due to a great victory be rendered.

**8.** For if we consider more diligently, even when anyone dies for the truth faithfully and laudably, death is guarded against. For some of it is undertaken for this reason, that the whole may not befall, and that the second, besides, which is never ended, may not be added. For the separation of the soul from the body is undertaken, lest, with God separated from the soul, the soul itself too be separated from the body, and thus, the first death of the whole man being completed, the second, everlasting, succeed.

Wherefore death indeed, as I have said[^22], when the dying suffer it, and when it acts in them that they may die, is good to none, but is laudably endured for the holding or attaining of a good; but when those are in it who are now called dead, it is not absurdly said to be both evil to the evil and good to the good. For the souls of the godly, separated from the body, are at rest, but those of the impious pay penalties, until the bodies of the former revive to eternal life, and of the latter to eternal death, which is called the second.

## No one dying does not live.

**9.** But that time in which souls separated from the body are either among good things or among evil—is it rather to be called "after death" or "in death"? For if it is after death, then it is not death itself, which is past and gone by, but the present life of the soul after it which is good or evil. But death was then evil to them when it was, that is, when they suffered it, while they were dying, since its grievous and troublesome feeling was present; which evil the good use well. But death, once completed, in what way is it either good or evil, which now is not?

Moreover, if we attend still more diligently, neither will that appear to be death whose grievous and troublesome feeling in the dying we have spoken of. For as long as they feel, they surely still live, and if they still live, they are to be said to be rather before death than in death; because death, when it comes, takes away all feeling of the body, which is troublesome as it draws near. And through this, how we should call those "dying" who are not yet dead, but, with death impending, are already tossed by the last and deadly affliction, is difficult to explain, even though these are rightly called dying, because, when the death which already impends has arrived, they are called not dying, but dead.

No one, therefore, is dying except one living, since, when he is in so great an extremity of life as those are whom we say are giving up the soul, surely he who has not yet lacked a soul still lives. The same person, therefore, is at once both dying and living—but approaching death, departing from life; yet still in life, because the soul is in the body; not yet in death, however, because it has not yet departed from the body. But if, when it has departed, he will not even then be in death, but rather after death: when shall anyone be in death? For neither will anyone be dying, if dying and living can in no way be at once. For as long as the soul is in the body, we cannot deny that he lives. Or if he is rather to be called dying, in whose body it is now being brought about that he die, and no one can be at once living and dying: I know not when he is living.

**10.** For from the time anyone has begun to be in this body that is to die, there is never a time when something is not being done in it that death may come. For this its changeableness brings about, throughout the whole time of this life (if indeed it is to be called life), that one may come to death. For there is no one who is not, after a year, nearer to it than he was before the year, and tomorrow than today, and today than yesterday, and a little later than now, and now than a little before; since whatever time is lived is taken away from the span of living, and daily there comes to be less and less of what remains, so that the time of this life is altogether nothing else than a course toward death, in which no one is permitted either to stand a little or to go somewhat more slowly; but all are urged on by an equal motion and impelled by no different advance.

For he whose life was shorter did not pass his day more swiftly than he whose was longer; but while equal moments were equally snatched away from both, the one had nearer, the other farther, the goal toward which both were running with no unequal speed. But it is one thing to have completed more of the road, another to have walked more slowly. He, therefore, who up to death passes more prolonged spans of time does not proceed more slowly, but accomplishes more of the journey.

Moreover, if anyone begins to die, that is, to be in death, from the time at which death itself, that is, the taking-away of life, begins to be brought about in him (because, when by taking away it has been finished, he will already be after death, not in death): surely, from the time he begins to be in this body, he is in death[^23].

For what else is being done in single days, hours, and moments, until the death which was being brought about, those being consumed, is completed, and there begins to be already the time "after death," which, while life was being taken away, was "in death"? Never, therefore, is man in life, from the time he is in this body that is dying rather than living, if he cannot be at once both in life and in death.

Or is he rather at once both in life and in death—in life, namely, in which he lives, until it is wholly taken away; but in death, because he is now dying, while life is being taken away? For if he is not in life, what is it that is being taken away, until its complete consummation comes about? But if he is not in death, what is the very taking-away of life?

For not in vain, when life has been wholly taken away from the body, is he now said to be "after death," except because death was, while it was being taken away. For if, that being taken away, the man is not in death but after death: when, except while it is being taken away, will he be in death?

**11. 1.** But if it is absurd that we should say a man, before he comes to death, is already in death (for to what does he draw near by passing through the times of his life, if he is already in it?), especially because it is too unusual that he be said to be at once both living and dying, since waking and sleeping cannot be at once: it must be asked when he will be dying. For before death comes, he is not dying, but living; but when death has come, he will be dead, not dying. The one, therefore, is still before death, the other already after death. When, then, is he in death? For then he is dying, so that, just as there are three things when we say "before death," "in death," "after death," so three single things may be rendered to the single ones: "living," "dying," and "dead."

When, therefore, he is dying, that is, in death—where he is neither living, which is before death, nor dead, which is after death, but dying, that is, in death—is most difficult to define. For as long as the soul is in the body, especially if feeling too is present, without doubt the man lives, who consists of soul and body, and through this is to be said to be still before death, not in death; but when the soul has departed and taken away all feeling of the body, he is now said to be after death and dead. Between the two, then, perishes that wherein he might be dying or in death; since, if he still lives, he is before death; if he has ceased to live, he is now after death. Never, then, is he comprehended as dying, that is, in death. So too, in the passing of times, the present is sought and not found, because it is without any span through which one passes from the future into the past[^24].

Is it not, then, to be seen lest, by this reasoning, the death of the body be said to be no death at all? For if it is, when is it, which can be in no one, and in which no one can be? Since indeed, if one is living, it is not yet, because this is before death, not in death; but if one has now ceased to live, it is no longer, because this too is after death, not in death. But again, if death is no death either before something or after, what is it that is called "before death" or "after death"? For this too is said in vain, if death is nothing. And would that, by living well in paradise, we had so acted that in very truth there were no death. But now it not only is, but is even so troublesome that it can be explained by no speech nor avoided by any reasoning.

**11. 2.** Let us speak, therefore, according to custom (for we ought not otherwise) and say "before death," before death befalls; as it is written, *before death praise no man*[^25]. Let us say also, when it has befallen: After the death of this one or that one, this or that came to pass. Let us speak also of the present time as we can, as when we so speak: "that one, dying, made his will," and: "to these and to those, dying, he left this and that"; although he could in no way do this except as living, and rather did it before death, not in death.

Let us speak also as the divine Scripture speaks, which does not hesitate to say that the dead too are not after death, but in death. For hence is that: *for in death there is no one who is mindful of you*[^26]. For until they revive, they are rightly said to be in death, just as anyone is said to be in sleep until he wakes; although we call those placed in sleep sleeping, yet we cannot in that way call those who are now dead dying. For they are not still dying who, as far as pertains to the death of the body, of which we are now discussing, are already separated from their bodies. But this is what I said cannot be explained by any speech, in what way either the dying are said to live, or those already dead are said still to be in death after death. For how after death, if still in death? Especially since we do not call them dying, as we call those who are in sleep sleeping, and those in sickness sick, and those in pain surely suffering pain, and those in life living; but the dead, before they rise again, are said to be in death, and yet cannot be called dying.

Whence I think it has happened not unfittingly nor incongruously—though not by human industry, perhaps by divine judgment—that this word, which is "he dies," neither could the grammarians in the Latin tongue inflect by that rule by which other such words are inflected. For from that which is "oritur" ("he rises") comes the past-tense verb "ortus est"; and any like words are inflected through the participles of the past tense. But from that which is "moritur" ("he dies"), if we seek the past-tense verb, the customary answer is "mortuus est," with the letter *u* doubled. For "mortuus" is so said as "fatuus," "arduus," "conspicuus," and any like words, which are not of the past tense, but, since they are nouns, are declined without tense. But that, as it were so that what cannot be inflected may be inflected, the noun is put in place of the past participle. Fittingly, therefore, it came about that, just as that which it signifies cannot be done by acting, so the word itself could not be inflected by speaking.

It can, however, be brought about, by the help of the grace of our Redeemer, that we may at least decline the second death. For that is the more grievous and the worst of all evils, which comes about not by the separation of soul and body, but rather by the embrace of both in eternal punishment. There, on the contrary, men will not be before death and after death, but always in death; and through this never living, never dead, but without end dying. For it will never be worse for man in death than where there will be death itself without death.

## The first death is of the body, the second of the soul.

**12.** When, therefore, it is asked with what death God threatened the first men, if they should transgress the commandment they had received and not keep obedience—whether that of the soul, or of the body, or of the whole man, or that which is called the second: the answer must be: all of them. For the first consists of two, the second of all, whole.

For just as the whole earth consists of many lands, and the universal Church of many churches: so the universal death consists of all. Since the first consists of two, one of the soul, the other of the body; so that the first death of the whole man is when the soul, without God and without the body, pays penalties for a time; but the second is where the soul, without God but with the body, pays eternal penalties.

When, therefore, God said to that first man, whom he had placed in paradise, concerning the forbidden food, *on whatever day you eat of it, you will die the death*[^27]: that threat embraced not only the former part of the first death, where the soul is deprived of God, nor only the latter, where the body is deprived of the soul, nor only that whole first death alone, where the soul, separated both from God and from the body, is punished; but whatever there is of death, up to the last, which is called the second, after which there is none later.

**13.** For after the transgression of the precept was committed, immediately, with divine grace forsaking them, they were confounded at the nakedness of their bodies[^28]. Whence they also covered their shameful parts with fig leaves, which were perhaps the first things found by the troubled pair; which same members were before the same, but were not shameful. They felt, then, a new motion of their disobedient flesh, as a reciprocal punishment of their own disobedience.

For already the soul, delighting in its own freedom toward the perverse and disdaining to serve God, was deprived of the former service of the body, and, because it had by its own choice forsaken its higher lord, it did not hold its lower servant to its choice, nor did it have the flesh subject in every way, as it could always have had it, if it had itself remained subject to God. Then, therefore, the flesh began to desire against the spirit[^29], with which conflict we are born, drawing the origin of death, and bearing in our members and our vitiated nature its contention or its victory from the first transgression.

**14.** For God created man upright, being the author of natures, not surely of vices; but, depraved of his own accord and justly damned, he begot offspring depraved and damned. For we were all in that one, when we all were that one, who fell into sin through the woman, who was made from him before the sin. Not yet was there a form created and distributed to us singly, in which we should each live; but already there was the seminal nature from which we were to be propagated; which, namely, being vitiated through sin and bound by the chain of death and justly damned, man would not be born from man of any other condition. And through this, from the evil use of free choice arose the series of this calamity, which leads the human race, depraved in its origin as by a corrupted root, by a connection of miseries up to the destruction of the second death, which has no end—those alone excepted who are freed by the grace of God.

**15.** Wherefore, even if in that which was said, *you will die the death*, since it was not said "the deaths," we understand only that death which comes about when the soul is forsaken by its life, which is God to it (for it was not forsaken in order to forsake, but forsook in order to be forsaken; for toward its own evil, its own will is prior; but toward its own good, the will of its Creator is prior, whether to make it, which was nothing, or to remake it, because, fallen, it had perished)—even if, then, we understand that God announced this death in that which he said, *on the day you eat of it, you will die the death*[^30], as though he were saying, "on the day you forsake me through disobedience, I will forsake you through justice": surely in that death the others too were announced, which without doubt were to follow.

For in that a disobedient motion arose in the flesh of the disobedient soul, on account of which they covered their shameful parts, one death was felt, in which God forsook the soul. This was signified by his words, when he said to the man hiding himself in mad fear: *Adam, where are you?*[^31]—asking surely not in ignorance, but admonishing by rebuke, that he might attend where he was, in which God was not.

But when the soul itself forsook the body, corrupted by age and worn out by old age, there came into experience the other death, concerning which God, still punishing sin, had said to man: *you are earth, and into earth you will go*[^32]; so that from these two there might be completed that first death which is of the whole man, which the second follows at the last, unless man be freed by grace. For the body, which is from the earth, would not return to the earth except by its own death, which befalls it when it is forsaken by its own life, that is, the soul.

Whence it is established among Christians who truly hold the catholic faith that even this death of the body itself was inflicted on us not by a law of nature, by which God made no death for man, but by the desert of sin; since God, avenging sin, said to the man, in whom we all then were: *you are earth, and into earth you will go*.

## What Plato thought concerning the soul . . .

**16. 1.** But the philosophers, against whose calumnies we are defending the city of God, that is, his Church, seem to themselves wisely to mock us, because we say that the separation of the soul from the body is to be reckoned among its punishments; since, namely, they suppose that its perfect blessedness then comes to it, when, stripped altogether of every body, it returns to God simple and alone and, in a manner, naked.

Wherein, if I found in their writings nothing by which this opinion might be refuted, I should have to dispute more laboriously, to demonstrate that it is not the body that is a burden to the soul, but the corruptible body. Whence is that which we mentioned from our Scriptures in the preceding book[^33]: *for the corruptible body weighs down the soul*[^34]. By adding, surely, *corruptible*, he showed that the soul is weighed down not by any kind of body, but by such as was made from the punishment following upon sin. And even if he had not added it, we ought to understand nothing else.

But since Plato most plainly proclaims that the gods made by the most high God have immortal bodies, and brings in God himself, by whom they were made, promising them as a great benefit that they will remain forever with their bodies and be loosed from them by no death: what is it that these men, to harass the Christian faith, pretend not to know what they know, or, even contradicting themselves, prefer to speak against their own selves, so long as they do not cease to contradict us?

For these are the words of Plato, as Cicero translated them into Latin, in which he brings in the most high God addressing the gods whom he made and saying: *you who are sprung from the seed of gods, attend: the works of which I am parent and maker, these are indissoluble against my will, although everything bound together can be loosed; but it is by no means good to wish to dissolve what is bound by reason. But since you have arisen, you indeed cannot be immortal and indissoluble; nevertheless you will by no means be dissolved, nor will any fates of death destroy you, nor will they be stronger than my counsel, which is a greater bond to your perpetuity than those by which you were bound together when you were being begotten*[^35]. Behold, Plato says the gods are both mortal by the binding-together of body and soul, and yet immortal by the will and counsel of the God by whom they were made.

If, then, it is a punishment for the soul to be bound in any kind of body, what is it that God, addressing them as though anxious lest perhaps they die, that is, be dissolved from the body, makes them secure of their immortality—not on account of their nature, which is compounded, not simple, but on account of his own most invincible will, by which he is able to bring it about that things arisen do not perish, nor things joined together are loosed, but persevere incorruptibly?

**16. 2.** And whether Plato says this truly of the stars is another question. For it is not at once to be conceded to him that those globes of lights, or little orbs shining with corporeal light above the earth, whether by day or by night, live by certain proper souls of their own, and these intellectual and blessed—which he insistently affirms also of the universal world itself, as of one greatest living being, by which all the other living beings are contained. But this, as I have said, is another question, which we have not now undertaken to discuss.

This only I thought should be mentioned against those who glory in being called or being Platonists, of which name's pride they are ashamed to be Christians, lest the term common to them with the crowd should make cheap the cloak-wearers, the more puffed up the more scanty their fewness;

and who, seeking what they may reprehend in Christian doctrine, harass the eternity of bodies, as though these were contrary to each other, that we should both seek the blessedness of the soul and wish it always to be in a body, as though bound in a wretched chain; whereas their author and master Plato says that it was granted as a gift from the most high God to the gods made by him that they should never die, that is, be separated from the bodies to which he joined them.

## The philosophers are deceived concerning the soul . . .

**17. 1.** These men contend also that earthly bodies cannot be everlasting, although they do not doubt that the whole earth itself is a member of their god—not indeed the most high, but nevertheless a great one, that is, of this whole world—set in the middle and everlasting. Since, then, that most high God made for them the other one whom they think a god, that is, this world, to be preferred to the other gods who are below him, and since they suppose this same world to be a living being—with a soul, namely, as they assert, rational or intellectual, enclosed in so great a mass of its body—and since he constituted the four elements as members, as it were, of that body, set and arranged in their own places, whose joining together they wish to be indissoluble and everlasting, lest their so great a god ever die: what reason is there that, in the body of the greater living being, the earth, as a middle member, should be eternal, while the bodies of other earthly living beings, if God should so will as he wills that, cannot be eternal?

But, they say, earth must be given back to earth, from which the bodies of land animals were taken; from which it follows, they say, that these bodies must necessarily be dissolved and die, and in that way be restored to the stable and everlasting earth from which they had been taken.

If anyone should affirm the same of fire as well, and say that the bodies which were taken from it to become heavenly animals must be given back to the universal fire, will not the immortality that Plato promised to such gods, as though the supreme God were speaking, perish under the violence of this argument? Or does this not happen there, precisely because God does not will it, whose will, as Plato says, no force overcomes?

What then prevents God from being able to bring this about for earthly bodies as well, since Plato confesses that God can act so that what has arisen does not perish, and what is bound is not loosed, and what is taken from the elements is not given back, and so that souls established in bodies neither ever forsake them nor cease to enjoy with them immortality and everlasting happiness? Why then could he not act so that earthly things too do not die? Is God powerful only as far as the Christians believe, but not as far as the Platonists are willing to allow? No doubt the philosophers were able to know the counsel and power of God, and the prophets were not able to know it; whereas on the contrary God's Spirit taught the prophets to announce his will, as far as he deigned to make it known, while human conjecture deceived the philosophers in coming to know it.

## . . . and speaking of an immortal body.

**17. 2.** But they ought not to have been so far deceived, not only by ignorance but also by obstinacy, as to contradict themselves most plainly with great forcefulness of argument: asserting, on the one hand, that in order for the soul to be happy not only an earthly body but every body must be fled[^36], and saying, on the other, that the gods have most happy souls and yet are bound to eternal bodies—the heavenly gods to fiery ones, but the soul of Jupiter himself, whom they hold to be this very world, enclosed in absolutely all the corporeal elements by which this whole mass rises from earth to heaven.

For Plato supposes that this soul is diffused and extended through musical numbers from the inmost center of the earth, which the geometers call the center, through all its parts up to the highest and outermost reaches of heaven, so that this world is a most great, most happy, everlasting living being, whose soul both holds the perfect felicity of wisdom and does not leave its own body, and whose body both lives forever from that soul and cannot dull or slow it, although it is not simple but compounded of so many and so great bodies.

Since, therefore, they grant these things to their own surmises, why are they unwilling to believe that by divine will and power earthly bodies can be made immortal, in which souls, separated from them by no death and weighed down by none of their burdens, may live everlastingly and happily—the very thing they assert their gods can do in fiery bodies, and Jupiter himself, their king, in all the corporeal elements?

For if, in order for the soul to be happy, every body must be fled, let their gods flee from the orbs of the stars, let Jupiter flee from heaven and earth; or if they cannot, let them be judged wretched. But these men want neither: they dare not grant their gods separation from bodies, lest they seem to worship mortal beings, nor deprivation of happiness, lest they confess them to be unhappy.

Not every body, then, is to be fled in order to attain happiness, but corruptible, burdensome, heavy, dying bodies—not such as the goodness of God made for the first human beings, but such as the punishment of sin compelled them to be.

## The body made immortal by a divine gift.

**18.** But, they say, it is necessary that natural weight either hold earthly bodies on the earth or force them down toward the earth, and therefore they cannot be in heaven. Now those first human beings were indeed on an earth wooded and fruitful, which obtained the name "paradise"; but since this too must be answered, whether on account of Christ's body with which he ascended into heaven, or on account of the bodies of the saints such as they will be in the resurrection, let them look a little more attentively at earthly weights themselves.

For if human skill can bring it about that from metals, which when placed in water sink at once, vessels fashioned in certain ways can even float, how much more credible and effectual is some hidden mode of operation of God—by whose most omnipotent will, as Plato says, what has arisen does not perish and what is bound cannot be dissolved, since incorporeal things are joined to corporeal far more wondrously than any bodies whatever are joined to any other bodies—

that he can grant to earthly masses that they be pressed down by no weight into the depths, and grant to those most perfectly happy souls that, though earthly, they may place their now incorruptible bodies where they will and move them where they will, with the easiest position and motion!

But if angels were to do this, and were to snatch up any land animals you please from wherever they pleased and set them down wherever they pleased, must we believe either that they could not do it or that they would feel the weight? Why, then, should we not believe that the perfected and blessed spirits of the saints can, by a divine gift, carry their bodies without any difficulty wherever they will and set them down wherever they will?

For although, as we are accustomed to feel weights in carrying them, the greater the quantity of earthly bodies the greater also their heaviness, so that many pounds press more than fewer, yet the soul carries the members of its own flesh more lightly when they are robust in health than when they are wasted in sickness. And although to others who carry him a man sound and strong is more burdensome than one thin and sickly, he himself is more agile in moving and carrying his own body when he has more bulk in good health than when he has the least strength in plague or famine. So much, even in earthly bodies—still corruptible and mortal as they are—does not the weight of quantity avail, but the measure of proportion. And who can explain in words how great is the distance between the present condition we call health and the immortality to come?

The philosophers, then, do not refute our faith on the score of the weight of bodies. For I do not wish to ask why they do not believe that an earthly body can be in heaven, when the whole earth is balanced upon nothing. For perhaps, regarding that very midpoint of the world, since all the heavier things come together into it, an argument may be held that is more like the truth.

This I say: if the lesser gods, to whom Plato says the making of the other land animals and of the human being too was entrusted, were able, as he says, to remove from fire the quality of burning while leaving the quality of shining that flashes out through the eyes, shall we then hesitate to grant to the supreme God—to whose will and power he conceded that things which have arisen do not die, and that things so diverse, so unlike, that is, corporeal and incorporeal, bound together cannot be separated by any dissolution—that he can take away corruption from the human flesh to which he grants immortality, leave its nature, retain the fitting arrangement of its shape and members, and withdraw the sluggishness of weight?

But the faith of the resurrection of the dead and of their immortal bodies must be discussed more carefully, if God will, at the end of this work[^37].

## Plato errs in affirming the transmigration of souls.

**19.** Now let us explain what we set out to say about the bodies of the first human beings; for that death which is held to be good for the good—and which is known not only to a few who understand or believe but to all—by which there comes about a separation of the soul from the body, by which, that is, the body of a living creature that was evidently alive evidently dies, could not have befallen them unless the deserving of sin had followed.

For although it is not lawful to doubt that the souls of the just and devout who have died live in rest, yet it would be so much better for them to live with their bodies in good health[^38] that even those who think it most blessed to be in every way without a body refute this opinion of theirs by a contrary judgment. For none of them will dare to set wise human beings—whether about to die or already dead, that is, either lacking bodies or about to leave their bodies—above the immortal gods, to whom the supreme God in Plato promises an immense gift, namely an indissoluble life, that is, an eternal partnership with their own bodies.

Yet this same Plato thinks it goes best with human beings—provided they have passed this life piously and justly—if, separated from their bodies, they are received into the bosom of the gods themselves, who never forsake their own bodies, *that is, forgetful, they may again revisit the vault above and begin to wish to return into bodies*[^39]; which Virgil is praised for having said in accordance with the Platonic teaching

(for thus he thinks that the souls of mortals can neither be forever in their own bodies, but are dissolved by the necessity of death, nor endure perpetually without bodies, but that by alternating turns the living are unceasingly made from the dead and the dead from the living);

so that the wise seem to differ from other human beings only in this, that after death they are carried to the stars, so that each may rest somewhat longer in a star suited to him, and from there, having again forgotten his former misery and overcome by the desire of possessing a body, return to the labors and hardships of mortals; while those who have led a foolish life are rolled back from close at hand into bodies owed to their deserts, whether of human beings or of beasts[^40].

In this most harsh condition, therefore, he places even good and wise souls, to whom such bodies have not been allotted as would let them live forever and immortally, so that they can neither remain in bodies nor endure without them in eternal purity.

Concerning this Platonic teaching we have already said in the earlier books[^41] that Porphyry, out of deference to the Christian age, was ashamed of it, and not only removed the bodies of beasts from human souls but also wished the souls of the wise to be so freed from corporeal bonds that, fleeing every body, they should be held blessed with the Father without end. And so, lest he seem to be conquered by Christ, who promises the saints perpetual life, he too placed purified souls in eternal felicity without any return to their former miseries; and, that he might oppose Christ, denying the resurrection of incorruptible bodies, he asserted that they would live forever not only without earthly bodies but without any bodies at all[^42].

Yet even with this opinion of whatever sort, he did not teach that they should at least not be subjected by religious obedience to embodied gods. Why so, except because he did not believe that those souls, though joined to no body, were better than these gods?

Wherefore, if these men will not dare—as I think they will not dare—to set human souls above the most blessed gods who are nonetheless established in eternal bodies, why does it seem absurd to them, what the Christian faith proclaims: both that the first human beings were so created that, had they not sinned, they would have been loosed from their bodies by no death, but, gifted with immortality for the deserving of obedience kept, would have lived with them forever; and that the saints will have in the resurrection those very bodies in which they labored here, in such a way that neither anything of corruption or difficulty can befall their flesh, nor anything of pain and unhappiness their blessedness?

**20.** Accordingly, the souls of the saints who have now died do not therefore have a grievous death, by which they have been separated from their bodies, because their flesh rests in hope[^43], however many insults it may seem to have received without any sensation at all. For they do not, as it seemed to Plato, long for bodies out of forgetfulness, but rather, because they remember what was promised them by him who deceives no one, who gave them assurance even concerning the integrity of their hairs[^44], they await with longing and patience the resurrection of the bodies in which they endured many hard things, destined to feel nothing further of such a kind in them.

For if they did not hate their own flesh[^45] when, in its weakness resisting their mind, they restrained it by the right of the spirit, how much more do they love it when it too is to be spiritual! For just as a spirit serving the flesh is not inappropriately called carnal, so flesh serving the spirit will rightly be called spiritual—not because it will be turned into spirit, as some suppose from what is written, *it is sown a natural body, it shall rise a spiritual body*[^46], but because it will be subject to the spirit with the highest and most wondrous ease of obedience, even to the fulfilling of the most secure will of indissoluble immortality, with every sense of trouble, all corruptibility and sluggishness taken away.

## [The body in paradise]

For it will not only not be such as it now is in any most excellent state of health, but not even such as it was in the first human beings before sin, who, although they would not have died unless they had sinned, nevertheless used food as human beings do, bearing bodies not yet spiritual but still natural and earthly. And although these bodies would not have grown old with age, so as to be brought of necessity to death (a state secured for them by the marvelous grace of God through the tree of life, which was in the midst of paradise together with the forbidden tree), yet they also took other foods besides the one tree that had been forbidden—not because it was itself an evil, but to commend the good of pure and simple obedience, which is a great virtue in a rational creature established under the Lord its Creator. For where no evil was being touched, surely, if what was forbidden were touched, the sin would be one of disobedience alone.

It came about, then, by the other things they took, that their natural bodies should feel no trouble of hunger and thirst; while from the tree of life it was tasted for this reason, that death should not creep upon them from any source, or that they should not perish, worn out by old age, when the spaces of time had run their course—as though the rest were for nourishment, but that tree for a sacrament; so that the tree of life is to be understood to have been in the corporeal paradise just as, in the spiritual—that is, the intelligible—paradise, the wisdom of God, of which it is written, *she is a tree of life to those who embrace her*[^47].

## Some refer paradise to intelligible things.

**21.** Hence some refer the whole of paradise itself—where the first human beings, the parents of the human race, are related by the truth of holy Scripture to have been—to intelligible things, and turn those trees and fruit-bearing woods into the virtues of life and into morals; as though those things were not visible and corporeal, but were spoken or written in that manner for the sake of signifying intelligible things.

As if a corporeal paradise could not have existed because a spiritual one can also be understood; as though there were not two women, Hagar and Sarah, and from them two sons of Abraham, one of the slave woman, the other of the free, because the Apostle says the two Testaments were figured in them[^48]; or as though no water flowed from any rock when Moses struck it[^49], because Christ can also be understood there by a figurative signification, the same Apostle saying, *and the rock was Christ*[^50].

No one, therefore, forbids us to understand paradise as the life of the blessed, its four rivers as the four virtues—prudence, fortitude, temperance, and justice—and its trees as all the useful disciplines, the fruits of the trees as the morals of the devout, the tree of life as wisdom herself, the mother of all good things, and the tree of the knowledge of good and evil as the experience of the commandment transgressed. For God appointed punishment for sinners rightly, indeed because justly; but a human being does not experience it for his own good.

These things can also be understood of the Church, so that we may take them better as prophetic signs preceding things to come: namely, paradise as the Church herself, as is read of her in the *Song of Songs*[^51]; the four rivers of paradise as the four Gospels, the fruit-bearing trees as the saints, their fruits as their works, the tree of life as the Holy of Holies, that is, Christ, the tree of the knowledge of good and evil as the will's own choice. For a human being, scorning the divine will, can use himself only ruinously, and so learns the difference between cleaving to the good common to all and delighting in his own. For loving himself, he is given over to himself, so that, filled thereby with fears and griefs, he sings in the Psalm, if indeed he feels his own ills, *my soul is troubled within me*[^52]; and, now corrected, he says, *I will keep my strength toward you*[^53].

For when a man despises the divine will, he can use even himself only to his own destruction, and thus learns the difference between cleaving to the good common to all and taking pleasure in his own private good. Loving himself, he is handed over to himself, so that, filled with fears and sorrows as a result, he may sing in the Psalm—if he perceives his own evils—*my soul is troubled within me*[^54]; and, now corrected, may say, *I will keep my strength for you*[^55].

These things, and whatever others may be more fittingly said about understanding paradise spiritually, let them be said, with no one forbidding, provided that the truth of that history is also believed, commended by a most faithful narrative of things that were done.

## What the nature of the spiritual body is.

**22.** The bodies of the just, then, which are to be in the resurrection, will need neither any tree to keep them from dying through any disease or worn-out old age, nor any other corporeal foods by which any trouble of hunger and thirst might be avoided; since they will be clothed with the sure and altogether inviolable gift of immortality, so that they will eat only if they wish, by possibility, not by necessity.

This the angels too did when they appeared visibly and tangibly—not because they had need, but because they were willing and able—so that they might suit themselves to human beings by a certain humanity of their ministry (for we must not believe that the angels ate in a phantasm, when human beings received them with hospitality[^56]), although to those who did not know whether they were angels they seemed to eat from a need like ours. Hence it is that the angel says in the book of Tobit, *you saw me eat, but you saw it with your own sight*[^57]; that is, you supposed that I took food from a need of restoring the body, as you do.

But if perhaps something more credible can be argued about the angels, certainly the Christian faith does not doubt concerning the Savior himself that even after the resurrection, in flesh now indeed spiritual yet true, he took food and drink with the disciples[^58]. For it is not the power, but the need, of eating and drinking that will be taken away from such bodies. Hence they will also be spiritual, not because they will cease to be bodies, but because they will subsist by a life-giving spirit[^59].

**23. 1.** For just as these bodies, which have a living soul, not yet a life-giving spirit, are called natural bodies, and yet are not souls but bodies, so those are called spiritual bodies; yet far be it from us to believe that they will be spirits, but rather that they will have the substance of the flesh, while suffering no sluggishness or carnal corruption, since they will be made alive by a life-giving spirit. Then the man will be no longer earthly, but heavenly: not because the body, which was made from the earth, will not be the same body, but because by a heavenly gift it will now be such that, with its nature not lost but its quality changed, it will be fit even to dwell in heaven.

## [The natural body and the spiritual body]

Now the first man, *of the earth, earthly*, was made into a living soul, not into a life-giving spirit[^60], which was being reserved for him after the deserving of obedience. Therefore his body, which had need of food and drink lest it be afflicted by hunger and thirst, and which was kept from the necessity of death not by that absolute and indissoluble immortality but by the tree of life and held in the flower of youth, was without doubt not spiritual but natural—yet by no means destined to die, unless by transgressing it had fallen into the sentence of God foretelling and threatening, and, with food indeed not denied even outside paradise but barred from the tree of life, had been handed over to be ended by time and age; at least in that life which, in a body though natural—until it should become spiritual by the deserving of obedience—he could have had perpetually in paradise, had he not sinned.

Wherefore, even if we understand this manifest death, by which there comes about a separation of the soul from the body, to have been signified at the same time in what God had said, *on the day you eat of it, you shall die the death*[^61], it ought not therefore to seem absurd that they were not loosed from the body on that very day on which they took the forbidden and death-bearing food. For on that day their nature, changed for the worse and vitiated and separated from the tree of life by a most just judgment, the necessity of even bodily death was brought about in them, the necessity with which we are born. On account of which the Apostle does not say, "the body is indeed about to die because of sin," but says, *the body indeed is dead because of sin, but the spirit is life because of righteousness*. Then he added, *but if the Spirit of him who raised Christ from the dead dwells in you, he who raised Christ from the dead will give life also to your mortal bodies through his Spirit who dwells in you*[^62].

Then, therefore, that body will be in a life-giving spirit which is now in a living soul; and yet the Apostle calls it dead, because it is already bound by the necessity of dying. But then it was so in a living soul, although not in a life-giving spirit, that it could not rightly be called dead, because it could not have the necessity of dying except by the perpetration of sin.

But since God, by saying, *Adam, where are you?*[^63], signified the death of the soul, which came about when he forsook it, and by saying, *you are earth and into earth you shall go*[^64], signified the death of the body, which befalls it when the soul departs, he must be believed to have said nothing about the second death precisely because he willed it to be hidden, on account of the dispensation of the New Testament, where the second death is most plainly declared[^65];

so that this first death, which is common to all, should first be shown to have come from that sin which in one man was made common to all; while the second death is by no means common to all, on account of those *who are called according to his purpose, whom* beforehand *he foreknew and predestined*, as the Apostle says, *to be conformed to the image of his Son, that he himself might be the firstborn among many brethren*[^66], whom God's grace freed from the second death through the Mediator.

**23. 2.** The Apostle, then, speaks thus of the first man's having been made in a natural body. For wishing to distinguish from the spiritual body, which is to be in the resurrection, this which is now natural, he says, *it is sown in corruption, it shall rise in incorruption; it is sown in dishonor, it shall rise in glory; it is sown in weakness, it shall rise in power; it is sown a natural body, it shall rise a spiritual body*. Then, to prove this, he says, *if there is a natural body, there is also a spiritual one*. And to show what a natural body is, he says, *so &lt;also&gt; it is written: the first man was made into a living soul*. In this way, then, he wished to show what a natural body is, although Scripture did not say of the first man, who was called Adam, when the soul was created in him by the breath of God, "and the man was made in a natural body," but, *the man was made into a living soul*. In that, then, which is written, *the first man was made into a living soul*, the Apostle wished the natural body of the man to be understood.

But how the spiritual body was to be understood he showed by adding, *the last Adam into a life-giving spirit*[^67], undoubtedly signifying Christ, who has now so risen from the dead that henceforth he can in no way die[^68]. Then it follows, and he says, *but not first that which is spiritual, but that which is natural, afterward the spiritual*[^69]. Here he declared much more plainly that he had indicated the natural body in that which is written, that the first man was made into a living soul, but the spiritual body in that which he says, *the last Adam into a life-giving spirit*.

For first comes the natural body, such as the first Adam had—though it would not have died, had he not sinned—such as we too now have, his nature changed and vitiated so far as this, that in him, after he sinned, there was brought about that whence he now had the necessity of dying (such a body Christ too deigned first to have for our sake, not indeed by necessity, but by power); but afterward the spiritual body, such as has already gone before in Christ as in our head[^70], and is to follow in his members at the final resurrection of the dead.

**23. 3.** The Apostle then adds the most evident difference of these two men, saying, *the first man was of the earth, earthly; the second man from heaven. As was the earthly, such also are the earthly; as is the heavenly, such also are the heavenly. And as we have borne the image of the earthly, let us also bear the image of him who is from heaven*[^71]. The Apostle so put this that now indeed it comes about in us according to the sacrament of regeneration, as he says elsewhere, *as many of you as were baptized into Christ have put on Christ*[^72]; but in fact it will be perfected then, when in us too what is natural by being born shall have been made spiritual by rising again. For, to use his words likewise, *by hope we were saved*[^73].

Now we have put on the image of the earthly man by the propagation of transgression and death, which generation has brought upon us; but we shall put on the image of the heavenly man by the grace of pardon and of perpetual life, which regeneration grants us, only through the *mediator of God and men, the man Christ Jesus*[^74]; whom he wishes to be understood as the heavenly man, because he came from heaven to be clothed with a body of earthly mortality, which he might clothe with heavenly immortality.

And he calls others heavenly too for this reason, that by grace they become his members, so that with them Christ is one, as head and body[^75]. This he puts more evidently in the same letter thus, *by a man came death, and by a man the resurrection of the dead. For as in Adam all die, so also in Christ shall all be made alive*[^76]—now indeed in the spiritual body which will be in a life-giving spirit; not because all who die in Adam will be members of Christ (for of these far more will be punished with the second death forever), but "all" and "all" were said because, just as no one dies in a natural body except in Adam, so no one is made alive in a spiritual body except in Christ.

Accordingly, it must by no means be thought that we shall have in the resurrection such a body as the first man had before sin; nor is what was said, *as was the earthly, such also are the earthly*, to be understood according to that which was brought about by the commission of sin. For it is not to be supposed that he had a spiritual body before he sinned, and that by the deserving of sin it was changed into a natural one.

For to suppose this is to give too little heed to the words of so great a teacher, who says, *if there is a natural body, there is also a spiritual one; as it is written: the first man* Adam *was made into a living soul*[^77]. Was this brought about after sin, since this is that first condition of man from which the most blessed Paul took this testimony of the law to display the natural body?

## What the first man was at the beginning.

**24. 1.** Hence too it has seemed to some, with too little consideration, that in what is read, *God breathed into his face the breath of life, and the man was made into a living soul*[^78], a soul was not then given to the first man, but that the one already within him was made alive by the Holy Spirit. For they are moved by the fact that the Lord Jesus, after he rose from the dead, breathed and said to his disciples, *receive the Holy Spirit*[^79]. Hence they suppose that some such thing was then done as was done at that time, as if here too the evangelist had gone on to say, "and they were made into a living soul."

And if this had indeed been said, we would understand by it that a certain life of souls is the Spirit of God, without whom rational souls must be reckoned dead, although by their presence bodies seem to live. But that it did not so happen when man was created, the very words of the book sufficiently testify, which run thus, *and God formed man, dust from the earth*. This some, thinking it should be interpreted more plainly, rendered, *and God fashioned man from the mud of the earth*, since it had been said above, *but a spring was rising from the earth and watering the whole face of the earth*[^80]; so that from this the mud seemed to be understood, formed of moisture and earth.

For where this is said, there immediately follows, *and God formed man, dust from the earth*, as the Greek manuscripts have it, from which this Scripture was rendered into the Latin tongue. Now whether one chooses to say "formed" or "fashioned" for what is said in Greek ἔπλασεν makes no difference to the matter; yet "fashioned" is said more properly. But an ambiguity seemed to be avoided by those who preferred to say "formed," because in the Latin tongue custom has rather established that those are said "to fashion" who put something together with a counterfeiting falsehood.

This man, then, formed from the dust or mud of the earth (for it was moist dust)—this man, I say, to speak more expressly, as Scripture spoke, this *dust from the earth* the Apostle teaches to have been made a natural body when it received a soul: *and* this *man was made into a living soul*[^81], that is, this dust, once formed, was made into a living soul.

**24. 2.** He already had a soul, they say; otherwise he would not be called a man, since a man is not the body alone or the soul alone, but one who consists of both soul and body. This indeed is true, that the soul is not the whole man, but the better part of man; nor is the body the whole man, but the lower part of man; but when both are joined together, it has the name "man," which yet the single parts do not lose, even when we speak of them singly. For who is forbidden to say, by a certain law of everyday speech, "that man has died and is now at rest" or "in punishment," when this can be said of the soul alone, and "in such and such a place that man was buried," when this can be understood only of the body alone? Or will they say that divine Scripture is not accustomed to speak thus? On the contrary, it so attests this to us that, even when the two are joined and the man lives, it nonetheless calls the single parts too by the name "man," calling the soul, namely, the "inner man," and the body the "outer man," as though there were two men[^82], when both together are one man.

But it must be understood in what respect man is said to be made to the image of God and man is said to be earth and destined to go into earth. For the former is said according to the rational soul, such as God put into man—that is, into man's body—by breathing or, if it is said more fittingly, by inspiring; the latter according to the body, such a man as God fashioned from the dust, to which a soul was given, that it might become a natural body, that is, a man into a living soul.

**24. 3.** Wherefore, in that which the Lord did when he breathed and said, *receive the Holy Spirit*[^83], he no doubt wished it to be understood that the Holy Spirit is not only the Spirit of the Father but also of the Only-begotten himself. For the same Spirit is the Spirit of both Father and Son, with whom the Trinity is Father and Son and Holy Spirit—not a creature, but the Creator. For that bodily breath proceeding from the mouth of the flesh was not the substance and nature of the Holy Spirit, but rather a signification by which we should understand, as I have said[^84], that the Holy Spirit is common to the Father and the Son, since they do not have one each singly, but he is the one Spirit of both.

## [The Greek words pneuma and pnoe]

Now this Spirit is always called in the holy Scriptures by the Greek word πνεῦμα, as Jesus also called him in this place when, signifying him by the breath of his bodily mouth, he gave him to the disciples; and in all the places of the divine utterances he has never come to my notice as named otherwise. But here, where it is read, *and God fashioned man, dust from the earth, and breathed* or *inspired into his face the breath of life*[^85], the Greek does not say πνεῦμα, which is what the Holy Spirit is usually called, but πνοήν, a name read more frequently of a creature than of the Creator; whence some Latin writers too, for the sake of the difference, preferred to call this word not *spirit* but *breath*. For this is what stands in the Greek at that place in Isaiah too, where God says, *every breath I have made*[^86], without doubt signifying every soul.

What, then, in Greek is called πνοή, our translators have rendered sometimes "breath," sometimes "spirit," sometimes "inspiration" or "aspiration," even when it is said of God;

but πνεῦμα never except "spirit," whether of a man (of whom the Apostle says, *for who among men knows the things of a man, except the spirit of the man which is in him?*[^87]), or of a beast (as is written in the book of Solomon, *who knows whether the spirit of a man ascends upward into heaven and the spirit of a beast descends downward into the earth?*[^88]), or that bodily spirit which is also called wind (for this is its name where it is sung in the Psalm, *fire, hail, snow, ice, the spirit of the storm*[^89]), or that which is now not created but the Creator, as is the one of whom the Lord says in the Gospel, *receive the Holy Spirit*, signifying him by the breath of his bodily mouth, and where he says, *go, baptize all nations in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit*[^90], where the Trinity itself is most excellently and most evidently commended, and where it is read, *God is spirit*[^91], and in very many other places of the sacred writings.

For in all these testimonies of the Scriptures, as far as the Greek is concerned, we see written not πνοήν but πνεῦμα; and as far as the Latin is concerned, not "breath" but "spirit." Wherefore in that which is written, *he inspired*, or if it must be said more properly, *he breathed into his face the breath of life*, even if the Greek had put not πνοήν, as is read there, but πνεῦμα, not even so would it follow that we should be compelled to understand the Creator Spirit, who is properly called the Holy Spirit in the Trinity; since it is manifest that πνεῦμα, as has been said, is wont to be said not only of the Creator but also of a creature.

**24. 4.** But when he had said *spirit*, they say, he would not have added *of life*, unless he wished that Holy Spirit to be understood; and when he had said, *the man was made into a soul*, he would not have added *living*[^92], unless he were signifying the life of the soul, which is imparted to it from God by the gift of the Spirit of God. For since the soul lives, they say, by the proper mode of its own life, what need was there to add *living*, unless that life were to be understood which is given to it through the Holy Spirit? What is this but to contend diligently on behalf of a human surmise and to attend negligently to the holy Scriptures? For what great thing was it not to go further, but to read a little above in the very same book, *let the earth bring forth a living soul*[^93], when all the land animals were created? Then, with some matter intervening, in that same book what great thing was it to notice what is written, *and all things that have the breath of life, and everything that was upon the dry land, died*[^94], when it conveyed that all things which were living on the earth perished in the flood?

If, then, we find both a living soul and a breath of life even in beasts, as divine Scripture is accustomed to speak—and since in this place too, where it is read, *all things that have the breath of life*, the Greek said not πνεῦμα but πνοήν—why do we not say, what need was there to add *living*, since a soul cannot exist unless it lives? Or what need was there to add *of life*, when he had said *breath*? But we understand that Scripture said *living soul* and *breath of life* in its own manner, when it wished animals—that is, animated bodies—to be understood, in which there was, through the soul, this plain sense of the body too.

But in the case of man's creation we forget how Scripture is accustomed to speak, when it has spoken in its own manner precisely to convey that man, having received even a rational soul—which it wished to be understood as created not, like the souls of other flesh, by waters and earth bringing them forth, but by God breathing—was nevertheless so made that he should live in a natural body, which comes to be by a soul living in it, just like those animals of which it said, *let the earth bring forth a living soul*, and which it likewise said had in them the breath of life; where also in the Greek it said not πνεῦμα but πνοήν, expressing by such a name not the Holy Spirit, of course, but their soul.

where even in Greek he did not say πνεῦμα but πνοή, using this term to express their soul, certainly not the Holy Spirit.

**24. 5.** But, they say, the breath of God is understood to have gone out from the mouth of God, and if we believe it to be a soul, it will follow that we confess it to be of the same substance and equal to that Wisdom which says, *I came forth from the mouth of the Most High*[^95]. Yet Wisdom did not say that she had been breathed out by the mouth of God, but that she came forth from his mouth.

But just as we can make a breath, when we blow, not out of our own nature, by which we are men, but out of this surrounding air, which by breathing in and out we draw and return: so almighty God was able to make a breath not out of his own nature, nor out of a subject creature, but even out of nothing, which, by inserting it into the body of man, he was most fittingly said to have inspired or breathed in—being incorporeal, an incorporeal thing; but being unchangeable, a changeable thing; because, not being created, a created thing.

But that these men, who wish to speak of the Scriptures and do not notice the Scriptures' modes of speech, may know that it is not only what is equal and of the same nature that is said to go out from the mouth of God, let them hear or read what is written with God speaking, *because you are lukewarm and neither hot nor cold, I will begin to spew you out of my mouth*[^96].

**24. 6.** There is therefore no reason why we should resist the Apostle when he speaks most plainly, where, distinguishing the natural body from the spiritual—that is, this in which we now are from that in which we shall be—he says, *it is sown a natural body, it shall rise a spiritual body; if there is a natural body, there is also a spiritual one; so also it is written: The first man Adam was made into a living soul, the last Adam into a life-giving spirit. But not first that which is spiritual, but that which is natural, afterward the spiritual. The first man was of the earth, earthly, the second man from heaven. As was the earthly, such also are the earthly; and as is the heavenly, such also are the heavenly. And as we have borne the image of the earthly, let us also bear the image of him who is from heaven*[^97]. Of all these apostolic words we have spoken above[^98].

The natural body, then, in which the Apostle says the first man Adam was made, was so made not that it could in no way die, but that it would not die unless the man sinned.

For that which will be spiritual and immortal by a life-giving spirit will be utterly unable to die, just as the soul was created immortal—which, though it is said to be dead by sin, lacking a certain life of its own, that is, the Spirit of God, by which it could also live wisely and happily, nevertheless does not cease to live by a certain proper, though wretched, life of its own, because it was created immortal;

just as the deserter angels too, although in a certain manner they are dead by sinning, because they forsook the fountain of life, which is God, by drinking from which they could live wisely and happily, nevertheless could not so die as to cease in every way to live and to feel, since they were created immortal; and thus they will be cast headlong into the second death after the last judgment in such a way that even there they will not lack life, since indeed they will not lack feeling either, when they are to be in pains.

But human beings who belong to the grace of God, fellow citizens of the holy angels who abide in the blessed life, will be so clothed with spiritual bodies that they will sin no more and die no more; clothed, however, with that immortality which, like that of the angels, cannot be taken away even by sin; the nature of the flesh indeed remaining, but no carnal corruptibility or sluggishness at all remaining.

## Whether, had Adam not sinned, in the book that follows.

**24. 7.** There follows, however, a question that must necessarily be treated and, with the help of the Lord God of truth, resolved: if the lust of the disobedient members arose from the sin of disobedience in those first human beings when divine grace had forsaken them—whence they opened their eyes upon their own nakedness, that is, noticed it more curiously, and, because a shameless motion was resisting the choice of one resolving against it, covered their shameful parts—how would they have propagated children, if, as they had been created, they had remained without transgression.

But because this book too must be brought to a close, and so great a question must not be cramped into the narrow limits of a discourse, it is deferred, with a more convenient arrangement, to the one that follows.
