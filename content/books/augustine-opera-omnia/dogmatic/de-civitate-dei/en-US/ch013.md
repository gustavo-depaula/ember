# Book XI: What the discussion is in the second part of the work

**1.** We call it the city of God, to which that Scripture bears witness which, not by the fortuitous movements of men's minds, but plainly by the disposition of the highest providence, has, by its surpassing divine authority, subjected to itself, above all the literatures of all nations, every kind of human ability. For there it is written: *glorious things are spoken of you, O city of God*[^1]; and in another Psalm it is read: *great is the Lord and greatly to be praised in the city of our God, in his holy mountain, spreading the rejoicings of the whole earth*[^2]; and a little later in the same Psalm: *as we have heard, so also have we seen, in the city of the Lord of hosts, in the city of our God; God has founded it forever*[^3]; likewise in another: *the rushing of a river makes glad the city of God; the Most High has sanctified his tabernacle; God is in the midst of it, it shall not be moved*[^4]. By these and similar testimonies, all of which it would be too long to recount, we have learned that there is a certain city of God, whose citizens we have longed to be by that love which its founder has inspired in us.

To this founder of the holy city the citizens of the earthly city prefer their own gods, not knowing that he is the God of gods[^5]—not of false gods, that is, of the impious and the proud, who, deprived of his unchangeable light common to all, and on that account reduced to a kind of needy power, pursue in a manner their own private powers and seek divine honors from their deceived subjects—but the God of the godly and holy gods, who delight rather to subject themselves to the One than to subject many to themselves, and rather to worship God than to be worshiped in God's place.

But to the enemies of this holy city we have replied, as far as we could, in the ten preceding books, with the help of our Lord and King. Now, however, recognizing what is already expected of me, and not unmindful of my debt, I shall set about to discuss, as far as I am able—relying everywhere on the aid of that same Lord and King of ours—the origin, the course, and the appointed ends of the two cities, the earthly, that is, and the heavenly, which we have said are for the time being in this world in a manner intertwined and mutually mingled; and I shall first say how the beginnings of these two cities went before in the diversity of the angels.

**2.** It is a great thing, and exceedingly rare, by an intent effort of the mind to pass beyond the whole of creation, both corporeal and incorporeal, once it has been considered and found to be changeable, and to arrive at the unchangeable substance of God, and there to learn from God himself that he made the whole of nature, which is not what he himself is, by none other than himself.

For God speaks with man not through some corporeal creature, sounding in bodily ears, so that the spaces of air are struck between the one speaking and the one hearing, nor through that kind of spiritual creature which is shaped by likenesses of bodies, as in dreams or in some other such way—for even thus he speaks as though to the ears of the body, because he speaks as though through a body and as though with an interval of bodily places set between, since such visions are very like to bodies—but he speaks by the truth itself, if anyone is fit to hear with the mind and not the body. For he speaks to that in man which is better than the other things of which man consists, and than which God alone is better.

## [Faith, the mediator, and the authority of Scripture]

For since man is most rightly understood, or, if this cannot be, at least believed, to have been made in the image of God[^6], he is surely nearer to the God who is above him in that part of himself by which he surpasses his own lower parts, which he has in common even with the beasts.

But because the mind itself, in which reason and intelligence are naturally present, is enfeebled by certain dark and inveterate vices, not only as regards cleaving to and enjoying the unchangeable light, but even as regards enduring it, until it is made fit for so great a happiness, being renewed and healed from day to day, it had first to be steeped in faith and purified. And that it might walk more confidently toward the truth, the truth itself, God the Son of God, by assuming manhood without consuming his Godhead, established and founded this same faith, that there might be for man a way to the God of man through the man who is God. For this is *the mediator of God and men, the man Christ Jesus*[^7].

For in that respect by which he is mediator, in that he is man, and in that also he is the way[^8]. Since, if between the one who is journeying and that toward which he journeys there is a way in between, there is hope of arriving; but if it is lacking, or if it is unknown by what road one must go, what good is it to know whither one must go? Now the only way most strongly fortified against all errors is that the very same one be both God and man: as God, the goal to which one goes; as man, the way by which one goes.

**3.** He, having spoken first through the Prophets, then through himself, afterward through the Apostles, as much as he judged to be sufficient, also established the Scripture which is called canonical, of the most eminent authority, to which we give faith concerning those matters which it is not expedient to be ignorant of, and which we are not fit to know by ourselves. For if those things can be known with ourselves as witnesses which are not remote from our senses, whether inner or even outer (whence they are also called "present" things, because we say they are thus "before the senses," as the things which are "at hand" are "before the eyes"):

then surely, as for those things which are remote from our senses, since we cannot know them by our own testimony, concerning these we seek out other witnesses and believe them—witnesses from whose senses we do not believe these things to be or to have been remote. Just as, therefore, concerning visible things which we have not seen we believe those who have seen them, and so concerning the other things which pertain to each one's own bodily sense:

so, concerning those things which are perceived by soul and mind (since the mind too is most rightly called a sense, whence the word "sentence" also took its name)—that is, concerning the invisible things which are remote from our inner sense—we ought to believe those who have learned these things as ordered in that incorporeal light, or who behold them abiding there.

## God creating, the world began with time . . .

**4. 1.** Of all visible things the greatest is the world; of all invisible things the greatest is God. But that the world is, we behold; that God is, we believe. That God, however, made the world, we believe none more safely than God himself. Where have we heard him? Nowhere, for now, better than in the holy Scriptures, where his prophet said: *in the beginning God made heaven and earth*[^9]. Was this prophet, then, present when God made heaven and earth[^10]? No; but there was the wisdom of God, through whom all things were made, which also transfers itself into holy souls, makes them friends of God and prophets[^11], and recounts his works to them inwardly without noise.

To them also speak the angels of God, who *always see the face of the Father*[^12], and announce his will to those to whom they ought. One of these was that prophet who said and wrote: *in the beginning God made heaven and earth*. He is so fit a witness, through whom God is to be believed, that by the same Spirit of God by which he came to know these things revealed to him, he also foretold so long beforehand our own faith that was to be.

**4. 2.** But why did it please the eternal God to make heaven and earth then, which he had not made before? Those who say this, if they wish the world to be seen as eternal without any beginning, and therefore not made by God, are too far turned away from the truth and rave with the deadly disease of impiety. For, apart from the prophetic utterances, the world itself, by its most orderly changeableness and movement and by the most beautiful appearance of all visible things, in a manner silently proclaims both that it was made, and that it could not have been made except by a God ineffably and invisibly great and ineffably and invisibly beautiful.

But those who confess that it was indeed made by God, yet do not wish it to have a beginning of time, but only of its creation, so that in some scarcely intelligible way it has always been made, do indeed say something whereby they seem to themselves to defend God, as it were, against fortuitous rashness, lest it be believed that the thought of making the world came suddenly into his mind, which had never come before, and that a new will befell him, though he is in no respect at all changeable;

but I do not see how this reasoning of theirs can hold up in other matters, and especially in the case of the soul; for if they contend that it is coeternal with God, they will in no way be able to explain whence there befell it the new misery which had never befallen it before through all eternity.

For if they say that its misery and blessedness have always alternated, they must of necessity say that they will also always alternate; whence that absurdity will follow them, that even when it is called blessed, in this very respect it is surely not blessed, if it foresees its future misery and disgrace; but if it does not foresee that it will be disgraceful and wretched, and always supposes itself blessed, then it is blessed by a false opinion—than which nothing more foolish can be said.

But if they suppose that the soul's misery has indeed always alternated with blessedness through infinite ages past, but that now, henceforth, when it has been set free, it will not return to misery, they are nonetheless convicted that it was never truly blessed, but begins henceforth to be so by a certain new and unfailing blessedness; and through this they will confess that something new befalls it, and this a great and notable thing, which had never befallen it back through eternity.

And if they deny that God had the cause of this newness in his eternal counsel, they will at the same time deny that he is the author of its blessedness, which is a thing of unspeakable impiety; but if they say that he too devised by a new counsel that the soul should henceforth be eternally blessed, how will they show him to be a stranger to that changeableness which displeases even them? Further, if they confess that the soul was created in time, but is thereafter to perish at no time—that, like a number, it has a beginning but no end—and that therefore, once it has experienced miseries, if it is set free from them, it will never afterward be wretched, they will surely not doubt that this comes about while the unchangeableness of God's counsel remains. Let them, then, likewise believe that the world too could be made in time, and yet that God, in making it, did not on that account change his eternal counsel and will.

But if they acknowledge that the soul was created in time yet will never perish in any subsequent time, and that, like number, it has a beginning but no end, so that after experiencing misery once, if freed from it, it will never again be miserable, they will certainly not doubt that this can occur while God’s purpose remains unchangeable. Let them therefore believe that the world too could have been made in time without God’s thereby changing his eternal purpose and will in making it.

**5.** Next we must see what those people answer concerning the place of the world, who agree that God is the founder of the world, and yet ask what we answer concerning the time of the world. For it is asked why it was made then rather than before, in the same way as it can be asked why it was made here, where it is, rather than elsewhere.

For if they imagine infinite spaces of time before the world, in which it does not seem to them that God could have rested from working, let them similarly imagine infinite spaces of places outside the world, in which, if anyone should say that the Almighty could not be idle, will it not follow that they are forced to dream, with Epicurus, of innumerable worlds—with this only difference, that he asserts these to be generated and dissolved by the fortuitous movements of atoms, whereas these men will say that they were made by the work of God,

if they are unwilling that he be idle throughout the boundless immensity of places lying open on every side outside the world, and that these same worlds cannot, by any cause, be dissolved—which is what they hold even concerning this one?

For we are dealing with those who hold, together with us, that God is incorporeal and the creator of all natures which are not what he himself is; and it is too unworthy to admit others to this discussion of religion, especially because, among those who think that the homage of sacred rites is to be paid to many gods, these men have surpassed the other philosophers in nobility and authority, for no other reason than that, though by a long interval indeed, they are nevertheless nearer than the rest to the truth.

Or will they perhaps say that the substance of God, which they neither enclose nor bound nor extend in place, but confess—as it is fitting to think concerning God—to be wholly everywhere by an incorporeal presence, is absent from such great spaces of places outside the world, and is occupied with only one place, and that, in comparison with that infinity, so tiny a place, in which the world is? I do not think they will go forward into these empty babblings.

Since, therefore, they say that there is one world, of a huge corporeal mass indeed, yet finite and bounded by its own place, and made by the working of God: let them answer themselves, concerning the infinite times before the world, why God rested from working in them, with the same answer they give concerning the infinite places outside the world, why God rests from working in them.

And just as it does not follow that God set up the world by chance rather than by divine reason in this place in which it is, and no other—since, while infinite places lie open everywhere alike, this one could be chosen by no more excellent merit, although no human reasoning can comprehend that same divine reason by which it was done—so it does not follow that we should suppose anything fortuitous to have befallen God, in that he founded the world at that time rather than at an earlier one, since the earlier times had passed away equally through an infinite stretch backward, and there would have been no difference whereby one time should be preferred to another in the choosing. But if they say that men's thoughts are empty when they imagine infinite places, since there is no place beyond the world, they are answered that in the same way men think emptily of the past times of God's idleness, since there is no time before the world.

**6.** For if eternity and time are rightly distinguished, in that time is not without some movable changeableness, while in eternity there is no change: who does not see that there would have been no times unless a creature were made which would change something by some motion—a motion in whose changing, as one thing and another, which cannot exist together, give way and follow in succession, in shorter or longer intervals of duration, time would result? Since, then, God, in whose eternity there is no change at all, is the creator and orderer of times, I do not see how he can be said to have created the world after spaces of time, unless it be said that some creature already existed before the world, by whose movements times might run on.

Further, if the sacred and most truthful Letters say that *in the beginning God made heaven and earth*[^13] in such a way that he is understood to have made nothing before—since this would rather be said to have been made in the beginning, if he had made anything before all the rest that he made—then without doubt the world was not made in time, but with time.

For what is made in time is made both after some time and before some time: after that which is past, before that which is future; but nothing could be past, since there was no creature by whose changeable movements it might be carried on. With time, then, was the world made, if in its founding a changeable motion was made, as even that order of the first six or seven days seems to be the case, in which both morning and evening are named, until all the things which God made in these days are completed on the sixth day, and on the seventh day God's resting is commended in a great mystery. Of what sort these days are, it is either exceedingly difficult or even impossible for us to conceive, much more to say.

## . . . and the world in time.

**7.** For we see that these familiar days have no evening except from the setting of the sun, nor morning except from the rising of the sun; but the first three of those days were completed without the sun, which is reported to have been made on the fourth day. And in the very first place, indeed, light was made by the word of God, and God is narrated to have separated between it and the darkness, and to have called that light day, but the darkness night[^14]; but of what sort that light is, and by what alternating motion, and what kind of evening and morning it made, is removed from our senses; nor can it be understood by us as it is—which nevertheless is to be believed without any hesitation.

For either it is some corporeal light, whether in the upper parts of the world far from our sight or in that from which the sun was afterward kindled; or by the name of light was signified the holy city, in the holy angels and blessed spirits, of which the Apostle says: *the Jerusalem which is above, our eternal mother in the heavens*[^15]; for he says also in another place: *for you are all sons of light and sons of the day; we are not of the night nor of the darkness*[^16]—if, that is, we are able in some fitting way to understand both the evening and the morning of this day.

For the knowledge of the creature, in comparison with the knowledge of the Creator, in a manner grows toward evening, and again grows light and becomes morning, when it too is referred to the praise and love of the Creator; nor does it turn to night, where the Creator is not forsaken by love of the creature. Finally, when Scripture numbered those days in order, it nowhere inserted the word "night." For it does not say anywhere, "Night was made," but: *evening was made, and morning was made, one day*[^17]. So the second day, and the rest.

For the knowledge of a creature in itself is, so to speak, more faded than when it is known in the wisdom of God, as in the art by which it was made. Therefore it can more fittingly be called evening than night; which evening, nevertheless, as I have said, when it is referred to the praising and loving of the Creator, runs back to morning.

And when it does this in the knowledge of itself, it is one day; when in the knowledge of the firmament, which between the lower and the upper waters was called heaven, the second day; when in the knowledge of the earth and the sea and all things that bring forth growth, which are joined by their roots to the earth, the third day; when in the knowledge of the greater and lesser luminaries and all the stars, the fourth day; when in the knowledge of all the animals that swim and fly from the waters, the fifth day; when in the knowledge of all the earthly animals and of man himself, the sixth day.

**8.** But when, on the seventh day, God rests from all his works and sanctifies it, this is by no means to be taken childishly, as though God had labored in working—he who *spoke, and they were made*[^18], by an intelligible and everlasting Word, not a sounding and temporal one. Rather, the rest of God signifies the rest of those who rest in God, just as the gladness of a house signifies the gladness of those who are glad in the house, even if it is not the house itself, but some other thing, that makes them glad.

How much more, if that same house should by its beauty make its dwellers glad, so that it is called glad not only in that manner of speaking by which we signify, through that which contains, that which is contained—as the theaters applaud, the meadows low, when in the former men applaud, in the latter oxen low—but also in that by which there is signified, through what produces, that which is produced; as a letter is called glad, signifying the gladness of those whom it makes glad by reading it.

Most fittingly, therefore, when prophetic authority narrates that God rested, there is signified the rest of those who rest in him, and whom he himself makes to rest; the prophecy also promising this to the men to whom it speaks, and for whose sake it was certainly written, that they too, after the good works which God works in them and through them—if they have first in this life approached him in a manner through faith—shall have in him everlasting rest. For this was also figured, by the resting of the Sabbath enjoined by the precept of the law, in God's ancient people, whereon I think it should be discussed more carefully in its proper place.

## The angels too are created in time.

**9.** Now, since I have undertaken to speak of the origin of the holy city, and have thought that what pertains to the holy angels should be said first—who are both a great part of this city and the more blessed in that they have never gone into exile—I shall take care to set forth, as far as shall seem sufficient, with God's bounty, what divine testimonies are at hand on this point.

Where the sacred Letters speak of the constitution of the world, it is not plainly said whether the angels were created, or in what order; but if they have not been passed over, they are signified either by the name of *heaven*, where it is said: *in the beginning God made heaven and earth*[^19], or rather of this "light" of which I am speaking. That they have not been passed over I conclude from this, that it is written that God rested on the seventh day from all his works which he had made[^20], since the book itself thus begins: *in the beginning God made heaven and earth*; so that before heaven and earth he seems to have made nothing else.

Since, then, he began from heaven and earth, and the earth itself, which he first made, was, as Scripture next states, invisible and without form, and light not yet having been made, there were of course darkness over the deep[^21]—that is, over a certain undistinguished confusion of earth and water (for where there is no light, there must be darkness)—and then all things were set in order by creation, which are narrated to have been completed through six days: how could the angels be passed over, as though they were not among the works of God from which he rested on the seventh day?

That the angels are a work of God, though here it is not passed over, is nevertheless not plainly expressed; but elsewhere holy Scripture testifies to this in the clearest words. For in the hymn of the three men in the furnace, when it had been said beforehand: *bless the Lord, all you works of the Lord*[^22], in the carrying out of those same works the angels too were named; and in the Psalm it is sung: *praise the Lord from the heavens, praise him in the heights; praise him, all his angels, praise him, all his hosts; praise him, sun and moon, praise him, all you stars and light; praise him, you heavens of heavens, and let the waters that are above the heavens praise the name of the Lord; for he spoke, and they were made; he commanded, and they were created*[^23]. Here too it is divinely said most plainly that the angels were made by God, since, when they have been mentioned among the other heavenly things, it is added with reference to all: *he spoke, and they were made*.

Who, moreover, will dare to suppose that the angels were made after all those things which were enumerated in the six days? But even if anyone is so foolish, that Scripture of equal authority refutes this vanity, where God says: *when the stars were made, all my angels praised me with a loud voice*[^24]. The angels, then, already existed when the stars were made. But the stars were made on the fourth day. Shall we then say that they were made on the third day? Far from it. For it is clear what was made on that day. The earth, surely, was separated from the waters, and these two elements took on the distinct forms of their own kind, and the earth brought forth whatever clings to it by its roots. On the second day, then? Not even this. For then the firmament was made between the upper and lower waters and was called heaven; in which firmament, on the fourth day, the stars were made.

Surely, then, if the angels pertain to the works of God of those days, they are that light which received the name of day, whose oneness, that it might be commended, was not called the first day, but one day. Nor is the second day or third or the rest another day; but that same one day was repeated to fill out the number six or seven, on account of a sevenfold knowledge: the sixfold, namely, of the works which God made, and the seventh of God's rest. For when God said, *let there be light, and there was light*[^25], if in this light the creation of the angels is rightly understood, then surely they were made partakers of the eternal light, which is the unchangeable wisdom of God itself, through whom all things were made, whom we call the only-begotten Son of God;

so that, illumined by that light by which they were created, they might become light and be called *day* by participation in the unchangeable light and day, which is the Word of God, through whom both they themselves and all things were made. For the *true light, which enlightens every man coming into this world*[^26], this enlightens also every clean angel, that he may be light, not in himself, but in God; from whom, if an angel turns away, he becomes unclean, as are all those who are called unclean spirits, and are no longer light in the Lord, but darkness in themselves, deprived of participation in the eternal light. For evil is no nature; rather, the loss of good has received the name of evil.

## God is one and triune . . .

**10. 1.** There is, therefore, one good that alone is simple and on that account alone unchangeable, which is God. By this good all good things were created, but they are not simple and on that account are changeable. Created, I say, that is, made, not begotten. For what is begotten of the simple good is equally simple and is what that is of which it is begotten; the two of which we call Father and Son; and both of these together with their Spirit are one God; which Spirit of the Father and of the Son is called *Holy* by a certain proper notion of this name in the sacred Letters.

He is other, however, than the Father and the Son, because he is neither the Father nor the Son; but I said "other," not "another thing," because he too is equally a simple and equally an unchangeable and coeternal good. And this Trinity is one God; nor is it on that account not simple because it is a Trinity. For we do not call this nature of the good simple because the Father alone, or the Son alone, or the Holy Spirit alone is in it, or because it is merely this Trinity of name without subsistence of persons, as the Sabellian heretics supposed; but it is called simple because it is what it has, except for what each person is said to be relatively to another. For the Father certainly has a Son, and yet is not himself the Son, and the Son has a Father, and yet is not himself the Father. In that, therefore, in which he is said to be relatively to himself, not to another, he is what he has; as he is said to be living relatively to himself, by having life, of course, and he himself is that same life.

**10. 2.** For this reason, therefore, a nature is called simple to which it does not belong to have anything that it can also lose, or in which the one having is one thing and what it has another; as a vessel some liquid, or a body color, or air light or heat, or the soul wisdom. For none of these is what it has; for neither is the vessel liquid, nor the body color, nor the air light or heat, nor is the soul wisdom. Hence it is that they can also be deprived of the things they have, and be turned and changed into other states or qualities; so that the vessel is emptied of the liquid with which it is full, and the body loses its color, and the air grows dark or cold, and the soul grows foolish.

But even if a body be incorruptible, such as is promised to the saints in the resurrection, it has indeed the unlosable quality of that incorruption, yet, while the corporeal substance remains, it is not the same thing as that incorruption itself. For that incorruption is whole through each of the parts of the body, and is not greater in one place and less in another; for no part is more incorruptible than another; but the body itself is greater in the whole than in a part; and although one part of it is larger, another smaller, the part that is larger is not more incorruptible than the one that is smaller. The body, therefore, which is not wholly itself everywhere, is one thing; the incorruption, which is wholly itself everywhere, is another; for every part of an incorruptible body, even though unequal to the rest, is equally incorrupt.

For, by way of example, the hand is not more incorruptible than the finger merely because the finger is smaller than the whole hand. Thus, though hand and finger are unequal, the incorruptibility of hand and finger is nevertheless equal. And through this, although incorruptibility is inseparable from an incorruptible body, yet the substance by which it is called a body is one thing, the quality by which it is called incorruptible another. And therefore even thus it is not what it has.

The soul too, even if it should always be wise, as it will be when it is set free for eternity, will nevertheless be wise by participation in the unchangeable wisdom, which is not what the soul is. For even if the air, when light is poured into it, is never forsaken by it, the air is still one thing, and the light by which it is illumined another. Nor would I say this as though the air were the soul, which some supposed who could not conceive an incorporeal nature. But these things have to those, even in great unlikeness, a certain similitude, so that it may not unfittingly be said that the incorporeal soul is illumined by the incorporeal light of the simple wisdom of God, just as the body of air is illumined by corporeal light; and just as the air grows dark when forsaken by that light (for what are called the shadows of any corporeal places are nothing but air lacking light), so the soul grows dark when deprived of the light of wisdom.

**10. 3.** According to this, therefore, those things are called simple which are principally and truly divine, because in them quality is not one thing and substance another, nor are they divine or wise or blessed by participation in other things. But in the holy Scriptures the Spirit of wisdom is called manifold[^27], because it has many things in itself; but what it has, this it also is, and it is, as one, all those things. For there are not many wisdoms, but one, in which are certain infinite—and yet, to it, finite—treasures of intelligible things, in which are all the invisible and unchangeable reasons of things, even of visible and changeable ones, which were made through it. For God did not make anything in ignorance, which cannot rightly be said even of any human craftsman; but if he made all things knowing them, he surely made those things which he knew. From which there comes to the mind something marvelous, yet nonetheless true: that this world could not be known to us unless it existed; but to God, unless it were known, it could not exist.

## The angels were created good.

**11.** Since this is so, in no way indeed were those spirits whom we call angels first darkness for any space of time; but as soon as they were made, they were made light. Yet they were not so created merely that they should somehow exist and somehow live, but were also illumined, that they might live wisely and blessedly. Certain angels, turned away from this illumination, did not obtain the excellence of a wise and blessed life, which without doubt is eternal and certain and secure of its own eternity; but they have a rational, though unwise, life in such a way that they cannot lose it, not even if they would.

But to what extent, before they sinned, they were partakers of that wisdom, who can determine? And how shall we say that, in their participation in it, they were equal to those who are for that reason truly and fully blessed, namely that they are by no means deceived concerning the eternity of their blessedness? Since, indeed, if they had been equal in it, these too would have remained equally blessed in its eternity, because equally certain. For just as a life cannot truly be called eternal merely because it lasts, however long, if it is to have an end—since it is called life only by living, but eternal by having no end—

wherefore, although not whatever is eternal is forthwith blessed (for the penal fire too is called eternal), yet if the truly and perfectly blessed life is none but the eternal, theirs was not such, being at some point to cease and therefore not eternal, whether they knew this or, not knowing it, supposed otherwise; because, if they knew, fear, and if they did not know, error, surely did not allow them to be blessed.

But if they were ignorant of this in such a way that they did not trust in false or uncertain things, but were borne by no firm assent to either side as to whether their good would be everlasting or would at some point have an end, then that very hesitation about so great a happiness did not possess that fullness of the blessed life which we believe to be in the holy angels. For we do not so contract the term "blessed life" to certain narrows of meaning that we call God alone blessed; who nevertheless is truly so blessed that there can be no greater blessedness—in comparison with which, the fact that the angels are blessed with a certain highest blessedness of their own, as great as can be in angels, is what, or how great a thing?

**12.** Nor do we think that they alone, as far as the rational or intellectual creature is concerned, are to be called blessed. For who would dare to deny that those first men in paradise were blessed before sin, although uncertain how long their blessedness would last, or whether it would be eternal (yet it would have been eternal, had they not sinned), when even today we not shamelessly call those blessed whom we see leading this life justly and piously, with hope of future immortality, without a crime laying waste their conscience, easily obtaining the divine mercy for the sins of this infirmity.

For although they are certain of the reward of their perseverance, they are nevertheless found uncertain about their perseverance itself. For what man knows that he will persevere to the end in the practice and advancement of righteousness, unless by some revelation he is made certain by the one who, by a just and hidden judgment, does not instruct all on this matter, but deceives no one? As far, therefore, as the delight of a present good is concerned, the first man was more blessed in paradise than any righteous man in this mortal infirmity; but as far as the hope of the future is concerned, any righteous man, in whatever bodily torments, is more blessed—one to whom it is clear, not by opinion but by certain truth, that he will have without end, free from all trouble, the fellowship of the angels in participation in the most high God—than that man was, uncertain of his own fall, in that great happiness of paradise.

**13.** Wherefore it now occurs to anyone without difficulty that, when both are joined together, there is brought about that blessedness which the intellectual nature desires by a right purpose, that is, that it both fully enjoy the unchangeable good, which is God, without any trouble, and have no hesitation through any doubt, nor be deceived by any error, that it will abide in him forever.

That the angels of light have this, we believe by godly faith; that the sinning angels, who by their own perversity were deprived of that light, did not have it even before they fell, we gather by consequent reasoning; yet that they had some blessedness, even if not a foreknowing one, is certainly to be believed, if they led a life before sin.

Or, if it seems hard, when the angels were made, to believe that some were made in such a way that they did not receive foreknowledge either of their perseverance or of their fall, while others were made in such a way that they knew the eternity of their blessedness by a most certain truth—but that all were created from the beginning of equal happiness, and so remained until those who are now evil fell by their own will from that light of goodness: without doubt it is much harder to think that the holy angels are now uncertain of their eternal blessedness, and that they themselves are ignorant about themselves of what we have been able to know about them through the holy Scriptures.

For what catholic Christian is ignorant that no new devil from among the good angels will ever come to be hereafter, just as this one will never return into the fellowship of the good angels? For Truth in the Gospel promises to the holy and faithful that they will be equal to the angels of God[^28]; to whom it is also promised that they will go into eternal life[^29].

Now if we are certain that we shall never fall from that immortal happiness, but they are not certain, then we shall already be superior, not equal, to them. But because Truth by no means deceives, and we shall be equal to them, surely they too are certain of their eternal happiness. And because those others were not certain of it (for theirs was not an eternal happiness of which they could be certain, since it was to have an end), it remains that either they were unequal, or, if they were equal, that after the ruin of the former, certain knowledge of their everlasting happiness came to the latter.

Unless perhaps someone says that what the Lord says of the devil in the Gospel, *he was a murderer from the beginning and did not stand in the truth*[^30], is to be taken in such a way that he was not only a murderer from the beginning—that is, from the beginning of the human race, from which time, of course, man was made, whom he could kill by deceiving—but also that from the beginning of his own condition he did not stand in the truth, and therefore was never blessed with the holy angels,

refusing to be subject to his Creator and rejoicing through pride in his own, as it were private, power, and through this being false and deceitful—because neither does anyone escape the power of the Almighty, and he who through godly subjection was unwilling to hold to what truly is, affects through proud elation to feign what is not—so that there is understood also what the blessed apostle John says: *from the beginning the devil sins*[^31], that is, from the time when he was created he refused righteousness, which only a will godly and subject to God could have.

Whoever assents to this opinion does not think with those heretics, that is, the Manichees, and whatever other plagues think likewise, that the devil has a certain proper nature of evil, as it were from some opposing principle; who are so foolish in such great vanity that, though they have these Gospel words in authority along with us, they do not notice that the Lord did not say, "he was a stranger to the truth," but: *he did not stand in the truth*,

where he meant a fall from the truth to be understood—in which, surely, had he stood, he would have remained, made a partaker of it, blessed with the holy angels.

**14.** But he added an indication, as though we had asked whence it might be shown that he did not stand in the truth, and said: *because the truth is not in him*. But it would be in him, if he had stood in it. The utterance, however, was made in a less usual manner. For it thus seems to sound: *he did not stand in the truth, because the truth is not in him*[^32], as though the cause of his not standing in the truth were that the truth is not in him; whereas rather the cause of the truth not being in him is that he did not stand in the truth.

This manner of speaking is also in the Psalm: *I have cried out, because you have heard me, O God*[^33]; when it would seem that it ought to have been said: "You have heard me, O God, because I have cried out." But when he had said, *I have cried out*, as though it were asked of him whence he might show that he had cried out, he showed, from the effect of God's hearing, the disposition of his crying out; as though he were saying: "Hence I show that I cried out, because you have heard me."

**15.** As for that, too, which John says of the devil, *from the beginning the devil sins*[^34], they do not understand that, if such is his nature, there is in no way any sin. But what is to be answered to the prophetic testimonies—whether what Isaiah says, marking the devil under the figured person of the prince of Babylon: *how did Lucifer fall, who rose in the morning*[^35]; or what Ezekiel says: *you were in the delights of the paradise of God, you were adorned with every precious stone*?

Where it is understood that he was at one time without sin. For a little later it is said to him more expressly: *you walked in your days without fault*[^36]. And if these things cannot more fittingly be understood otherwise, we must also take that which was said, *he did not stand in the truth*[^37], in this way: that he was in the truth, but did not remain; and that *from the beginning the devil sins* is not to be supposed to mean that he sins from the beginning, from the time he was created, but from the beginning of sin, in that sin began to be from his own pride.

Nor is that which is written in the book of Job, when the discourse was of the devil—*this is the beginning of the molding of the Lord, which he made for his angels to mock*[^38] (with which the Psalm too seems to accord, where it is read: *this dragon, which you formed to mock him*[^39])—to be so understood that we should suppose him to have been from the beginning created such, to be mocked by the angels, but rather ordained to this punishment after sin.

His beginning, therefore, is a molding of the Lord; for there is no nature, even in the lowest and meanest little beasts, which he did not constitute, from whom is every measure, every form, every order, without which nothing of things can be found or even conceived; how much more the angelic creature, which precedes in dignity of nature all the rest that God founded!

**16.** For among those things which in some way exist and are not what God is, by whom they were made, the living are set before the non-living, as those which have the power of begetting, or even of desiring, before those which lack that motion; and among those which live, the sentient are set before the non-sentient, as animals before trees; and among those which perceive, the intelligent are set before the non-intelligent, as men before beasts; and among those which understand, the immortal are set before the mortal, as angels before men.

But these are set before by the order of nature; there is, however, another and another measure of valuation according to each one's use, by which it comes about that we set certain things lacking sense before certain sentient things—so much so that, if we had the power, we would wish to remove them altogether from the nature of things, whether ignorant of what place they hold in it, or, even if we know, ranking them below our own conveniences.

For who would not rather have bread in his house than mice, money than fleas? But what wonder, when even in the valuation of men themselves, whose nature is certainly of so great a dignity, very often a horse is bought at a higher price than a slave, a gem than a serving-maid?

Thus, by the liberty of judging, the reason of one who considers differs greatly from the necessity of one in need or the pleasure of one who craves; since reason considers what a thing is worth in itself in the grades of things, while necessity considers what it seeks for the sake of what, and reason considers what truth appears to the light of the mind, while pleasure considers what agreeable thing flatters the senses of the body.

But so much weight has, in rational natures, a certain, as it were, weight of will and love, that, although by the order of nature angels are above men, yet by the law of justice good men are preferred to evil angels.

**17.** On account of nature, therefore, not on account of the malice of the devil, we rightly understand it to have been said: *this is the beginning of the molding of the Lord*[^40]. For without doubt, where there is the vice of malice, a nature not vitiated went before. But vice is so contrary to nature that it can do nothing but harm to nature. To withdraw from God, therefore, would not be a vice, unless it belonged rather to the nature whose vice it is to be with God.

Wherefore even an evil will is a great testimony to a good nature. But just as God is the best creator of good natures, so he is the most just orderer of evil wills; so that, while those make ill use of good natures, he makes good use even of evil wills. And so he brought it about that the devil—good by his institution, evil by his own will—being ordained in the lower regions, is mocked by his angels; that is, that his temptations may profit the saints, whom he desires them to harm.

And since God, when he founded him, was certainly not ignorant of his future malignity, and foresaw what goods he himself would make out of his evil, therefore the Psalm says: *this dragon, which you formed to mock him*[^41], so that, in the very fact that he formed him—good indeed through his own goodness—he may be understood already, through his foreknowledge, to have prepared how he would use him even though evil.

**18.** For God would not create any—I do not say of the angels, but even of men—whom he foreknew would be evil, unless he equally knew to what uses of good he would turn them, and thus adorn the order of the ages, like a most beautiful poem, even with certain, as it were, antitheses. For what are called antitheses are most becoming among the ornaments of speech; for them to be called in Latin *opposita*, or, what is more expressly said, *contraposita*, is not customary among us, although Latin speech too uses these same ornaments of expression—indeed, the languages of all nations do. By these antitheses the apostle Paul also gracefully sets forth that passage in the second epistle to the Corinthians, where he says: *by the weapons of righteousness on the right hand and the left; through glory and dishonor, through ill repute and good repute; as deceivers, and yet truthful; as unknown, and yet well known; as dying, and behold, we live; as chastened, and not put to death; as sorrowful, yet always rejoicing; as poor, yet making many rich; as having nothing, and possessing all things*[^42]. As, therefore, these contraries set against contraries render the beauty of speech, so, by a certain eloquence not of words but of things, the beauty of the age is composed by the opposition of contraries. This is set forth most plainly in the book of Ecclesiasticus in this manner: *good is set against evil, and life against death; so the sinner against the godly. And so look upon all the works of the Most High, two by two, one against one*[^43].

## Between the good and the evil angels, light and darkness.

**19.** Although, therefore, the obscurity of the divine discourse is useful even for this, that it brings forth many opinions of truth and brings them out into the light of knowledge, while one understands it in this way, another in that;

yet in such a way that what is understood in an obscure place is asserted either by the attestation of manifest things or by other places in no way doubtful; whether, when many things are treated, one arrives also at that which he who wrote it meant, or whether that lies hidden, but, on the occasion of treating the deep obscurity, certain other true things are said:

it does not seem to me an opinion absurd as regards the works of God, if, when that first light was made, the angels are understood to have been created, that there was a separation made between the holy angels and the unclean, where it was said: *and God divided between the light and the darkness; and God called the light day, and the darkness he called night*[^44]. For he alone could distinguish these who could also, before they fell, foreknow that they would fall, and that, deprived of the light of truth, they would remain in shadowy pride.

For between this day, so well known to us, and night—that is, between this light and these shadows—he commanded the luminaries of heaven, most familiar to our senses, to divide: *let there be*, he says, *luminaries in the firmament of heaven, to give light upon the earth and to divide between day and night*; and a little later: *and God made*, he says, *two great luminaries, the greater luminary for the beginning of the day, and the lesser luminary for the beginning of the night, and the stars; and God set them in the firmament of heaven to give light upon the earth and to rule over day and night and to divide between light and darkness*[^45]. But between that light, which is the holy society of the angels shining intelligibly by the illumination of truth, and the darkness contrary to it—that is, the most foul minds of the evil angels turned away from the light of justice—he alone could divide, to whom the evil to come, an evil not of nature but of will, could not be hidden or uncertain.

**20.** Finally, neither is that to be passed over in silence, that, where God said, *let there be light, and there was light*, there was immediately added: *and God saw the light, that it is good*[^46]—not after he had separated between the light and the darkness and called the light day and the darkness night, lest he should seem to have given the testimony of his approval, together with the light, to such darkness as well.

For where the darkness is blameless, between which and this light visible to our eyes the luminaries of heaven divide, it is added not before but after: *and God saw that it is good. He set them*, it says, *in the firmament of heaven to give light upon the earth and to rule over day and night and to separate between light and darkness. And God saw that it is good*[^47]. Both pleased him, because both are without sin.

But where God said, *let there be light, and there was light. And God saw the light, that it is good*; and afterward it is added, *and God separated between the light and the darkness; and God called the light day, and the darkness he called night*[^48], it was not added in this place, *and God saw that it is good*, lest both should be called good, since one of them was evil—by its own vice, not by nature. And therefore only the light there pleased the Founder; but the angelic darkness, although it had to be ordained, did not have to be approved.

## God's work is not other than his art.

**21.** For what else is to be understood in that which is said throughout, *God saw that it is good*[^49], except the approval of a work made according to the art, which is the Wisdom of God? But God so far from then, when it was made, learning that it was good, that none of those things would have been made if it had been unknown to him.

While, therefore, he "sees" that it is good—which, unless he had seen it before it was made, would surely not have been made—he teaches that it is good; he does not learn. And Plato indeed dared to say more: namely, that God was lifted up with joy when the totality of the world was completed. Wherein he too was not so foolish as to suppose that God was made more blessed by the newness of his work; but he wished thus to show that what had pleased to be made in the art had now, when made, pleased its maker;

not that God's knowledge is in any way varied, so that things which do not yet exist make one thing in it, things which already exist another, things which have been another; for he does not, after our manner, either look ahead to what is future, or look at what is present, or look back at what is past, but in a certain other way, far and deeply diverse from the custom of our thoughts.

For he sees not by passing in thought from this to that, but altogether unchangeably; so that those things which come to pass in time are indeed not yet future, and already present, and already not past, while he comprehends all these things by a stable and everlasting present; nor in one way with the eyes, in another with the mind, for he does not consist of soul and body; nor in one way now and in another before and in another after; since his knowledge too is not, like ours, changed by the variety of the three times—the present, namely, and the past or future—he *with whom there is no change nor shadow of a moment*[^50].

For his attention does not pass from thought to thought, in whose incorporeal gaze all things that he knows are present at once; since he knows times by no temporal notions of his own, just as he moves temporal things by no temporal movements of his own.

There, then, he saw that what he made was good, where he saw that it was good that he should make it; nor, because he saw it made, did he double his knowledge or increase it in any part, as though he had been of lesser knowledge before he made what he saw—he who would not work so perfectly except by a knowledge so perfect that nothing would be added to it from his works.

Wherefore, if it were only to be conveyed to us who made the light, it would be enough to say, God made the light; but if not only who made it, but also through what he made it, it would be enough to be stated thus: *and God said: let there be light, and there was light*; that we might know that God made the light not only, but also through the Word.

But because three things especially needed to be made known to us concerning the creature—who made it, through what he made it, why he made it—*God said*, it says, *let there be light, and there was light. And God saw the light, that it is good*[^51].

If, then, we ask who made it: "It is God"; if through what he made it: "He said, let it be, and it was made"; if why he made it: "Because it is good."

Neither is there an author more excellent than God, nor an art more effective than the Word of God, nor a better cause than that good should be created by the good God. This Plato too calls the most just cause of founding the world, that good works should be made by the good God; whether he read these things, or perhaps came to know them from those who had read them; or whether by a most keen genius he beheld the invisible things of God, understood through the things that are made[^52], or whether he himself learned them from those who had beheld them.

**22.** Yet this cause—that is, the goodness of God for the creating of good things—this cause, I say, so just and so fitting, which, diligently considered and piously pondered, ends all controversies of those who seek the origin of the world, certain heretics did not see, because the needy and frail mortality of this flesh, already coming from a just punishment, gives offense in very many ways while things are at variance with it, such as fire, or cold, or a wild beast, or anything of this kind;

nor do they observe how vigorous these things are in their own places and natures, and how they are arranged in a beautiful order, and how much they contribute, each according to its portions of comeliness, to the totality of things as to a common commonwealth, or how much advantage they bestow even on us ourselves, if we use them fittingly and knowingly—so that even poisons, which through unfitting use are pernicious, when fittingly applied are turned into wholesome medicines; while, on the contrary, even these things in which they delight, such as food and drink and this very light, are felt to be harmful by immoderate and inopportune use.

Whence divine providence admonishes us not foolishly to find fault with things, but diligently to inquire into the usefulness of things, and, where our genius or weakness fails, to believe the usefulness to be hidden, as were certain things which we could scarcely find out; because that very concealment of usefulness is either an exercise of humility or a wearing-down of pride; since there is no nature at all that is "evil," and this name is nothing but the privation of good.

But from earthly things up to heavenly, and from visible things up to invisible, some goods are better than others, unequal for this purpose, that all might exist; God, moreover, is so great an artificer in great things that he is not lesser in small ones—which small things are to be measured not by their own greatness (for they have none), but by the wisdom of the artificer;

as in the appearance of a visible man, if one eyebrow be shaved, how nearly nothing is taken from the body, and how much from the beauty, since this consists not in mass but in the evenness and proportion of the members!

Nor, indeed, is it much to be wondered at that those who think there is some evil nature, arisen and propagated from a certain principle of its own opposed to God, are unwilling to accept this cause of the creation of things, that the good God founded good things,

believing rather that he was led, by an extreme necessity of repelling the evil rebelling against him, to these worldly endeavors, and that, by curbing and overcoming the evil, he mingled his good nature with it—a nature most foully polluted and most cruelly captured and oppressed, which he scarcely cleanses and sets free by great labor; not the whole of it, however, but that part of it which could not be purified from that defilement is to be the covering and bond of the conquered and imprisoned enemy.

The Manichees, however, would not thus be foolish, or rather mad, if they believed the nature of God to be, as it is, unchangeable and altogether incorruptible, which nothing could harm; but the soul, which could be changed for the worse by will and corrupted by sin, and thus deprived of the light of unchangeable truth, they would perceive, with Christian soundness, to be no part of God nor of his nature, which is God's, but founded by him, far unequal to its Founder.

**23. 1.** But it is much more to be wondered at, that even certain men who believe with us that there is one principle of all things, and that no nature which is not what God is can exist except from that Founder, were nevertheless unwilling well and simply to believe this cause—so good and simple—of the fashioning of the world: that the good God founded good things, and that there should be, after God, things which were not what God is, yet good, which none but the good God would make;

but they say that souls—not parts of God, indeed, but made by God—sinned by withdrawing from their Founder, and, by various stages according to the diversity of their sins, deserved various bodies, as it were chains, from the heavens down to the earth; and that this is the world, and that this was the cause of making the world, not that good things should be founded, but that evils should be restrained.

Hence Origen is rightly blamed. For in the books which he calls περὶ ἀρχῶν, that is, *On First Principles*, this he thought, this he wrote.

And there I wonder, more than can be said, that a man so learned and practiced in the Ecclesiastical writings did not observe, first, how this was contrary to the intention of this Scripture of such great authority, which, appending to each of the works of God, *and God saw that it is good*[^53], and, when all were completed, adding, *and God saw all the things that he had made, and behold, they were very good*[^54], wished no other cause of making the world to be understood than that good things should be made by the good God.

Wherein, if no one had sinned, the world would be adorned and filled only with good natures; and because there is sin, not on that account are all things filled with sins, since a far greater number of good things in the heavenly realms keeps the order of its nature; nor did an evil will, because it was unwilling to keep the order of nature, on that account escape the laws of the just God who orders all things well; since, just as a picture with a black color placed in its proper place, so the totality of things, if one can behold it, is beautiful even with sinners, although, considered in themselves, their own deformity disfigures them.

**23. 2.** Next, Origen and whoever thinks these things ought to have seen that, if this opinion were true, that the world was made for this reason, that souls should receive bodies, according to the deserts of their sins, as prison-houses in which they might be penally enclosed—the higher and lighter for those who sinned less, the lower and heavier for those who sinned more—then the demons, than whom nothing is worse, ought to have had earthly bodies, than which nothing is lower and heavier, rather than men, even good men.

But as it is, that we might understand that the deserts of souls are not to be weighed by the qualities of bodies, a most wicked demon has an aerial body, while man—even now, though evil, of a far lesser and milder malice, and certainly before sin—received nevertheless a body of clay[^55].

And what more foolish thing can be said than that the artificer God, in providing that there should be one sun in one world, consulted not for the comeliness of beauty or even for the welfare of corporeal things, but that this rather came about because one soul had so sinned that it deserved to be enclosed in such a body? And through this, if it had happened that not one, but two—nay, not two, but ten or a hundred—had similarly and equally sinned, would this world have a hundred suns? That this might not be, it was not provided, by the marvelous foresight of the maker, for the welfare and comeliness of corporeal things, but rather it came about by so great an advance in sinning of one soul, that it alone deserved such a body.

Plainly it is not the advance of souls—about which they do not know what they are saying—but the advance of those very men who think such things, that is far from the truth and deservedly to be restrained.

These three things, then, which I commended above, when they are sought in each creature—who made it, through what he made it, why he made it—so that the answer may be: God, through the Word, because it is good; whether by a mystical depth the Trinity itself is conveyed to us, that is, the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit, or whether something occurs which forbids that to be taken in this place of the Scriptures, is a question of much discourse, and we must not press to explain everything in one volume.

## The city of God compared to the Trinity.

**24.** We believe and hold and faithfully preach that the Father begot the Word, that is, the Wisdom through whom all things were made, the only-begotten Son—one begetting one, the eternal begetting the coeternal, the supremely good begetting the equally good; and that the Holy Spirit is at once the Spirit both of the Father and of the Son, and is himself consubstantial and coeternal with both; and that this whole is both a Trinity on account of the property of the persons, and one God on account of the inseparable divinity, just as one Almighty on account of the inseparable omnipotence;

yet in such a way that, even when the question is asked about each singly, each of them is answered to be both God and almighty; but when about all together, not three gods or three almighties, but one almighty God—so great there is in the three an inseparable unity, which willed itself to be thus preached.

But whether the Holy Spirit of the good Father and the good Son, because he is common to both, can rightly be called the goodness of both, I do not dare to hurl forth a rash opinion; nevertheless I would more easily dare to call him the holiness of both—not the holiness of both as a quality, but himself also a substance and the third person in the Trinity. To this I am more probably led by the fact that, although the Father is spirit and the Son is spirit, and the Father is holy and the Son is holy, yet he himself is properly called the Holy Spirit, as the substantial and consubstantial holiness of both.

But if divine goodness is nothing other than holiness, then surely it is also a diligence of reason, not an audacity of presumption, that, in the works of God, by a certain secret manner of speaking by which our attention is exercised, the same Trinity may be understood to be conveyed to us—who made each creature, through what he made it, on account of what he made it. For the Father is understood, the Father of the Word, who said "let it be made"; but what was made when he said this was without doubt made through the Word; and in that which is said, *God saw that it is good*, it is sufficiently signified that God made what was made by no necessity, by no need of any advantage of his own, but by goodness alone, that is, because it is good;

which is said after it was made, so that it may be indicated that the thing which was made agrees with the goodness for the sake of which it was made. And if this goodness is rightly understood to be the Holy Spirit, the whole Trinity is conveyed to us in its works.

Thence is the origin, the forming, and the blessedness of the holy city, which is above in the holy angels. For if it be asked whence it is: God founded it; if whence it is wise: it is illumined by God; if whence it is happy: it enjoys God; subsisting, it is given measure; contemplating, it is illumined; cleaving, it is gladdened; it is, it sees, it loves; in the eternity of God it flourishes, in the truth of God it shines, in the goodness of God it rejoices.

**25.** As far as it is given to understand, hence the philosophers wished the discipline of wisdom to be threefold—or rather were able to observe that it is threefold (for they did not appoint it to be so, but rather found it to be so)—one part of which should be called physics, another logic, the third ethics (whose Latin names are now frequented in the writings of many, so that they were called natural, rational, and moral; which we have also briefly touched on in the eighth book);

not that it follows that they thought anything, in these three, concerning the Trinity according to God, although Plato is said to have first discovered and commended this division—to whom neither the author of all natures seemed to be any but God, nor the giver of intelligence, nor the inspirer of the love by which one lives well and blessedly.

But certainly, although concerning the nature of things, and concerning the method of investigating truth, and concerning the end of good, to which we ought to refer all that we do, different men think different things, yet in these three great and general questions all their attention is engaged. So, although in each of them there is a manifold disagreement of opinions as to what each man pursues, nevertheless no one hesitates that there is some cause of nature, some form of knowledge, some highest good of life.

There are also three things which are regarded in every man who is a craftsman, that he may make something: nature, instruction, use; nature is to be judged by ability, instruction by knowledge, use by fruit.

Nor am I ignorant that, properly, fruit belongs to one enjoying, use to one using, and that this difference seems to lie between them, that we are said to enjoy that thing which delights us in itself, not to be referred to anything else, but to use that thing which we seek for the sake of something else

(whence temporal things are rather to be used than enjoyed, that we may deserve to enjoy eternal things; not like the perverse, who wish to enjoy money but to use God, since they do not spend money for the sake of God, but worship God for the sake of money); nevertheless, in that manner of speaking which custom has more largely prevailed in, we both use fruits and enjoy uses; for fruits, indeed, are now properly said to be of the fields, which we all certainly use temporally.

In this customary sense, then, I would speak of "use" in these three things which I have noted as to be regarded in man, which are nature, instruction, use. From these, for the sake of obtaining the blessed life, the discipline was found by the philosophers to be threefold, as I have said—natural on account of nature, rational on account of instruction, moral on account of use.

If, then, our nature were from ourselves, we would surely also have begotten our own wisdom, and would not care to perceive it by instruction, that is, by learning from elsewhere; and our love, proceeding from ourselves and referred back to ourselves, would suffice for living blessedly, nor would it need any other good to enjoy; but as it is, because our nature, that it might exist, has God as its author, without doubt, that we may be truly wise, we ought to have him as our teacher, and him also, that we may be blessed, as the bestower of inmost sweetness.

**26.** And we ourselves indeed recognize in us, although not an equal—nay, a very far distant—and not coeternal, and, to say the whole more briefly, not of the same substance of which God is, image of God, that is, of that highest Trinity, by which nevertheless no nature among the things made by him is nearer to God; an image yet to be perfected by reformation, that it may be also nearest in likeness. For we both are, and know that we are, and love that being and knowing.

And in these three things which I have named, no falsehood resembling the truth disturbs us. For we do not touch these, as we touch those things which are outside, by any sense of the body,

as, for instance, we perceive colors by seeing, sounds by hearing, odors by smelling, savors by tasting, hard and soft things by touching; of which sensible things we also turn over in thought the images, most like to them and no longer corporeal, hold them in memory, and through them are stirred to desires of these things; but that I am, and that I know this and love it, is most certain to me, without any deluding imagining of fancies or phantasms.

In these truths I have no fear of the arguments of the Academics who say: What if you are deceived? For if I am deceived, I am. For he who does not exist surely cannot be deceived either; and through this I am, if I am deceived. Since, therefore, I am if I am deceived, how am I deceived in believing that I am, when it is certain that I am, if I am deceived? Since, then, I who would be deceived would be, even if I were deceived, without doubt I am not deceived in this, that I know that I am.

And it follows that I am not deceived in this either, that I know that I know. For just as I know that I am, so I also know this very thing, that I know.

And when I love these two things, I add this same love as a certain third thing, of no lesser estimation than the things which I know. For I am not deceived that I love, since in those things which I love I am not deceived; although, even if those things were false, it would be true that I love false things. For how could I rightly be reproved and rightly forbidden from the love of false things, if it were false that I love them? But since those things are true and certain, who doubts that, when they are loved, the love of them is itself also true and certain? Moreover, just as there is no one who does not wish to be, so there is no one who does not wish to be blessed. For how can one be blessed, if he be nothing?

**27. 1.** So pleasant, indeed, by a certain natural force, is existence itself, that for no other reason even those who are wretched are unwilling to perish; and, when they feel themselves to be wretched, they wish that not they themselves, but rather their wretchedness, should be taken away from the order of things.

Even to those who both appear to themselves most wretched and plainly are so, and are judged wretched not only by the wise, since they are foolish, but also by those who think themselves blessed, because they are poor and beggars,

if anyone were to give immortality, by which their very wretchedness too would not die, on this proposal set before them: that if they were unwilling to be always in the same wretchedness, they would be no one and nowhere, but would in every way perish—they would surely exult with joy and thus would always choose to be rather than not to be at all. A witness to this is their most familiar feeling. For why do they fear to die, and prefer to live in that distress rather than to end it by death, except because it is sufficiently apparent how nature shrinks from non-being? And therefore, when they know that they are about to die, they desire that this mercy be expended on them as a great benefit, that they may live somewhat more prolongedly in the same wretchedness and die more slowly. Without doubt, then, they show with what gratitude they would receive immortality, at least such as would have no end of beggary.

What of this? Do not all animals, even the irrational, to whom it is not given to ponder these things, from immense dragons down to tiny little worms, show by all the movements they can that they wish to be and therefore flee destruction? What of this? Do not all shrubs and bushes, which have no sense for avoiding ruin by a manifest motion, while they send up the safe sprout of their top into the air, fix another root in the earth, by which they draw nourishment and thus in a manner preserve their own being? Finally, do not those very bodies, which have not only no sense but not even any seminal life, nonetheless either leap up to the heights or descend to the depths or are balanced in the middle, so as to guard their own essence in the place where they can be according to nature?

**27. 2.** And now how much knowing is loved, and how much human nature is unwilling to be deceived, can be understood even from this, that anyone would rather lament in a sound mind than rejoice in madness. This great and marvelous power belongs to no living being among mortals except man, although some of them have a far keener sense of the eyes than we for beholding this light; but they cannot touch that incorporeal light by which our mind is in a manner irradiated, that we may be able to judge rightly of all these things. For in so far as we grasp it, in so far are we able to do so.

Nevertheless there is in the senses of irrational living beings, even if knowledge in no way, yet certainly a certain likeness of knowledge; but the rest of corporeal things were called sensible not because they perceive, but because they are perceived. Of these, in the case of shrubs, this is like the senses, that they are nourished and bring forth. Yet both these and all corporeal things have causes hidden in nature; but they offer their forms, by which the structure of this visible world is comely, to be perceived by the senses, so that, in place of the knowing which they cannot have, they seem, as it were, to wish to become known.

But we grasp these things by the sense of the body in such a way that we judge of them not by the sense of the body. For we have another sense of the inner man, far more excellent than that, by which we perceive just and unjust things—the just by an intelligible form, the unjust by the privation of it. To the office of this sense there comes neither the keenness of the pupil, nor the opening of the little ear, nor the breathing-passages of the nostrils, nor the taste of the gullet, nor any bodily touch. There I am certain both that I am and that I know this, and these things I love, and that I love them I am likewise certain.

**28.** But concerning those two, namely being and knowing, how much they are loved in us, and in what way there is found also in the other things which are below us, even if a differing, yet a certain likeness of them, we have said enough, as far as the plan of this undertaken work seemed to require; but concerning the love by which they are loved, whether this love too is loved, it has not been said. But it is loved; and from this we prove it, that in men who are more rightly loved, the love itself is more loved. For a man is not deservedly called good who knows what is good, but who loves it. Why, then, do we not also feel, in our very selves, that we love that very love by which we love whatever good we love? For there is also a love by which is loved even what ought not to be loved, and this love he hates in himself who loves that by which is loved what ought to be loved. For both can be in one man, and it is good for a man that, with the increase of that by which we live well, this should decrease by which we live ill, until the whole of what we live is healed to perfection and changed into good.

For if we were cattle, we would love the carnal life and what is according to its sense, and that would be our sufficient good, and according to this, when it was well with us, we would seek nothing else. Likewise, if we were trees, we could love nothing by any perceiving motion, yet we would seem, as it were, to desire that by which we would be more fruitful and more abundantly productive. If we were stones or waves or wind or flame or anything of this kind, without any sense or life, we would nevertheless not lack a certain, as it were, desire of our own places and order. For the movements of weights are, as it were, the loves of bodies, whether they strive downward by gravity or upward by lightness. For a body is borne by its weight, just as the mind by its love, wherever it is borne.

Since, therefore, we are men created in the image of our Creator, whose is true eternity, eternal truth, eternal and true love, and who is himself an eternal and true and dear Trinity, neither confused nor separated: in these things indeed which are below us—since they too would neither exist in any way, nor be contained in any form, nor either desire or hold any order, unless they had been made by him who supremely is, who is supremely wise, who is supremely good—let us, as it were running through all the things which he has made with marvelous stability, gather, as it were, certain footprints of his, here more, there less, impressed; but, beholding his image in ourselves, like that younger son in the Gospel[^56], let us return to ourselves and rise up and go back to him from whom by sinning we had withdrawn.

There our being will have no death, there our knowing will have no error, there our loving will have no offense. But now, although we hold these three things of ours as certain, and do not believe them on the testimony of others, but ourselves perceive them as present and discern them by an inner and most truthful gaze, yet, as to how long they will be, or whether they will never be lacking, and whither they will come if they are done ill, but whither if they are done well—since we cannot know this through ourselves, we either seek or have other witnesses on this point; concerning whose trustworthiness, why there ought to be no doubt, this is not the place, but a later one will be, to discuss more diligently.

But in this book, concerning the city of God, which does not go into exile in the mortality of this life, but is immortal, always in the heavens—that is, concerning the holy angels cleaving to God, who neither ever were nor ever will be deserters, between whom and those who, deserting the eternal light, were made darkness, we have already said that God first divided—let us, with his help, set forth as we can what we have begun.

## The angels see in the Word of God and in themselves.

**29.** Those holy angels indeed learn God not through sounding words, but through the very presence of unchangeable truth, that is, his only-begotten Word; and that this same Word, and the Father, and their Holy Spirit—and that this is an inseparable Trinity, and that the single persons in it are each a substance, and yet that all are not three gods, but one God—they so know that these things are better known to them than we ourselves are known to ourselves.

The creature itself, too, they know better there, that is, in the wisdom of God, as in the art by which it was made, than in the creature itself; and through this they know themselves too better there than in themselves, yet also in themselves. For they were made, and are other than he who made them. There, then, as in a daytime knowledge, but in themselves as in an evening one, as we have already said above.

For it makes a great difference whether something is known in that reason according to which it was made, or in itself; just as the straightness of lines or the truth of figures is known in one way when it is beheld as understood[^57], in another when it is drawn in the dust; and justice in one way in the unchangeable truth, in another in the soul of a just man.

And so, in like manner, the rest: as the firmament between the upper and lower waters, which was called heaven; as, below, the gathering of the waters and the laying bare of the earth and the establishment of grasses and trees; as the founding of the sun and moon and stars; as the animals from the waters, namely birds and fishes and swimming beasts; as those walking and creeping on the earth, and man himself, who would excel all things on the earth.

All these things are known by the angels in the Word of God in one way, where they have their causes and reasons—that is, those according to which they were made—abiding unchangeably, and in another way in themselves; the former by a clearer, the latter by an obscurer knowledge, like that of the art and of the works;

which works, nevertheless, when they are referred to the praise and veneration of the Creator himself, dawn, as it were, like morning in the minds of those contemplating.

**30.** But on account of the perfection of the number six, the same *day* being repeated six times, these things are narrated to have been perfected in six days—not because there was for God a necessary delay of times, as though he could not create all things at once, which would thereafter, by fitting movements, carry out the times; but because by the number six the perfection of the works was signified. For the number six is the first that is completed by its parts, that is, by its sixth part and its third and its half, which are one and two and three, which, drawn into a sum, make six. But in this consideration of numbers those parts are to be understood of which it can be said which fraction they are; as a half, a third, a fourth, and so on, named from some number. For, by way of example, not because in the number nine four is some part of it, can it therefore be said which fraction of it it is; but one can, for it is its ninth; and three can, for it is its third. But these two parts of it joined together, namely the ninth and the third—that is, one and three—are far from its whole sum, which is nine. And likewise in ten, four is some part of it; but which fraction it is cannot be said; but one can, for it is its tenth part. It has also a fifth, which is two; it has also a half, which is five. But these three parts of it, the tenth and the fifth and the half—that is, one and two and five—drawn together do not complete ten; for they are eight. But the parts of the number twelve, drawn into a sum, exceed it; for it has a twelfth, which is one; it has a sixth, which is two; it has a fourth, which is three; it has a third, which is four; it has also a half, which is six; but one and two and three and four and six make not twelve, but more, that is, sixteen.

This I thought should be briefly recalled, to recall the perfection of the number six, which, as I have said, is the first that is itself perfected by its parts reduced to a sum; in which God perfected his works. Whence the reasoning of number is not to be despised, which, in many places of the holy Scriptures, shines forth to those who attentively observe how greatly it is to be esteemed. Nor was it in vain said in the praises of God: *you have ordered all things in measure and number and weight*[^58].

**31.** But on the seventh day, that is, on the same day repeated seven times—which number too is itself perfect by another reckoning—God's rest is commended[^59], in which sanctification first sounds. Thus God was unwilling to sanctify this day in any of his works, but in his rest, which has no evening; for there is no creature that, being known in one way in the Word of God and in another in itself, would make one knowledge, as it were daytime, and another, as it were evening.

Concerning the perfection of the number seven, indeed, more things could be said; but this book is already lengthy, and I fear that, having found the occasion, we may seem to wish to make a show of our little knowledge more lightly than usefully. Account must therefore be taken of moderation and gravity, lest perhaps, while we speak much of number, we be judged to neglect measure and weight.

Let it therefore be enough to note this, that the first wholly odd number is three, the wholly even four; from which two the seven is composed. Therefore it is often put for the whole, as it is: *seven times the just man shall fall, and shall rise again*[^60]; that is: However often he shall have fallen, he shall not perish; which he wished to be understood not of iniquities, but of tribulations leading to humility; and: *seven times in the day I will praise you*[^61]; which is said elsewhere in another way: *his praise is always in my mouth*[^62]; and many things of this kind are found in the divine authorities, in which the number seven, as I have said, is wont to be put for the totality of any thing. On this account the Holy Spirit is often signified by the same number, of whom the Lord says: *he will teach you all truth*[^63].

There is the rest of God, by which there is rest in God. For in the whole, that is, in full perfection, is rest; but in a part is labor. Therefore we labor as long as we know in part, *but when that which is perfect shall come, that which is in part shall be done away*[^64]. Hence it is that we search out even these Scriptures with labor. But the holy angels, for whose society and gathering we sigh in this most laborious pilgrimage, just as they have the eternity of abiding, so they have the ease of knowing and the felicity of resting. For they help us without difficulty, since by their spiritual movements, pure and free, they do not labor.

## The angels were made before all things.

**32.** But lest anyone contend and say that the holy angels were not signified in that which is written, *let there be light, and there was light*[^65], but should suppose or teach that some corporeal light was then first made; and that the angels were made earlier, not only before the firmament, which, made between the waters and the waters, was called heaven, but before that of which it is said, *in the beginning God made heaven and earth*[^66]; and that what was said, *in the beginning*, was not said as though this were made first, since he made the angels before, but because he made all things in wisdom, which is his Word, and which Scripture itself has named the beginning (as he himself in the Gospel answered the Jews who asked who he was, that he was the beginning[^67]):

I will not, on the contrary, bring back a contention, especially because this delights me very much, that even in the very opening of the holy book of *Genesis* the Trinity is commended. For when it is thus said, *in the beginning God made heaven and earth*, so that the Father is understood to have made it in the Son, as the Psalm attests, where it is read, *how magnified are your works, O Lord! you have made all things in wisdom*[^68]: most fittingly, a little later, the Holy Spirit too is mentioned. For when it had been said what kind of earth God first made, or what mass or matter of the future construction of the world he had called by the name of heaven and earth, subjoining and adding, *but the earth was invisible and without form, and darkness was over the deep*: presently, that the mention of the Trinity might be completed, *and the Spirit*, it says, *of God was borne over the water*[^69].

Accordingly, let each take as he will what is so profound that, for the exercise of readers, it can, without departing from the rule of faith, generate several opinions—provided only that no one doubts that the holy angels in their lofty seats, though not indeed coeternal with God, are nevertheless secure and certain of their everlasting and true felicity. To whose society the Lord, teaching that his little ones belong, not only says that, *they will be equal to the angels of God*[^70]; but he also shows what contemplation the angels themselves enjoy, where he says: *see that you do not despise one of these little ones; for I say to you that their angels in heaven always see the face of my Father, who is in heaven*[^71].

**33.** But that certain angels sinned and were thrust down into the depths of this world, which is to them as a prison, until their final damnation to come on the day of judgment, the apostle Peter most plainly shows, saying that God did not spare the sinning angels, but, thrusting them down into the prisons of the darkness of hell, delivered them to be reserved for punishment at the judgment[^72].

Between these, then, and the others, who can doubt that God divided, either by foreknowledge or by act? And who can gainsay that the latter are deservedly called light? Since indeed we, still living in faith and still hoping for equality with them, surely not yet holding it, have already been called light by the Apostle: *for you were*, he says, *at one time darkness, but now light in the Lord*[^73]. But that those deserters are most aptly called darkness, surely those perceive who either understand or believe that they are worse than unbelieving men.

Wherefore, even if another light is to be understood in this place of this book, where we read, *God said: let there be light, and there was light*, and other darkness was signified in that which is written, *God divided between the light and the darkness*[^74]: we nevertheless think that these two angelic societies—one enjoying God, the other swelling with pride; one to which it is said, *adore him, all his angels*[^75], the other whose prince says, *all these things I will give you, if you fall down and adore me*[^76]; one burning with holy love of God, the other smoking with the unclean love of its own loftiness—

and since, as it is written, *God resists the proud, but gives grace to the humble*[^77], the former dwelling in the heaven of heavens[^78], the latter, cast down thence, tumultuating in this lowest, airy heaven; the former tranquil with luminous godliness, the latter turbulent with shadowy desires; the former, at God's nod, mercifully succoring and justly avenging, the latter seething with the craving of its own pride to subject and to harm; the former a minister to God's goodness, that it may help as much as it wills, the latter bridled by God's power, lest it harm as much as it wills; the former mocking the latter, so that against its will it profits by its persecutions, the latter envying the former, when it gathers in its pilgrim citizens—we, then, think that these two angelic societies, unlike and contrary to each other, the one both good by nature and right by will, the other indeed good by nature but perverse by will,

declared by other more manifest testimonies of the divine Scriptures, were signified also in this book, whose name is *Genesis*, by the words *light and darkness*; even if perhaps he who wrote it meant something else in this place, the obscurity of this passage has not been treated unprofitably, because, although we could not trace out the will of the author of this book, we have nevertheless not departed from the rule of faith, which is sufficiently known to the faithful through other sacred Letters of the same authority.

For even if the works of God here mentioned are corporeal, they have without doubt some likeness of spiritual things, according to which the Apostle says: *for you are all sons of light and sons of the day; we are not of the night nor of the darkness*[^79].

But if he who wrote it also meant this, our intention has reached a more perfect end of the discussion, that a man of God of such surpassing and divine wisdom—nay, through him the Spirit of God—in mentioning the works of God, all of which he says were perfected on the sixth day, may in no way be believed to have passed over the angels,

whether it was written *in the beginning* because he made them first, or, what is more fittingly understood, *in the beginning* because he made them in the only-begotten Word, *in the beginning God made heaven and earth*[^80]; by which names the universal creature was signified, either spiritual and corporeal, which is more credible, or the two great parts of the world, by which all things that were created are contained, so that he might first set forth the whole of it, and then follow out its parts according to the mystical number of the days.

**34.** Although some have thought that by the name of waters were signified in some manner the peoples of the angels, and that this is what was said, *let there be a firmament between water and water*[^81], so that above the firmament the angels are understood, but below it either these visible waters, or the multitude of evil angels, or the nations of all men.

And if this is so, it does not there appear where the angels were made, but where they were separated; although certain men, which is of a most perverse and impious vanity, even deny that the waters were made by God, since it is nowhere written, "God said: let the waters be made."

Which they can, by a similar vanity, say of the earth too; for it is nowhere read, "God said: let the earth be made." But, they say, it is written, *in the beginning God made heaven and earth*[^82]. There, then, water too is to be understood; for both were comprehended under one name. For *the sea is his*, as it is read in the Psalm, *and he made it, and his hands formed the dry land*[^83].

But those who wish the angels to be understood by the name of the waters *which are above the heavens* are moved by the weights of the elements, and therefore do not think that the fluid and heavy nature of waters could be set in the upper places of the world; who, according to their own reasonings, if they themselves could make a man, would not put in his head the phlegm, which in Greek is called φλέγμα and holds, as it were among the elements of our body, the place of water. For there is the seat of the phlegm, according to the work of God most aptly indeed, but according to the conjecture of these men so absurdly that, if we did not know this, and it had been similarly written in this book that God placed a fluid and cold and therefore heavy humor in the part of the human body higher than all the rest, these "weighers of the elements" would by no means believe it, and, if they were subject to the authority of the same Scripture, would judge that something else must be understood by it.

## Conclusion

But since, if we diligently scrutinize and treat the single things which are written in that divine book concerning the constitution of the world, both many things must be said and we must digress far from the plan of the undertaken work, and since we have now discussed, as far as has seemed sufficient, these two angelic societies, diverse from and contrary to each other, in which there are certain beginnings of the two cities also in human affairs, of which I have undertaken to speak next: let us at length close this book too.
