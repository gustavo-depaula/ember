# Book XXII: What the book treats concerning eternity

**1. 1.** As we promised in the immediately preceding book, this last book of the whole work will contain a discussion of the eternal blessedness of the city of God, which has received the name of eternity not because it will endure for a length of age through many centuries while nevertheless coming to an end at some point, but, as it is written in the Gospel, *of his kingdom there will be no end*[^1]; nor in such a way that an appearance of perpetuity is seen in it as some die and pass away and others arise and succeed them, just as in a tree clothed with perennial foliage the same greenness seems to remain while, with leaves slipping and falling, others, which are born, continually preserve the look of shade; but all the citizens in it will be immortal, men too obtaining what the holy angels never lost. This the most omnipotent God, its founder, will do. For he has promised and cannot lie, and to make us believe this too, he has already done many of his works, both unpromised and promised.

**1. 2.** For he it is who in the beginning founded the world, full of all good things both visible and intelligible, in which he established nothing better than the spirits to whom he gave understanding and whom he rendered fit for his contemplation and capable of him, and bound together in one society, which we call the holy and heavenly city, in which the reality by which they are sustained and made blessed is God himself for them, as a common life and sustenance; who granted to that same intellectual nature free choice of such a kind that, if it should will, it might forsake God—that is, its own blessedness—with misery immediately to follow; who, although he foreknew that certain angels, through the pride by which they would wish to be sufficient for themselves for the blessed life, would be deserters of so great a good, did not take from them this power, judging it more powerful and better to bring good even out of evils than not to allow evils to be;

evils which would be no evils at all unless a mutable nature, though good and established by the supreme God and unchangeable good, who founded all good things, had made them for itself by sinning—by which very sin too it is convicted, with itself as witness, that it was created a good nature; for unless it too were a great good, though not equal to the Creator, surely the desertion of God, as of its own light, could not be its evil. For just as the blindness of an eye is a defect, and that very thing indicates that the eye was created for seeing light, and so even by its own defect the member capable of light is shown to be more excellent than the other members (for there would be no other cause for its defect to be the lack of light); so the nature that enjoyed God teaches, even by the very defect by which it is miserable because it does not enjoy God, that it was created most excellent;

who bound the voluntary fall of the angels with the most just penalty of everlasting unhappiness, and to the others who remained in that supreme good gave, as it were as the reward of perseverance itself, that they should be certain of their perseverance without end; who made man himself also upright, with the same free choice, an earthly animal indeed, but worthy of heaven if he should cleave to his author, with misery likewise to follow, of the kind that would befit such a nature, if he should forsake him

(whom, since he foreknew that he likewise would sin, by the transgression of the law of God through the desertion of God, he did not take from him the power of free choice, foreseeing at the same time what good he himself would bring out of his evil); who from a mortal progeny, deservedly and justly damned, gathers by his grace so great a people that from it he supplies and restores the part of the angels that fell, and so that beloved and heavenly city is not defrauded of the number of its citizens, but perhaps even rejoices in a more abundant one.

## God, foreknowing and disposing, is not changed . . .

**2. 1.** For many things are indeed done by the wicked against the will of God; but he is of such great wisdom and such great power that all things which seem contrary to his will tend toward those outcomes or ends which he himself foreknew to be good and just. And for this reason, when God is said to change his will—so that, to those to whom he was gentle, for example, he becomes angry—it is they rather than he who are changed, and they find him in a manner "changed" in the things they suffer; just as the sun is "changed" for injured eyes and becomes in a manner harsh from being mild, and troublesome from being delightful, while in itself it remains the same as it was.

There is also called "the will of God" that which he works in the hearts of those who obey his commands, of which the Apostle says: *for it is God who works in you both to will*[^2]; just as "the righteousness of God" is said to be not only that by which he himself is righteous, but also that which he works in the man who is justified by him[^3].

So too "his law" is the name given to what is rather that of men, but given by him; for surely those were men to whom Jesus said, *in your law it is written*[^4], while in another place we read, *the law of his God is in his heart*[^5].

According to this will, which God works in men, he is said even to will what he himself does not will, but makes his own people will; just as he is said to have come to know what he made to be known by those to whom it was unknown. For when the Apostle says, *but now that you have come to know God, or rather to be known by God*[^6], it is not lawful for us to believe that God then came to know those whom he foreknew before the foundation of the world[^7]; but he was said then to have come to know what he then brought about to be known. I recall that these modes of expression have already been discussed in the preceding books too[^8]. According to this "will," then, by which we say that God wills what he makes others will—others by whom things to come are not known—he wills many things and does not do them.

**2. 2.** For his saints will many things to be done, by a holy will inspired by him, and they are not done; as when they pray piously and holily for certain people, and he does not do what they pray for, though he himself by his Holy Spirit has worked in them this will to pray. And for this reason, when the saints will and pray according to God that someone be saved, we can say, in that mode of expression, "God wills and does not do it," so that we call him the one who wills, who makes them will. But according to that will of his, which is eternal together with his foreknowledge, surely in heaven and on earth he has already done all things whatsoever he willed, not only past and present, but even future ones[^9]. But before the time comes at which he willed it to come to pass—what before all ages he foreknew and disposed—we say, "It will come to pass when God shall will it"; but if we are ignorant not only of the time at which it is to be, but even whether it is to be, we say, "It will come to pass, if God shall will it"; not because God will then have a new will which he did not have, but because that which from eternity has been prepared in his immutable will shall then be.

**3.** Wherefore, to pass over so many other things, just as we now see fulfilled in Christ what he promised to Abraham, saying, *in your seed all nations shall be blessed*[^10], so what he promised to that same seed of his will be fulfilled, where he says through the Prophet, *those who were in the tombs shall rise*[^11], and what he says: *there shall be a new heaven and a new earth, and they shall not remember the former things, nor shall* they come up *into their heart, but they shall find joy and exultation in it. Behold, I will make Jerusalem an exultation and my people a joy; and I will exult in Jerusalem and rejoice in my people, and no more shall the voice of weeping be heard in her*[^12]; and what he foretold through another Prophet, saying to that same Prophet, *at that time your people shall be saved, everyone who is found written in the book, and many of those who sleep in the dust of the earth* (or, as some have interpreted it[^13], *in the mound*) *shall arise, these to eternal life and these to reproach and to eternal confusion*[^14]; and in another place through the same Prophet: *the saints of the Most High shall receive the kingdom and possess it forever and ever and ever*[^15]; and a little later he says, *his kingdom is an everlasting kingdom*[^16];

and the other things pertaining to this which I set down in the twentieth book[^17], or those which I did not set down and yet are written in those same Scriptures—these too will come, just as those have come which unbelievers did not think would come. For the same God promised both, foretold both would come—the God whom the deities of the pagans dread, even on the testimony of Porphyry, the most noble of the pagan philosophers[^18].

## What learned men say against the resurrection of bodies.

**4.** But, you see, learned and wise men think they argue acutely against the resurrection of bodies, in the face of the force of so great an authority, which has turned all kinds of men, as it foretold so long before, to believing and hoping for this; and they say what is recorded by Cicero in the third book of *On the Republic*. For when he was asserting that Hercules and Romulus had been made gods out of men, he said: *whose bodies were not raised into heaven; for nature would not allow that what was from the earth should remain anywhere but in the earth*[^19].

This is the great reasoning of the wise, *whose thoughts the Lord knows, that they are vain*[^20]. For if we were only souls, that is, spirits without any body, and dwelling in heaven knew nothing of earthly animals, and it were told us that it would come to pass that we should be bound by some wondrous bond to giving life to earthly bodies, would we not argue much more forcefully, refusing to believe this, and say that nature does not allow an incorporeal thing to be bound by a corporeal tie? And yet the earth is full of souls giving life to these earthly members, connected and entwined with them in a wondrous manner. Why, then, with the same God willing it, who made this animal, will an earthly body not be able to be lifted up into a heavenly body, if a soul, more excellent than every body—and so even than a heavenly one—could be bound to an earthly body?

Or could so tiny a particle of earth hold within itself something better than a heavenly body, so as to have sensation and life, and yet will heaven disdain to receive that earthly thing when it feels and lives, or, once received, be unable to sustain it, when it has sensation and life from a reality better than every heavenly body is? But the reason it does not now happen is that the time has not yet come at which he willed it to happen who has done this thing—which, now that it is seen, has already become cheap to us—far more wondrously than that thing which is not believed by these men.

For why do we not more vehemently marvel that incorporeal souls, more powerful than a heavenly body, are bound to earthly bodies, than that bodies, though earthly, are raised on high to seats that are heavenly, but nonetheless corporeal—except that we have grown accustomed to seeing this, and this we ourselves are, while that we are not yet, nor have we ever yet seen it? For surely, when sober reason is consulted, it is found to be the more wondrous divine work to weave corporeal things together, as it were, with incorporeal ones, than to join, though diverse—because the one are heavenly and the others earthly—nonetheless bodies with bodies.

## The world has believed what the sacred Scriptures say.

**5.** But this may once have been incredible: behold, the world has now believed that the earthly body of Christ was taken up into heaven; the resurrection of the flesh and the ascension into the heavenly seats both the learned and the unlearned have already believed, with very few, whether learned or unlearned, remaining and astonished. If they believed a credible thing, let them see how foolish are those who do not believe; but if an incredible thing was believed, then this too is surely incredible, that what is incredible was so believed. These two incredible things, then—namely, the resurrection of our body unto eternity, and a thing so incredible's being believed by the world—the same God, before either of them came to pass, foretold that both would come to pass[^21].

We see one of the two incredible things already accomplished—that the world believed what was incredible; why is the one that remains despaired of, that this too may come to pass which the world incredibly believed, just as that has already come to pass which was likewise incredible, that the world should believe a thing so incredible—since indeed both of these incredible things, of which we see one and believe the other, were foretold in the same Scriptures through which the world believed?

And the very manner in which the world believed, if it be considered, is found more incredible still. Men untrained in the liberal disciplines and altogether, as far as those men's doctrines are concerned, unpolished, not skilled in grammar, not armed with dialectic, not puffed up with rhetoric—fishermen—Christ sent, very few of them, with the nets of faith to the sea of this world, and thus caught fish of every kind, so many of them, and the more wondrous the rarer, even the philosophers themselves. To those two incredible things, if you please—indeed, because it ought to please—let us add this third.

There are now, then, three incredible things which nevertheless have come to pass. It is incredible that Christ rose in the flesh and ascended into heaven with the flesh; it is incredible that the world believed a thing so incredible; it is incredible that men ignoble, lowly, very few, unskilled, could so effectively persuade the world—and in it the learned too—of a thing so incredible. Of these three incredible things, those with whom we are dealing refuse to believe the first; the second they are compelled even to perceive, and they cannot find how it came to pass, unless they believe the third. The resurrection of Christ, certainly, and his ascension into heaven with the flesh in which he rose, is already preached and believed throughout the whole world; if it is not credible, how is it that it has already been believed throughout the whole globe of the earth?

If many, noble, exalted, learned men had said they had seen it and had taken care to spread abroad what they saw, it would be no wonder that the world believed them, but for these people still to refuse to believe would be very hard; but if, as is the truth, the world believed a few, obscure, very lowly, unlearned men who said and wrote that they had seen it, why do the few most obstinate ones who remain still not believe the world that already believes? The world believed a tiny number of ignoble, lowly, unskilled men precisely because in witnesses so contemptible the divinity persuaded of itself far more wondrously.

For the eloquence of those who persuaded, what they said, consisted of marvelous deeds, not words. For those who had not seen Christ rise in the flesh and ascend with it into heaven believed those who narrated that they had seen this, not merely speaking but also working marvelous signs.

For men whom they had known to be of one tongue, or at most of two, they wondrously heard suddenly speaking in the tongues of all nations[^22]; a man lame from his mother's womb they saw, at their word in the name of Christ, after forty years stand up whole[^23];

handkerchiefs taken from their bodies, they saw, profited the sick toward healing; countless people laboring under various diseases, set in a row along the way by which they were about to pass, so that as they walked their shadow might pass over them[^24], were wont to recover health at once; and many other astonishing things done through them in the name of Christ; finally, they saw even the dead rise again[^25].

If they grant that these things were done as they are read, behold, we add so many incredible things to those three incredible ones; and so that one incredible thing may be believed—what is said about the resurrection of the flesh and the ascension into heaven—we heap up the testimonies of so many incredible things, and still we do not bend the unbelievers, in their dreadful hardness, to believing.

But if they do not believe that these miracles too were worked through the Apostles of Christ, so that they might be believed as they preached the resurrection and ascension of Christ, this one great miracle suffices for us: that the globe of the earth believed it without any miracles.

**6. 1.** Let us recall here too that which Tully marvels at concerning the believed divinity of Romulus. I will insert his words as they are written. *It is the more to be marveled at in Romulus*, he says, *that the others who are said to have been made gods out of men lived in less learned ages of mankind, so that there was a ready inclination to invent, since the uninstructed were easily impelled to believe; but we see that the age of Romulus was less than six hundred years ago, when letters and learning were already of long standing, and all that ancient error arising from the uncultivated life of men had been done away*[^26]. And a little later he speaks thus of the same Romulus, in a way that pertains to this sense: *from which it can be understood*, he says, *that Homer lived very many years before Romulus, so that, men and the very times being now learned and cultivated, there was scarcely any room for inventing. For antiquity received fables, sometimes even crudely fabricated; but this age, now refined, especially rejects with mockery everything that cannot happen*[^27].

One of the number of the most learned men, and likewise the most eloquent of all, Marcus Tullius Cicero, says for this reason that the divinity of Romulus was marvelously believed: because the times were already learned, such as would not receive the falsehood of fables. But who believed Romulus to be a god except Rome, and that while it was small and just beginning? Then afterward it was necessary for posterity to preserve what they had received from their elders, so that the city, growing up with this superstition imbibed as it were in its mother's milk, might attain to so great an empire that from its summit, as from some loftier place, it might steep also the other nations over which it ruled in this opinion of its own—so that they did not indeed believe, but nevertheless called Romulus a god, lest they offend the city they served concerning its founder, by naming him otherwise than Rome did, which had believed this not indeed out of love of this error, but nevertheless through the error of love.

Christ, however, although he is the founder of the heavenly and everlasting city, was nevertheless not believed by it to be God because it was founded by him; rather it is to be founded for this reason: because it believes. Rome, once built and dedicated, worshiped its founder as a god in a temple; but this Jerusalem set its founder God, Christ, in the foundation of faith, so that it might be built and dedicated.

The one, by loving him, believed he was a god; the other, by believing this one to be God, loved him. Just as, then, in the one there came first that from which it might love and might, concerning the one already loved, gladly believe even a false good, so in the other there came first that from which it might believe, so that by right faith it might love not rashly what was false, but what was true.

For apart from so many and so great miracles, which persuaded that Christ is God, the divine prophecies, most worthy of faith, also came first, which are not, as by the fathers, still believed to be going to be fulfilled in him, but are now shown to have been fulfilled;

but concerning Romulus, because he founded Rome and reigned in it, what was done is heard or read, not what was prophesied before it was done; but that he was received among the gods, the records hold as believed, they do not teach as a fact[^28]. For by no signs of marvelous things is it shown that this truly befell him. As for that she-wolf his nurse, which seems to have appeared as some great portent, of what sort or how great is it for demonstrating a god[^29]? For surely, even if that she-wolf was not a harlot but a beast, since she was common to both of them, his brother is nevertheless not held to be a god. But who was forbidden to call either Romulus or Hercules or other such men gods, and chose rather to die than not to call them so? Or would any of the nations worship Romulus among its gods, unless the fear of the Roman name compelled it?

Who, moreover, could count how many chose to be killed by however great a savagery of cruelty rather than to deny that Christ is God? Accordingly, the fear of even a slight indignation, which was thought capable of arising in the minds of the Romans if it were not done, compelled some cities placed under Roman law to worship Romulus as a god; but from worshiping Christ as God—indeed, even from confessing him—the fear, throughout the peoples of the world, not of a slight offense to minds, but of immense and various punishments and of death itself, which is dreaded more than the rest, could not call back so great a multitude of martyrs. Nor did the city of Christ then, although it was still on pilgrimage on earth and yet had columns of great peoples, fight against its impious persecutors for temporal welfare; but rather, that it might obtain the eternal one, it did not fight back. They were bound, shut up, beaten, tortured, burned, torn to pieces, slaughtered—and they multiplied. There was no fighting for their welfare for them, except to despise welfare for the sake of the Savior.

**6. 2.** I know that in the third book of Cicero's *On the Republic*, unless I am mistaken, it is argued that no war is undertaken by the best state except either for faith or for safety. But what he means by "for safety," or what safety he intends, he shows in another place: *but from those penalties*, he says, *which even the most foolish feel—want, exile, chains, lashes—private persons often escape by the offered swiftness of death; for states, however, death itself, which seems to free individuals from punishment, is the penalty. For a state ought to be so constituted that it is eternal. And so there is no natural destruction of a commonwealth, as there is of a man, in whom death is not only necessary but very often even to be desired. But when a state is destroyed, blotted out, extinguished, it is in a manner like—to compare small things with great—as if this whole world should perish and collapse*[^30]. Cicero said this because, with the Platonists, he holds that the world will not perish.

It is established, then, that he wished war to be undertaken by a state for that safety by which it comes about that a state here remains, as he says, eternal, although with individuals dying and being born, just as the shade of the olive or the laurel and of the other trees of this kind is perennial through the falling and springing up of individual leaves. For death, as he says, is the penalty not of individual men but of the whole state, which for the most part frees individuals from punishment.

Hence it is rightly asked whether the people of Saguntum acted rightly when they chose that their whole state should perish rather than break the faith by which they were bound together with the Roman commonwealth itself; and in this deed of theirs they are praised by all the citizens of the earthly commonwealth. But how they could obey this argument I do not see, where it is said that no war is to be undertaken except either for faith or for safety, and it is not said what is rather to be chosen, if these two should so come together into one and the same peril that the one cannot be held without the loss of the other. For surely, if the Saguntines had chosen safety, faith would have had to be deserted by them; if faith were to be held, safety would of course have had to be lost, as in fact happened[^31].

But the safety of the city of God is such that it can be held, or rather acquired, with faith and through faith; while, faith being lost, no one can come to it. It was this thought, of a most steadfast and most patient heart, that made so many and so great martyrs, of whom Romulus did not have, nor could have, even a single one when he was believed to be a god.

**7.** But it is very ridiculous to make mention of the false divinity of Romulus when we are speaking of Christ. Nevertheless, since Romulus lived some six hundred years before Cicero, and that age is said to have been already cultivated with learning[^32], so that it rejected everything that cannot happen: how much more, six hundred years later, in Cicero's own time and especially afterward under Augustus and Tiberius, in times surely more learned, would the human mind have been unable to bear the resurrection of Christ's flesh and his ascension into heaven, as something that cannot happen, and would have rejected it from its ears and hearts with mockery, unless the divinity of truth itself, or the truth of divinity, and the attesting signs of miracles had shown that it was able to happen and had happened;

so that, with so many and so great persecutions terrifying and gainsaying, the resurrection and immortality of the flesh—first preceding in Christ, then to follow in the rest at the new age—might both be most faithfully believed and fearlessly preached, and, destined to sprout more fruitfully throughout the world, be sown with the blood of the martyrs. For the preceding heralds of the Prophets were read, the portents of mighty works concurred, and the new truth was persuasively commended to custom—truth not contrary to reason—until the world, which persecuted in fury, followed in faith.

## Miracles happen that we may believe.

**8. 1.** Why, they say, do those miracles which you preach to have happened not happen now[^33]? I could indeed say that they were necessary before the world believed, to the end that the world might believe. Whoever still demands prodigies in order to believe is himself a great prodigy, in not believing when the world believes. But they say this so that those miracles may not be believed to have happened even then.

Whence, then, is Christ everywhere sung with such great faith as taken up into heaven with the flesh? Whence, in learned times that rejected everything that cannot happen, did the world, exceedingly wondrously, believe incredible things without any miracles? Or perhaps they will say that they were credible and therefore were believed? Why, then, do they themselves not believe?

Our summary, then, is brief: either other incredible things, which nevertheless were happening and were seen, created belief in an incredible thing which was not seen; or surely the thing was so credible that it needed no miracles by which to persuade of it, and this convicts the excessive unbelief of these men.

This I would say to refute the most vain. For that many miracles were done which would attest to that one great and saving miracle, by which Christ ascended into heaven with the flesh in which he rose, we cannot deny. For in those same most truthful books all things are written down, both what was done and on account of what it was done to be believed. These things became known in order to create faith; through the faith which they created they become known far more clearly. For they are read among the peoples that they may be believed; and yet they would not be read among the peoples unless they were believed.

For even now miracles are done in his name, whether through his sacraments or through the prayers or the memorials of his saints; but they are not illumined with the same brightness, so as to be spread abroad with as great glory as those. For the canon of the sacred Scriptures, which had to be settled, causes those to be recited everywhere and to cling to the memory of all peoples; but these, wherever they happen, are scarcely known there, even by the whole of that very city or whatever place of residence.

For very often even there only very few know them, with the rest ignorant, especially if the city is large; and when they are narrated elsewhere and to others, no such authority commends them that they are believed without difficulty or doubt, although they are reported to faithful Christians by the faithful.

**8. 2.** The miracle which was done at Milan while we were there, when a blind man was given sight, could come to the notice of many, because the city is large and the Emperor was there at the time, and the deed was done with an immense people as witness, who had gathered at the bodies of the martyrs Protasius and Gervasius; which bodies, while they lay hidden and were utterly unknown, were revealed to bishop Ambrose through a dream and were found; whereupon that blind man, the old darkness driven away, saw the day[^34].

**8. 3.** But at Carthage, who knows, besides very few indeed, of the healing that was done for Innocentius, formerly an advocate of the vicarial prefecture, at which we were present and which we beheld with our own eyes? For when my brother Alypius and I came from overseas—not yet clerics, indeed, but already serving God—he had received us, being himself, with his whole household, most devout, and we were then living with him. He was being treated by physicians for fistulas, of which he had many and intricate ones in the rear and lowest part of his body. They had already cut him and were treating the rest with the medicaments of their art. But he had suffered, in that cutting, both prolonged and bitter pains.

But one fold among the many had escaped the physicians and had so lain hidden that they did not touch the one they ought to have opened with the knife. Finally, when all those they had opened were healed, this one alone remained, on which labor was being expended in vain. Holding these delays suspect and greatly dreading lest he be cut again

(which another physician, a member of his household, had foretold him—a man whom those others had not admitted even to see, when he was first cut, how they did it, and whom, in anger, he had thrown out of the house and scarcely received back), he burst out and said: *are you going to cut me again? Am I to come to the words of the man whom you were unwilling to have present?* They began to mock the unskilled physician and to soothe the man's fear with good words and promises.

Very many more days passed and everything that was done availed nothing. The physicians, however, stood by their promise, that they would close that fold not with the knife but with medicaments. They also brought in another physician, already aged and sufficiently praised in that art (for he was still living), Ammonius, who, having inspected the place, promised the same thing that those had, out of their diligence and skill. Made secure by his authority, Innocentius mocked, with witty merriment, as though already healed, his own household physician who had foretold that there would be another cutting.

What more? So many days afterward were consumed and passed in vain that, weary and confounded, they confessed that he could in no way be healed except by the knife. He was terrified, he grew pale, troubled by excessive fear, and when he had collected himself and could speak, he ordered them to go away and to come to him no more; and nothing else occurred to him, worn out with tears and now constrained by that necessity, except to bring in a certain Alexandrian, who was then held to be a marvelous surgeon, so that he himself might do what those men, in his anger, he did not wish to be done by.

But after he came and, as an expert, saw their labor in the scars, fulfilling the office of a good man, he persuaded the man rather that those who had labored on him as much as he himself, on inspecting it, marveled at, should enjoy the completion of his cure—adding that in very truth he could not be well unless he were cut; that it greatly went against his own character to take away the palm of so great a labor from men whose most skillful work, industry, and diligence he saw and admired in his scars, on account of the little that remained.

They were restored to his good will, and it was decided that, with that same Alexandrian assisting, they themselves should open with the knife that fold, which now by the consent of all was thought otherwise incurable. This matter was put off to the following day. But when they had departed, from the master's excessive grief so great a sorrow arose in that house that, like the lamentation of a funeral, it was scarcely repressed by us.

Holy men visited him daily: Saturninus, of blessed memory, then bishop of Uzalis, and the presbyter Gulosus, and the deacons of the church of Carthage; among whom was, and of whom alone now remains among human affairs, the man now to be named with the honor due from us, the bishop Aurelius, with whom, recalling the marvels of God's works, we have often conversed about this matter, and have found that he very well remembers what we relate. When they visited him in the evening, as they were accustomed, he begged them with pitiable tears that they would deign to be present in the morning at his funeral rather than at his suffering. For so great a fear from the earlier punishments had invaded him that he did not doubt he would die among the physicians' hands. They consoled him and exhorted him to trust in God and to bear his will manfully.

Then we went in to pray; and when we, according to custom, were bending our knees and leaning upon the ground, he threw himself down in such a way as though he had been prostrated by someone violently thrusting him, and he began to pray—in what manner, with what affection, with what movement of soul, with what a flood of tears, with what groans and sobs that shook all his limbs and almost cut off his breath, who could explain in any words? Whether the others were praying, and whether their attention was not turned to these things, I did not know. I, however, was utterly unable to pray; this only I said briefly in my heart: "Lord, whose prayers of your own do you hear, if you do not hear these?" For it seemed to me that nothing could now be added, except that he should breathe his last in praying. We rose, and, having received the bishop's blessing, we departed, he begging that they be present in the morning, they exhorting him to be of even mind.

The dreaded day dawned, the servants of God were present, as they had promised they would be; the physicians came in, all things that the hour demanded were prepared, the dreadful instruments were brought out, with all astonished and in suspense. While those whose authority was greater were raising his failing spirit by consoling him, his limbs were arranged on the little bed for the hands of the one about to cut, the knots of the bandages were loosened, the place was bared, the physician inspected it, and, armed and intent, sought the fold that was to be cut. He searched with his eyes and felt with his fingers, and tried, finally, in every way: he found a most firm scar. Now that joy and praise and giving of thanks to the merciful and omnipotent God, which was poured out from the mouth of all with tears of gladness, is not to be entrusted to my words; let it be thought rather than spoken.

**8. 4.** In the same Carthage, Innocentia, a most devout woman, of the leading people of that city, had a cancer in her breast, a thing, as the physicians say, curable by no medicaments. Either, then, it is customary to cut away and to separate from the body the member where it arises, or, that a person may live somewhat longer—though death will nonetheless follow from it, however more slowly—according to the opinion of Hippocrates, as they say, all treatment is to be omitted[^35]. This she had heard from a skilled physician who was a close friend of her household, and she had turned by prayer to God alone. As Easter drew near, she was admonished in her sleep that, watching in the women's section at the baptistery, she should mark for herself, with the sign of Christ, whatever woman first newly baptized should meet her. She did so, and health immediately followed.

The physician, indeed, who had told her to apply no treatment if she wished to live a little longer, when he had afterward inspected her and found her perfectly well, who he had earlier learned by such inspection had that disease, asked her vehemently what treatment she had applied, desiring, as far as can be understood, to know the medicament by which Hippocrates's ruling was overcome. And when he had heard from her what had been done, with a voice and countenance as of one despising it—so much so that she feared lest he utter some insulting word against Christ—he is said to have replied, with religious courtesy: *I thought*, he said, *that you were going to tell me something great*. And when she now shuddered, he soon added: *what great thing did Christ do to heal a cancer, who raised a man four days dead?*[^36].

When I had heard this, and was vehemently vexed that so great a miracle, done in that city and in a person by no means obscure, should so lie hidden, I thought she should be both admonished and almost rebuked about it. And when she answered me that she had not been silent about it, I asked those matrons whom she happened to have with her at the time, very dear friends, whether they had known this before. They answered that they had altogether not known it.

*See,* I said, *how you are not silent, when not even these women, who are joined to you in such great intimacy, hear of it*. And because I had questioned her briefly, I had her, with them listening and greatly marveling and glorifying God, set forth the whole in order, how it had been done.

**8. 5.** A certain physician afflicted with gout in the same city, who, when he had given in his name for baptism and on the day before he was to be baptized had been forbidden, in his sleep, to be baptized that year by black curly-haired boys whom he understood to be demons, and, not obeying them even when they trod upon his feet into a most acute pain such as he had never experienced, had gone and, overcoming them, had not deferred to be washed in the laver of regeneration as he had vowed—who knows that in the baptism itself he was rid not only of the pain by which he was tormented beyond the usual, but even of the gout, and that, when he had lived a long time afterward, his feet hurt no more? We, however, know it, and very few brethren to whom it could come.

**8. 6.** A certain former actor of Curubis, when he was baptized, was made well not only of paralysis but also of a deformed swelling of the genitals, and, freed of both troubles, came up from the font of regeneration as though he had had no evil in his body. Who knows this besides Curubis and besides a very few others who could anywhere hear of it? But when we had learned of it, by the order of the holy bishop Aurelius we even had the man come to Carthage, although we had previously heard it from such people about whose faith we could not doubt.

**8. 7.** Hesperius, a man of tribunician rank, is among us; he has, in the territory of Fussala, an estate called Zubedi; where, when he had learned that his household was suffering the harmful violence of malignant spirits, with affliction of his animals and his slaves, he asked our presbyters, in my absence, that one of them go there, by whose prayers they might yield. One went, offered there the sacrifice of the body of Christ, praying as much as he could that that vexation might cease: God at once having mercy, it ceased. Now he had received from a friend of his some holy earth brought from Jerusalem, where Christ, buried, rose on the third day, and he had hung it up in his bedroom, lest he too suffer any evil.

But when his house was purified of that infestation, he considered what should be done with that earth, which for reverence's sake he did not wish to keep any longer in his bedroom. It happened by chance that I and my colleague at the time, Maximinus, bishop of the church of Sinitum, were nearby; he asked us to come, and we came. And when he had related everything to us, he asked also this: that it be buried somewhere and that a place of prayer be made there, where Christians too might be able to gather to celebrate the things that are God's. We did not refuse; it was done. There was there a young peasant who was a paralytic. Hearing this, he asked his parents to carry him without delay to that holy place. When he had been brought there, he prayed, and from there he immediately departed on his own feet, made well.

**8. 8.** There is a villa called Victoriana, less than thirty miles from Hippo Regius. There is there a memorial of the martyrs of Milan, Protasius and Gervasius. A certain young man was carried there, who, when at midday in the summer season he was washing a horse in a pool of the river, was attacked by a demon. While he lay there, either near to death or very like a dead man, the lady of the estate entered there, as was her custom, for the evening hymns and prayers with her maidservants and certain consecrated women, and they began to sing hymns. At that sound the young man was, as it were, struck and roused, and with a terrible roaring seized the altar, not daring—or not able—to move it, holding it as though he had been bound or fastened to it, and with great wailing, begging to be spared, he confessed where and when and how he had entered the young man. Finally, announcing that he would come out, he named each of the man's limbs, which he threatened to cut off as he departed, and amid these words he departed from the man. But the man's eye, fallen onto his cheek, hung by a slender little vein from its inner root, as it were, and all its middle, which had been blackish, had turned white.

Seeing this, those who were present (and others too had run together, summoned by his cries, and had all prostrated themselves in prayer for him), although they rejoiced that he stood in his right mind, yet, saddened again on account of his eye, said that a physician must be sought.

There the husband of his sister, who had carried him there, said: *God, who put the demon to flight, is able by the prayers of the saints to restore his sight*. Then, as well as he could, he recalled the fallen and hanging eye to its place and bound it with a kerchief, and thought it should not be unbound until after seven days. When he had done so, he found it perfectly sound. Others too were healed there, of whom it would be long to tell.

**8. 9.** I know of a certain virgin of Hippo who, when she had anointed herself with oil into which the presbyter praying for her had let fall his tears, was soon healed of a demon. I know also of a bishop who once prayed for a young man whom he did not see, and that young man was immediately rid of the demon.

**8. 10.** There was a certain old man, Florentius, one of our people of Hippo, a devout and poor man; he fed himself by the trade of a tailor; he had lost his little cloak and had nothing with which to buy himself one; at the shrine of the Twenty Martyrs, whose memorial among us is most renowned[^37], he prayed in a clear voice that he might be clothed. Some young men who happened to be present, mockers, heard him, and as he departed pursued him, jeering, as though he had asked the martyrs for fifty folles to buy a garment. But he, walking on in silence, saw a great fish cast up and flapping on the shore, and with those very men favoring and helping, he caught it and sold it for three hundred folles to a certain cook, Cattosus by name, a good Christian, for the relish-kitchen, telling him what had happened, intending to buy wool from it, so that his wife might make for him, as she could, something to wear. But the cook, cutting up the fish, found a gold ring in its belly, and, soon moved by compassion and thoroughly struck by religion, gave it back to the man, saying: *see how the Twenty Martyrs have clothed you*.

**8. 11.** At the waters of Tibilis, when bishop Praiectus was bringing the memorial of the most glorious martyr Stephen, with the running together and meeting of a great multitude, a blind woman there prayed to be led to the bishop carrying it; she gave the flowers she was carrying, received them back, applied them to her eyes, and immediately saw. As those who were present were astonished, she went on ahead exulting, taking the road and no longer needing a guide for the road.

**8. 12.** The memorial of the aforementioned martyr, which is set in the fort of Sinitum, which is near to the colony of Hippo, Lucillus, bishop of that place, was carrying, with the people going before and following. A fistula, with whose trouble he had now long labored, and for which he was awaiting the hand of his most familiar physician to cut him, was suddenly healed by the carrying of that holy burden; for thereafter he did not find it in his body.

**8. 13.** Eucharius is a presbyter from Spain, dwelling at Calama, who labored under the old disease of the stone; through the memorial of the aforesaid martyr, which bishop Possidius had brought there, he was made well. The same man afterward, with another disease prevailing, lay dead in such a way that his thumbs were now being bound; by the help of the aforesaid martyr, when the presbyter's tunic had been brought back from his memorial and laid over the body of the man as he lay, he was raised up.

**8. 14.** There was there a man, foremost in his rank, Martialis by name, now heavy with age and greatly averse to the Christian religion. He had, indeed, a faithful daughter and a son-in-law baptized that same year. When these, as he lay sick, begged him with many and great tears to become a Christian, he utterly refused and drove them from him with turbulent indignation. It seemed good to his son-in-law to go to the memorial of holy Stephen and there pray for him as much as he could, that God might give him a good mind, by which he should not defer to believe in Christ. He did this with great groaning and weeping and with a sincerely burning affection of devotion; then, as he departed, he took some of the flowers from the altar, what came to hand, and laid them at his head when it was now night; then they went to sleep.

And behold, before dawn he cried out for someone to run to the bishop, who happened then to be with me at Hippo. When, therefore, he had heard that he was absent, he asked the presbyters to come. They came; he said he believed, and, with all marveling and rejoicing, he was baptized. As long as he lived, he had this on his lips: *Christ, receive my spirit*; though he did not know that these were the last words of the most blessed Stephen when he was stoned by the Jews[^38]; which were also the last for this man; for not long afterward he too departed this life.

**8. 15.** There too, through the same martyr, two citizens afflicted with gout were healed, and one stranger: but the citizens completely; the stranger, however, heard by a revelation what he should apply when he was in pain; and when he had done this, the pain immediately subsided.

**8. 16.** Audurus is the name of an estate, where there is a church and in it a memorial of the martyr Stephen. A certain little boy, while he was playing on the threshing-floor, was crushed by the wheel when the oxen drawing a cart went off course, and immediately he lay quivering, breathing his last. His mother snatched him up and laid him at that same memorial, and not only did he revive, but he even appeared unharmed.

**8. 17.** A certain consecrated woman on a neighboring estate, which is called Caspaliana, when she was laboring with sickness and was despaired of, had her tunic brought to that same memorial; but before it could be brought back, she died. Her parents nevertheless covered her corpse with this tunic, and, her spirit being recovered, she was made well.

**8. 18.** At Hippo, a certain Bassus, a Syrian, was praying at the memorial of the same martyr for his sick and imperiled daughter, and had brought her garment there with him, when behold, slaves ran from the house to announce to him that she was dead. But when, as he was praying, they were intercepted by his friends, these forbade them to tell him, lest he should mourn in public. When he had returned home, now resounding with the wailings of his household, and had thrown over her the garment of his daughter which he was carrying, she was restored to life.

**8. 19.** Again in the same place, among us, the son of a certain Irenaeus, a tax collector, was carried off by sickness. And when the body lay lifeless and, amid the mourners and lamenters, the funeral rites were being prepared, one of his friends suggested, among the words of others consoling him, that the body be anointed with the oil of the same martyr. It was done, and he revived.

**8. 20.** Likewise among us, Eleusinus, a man of tribunician rank, laid his infant son, lifeless from sickness, upon the memorial of the martyrs which is in his suburban property, and after the prayer which he poured out there with many tears, he lifted him up alive.

**8. 21.** What shall I do? The promise of this work, to be fulfilled, presses upon me, so that I cannot here recall everything I know; and beyond doubt very many of our people, when they read these things, will grieve that I have passed over so many, which they surely know along with me. Whom I now beg to pardon me, and let them consider how prolonged a labor it would be to do what the necessity of the work I have undertaken compels me not to do here.

For if I wished to write down—to say nothing of others—only those miracles of healings which were done through this martyr, that is, the most glorious Stephen, in the colony of Calama and in our own, very many books would have to be completed, and yet not all of them could be gathered, but only those concerning which booklets were given to be recited among the people. For we wished this to be done, when we saw that signs of divine power like those of old were frequent even in our times, and that they ought not to perish from the knowledge of many. It is not yet two years since this memorial began to be at Hippo Regius, and although—as is most certain to us—booklets were not given by many concerning the things that were marvelously done, those very ones that were given had reached nearly the number of seventy when I wrote these things. But at Calama, where that memorial both began to be earlier and booklets are given more frequently, they surpass them by an incomparable multitude.

**8. 22.** At Uzalis too, which is a colony near to Utica, we have learned that many notable things were done through the same martyr; whose memorial there was established by bishop Evodius long before it was among us. But the custom of giving booklets is not there—or rather was not; for perhaps it has now already begun to be. For when we were recently there, we exhorted Petronia, a most illustrious woman, who there was marvelously healed of a great and prolonged illness, in which all the aids of physicians had failed, that she should give a booklet to be recited among the people, with the aforesaid bishop of the place willing it, and she most obediently complied. In it she set down also something which I cannot here pass over in silence, although I am compelled to hasten toward the matters that this work presses upon me.

She said that she had been persuaded by a certain Jew to insert into a hair-band a ring, with which she should be girded beneath all her clothing against her bare body; which ring had, beneath its gem, a stone found in the kidneys of an ox. Bound with this as it were as a remedy, she was coming to the thresholds of the holy martyr. But having set out from Carthage, when she had lodged at her estate on the border of the river Bagrada, rising to complete her journey she saw that ring lying before her feet, and, marveling, she felt the hair-girdle with which it had been bound. When she had found it, just as it was, fastened with its most firm knots, she supposed that the ring had snapped and sprung out; but when that very ring too was found perfectly whole, she presumed that she had received, in a manner, from so great a miracle a pledge of future healing, and, untying that band, she threw it together with that same ring into the river.

Let those not believe this who also do not believe that the Lord Jesus was brought forth through the intact virginal parts of his mother and entered to the disciples through closed doors; but let them at least investigate this, and, if they find it true, let them believe those things.

She is a most illustrious woman, nobly born, nobly married, dwelling at Carthage; a great city and a great person do not allow those who inquire into the matter to remain in the dark.

The martyr himself, certainly, by whose intercession she was healed, believed in the Son of a virgin who remained a virgin; he believed in him who entered to the disciples through closed doors; finally—on account of which all these things are said by us—he believed in him who ascended into heaven with the flesh in which he had risen; and therefore through him such great things are done, because for that faith he laid down his life.

Even now, then, many miracles are done, the same God doing them, through whom he will and in what manner he will, who did also those things which we read; but these are neither made known in like manner, nor, that they may not slip from the mind, are they, as it were the gravel of memory, beaten in by frequent reading.

For even where there is the diligence, which has now begun to be among us, that the booklets of those who receive benefits be recited among the people, those who are present hear this once, and many are not present, so that not even those who were present retain in mind, after some days, what they heard, and scarcely anyone of them is found who reports what he heard to one whom he knows was not present.

**8. 23.** One thing was done among us, not greater than those I have told, but so clear and illustrious a miracle that I think there is no one of the people of Hippo who did not either see it or learn of it, no one who could by any means forget it.

Ten certain siblings (of whom seven were male, three female) from Caesarea in Cappadocia, not ignoble among their own citizens, by the curse of their mother—bereft by the recent death of their father, who took most bitterly the wrong done her by them—were divinely restrained with such a punishment that they were all horribly shaken with trembling of their limbs; and, not bearing the eyes of their fellow citizens in this most hideous condition, wherever it seemed good to each to go, they wandered through almost the whole Roman world.

Of these, two also came to us, a brother and sister, Paul and Palladia, already known in many other places, their misery spreading the report of them[^39]. They came about fifteen days before Easter, and daily they frequented the church and, in it, the memorial of the most glorious Stephen, praying that God might now be appeased toward them and restore their former health. Both there and wherever they went they turned the city's gaze upon themselves. Some, who had seen them elsewhere and had learned the cause of their trembling, told it to others as each could.

Easter came, and on the very Lord's day in the morning, when a numerous people was now present, and the young man, praying, was holding the railings of the holy place where the shrine was, he was suddenly thrown down and lay most like one asleep, yet not trembling, as they were wont to do even in their sleep. As those who were present were astonished, and some afraid, some grieving, when some wished to lift him up, others forbade it and said rather that the outcome should be awaited. And behold, he rose, and was not trembling, because he had been healed, and stood whole, gazing at those who gazed at him.

Who, then, held back from the praises of God? The church was filled everywhere with the voices of those crying out and giving thanks. From there they ran to me, where I was sitting, now about to come forth; one after another rushed in, every later one announcing as new what an earlier one had told; and as I rejoiced and within myself gave thanks to God, he too came in with many, was bowed at my knees, was raised to my kiss. We went forth to the people; the church was full, resounding with the voices of joy: "Thanks be to God, praise to God!" with no one silent, but crying out on every side. I greeted the people, and again they cried out the same with a more fervent voice. When silence was at last made, the solemn lessons of the divine Scriptures were read. But when it came to the place of my sermon, I said a few words, suited to the time and to the pleasantness of that gladness[^40]. For I rather allowed them, in the divine work, not to hear but to consider a certain eloquence of God. The man dined with us and diligently set forth to us the whole history of his own and his brother's and his mother's calamity. And so the following day, after the sermon was delivered, I promised that the booklet of his narration would be recited to the people on the morrow[^41]. When this was done, on the third day from the Lord's day of Easter, on the steps of the apse, from which higher place I was speaking, I had both siblings stand, while their booklet was read.

The whole people of both sexes gazed upon the one standing without unsightly motion, and the other trembling in all her limbs. And those who had not seen the young man himself discerned in his sister what divine mercy had been done in him.

For they saw what in him was to be rejoiced over, what for her was to be prayed for. Amid these things, their booklet having been recited, I directed them to depart from the sight of the people[^42], and I had begun to discuss the whole matter somewhat more diligently, when behold, as I was discussing it, other voices of new thanksgiving were heard from the memorial of the martyr. Those who were listening to me turned that way and began to run together[^43]. For she, when she came down from the steps on which she had stood, had gone to pray to the holy martyr; and as soon as she touched the railings, she collapsed likewise as if into sleep, and rose up healed.

While, then, we were inquiring what had been done, whence that joyful uproar had arisen, they came into the basilica in which we were, bringing her, healed, from the place of the martyr. Then indeed so great a cry of wonder arose from both sexes that the sound, continued with tears, seemed unable to be ended. She was led to the place where a little before she had stood trembling. They exulted that she was now like her brother, over whom they had grieved that she remained unlike him, and they discerned that their prayers for her, not yet poured out, yet their will going before them, had been so quickly heard. They exulted in the praise of God with a voice without words, with so great a sound that our ears could scarcely bear it. What was in the hearts of those exulting except the faith of Christ, for which the blood of Stephen was shed?

## The miracles bear witness to the faith concerning the resurrection of the flesh.

**9.** To what, if not to this faith, do those miracles bear witness, in which it is preached that Christ rose in the flesh and ascended into heaven with the flesh? For the martyrs themselves too were martyrs of this faith—that is, witnesses of this faith; bearing witness to this faith, they endured a most hostile and most cruel world, and conquered it not by fighting back but by dying; for this faith they died, who can obtain these things from the Lord, for whose name they were killed; for this faith their wondrous patience came first, that in these miracles such great power might follow.

For if the resurrection of the flesh unto eternity either did not come first in Christ or is not to come, as it is foretold by Christ or as it was foretold by the Prophets, by whom Christ was foretold: why can even dead men do such great things—men who were killed for that faith by which this resurrection is preached?

For whether God himself, through himself, in the wondrous way in which, eternal, he works temporal things, or whether through his ministers he does these things;

and whether those very things which he does through ministers he does, some of them, even through the spirits of the martyrs, as through men still set in the body,

or whether he works all these things through the angels, whom he commands invisibly, incorporeally, immutably, so that what are said to be done through the martyrs are done by their praying and obtaining only, not also by their working; or some in these ways, others in those, which can in no way be comprehended by mortals: yet they bear witness to that faith in which the resurrection of the flesh unto eternity is preached.

**10.** Here perhaps they will say that their gods too did certain marvelous things. Well, if they now begin to compare their gods with our dead men. Or will they say that they too have gods made from dead men, such as Hercules, such as Romulus, such as many others, whom they suppose to have been received into the number of the gods? But for us the martyrs are not gods, because we know that there is one and the same God, both ours and the martyrs'.

Nor yet are the miracles which are reported to have been done through those gods' temples in any way to be compared with the miracles which are done through the memorials of our martyrs. But if any seem similar, then, just as the magicians of Pharaoh were conquered by Moses[^44], so their gods have been conquered by our martyrs. Now the demons did those things with that pride of impure arrogance by which they wished to be gods; but the martyrs—or rather God, either with them cooperating or praying—do these things, so that that faith may advance by which we believe that they are not our gods, but have, with us, one God.

Finally, those people both built temples to such gods of theirs and set up altars, and appointed priests and offered sacrifices; but to our martyrs we build, not temples as to gods, but memorials as to dead men whose spirits live with God; nor do we there erect altars on which we sacrifice to the martyrs, but to the one God, both the martyrs' and ours;

at which sacrifice, as men of God who conquered the world in his confession, they are named in their own place and order, yet are not invoked by the priest who sacrifices. For he sacrifices to God, not to them, although he sacrifices in their memorial, because he is a priest of God, not of them. The sacrifice itself, indeed, is the body of Christ, which is not offered to them, because they themselves too are this.

To whom, then, is greater belief to be given as workers of miracles? To those who wish themselves to be held as gods by those for whom they do them, or to those who do whatever marvelous thing they do so that one may believe in God, who is also Christ? To those who wished even their crimes to be their sacred rites, or to those who do not wish even their own praises to be their sacred rites, but wish the whole of that for which they are truly praised to advance the glory of him in whom they are praised? For in the Lord their souls are praised[^45].

Let us, then, believe those who both speak truths and do marvels. For by speaking truths they suffered, that they might be able to do marvels. Among those truths the chief is that Christ rose from the dead and first showed the immortality of resurrection in his own flesh, which he promised would be present to us either at the beginning of the new age or at the end of this one.

## The learned raise objections from the weights of the elements.

**11. 1.** Against this great gift of God these reasoners, *whose thoughts the Lord knows, that they are vain*[^46], argue from the weights of the elements; namely because, with Plato as their teacher, they have learned that the two greatest and outermost bodies of the world are coupled and joined by two intermediate ones, that is, by air and water[^47]. And for this reason, they say, since from here upward the earth is first, water second above the earth, air third above the water, heaven fourth above the air, an earthly body cannot be in heaven; for the individual elements are balanced by their proper momenta, so as to keep their own order[^48].

See with what sort of arguments human weakness, which vanity possesses, contradicts the omnipotence of God. What, then, are so many earthly bodies doing in the air, when the air is third from the earth? Unless perhaps he who granted to the earthly bodies of birds, through the lightness of plumage and feathers, that they should be borne in the air, will be unable to grant to the bodies of men, made immortal, the power by which they may be able even to dwell in the highest heaven. The earthly animals themselves too, which cannot fly, among which men are also, ought, just as fish, which are the animals of the waters, live under the water, likewise to have lived under the earth.

Why, then, does an earthly animal draw this life not even from the second element, that is, from the waters, but from the third element? Why, when it belongs to the earth, if it be compelled to live in the second element, which is above the earth, is it at once suffocated, and, in order to live, lives in the third? Or is this order of the elements mistaken—or rather does it fail not in the nature of things, but in these men's arguments? I forbear to say what I already said in the thirteenth book[^49], how many heavy earthly bodies there are, such as lead, and yet they receive from a craftsman a form by which they are able to float upon the water; and is the omnipotent craftsman gainsaid, that the human body should receive a quality by which it can be borne into heaven and be in heaven?

**11. 2.** Now indeed, against what I have already said above, even considering and handling this order of the elements in which they trust, they find nothing at all to say. For from here upward the earth is so first, water second, third air, fourth heaven, that above all things is the nature of the soul. For Aristotle said it was a fifth body[^50], and Plato that it was none. If it were a fifth, surely it would be higher than the rest; but since it is none, much more does it surpass all things.

In an earthly body, then, what is it doing? In this mass, what does it do, more subtle than all things? In this weight, what does it do, lighter than all things? In this sluggishness, what does it do, swifter than all things? Will it then, through the merit of so excellent a nature, be unable to be brought about that its body be lifted into heaven; and, while the nature of earthly bodies can now press souls downward, will souls some day be unable to lift earthly bodies upward?

**11. 3.** Now if we come to their miracles, which they oppose to our martyrs as having been done by their gods, will not these too be found to work for us and to profit us altogether? For among the great miracles of their gods this surely is a great one which Varro records: that a Vestal virgin, when she was in danger over a charge of unchastity by a false suspicion, filled a sieve with water from the Tiber and carried it to her judges with no part of it trickling out[^51]. Who held the weight of the water above the sieve? Who, with so many openings gaping, permitted nothing of it to fall thence to the ground?

They will answer: "Some god or some demon." If a god, is he then greater than the God who made this world? If a demon, is he then more powerful than the angel who serves God, by whom the world was made? If, then, a lesser god, or an angel, or a demon, could so suspend the weight of the watery element that the nature of the waters seemed to have been changed, will the omnipotent God, then, who created all these elements themselves, be unable to take away its heavy weight from an earthly body, so that the vivified body may dwell in the same element in which the vivifying spirit shall have willed?

**11. 4.** Furthermore, since they place air in the middle between fire above and water below, what is it that we often find it between water and water, and between water and earth? For what do they wish the watery clouds to be, between which and the seas air is found in the middle? By what weight, I ask, and order of the elements is it brought about that the most violent and most swollen torrents, before they run beneath the air upon the earth, hang above the air in the clouds? Why, finally, is air in the middle between the heights of heaven and the bare surface of the lands, wherever the globe extends, if its place is set between heaven and the waters, as that of the waters is set between it and the lands?

**11. 5.** Finally, if the order of the elements is so arranged that, according to Plato, by two intermediates, that is, air and water, the two extremes, that is, fire and earth, are joined, and that one holds the place of the topmost heaven, while this one holds the lowest, as it were the foundation of the world, and therefore earth cannot be in heaven[^52]—why is fire itself on the earth? For according to this reasoning these two elements ought so to be in their proper places, lowest and highest, earth and fire, that, just as they refuse that what is of the lowest can be in the highest, so neither could what is of the highest be in the lowest. As, then, they think there is, or will be, no particle of earth in heaven, so we ought to see no particle of fire on the earth.

But now it is not only on the lands but even beneath the lands, in such a way that the peaks of mountains belch it forth, besides that, in the uses of men, we see both that fire is on the earth and that it arises from the earth; since indeed it arises both from wood and from stones, which are bodies without doubt earthly. But that fire, they say, is tranquil, pure, harmless, everlasting; this one, however, is turbid, smoky, corruptible, and a corrupter. And yet it does not corrupt the mountains in which it perpetually seethes, nor the caverns of the lands.

But be it so, let this one be unlike that, so as to suit earthly dwellings: why, then, do they refuse that we believe that the nature of earthly bodies, made some day incorruptible, will be suited to heaven, just as now corruptible fire is suited to these lands? They bring forward nothing, therefore, from the weights and order of the elements, by which to lay down a rule against the omnipotent God, that he should not make our bodies such that they can even dwell in heaven.

## What equality will be had . . .

**12. 1.** But they are accustomed to inquire most scrupulously and, by so inquiring, to mock the faith by which we believe that the flesh will rise again: whether aborted fetuses will rise; and, since the Lord says, *truly, I say to you, not a hair of your head will perish*[^53], whether stature and strength will be equal for all, or whether there will be diverse sizes of bodies.

For if there will be an equality of bodies, whence will those aborted ones have what they did not have here in bodily mass, if they too will rise? Or if they will not rise, because they were not even born but cast out, they raise the same question about little children: whence will there accrue to them the measure of body which we now see was lacking, when they die at that age. For we will not say that those will not rise who are capable not only of generation but also of regeneration.

Then they ask what measure that equality itself will have. For if all will be as great and as tall as those were here who were largest and tallest, they ask, not only about little children but about very many, whence will accrue to them what was lacking here, if each receives this which he had here;

but if, as the Apostle says, we shall all meet *at the measure of the age of the fullness of Christ*[^54], and that other saying, *whom he predestined to be conformed to the image of his Son*[^55], is to be understood in such a way that the stature and measure of the body of Christ will be that of all the human bodies of those who will be in his kingdom: "for many," they say, "much must be taken away from the greatness and tallness of the body; and where then will be, *not a hair of your head will perish*, if from the very size of the body so much will perish?" Although even about the hairs themselves it can be inquired whether all that fell to those who shave it returns. And if it is to return, who would not shudder at that deformity?

For this seems necessarily to follow about the nails too, that there return as much as the trimming of the body cut off. And where will be the beauty, which surely ought to be greater in that immortality now than it could be in this corruption? But if it will not return, then it will perish. How, then, they say, will not a hair of the head perish?

They argue similarly also about thinness or fatness. For if all will be equal, surely some will not be thin and others fat. Therefore to some something will accrue, from others something will be diminished; and for this reason not what was had is to be received back, but somewhere there is to be added what was not, and somewhere there is to be lost what was.

**12. 2.** About the very corruptions and dissolutions of dead bodies, too—since one part turns to dust, another is breathed out into the air, some are consumed by beasts, some by fire, while some perish by shipwreck or by waters of any kind in such a way that rottenness dissolves their flesh into liquid—they are not a little disturbed, and do not believe that all these things can be gathered back into flesh and made whole again. They also pursue various foulnesses and defects, whether they befall or are born with one, where they recall, with horror and mockery, monstrous births too, and ask what the resurrection of each deformity will be. For if we say that nothing of the kind will return into the body of a man, they presume that they will confute our answer about the marks of the wounds with which we preach that the Lord Christ rose.

But among all these things, that most difficult question is proposed: into whose flesh that flesh will return by which the body of another, eating human inwards under the compulsion of famine, is nourished. For it has been converted into the flesh of him who lived on such food, and has supplied the losses which thinness had displayed. Whether, then, it returns to that man whose flesh it first was, or rather to that man whose it afterward became, they inquire to this end: to mock the faith of the resurrection and so, regarding the human soul, either to promise it, alternating, as Plato does, true unhappinesses and false blessednesses, or to grant that after many revolutions likewise through diverse bodies it nevertheless at some time, as Porphyry holds, ends its miseries and never returns to them—yet not by having an immortal body, but by fleeing every body[^56].

## What aborted fetuses will be . . .

**13.** To these things, then, which from their opposing side seem to me, as I marshal them, to be set against us, let me answer, with the mercy of God bringing aid to my efforts.

Aborted fetuses, which, having already lived in the womb, died there—that they will rise again, I dare neither to affirm nor to deny; although I do not see how the resurrection of the dead does not pertain to them, if they are not removed from the number of the dead. For either not all the dead will rise, and there will be some human souls without bodies in eternity, which carried human bodies, though within their mother's inwards; or, if all human souls will receive back their rising bodies, which they had wherever, living and dying, they left them, I do not find how I may say that whatever dead—even in their mothers' wombs—do not pertain to the resurrection of the dead. But whichever of these anyone may hold concerning them, what we shall say about infants already born is to be understood about these too, if they shall rise.

**14.** What, then, shall we say about infants, except that they will rise not in that smallness of body in which they died, but what was to come to them more slowly in time, this they will receive by that wondrous and most swift work of God?

For in the saying of the Lord, where he says, *not a hair of your head will perish*[^57], it is said that what was will not be lacking, but it is not denied that what was lacking will be present. Now what was lacking to the dead infant was the perfected size of its body; for even to a perfect infant there is of course lacking the perfection of bodily greatness, which, when it has come, the stature can no longer be taller. This measure of perfection all have in such a way that they are conceived and born with it; but they have it in the principle, not in the mass; just as all the members themselves are already latently in the seed, while even in those already born some are still lacking, such as teeth and the like. In this principle, implanted in the corporeal matter of each, what is not yet—or rather what lies hidden, but will be, or rather will appear, with the passing of time—seems in a manner already, so to speak, threaded in. In this principle, then, an infant is already short or tall, who is going to be short or tall.

According to this principle, surely, in the resurrection of the body we do not fear losses of the body, because, even if there were to be an equality of all, so that all reached the size of giants, lest those who were largest should have something less in stature, which would perish for them against the sentence of Christ, who said that not even a hair of the head would perish—for the Creator, of course, who created all things from nothing, how could there be lacking to him whence to add what the wondrous craftsman knew was to be added?

**15.** But surely Christ rose in that measure of body in which he died, and it is not lawful to say that, when the time of the resurrection of all has come, there will accrue to his body that greatness which it did not have when he appeared to the disciples in that form in which he was known to them, so that he could be made equal to the tallest. But if we say that even the larger bodies of any are to be reduced to the measure of the Lord's body, very much will perish from the bodies of many, since he himself promised that not even a hair would perish.

It remains, then, that each receive back his own measure, which he either had in youth, even if he died old, or would have had, if he died before; and that we either understand what the Apostle mentioned, *about the measure of the age of the fullness of Christ*[^58], to have been said with reference to something else—that is, so that, with the perfection of all the members accruing to that head among the Christian peoples, the measure of his age may be completed—or, if this was said about the resurrection of bodies, that we so take it to be said that the bodies of the dead rise neither below nor beyond the form of youth, but in the age and strength up to which we know Christ to have come here (for even the most learned men of this age too have defined youth to be around thirty years; and when this has been bounded by its proper span, from then on a man inclines toward the losses of a heavier and senile age); and that therefore it was said not "at the measure of the body" or "at the measure of the stature," but *at the measure of the age of the fullness of Christ*.

**16.** That too which he says, that the predestined are to be made *conformed to the image of the Son of God*[^59], can also be understood according to the inner man (whence he says to us in another place, *do not be conformed to this age, but be reformed in the newness of your mind*[^60]; where, then, we are reformed, lest we be conformed to this age, there we are conformed to the Son of God). It can also be taken thus: that, as he was made conformed to us in mortality, so we are made conformed to him in immortality; which indeed pertains also to the very resurrection of bodies. But if in these words too we are being advised in what form the bodies are to rise, then, just as that *measure*, so too this conformation is to be understood not of size but of age.

All, therefore, will rise as great in body as they either were or were going to be in youthful age; although it will be no hindrance even if the form of body be infantile or senile, where no infirmity of either mind or of the body itself will remain. Hence, even if anyone contends that each will rise in that measure of body in which he died, there is no need to fight with him in laborious contradiction.

## There will be women in immortality.

**17.** Some, on account of what was said—*until we all meet in the unity of faith, in the perfect man, at the measure of the age of the fullness of Christ*[^61]; and, *conformed to the image of the Son of God*[^62]—believe that women will not rise in the female sex, but say that all will rise in the male, since God made only the man from clay, the woman from the man. But to me those seem to be wiser who do not doubt that both sexes will rise. For there will be no lust there, which is the cause of shame. For before they sinned, they were naked, and the man and the woman were not ashamed. From those bodies, then, defects will be taken away, but the nature will be preserved.

But the female sex is not a defect, but a nature, which then indeed will be free both from intercourse and from childbearing; yet there will be female members, not accommodated to the old use, but to a new beauty, by which the concupiscence of the beholder is not enticed—for there will be none—but the wisdom and clemency of God is praised, who both made what was not and freed from corruption what he made.

For just as in the beginning of the human race the woman was made from the rib drawn from the side of the sleeping man[^63], so by such a deed Christ and the Church had to be prophesied even then. For that sleep of the man was the death of Christ[^64], whose side, as he hung lifeless on the cross, was pierced by the lance, and from it flowed blood and water[^65]; which we know to be the sacraments by which the Church is built. For Scripture used this very word too, where we read not "formed" or "fashioned," but, *he built her into a woman*[^66]; whence the Apostle too speaks of the building up of the body of Christ, which is the Church[^67].

The woman, then, is a creation of God, as is the man; but, that she should be made from the man, unity was commended; and, that she should be made in that manner, Christ, as has been said, and the Church were figured. He, then, who instituted both sexes will restore both.

Finally, Jesus himself, questioned by the Sadducees, who denied the resurrection, whose wife, of the seven brothers, she would be, whom each had had, while each of them wished to raise up seed for the deceased, as the law had prescribed, said: *you err, not knowing the Scriptures, nor the power of God*[^68]; and when there was room for him to say, "for she about whom you ask me will herself be a man, not a woman," he did not say this, but said, *for in the resurrection they neither marry nor are given in marriage, but are like the angels of God in heaven*[^69]; equal, of course, to the angels in immortality and happiness, not in flesh; nor in the resurrection, which the angels did not need, since they could not even die.

The Lord, then, denied that there will be marriages in the resurrection, not women, and he denied it where such a question was being raised that he would have dissolved it with quicker ease by denying the female sex, if he foreknew that it would not be there; indeed he even confirmed that it would be, by saying, *they will not marry*, which pertains to women, *nor be given in marriage*, which pertains to men.

There will be, then, those who are wont here either to marry or to take wives; but there they will not do so.

## We shall be conformed because we are the body of Christ.

**18.** Accordingly, as to what the Apostle says, that we shall all meet in the perfect man, we ought to consider the context of the whole passage itself, which is as follows: *he who descended*, he says, *is the same also who ascended above all the heavens, that he might fill all things. And he himself gave some as Apostles, some as Prophets, some as Evangelists, some as pastors and teachers, for the perfecting of the saints, for the work of ministry, for the building up of the body of Christ, until we all meet in the unity of faith and the knowledge of the Son of God, in the perfect man, at the measure of the age of the fullness of Christ; that we may no longer be little children tossed about and carried around by every wind of doctrine, in the deceit of men, in craftiness toward the contriving of error, but, doing the truth in love, may grow up in him in all things, who is the head, Christ; from whom the whole body, knit together and compacted through every joint of supply, according to the working in the measure of each part, makes the increase of the body for the building up of itself in love*[^70].

Behold who is the *perfect man*: the head and the body, which consists of all the members, which will be completed in their own time, yet daily accrue to the same body, while the Church is built up, to which it is said, *but you are the body of Christ and its members*[^71], and elsewhere, *for his body, which is the Church*[^72], and likewise elsewhere, *we who are many are one bread, one body*[^73].

Concerning the building up of which body it was also said here: *for the perfecting of the saints, for the work of ministry, for the building up of the body of Christ*[^74]; and then there was added what we are now treating: *until we all meet in the unity of faith and the knowledge of the Son of God, in the perfect man, at the measure of the age of the fullness of Christ*, and the rest[^75]; until he showed in what body that measure was to be understood, saying: *may we grow up in him in all things, who is the head, Christ; from whom the whole body, knit together and compacted through every joint of supply, according to the working in the measure of each part*[^76]. Just as, then, there is a measure of each part, so of the whole body, which consists of all its parts, there is surely a measure of fullness, of which it was said, *at the measure of the age of the fullness of Christ*[^77]. Which fullness he mentioned also in that place where he says of Christ: *and him he gave as head over all things to the Church, which is his body, the fullness of him who is filled, all in all*[^78].

But if this were to be referred to the form of the resurrection, in which each one will be, what would hinder us from understanding, under the name "man," the woman too, so that we take "man" as put for "human being"? Just as in what is said, *blessed is the man who fears the Lord*[^79], surely there are there also women who fear the Lord.

## What the members of the bodies will be . . .

**19. 1.** What shall I now answer about the hairs and nails? For once it is understood that nothing will perish from the body in such a way that there be anything deformed in the body, it is at the same time understood that those things which would have made a deformed disproportion will accrue to the mass itself, not to the places where the form of the members would be disfigured.

As if a vessel were made from clay, which, reduced again into the same clay, were made again wholly from the whole, it would not be necessary that that part of the clay which had been in the handle return to the handle, or that what had made the bottom should itself again make the bottom,

provided only that the whole returned into the whole—that is, that all that clay went back into the whole vessel with no part of itself lost.

Wherefore, if the hairs so often cut and the nails so often trimmed would return deformingly to their own places, they will not return; nor yet will they perish for anyone rising again, because they will be turned into the same flesh, to hold whatever place of the body they may there, by the mutability of matter, with the fitness of the parts preserved.

Although what the Lord says, *not a hair of your head will perish*[^80], can much more fittingly be understood as said not of the length but of the number of hairs; whence he says elsewhere, *the hairs of your head are all numbered*[^81]. Nor would I say this because I suppose that anything will perish from any body which was naturally in it; but that what was born deformed (surely for no other reason than that hence too it might be shown how penal this condition of mortals is) will so return that, the integrity of the substance being preserved, the deformity perishes.

For if a human craftsman can melt down a statue which he had made deformed for some reason, and render it most beautiful, in such a way that nothing of the substance, but only the deformity, perishes—and if anything in that earlier figure projected unbecomingly and did not match in the parity of the parts, he can, not amputate and separate it from the whole from which he had made it, but so sprinkle and mix it throughout the whole that it neither makes foulness nor diminishes the quantity—what is to be thought of the omnipotent Craftsman?

Will he, then, be unable so to take away and do away with whatever deformities of human bodies—not only the usual but even the rare and monstrous, which suit this wretched life but are abhorrent to that future happiness of the saints—that whatever superfluities of the corporeal substance produce them, though natural, yet unbecoming, are removed without any diminution of it?

**19. 2.** And for this reason the thin and the fat need not fear that there too they will be such as, if they could, they would not have wished to be even here. For all bodily beauty is a fitness of the parts with a certain sweetness of color. But where there is no fitness of the parts, something offends either because it is crooked, or because it is too little, or because it is too much.

Accordingly there will be no deformity, which a lack of fitness of the parts produces, where both what is crooked will be corrected, and what is less than is becoming will be supplied from whence the Creator knows, and what is more than is becoming will be taken away, the integrity of the matter being preserved. But how great will be the sweetness of color, where *the righteous will shine like the sun in the kingdom of their Father*[^82]!

Which brightness, in the body of Christ, when he rose, is to be believed to have been hidden from the eyes of the disciples rather than lacking. For human and weak sight would not have borne it, when he had to be regarded by his own in such a way that he could be recognized. To which it also pertained that he showed the scars of his wounds to those who touched him, and that he even took food and drink[^83], not from need of nourishment, but by that power by which he was able to do this too.

Now when something is not seen, though it is present, by those by whom other things, equally present, are seen—as we say that brightness was present, unseen, to those by whom other things were seen—it is called in Greek aorasia, which our translators, unable to express it in Latin, in the book of Genesis rendered *blindness*. For the Sodomites suffered this when they were seeking the door of the righteous man and could not find it[^84]. If this had been a blindness by which it comes about that nothing can be seen, they would have sought, not a door by which to enter, but guides of the way by whom they might be led away from there.

**19. 3.** But in some way I know not, we are so affected by love of the blessed martyrs that we would wish to see in that kingdom, on their bodies, the scars of the wounds which they endured for the name of Christ; and perhaps we shall see them. For there will be in them not deformity but dignity, and a certain beauty—though in the body, yet not of the body, but of virtue—will shine forth. Nor yet, on this account, if any members were amputated and taken away from the martyrs, will they be without those very members in the resurrection of the dead, to whom it was said, *not a hair of your head will perish*[^85]. But if it will be becoming in that new age that the marks of glorious wounds be discerned in that immortal flesh, there, where the members were struck or cut to be severed, scars will appear—yet with those same members restored, not lost. Although, therefore, all the defects which befell the body will then not be, yet they are not to be reckoned or called defects, the marks of virtue.

**20. 1.** But far be it that, for raising up the bodies and restoring them to life, the omnipotence of the Creator should be unable to recall all that either beasts or fire consumed, or that collapsed into dust or ash, or was dissolved into liquid, or was breathed out into the air. Far be it that any recess and secret of nature should so receive anything withdrawn from our senses that it either lies hidden from the knowledge or escapes the power of the Creator of all things. Cicero, certainly—so great an authority among them—wishing to define God as best he could, says: *he is a certain mind, loosed and free, secret from all mortal admixture, perceiving all things and moving them, and himself endowed with everlasting motion*[^86]. This he found in the doctrines of the great philosophers. To speak, then, according to them: how does anything either lie hidden from one who perceives all things, or irrevocably escape one who moves all things?

**20. 2.** Whence now also that question is to be solved which seems more difficult than the rest, where it is asked, when the flesh of a dead man becomes the flesh of another living one, to which of them rather it is to be restored in the resurrection. For if anyone, worn out by famine and compelled, feeds on the corpses of men—an evil which both old history attests to have happened at times and the unhappy experiences of our times have taught—will anyone contend, by truthful reasoning, that the whole was digested through the lowest passages, and that nothing of it was changed and converted into his flesh, when the very thinness, which was and is not, sufficiently indicates what losses were supplied by those foods?

And so I have already set forth a little before certain things which ought to avail for solving this knot too. For whatever flesh famine consumed was surely breathed out into the air, whence we said that the omnipotent God can recall what escaped. That flesh, then, will be restored to the man in whom human flesh first began to be. For by that other one it is to be reckoned as taken, as it were, on loan; which, like a debt, must be paid back to him from whom it was taken.

But his own flesh will be restored to him whom famine had emptied, by him who can recall even what was breathed out. Although, even if it had perished in every way and no matter of it had remained in any hiding place of nature, the Omnipotent would repair it from wherever he willed. But on account of the sentence of Truth, by which it was said, *not a hair of your head will perish*[^87], it is absurd that we should suppose, when a man's hair cannot perish, that so much flesh, devoured and consumed by famine, could perish.

**20. 3.** All these things being considered and handled according to our little measure, this sum is reached: that in the resurrection of the flesh unto eternity the greatness of the bodies has those measures which the principle of youth, implanted in each body to be perfected or already perfected, had—the fitting comeliness being preserved also in the measures of all the members.

And that this comeliness may be preserved, if something is taken away from some unbecoming bigness set in some part, so that it may be spread throughout the whole, so that it neither perishes nor is the fitness of the parts kept everywhere lost: it is not absurd that we should believe that something can be added thence even to the stature of the body, when there is distributed to all the parts, that they may guard their comeliness, that which, if it were enormously in one, would surely not be becoming.

Or if it is contended that each will rise in that stature of body in which he died, it is not to be resisted contentiously; only let there be absent all deformity, all infirmity, all sluggishness, and all corruption, and whatever else does not become that kingdom in which the sons of the resurrection and of the promise will be equal to the angels of God[^88], if not in body, not in age, at least in happiness.

**21.** There will be restored, then, whatever perished from living bodies, or after death from corpses, and, together with what remained in the tombs, it will rise, changed from the oldness of the animal body into the newness of the spiritual body, clothed with incorruption and immortality[^89]. But even if, by some grave accident or by the savagery of enemies, the whole be utterly ground into dust and, dispersed into the air or into the waters, be allowed, as far as can be, to be nowhere at all: in no way will it be able to be withdrawn from the omnipotence of the Creator, but not a hair of its head will perish. The flesh, then, will be subject to the spirit—spiritual flesh, yet flesh, not spirit; just as the spirit itself was subject to the flesh—carnal spirit, yet spirit, not flesh.

Of which thing we have an experience in the deformity of our punishment. For those to whom the Apostle said, *I could not speak to you as to spiritual men, but as to carnal*[^90], were carnal not according to the flesh but surely according to the spirit; and a man is called spiritual in this life in such a way that he is nevertheless still carnal in body and sees another law in his members warring against the law of his mind[^91]; but he will be spiritual even in body, when the same flesh will so have risen that what is written may come to pass: *it is sown an animal body, it will rise a spiritual body*[^92]. But what, and how great, the grace of the spiritual body may be, since it has not yet come into experience, I fear lest every utterance that is brought forth about it be rash.

## [The gift of the future life]

Nevertheless, since the joy of our hope is not to be kept silent on account of the praise of God, and from the innermost marrow of burning holy love it was said, *Lord, I have loved the beauty of your house*[^93]: let us, with his help, conjecture, as we can, from his gifts, which in this most toilsome life he bestows on the good and the bad, how great that thing is which, not yet having experienced, we are surely unable worthily to express. For I pass over the time when he made man upright; I pass over that life of the two spouses, happy in the fruitfulness of paradise[^94], since it was so brief that it did not even reach the awareness of their offspring: in this life, which we know, in which we still are—whose temptations, or rather which whole temptation, as long as we are in it, however much we make progress, we do not cease to endure—who will be able to set forth what the signs of God's goodness toward the human race may be?

## What faults infect this life.

**22. 1.** For, as far as pertains to the first origin, that the whole progeny of mortals was damned, this very life—if it is to be called life—full of so many and so great evils, attests. For what else does that horrendous depth of ignorance indicate, from which all error arises, which has taken up all the sons of Adam in a certain dark bosom, so that a man cannot be freed from it without toil, grief, and fear? What does the very love of so many vain and harmful things indicate, and from this the biting cares, perturbations, griefs, dreads, mad joys, discords, lawsuits, wars, plots, fits of anger, enmities, deceit, flattery, fraud, theft, robbery, perfidy, pride, ambition, envy, murders, parricides, cruelty, savagery, baseness, licentiousness, wantonness, shamelessness, unchastity, fornications, adulteries, incests, and so many defilements and impurities against the nature of both sexes, which are shameful even to mention, sacrileges, heresies, blasphemies, perjuries, oppressions of the innocent, calumnies, deceptions, prevarications, false testimonies, unjust judgments, violences, robberies, and whatever of such evils does not come to mind and yet does not depart from this life of men? But these are the evils of men, coming nevertheless from that root of error and perverse love with which every son of Adam is born. For who is ignorant with how great an ignorance of the truth, which is already manifest in infants, and with how great an abundance of vain desire, which begins to appear in boys, a man comes into this life, so that, if he be allowed to live as he wishes and to do whatever he wishes, he arrives at these crimes and disgraceful acts which I have mentioned and which I could not mention, either all or many of them?

**22. 2.** But, with the divine governance not in every way deserting the damned, and God not withholding his mercies in his anger[^95], in the very senses of the human race prohibition and instruction keep watch against those shadows with which we are born, and are set against these onslaughts—yet themselves also full of toils and griefs. For what do the manifold dreads mean which are applied to checking the vanities of little children[^96]? What do the tutors mean, what the masters, what the ferules, what the straps, what the rods, what that discipline by which Holy Scripture says the sides of a beloved son are to be beaten, lest he grow up untamed and, when now hard, can be tamed only with difficulty, or perhaps cannot be tamed at all? What is brought about by all these punishments, except that ignorance is conquered and depraved desire is bridled, with which evils we come into this world? For what is it that we remember with toil, forget without toil; learn with toil, are ignorant without toil; are diligent with toil, are slothful without toil? Does it not hence appear toward what, as by its own weight, vicious nature is prone and inclined, and how great a help it needs in order to be freed from it? Idleness, sloth, laziness, negligence are surely faults by which toil is shunned, since toil itself, even that which is useful, is a punishment.

**22. 3.** But besides the childhood punishments, without which what their elders—who scarcely will anything usefully—wish them to learn cannot be learned, with how many and how great punishments the human race is harassed, which pertain not to the malice and wickedness of the unjust but to the common condition and misery—who can set it out in any discourse? Who can comprehend it in any thought? How great is the fear, how great the calamity, from bereavements and grief, from losses and condemnations, from the deceptions and lies of men, from false suspicions, from all the violent crimes and wickednesses of others! Since from them there often befall both plundering and captivity, and chains and prisons, and exiles and tortures, and the amputation of members and the deprivation of the senses, and the oppression of the body to satisfy the obscene lust of the oppressor, and many other horrible things.

What of the countless accidents which are dreaded for the body from without—heats and colds, storms, rains, floods, lightning-flash, thunder, hail, thunderbolt, the movements and gapings of the earth, the crushing of collapsing buildings, the stumblings and panic or even malice of beasts of burden, so many poisons of shrubs, waters, airs, beasts, the merely troublesome or even deadly bites of wild animals, the rabies that comes from a rabid dog, so that even a fawning beast, friendly to its own master, is sometimes more vehemently and bitterly feared than lions and serpents, and makes the man whom it has perhaps tainted so rabid, by its pestilent contagion, that he is dreaded by his parents, spouse, and children worse than any beast? What evils do voyagers suffer! What those traversing journeys by land! Who walks anywhere not subject to unexpected accidents? A certain man, returning home from the forum, fell on his own sound feet, broke his foot, and from that wound ended this life. What seems safer than one sitting? The priest Eli fell from the chair on which he was sitting, and died[^97]. What and how great accidents to the fruits of their fields do farmers—indeed all men—dread from heaven and earth or from destructive animals! Yet they are wont to be secure about the grain at last gathered and stored. But for certain men, as we know, an unforeseen flood cast forth from the granaries and carried away the finest crop of grain, with the men fleeing.

Who trusts in his own innocence against the thousand-formed onslaughts of demons? Since indeed, lest anyone should trust in it, they sometimes so vex even baptized little children, than whom surely nothing is more innocent, that in them especially, with God permitting these things, the calamity of this life that is to be wept over and the happiness of the other that is to be desired are shown. But now, from the body itself there arise so many evils of diseases that not all are comprehended even in the books of physicians; and in very many of them, indeed almost all, even the very aids and medicaments are torments, so that men are rescued from the ruin of their punishments by a punishing remedy.

Has not a monstrous burning thirst brought men to this, that they drank even human urine, or even their own? Has not famine brought them to this, that they could not abstain from the flesh of men, and consumed men found dead—no, men killed for this very purpose—and not just any strangers, but even mothers their own children, with an incredible cruelty which raging hunger produced? Finally, who can set forth in words how unquiet sleep itself, which has properly received the name of rest, often is by the visions of dreams, and with how great terrors—though of false things—which it so presents and in a manner expresses that we cannot distinguish them from true ones, it disturbs the wretched soul and the senses? By which falsehood of visions even the waking are more wretchedly harassed in certain diseases and poisonings; although by manifold variety of deceit malignant demons sometimes deceive even healthy men by such visions, so that, even if they cannot draw them through these to their own side, they nevertheless mock their senses by the mere appetite for somehow persuading them of falsehood.

**22. 4.** From this so wretched a life, as from a kind of hell, nothing frees us but the grace of the Savior Christ, our God and Lord (for this is the very name "Jesus"; it is interpreted "Savior"[^98]), especially lest, after this one, a more wretched and everlasting life—or rather not a life, but a death—should receive us.

For although in this life there are, through holy things and holy persons, great solaces of healings, yet for this reason even those very benefits are not always granted to those who ask, lest religion be sought on account of them, which is rather to be sought on account of the other life, where there will be no evils at all; and to this end grace helps the better sort in these evils, that the more faithful the heart, the more bravely they may be borne.

To this end the learned of this age say that philosophy too is of profit, which, Tully says, the gods gave true to a certain few; nor, he says, was a greater gift either given to men by them or could any be given[^99]. So far were even those against whom we are arguing compelled, in some way, to confess divine grace in the having of philosophy—not just any, but true philosophy. Furthermore, if to a few it was divinely given to have the sole help of true philosophy against the miseries of this life, this too sufficiently shows that the human race is condemned to pay the penalties of miseries. But as this, by their own admission, is no greater divine gift, so it is to be believed to be given by no god except by him than whom even those who worship many gods say there is none greater.

## Here there will never be tranquillity.

**23.** But besides these evils of this life, common to the good and the bad, the righteous have in it also certain labors of their own, by which they war against the vices and are engaged in the temptations and perils of such battles. For sometimes more keenly, sometimes more remissly, yet the flesh does not cease to lust against the spirit and the spirit against the flesh[^100], so that we do not do the things we wish—consuming all evil concupiscence—but subdue it to ourselves, as far as, divinely helped, we can, by not consenting to it,

keeping watch with continual vigilance, lest a verisimilar opinion deceive us, lest a crafty word mislead us, lest the darkness of some error spread itself over us, lest what is good be believed to be evil or what is evil good, lest fear call us back from the things that are to be done, lest desire hurl us into the things that are not to be done, lest the sun go down upon our anger[^101], lest enmities provoke us to repay evil for evil[^102], lest dishonorable or immoderate sadness swallow us up, lest an ungrateful mind bring on torpor in bestowing benefits, lest a good conscience be wearied by slanderous rumors, lest a rash suspicion of ours about another deceive us, lest another's false suspicion about us break us, lest sin reign in our mortal body to obey its desires, lest our members be presented as weapons of iniquity to sin[^103], lest the eye follow concupiscence, lest the desire of vengeance conquer, lest in what wrongly delights either the sight or the thought linger, lest a wicked or unseemly word be willingly heard, lest what is not lawful be done even if it pleases, lest in this war most full of labors and perils victory be either hoped to be won by our own strength or attributed, once won, to our own strength, but to the grace of him of whom the Apostle says, *but thanks be to God, who gives us the victory through our Lord Jesus Christ*[^104]; who also says in another place, *in all these things we more than conquer through him who loved us*[^105]. Yet let us know that, with however great virtue of fighting we resist the vices, or even overcome and subjugate the vices, as long as we are in this body, there cannot be lacking to us whence we may say to God, *forgive us our debts*[^106].

But in that kingdom, where we shall be forever with immortal bodies, there will be for us neither any battles nor any debts; which would nowhere and never be, if our nature, as it was created upright, had remained so. And for this reason even this conflict of ours, in which we are imperiled and from which we desire to be freed by the final victory, pertains to the evils of this life, which by the testimony of so many and so great evils we prove to be condemned.

## What the goods of this life are: (a) propagation;

**24. 1.** Now it must be considered with what and how many goods the goodness of the same God—who administers all things that he created—has filled this very misery of the human race, in which the justice of the one who punishes is praised. First, that blessing which he had brought forth before sin, saying, *increase and multiply and fill the earth*[^107], he did not wish to withhold even after sin, and the granted fruitfulness remained in the condemned stock; nor could the fault of sin—by which the necessity of death too was driven upon us—take away that marvelous power of seeds, or rather the still more marvelous power by which even the seeds themselves are produced, implanted in human bodies and in a manner woven into them; but both run together at once in this, as it were, river and torrent of the human race: the evil which is drawn from the parent, and the good which is bestowed by the Creator.

In the original evil there are two things, sin and punishment; in the original good two others, propagation and conformation. But as far as pertains to our present intention, concerning the evils—of which one comes from our own audacity, that is, sin, the other from the judgment of God, that is, punishment—we have already said enough.

Now I have undertaken to speak of the goods of God, which he conferred, or even up to now confers, on the very nature that is vitiated and condemned. For in condemning it he neither took away the whole of what he had given—otherwise it would not exist at all—nor removed it from his own power even when he penally subjected it to the devil, since he has not alienated even the devil himself from his own dominion; for, that the nature even of the devil himself may subsist, he brings it about who supremely is and makes to be whatever in any way is.

**24. 2.** Of those two goods, then, which we said flow, even into the nature vitiated by sin and condemned by punishment, from a certain fountain, as it were, of his goodness, propagation he bestowed by a blessing among the first works of the world, from which works he rested on the seventh day; but conformation is in that work of his by which up to now he works[^108]. For if he were to withdraw his efficacious power from things, they would neither be able to advance and to accomplish their times by their measured motions, nor would they remain at all in that in which they were created. So, then, God created man in such a way that he added to him a certain fertility, by which he might propagate other men, begetting along with them also the very possibility of propagating, not the necessity: which from such men as he willed God took away, and they were sterile; yet from the human race he did not take away the blessing once given to the first two spouses by a general blessing. This propagation, then, although it was not taken away by sin, is nevertheless not itself such as it would have been if no one had sinned. For since man, set in honor, after he transgressed, was compared to the beasts[^109], he generates in like manner; yet in him there has not been utterly extinguished a certain spark, as it were, of reason, in which he was made to the image of God[^110].

But if to this propagation conformation were not applied, neither would propagation itself proceed into the forms and measures of its own kind. For if men had not lain together, and God nonetheless wished to fill the lands with men: as he created one without the mingling of male and female, so he could create all; but those who lie together cannot be generators except with him creating.

Just as, then, the Apostle says of spiritual formation, by which a man is formed to devotion and righteousness, *neither he who plants is anything, nor he who waters, but God who gives the increase*[^111]: so here too it can be said, "neither he who lies together nor he who sows is anything, but God who forms; nor is the mother, who carries the conceived and nourishes the offspring, anything, but God who gives the increase." For he himself, by the operation by which up to now he works, brings it about that the seeds unfold their numbers and develop from certain latent and invisible wrappings into the visible forms of this beauty which we behold; he himself, coupling and joining in wondrous ways the incorporeal and corporeal nature—the one set over, the other subjected—makes a living being.

Which work of his is so great and marvelous that not only in man, who is a rational animal and on this account more excellent and outstanding than all earthly animals, but in any tiniest little fly, it strikes the mind of one who considers well with amazement and begets praise of the Creator.

**24. 3.** He, then, gave to the human soul a mind, where reason and understanding are in a manner asleep in the infant, as though there were none, to be awakened, that is, and brought forth with the advance of age, by which it may be capable of knowledge and of learning, and fit for the perception of truth and the love of the good; by which capacity it may drink in wisdom and be endowed with the virtues, by which it may fight prudently, bravely, temperately, and justly against errors and the other inborn vices, and conquer them by the desire of nothing but that supreme and immutable good.

And even if it does not do this, how great a good, how marvelous a work of the Omnipotent, is the very capacity for such goods, divinely instituted in the rational nature—who can fittingly express it or conceive it?

For besides the arts of living well and of arriving at immortal happiness, which are called virtues and are granted only by the grace of God, which is in Christ, to the children of the promise and of the kingdom, have not so many and so great arts been discovered and practiced by human genius, partly necessary, partly for pleasure, so that the very excellent power of the mind and reason, even in those things which it seeks as superfluous—indeed, even as perilous and pernicious—bears witness to how great a good it has in its nature, whence it could discover, or learn, or practice these things?

To what marvelous, what astonishing works of garments and buildings human industry has come; how far it has advanced in agriculture, how far in navigation; what it has devised and accomplished in the fabrication of vessels of every sort, or even in the variety of statues and paintings; what marvels for spectators it has labored to do and exhibit in the theaters, incredible to hearers; what and how great things it has discovered for the capturing, killing, and taming of irrational animals; against men themselves, how many kinds of poisons, of weapons, of engines, and for the guarding and restoring of mortal welfare, how many medicaments and aids it has grasped; for the pleasure of the palate, how many seasonings and provocations of the appetite it has discovered;

for indicating and persuading thoughts, what a multitude and variety of signs, in which words and letters hold the chief place; for delighting minds, what ornaments of elocution, what abundance of various poems; for soothing the ears, how many musical instruments, what modes of song it has devised; how great a skill of measurements and numbers, and with how great sagacity it has grasped the courses and orders of the stars; with how much knowledge of worldly things it has filled itself—who could express it, especially if we wished not to heap up all things in a mass, but to linger over individual ones?

Finally, how great talents of philosophers and of heretics have shone forth even in defending errors and falsehoods themselves, who could estimate? For we are now speaking of the nature of the human mind, with which this mortal life is adorned, not of the faith and the way of truth, by which that immortal life is acquired. Since the Founder of so great a nature is surely the true and supreme God, administering all things that he made, and having supreme power and supreme justice, the human race would surely never have fallen into these miseries, and from them—except for those alone who will be freed—would never have been going into eternal ones, unless an exceedingly great sin had gone before in the first man, from whom the rest have arisen.

**24. 4.** Now indeed in the body itself, although it is common to us with the beasts in mortality and is found weaker than that of many of them, how great is the goodness of God, how great the providence of so great a Creator that appears! Are not the places of the senses and the other members so arranged in it, and the very form and figure and stature of the whole body so fashioned, that it shows itself to have been made for the service of the rational soul? For man was not created bent toward the earth, as we see the animals devoid of reason to be; but the upright form of his body toward heaven admonishes him to relish the things that are above[^112]. Furthermore, the wonderful mobility which has been granted to the tongue and the hands, apt and suitable for speaking and writing and for completing the works of very many arts and offices—does it not sufficiently show for what kind of soul such a body was joined, that it might serve it?

although, even with the necessities of working set aside, the fitness of all the parts is so numerous and answers to itself with such a beautiful parity that you would not know whether, in its founding, greater account was had of usefulness or of comeliness. For surely we see nothing created in the body for the sake of usefulness which does not also have a place of comeliness. And this would appear to us more, if we knew the numbers of the measures by which all things are connected and fitted together among themselves; which perhaps, with deliberate effort, human skill could trace in those things which project outwardly; but those which are covered and removed from our sight, such as the great intricacy of the veins and sinews and inwards, the secret things of the vitals, no one can discover.

For although the diligence of physicians, those whom they call anatomists[^113], has cruelly torn apart the bodies of the dead, or even, among the hands of one cutting and searching, those of the dying, and has searched out, inhumanely enough, all the hidden things in human flesh, that they might learn what and how and in what places it should be treated: yet the numbers of which I speak, by which the fitting-together—which in Greek is called harmonia, as of some instrument—of the whole body, outside and inside, consists, what shall I say, no one has been able to discover, which no one has dared to seek?

which, if they could have been known, the beauty of reason would so delight us even in the inner inwards, which display no comeliness, that it would be preferred, by the judgment of the mind itself, which uses the eyes, to every appearing form which pleases the eyes.

But there are certain things so placed in the body that they have only comeliness, not also use; as the male chest has nipples, as the face has a beard, which the smooth faces of women—who, being weaker, it would surely be safer to protect—show is not for protection but for male adornment. If, then, there is no member, among these conspicuous ones at least (whereof no one is in doubt), which is so accommodated to some work that it is not also comely; but there are some whose comeliness alone there is and no use: I think it is easily understood that in the founding of the body dignity was preferred to necessity.

For necessity is going to pass away, and the time will come when we shall enjoy one another in beauty alone, without any lust; which is especially to be referred to the praise of the Creator, to whom it is said in the Psalm, *you have put on confession and comeliness*[^114].

**24. 5.** Now the rest of the beauty and usefulness of creation, which has been granted to man—though cast and condemned into these labors and miseries—to behold and to take by the divine bounty, by what discourse can it be bounded? In the manifold and various beauty of heaven and earth and sea; in the great abundance and so marvelous appearance of light itself; in the sun and moon and stars; in the shade of groves; in the colors and odors of flowers; in the diversity and multitude of chattering and brightly colored birds; in the manifold form of so many and so great living creatures, of which those that have least bulk have the more wonder (for we are more amazed at the works of little ants and bees than at the immense bodies of whales); in the very so grand spectacle of the sea itself, when it clothes itself with different colors as with garments, and is now green—and this in many ways—now purple, now blue.

How delightfully, moreover, it is beheld even whenever it is stirred up, and from this there comes a greater sweetness, because it so soothes the beholder that it does not toss and shake the voyager! What of the abundance everywhere of foods against hunger? What of the diversity of flavors against weariness, poured out by the riches of nature, not sought by the art and labor of cooks? What aids, in so many things, for guarding or recovering health! How welcome the alternation of day and night succeeding each other! How gentle the mildness of the breezes! In shrubs and flocks, how great a material for making garments! Who could mention all things? But if I wished to untie and shake out, as it were bound bundles, these things alone, which have been compressed by me, as it were, into a kind of heap, how great a delay would there be for me in the individual ones, in which very many things are contained! And all these things are the solaces of the wretched and the condemned, not the rewards of the blessed.

## [The goods of the future life]

What, then, are those goods, if these are so many and such and so great? What will he give to those whom he predestined to life, who gave these things even to those whom he predestined to death? What goods will he make those take in that blessed life, for whom in this wretched one he willed his only-begotten Son to endure so great evils even unto death? Whence the Apostle, speaking of those very ones predestined to that kingdom, says: *he who did not spare his own Son, but gave him up for us all, how will he not also with him give us all things?*[^115].

When this promise is fulfilled, what shall we be! What sort shall we be! What goods shall we receive in that kingdom, since indeed, with Christ dying for us, we have already received such a pledge! What will be the spirit of man, having no fault at all, neither under which it lies, nor to which it yields, nor against which it fights at least laudably, perfected with a most peaceful virtue! How great, how beautiful, how certain will be the knowledge there of all things, without any error or labor, where the wisdom of God will be drunk from its very own fountain, with supreme happiness, without any difficulty! What will the body be, which, in every way subject to the spirit and sufficiently vivified by it, will need no nourishment! For it will be not animal, but spiritual, having indeed the substance of flesh, but without any carnal corruption.

## The world has believed.

**25.** But concerning the goods of the soul, which the most blessed will enjoy after this life, the noble philosophers do not dissent from us: about the resurrection of the flesh they contend, and deny it as much as they can. But the many who believed have left the deniers very few, and have turned with faithful heart to Christ, who showed in his own resurrection this which seems absurd to those men—the learned and the unlearned, the wise of the world and the foolish.

For the world believed this which God foretold, who also foretold this: that the world would believe this thing. For he was not compelled by the sorceries of Peter to foretell it, with such praise of believers, so long before. For he is that God whom (as I have already said several times[^116], and it does not irk me to remind us) the very deities dread, with Porphyry confessing it and wishing to prove it by the oracles of his own gods; whom he so praised that he called him both God the Father and King[^117].

For far be it that what he foretold should be understood in such a way as these wish, who did not believe, with the world, this which he foretold the world would believe. For why not rather thus, as the world was foretold so long before that it would believe, not as the very few babble, who refused to believe, with the world, this which it was foretold would believe?

For if they say that these things are to be believed in another way for this reason—lest, if they say that vain things have been written, they do injury to that God to whom they bear so great a testimony—they do him just as great, or even a graver, injury if they say that they are to be understood otherwise, not as the world believed them, which he himself praised as about to believe, he himself promised, he himself fulfilled.

For is it that he cannot bring it about that the flesh rise again and live forever; or is it for this reason not to be believed that he will do it, because it is evil and unworthy of God? But about his omnipotence, by which he does so many and so great incredible things, we have already said much. If they wish to find what the Omnipotent cannot do, they have it indeed—I will say it: he cannot lie. Let us, then, believe what he can, by not believing what he cannot. Not believing, therefore, that he can lie, let them believe that he will do what he promised he would do; and let them so believe, as the world believed, which he foretold would believe, which he praised as about to believe, which he promised would believe, which he has already shown to have believed.

But whence do they show that this is evil? There will be there no corruption, which is the evil of the body. About the order of the elements we have already disputed; about the other conjectures of men we have said enough; how great will be the ease of motion in the incorruptible body, we showed sufficiently, as I think, in the thirteenth book, from the temperateness of present good health, which is surely in no way to be compared with that immortality[^118]. Let those read the earlier parts of this work who either have not read them or wish to recall what they read.

how easily the incorruptible body will move we have, I think, sufficiently shown in book thirteen from the balanced condition of good health in the present life, which is certainly in no way comparable to that immortality[^119].

Let those who have not read the earlier portions of this work, or who wish to recall what they have read, read them.

**26.** But Porphyry says, they object, that for the soul to be blessed, every body is to be fled[^120]. It is of no profit, then, that we said the body would be incorruptible, if the soul will not be blessed unless it has fled every body. But I have already disputed about this too, as far as was needful, in the book mentioned[^121]; but here I will recall only one thing from it. Let Plato, the master of all these, emend his own books and say that their gods, in order to be blessed, will flee their own bodies—that is, will die—those gods whom he said were enclosed in heavenly bodies; to whom, nevertheless, God, by whom they were made, that they might be able to be secure, promised immortality, that is, eternal permanence in those same bodies, their nature not having this, but his counsel prevailing[^122].

Here he also overturns what they say—that, because it is impossible, therefore the resurrection of the flesh is not to be believed. For most plainly, according to the same philosopher, where the unmade God promised immortality to the gods made by himself, he said that he would do what is impossible. For Plato relates that he spoke thus: *since you have come into being*, he says, *you cannot be immortal and indissoluble; yet you will not be dissolved, nor will any fates of death destroy you, nor will they be more powerful than my counsel, which is a greater bond toward your perpetuity than those by which you are bound together*[^123]. If those who hear these things are not only not absurd but not deaf, surely they do not doubt that, according to Plato, what is impossible was promised to the gods made by that God who made them. For he who says, *you indeed cannot be immortal, but by my will you will be immortal*, what else does he say than, "you will nevertheless be that which cannot come to pass, with me bringing it about"?

He, then, will raise up incorruptible, immortal, spiritual flesh, who, according to Plato, promised that he would do what is impossible. Why do they still cry out that what God promised—what the world, which was itself also promised as about to believe, believed God promising—is impossible, since indeed we cry out that God, who according to Plato too does impossible things, will do it? Not, then, in order that souls may be blessed, is every body to be fled, but an incorruptible body to be received. And in what body will they more fittingly rejoice, incorruptible, than that in which, corruptible, they groaned? For thus there will not be in them that dire desire which Virgil set down from Plato, where he says, *and again they begin to wish to return into bodies*[^124]; thus, I say, they will not have the desire of returning to bodies, when they will have with them the bodies into which they desire to return, and will have them in such a way that they never do not have them, never lay them down at all, even for any small time, by any death.

**27.** Plato and Porphyry each said certain things which, if they could have communicated them to each other, they might perhaps have become Christians. Plato said that souls cannot be without bodies forever. For he therefore said that even the souls of the wise, after however long a time, would nevertheless return to bodies. Porphyry, however, said that the most purified soul, when it has returned to the Father, would never return to these evils of the world[^125].

And for this reason, if Plato had given to Porphyry what he saw to be true—that even the most purified souls of the righteous and the wise would return to human bodies; and again, if Porphyry had given to Plato what he saw to be true—that holy souls would never return to the miseries of the corruptible body—so that not each said these things singly, but both, and each, said both: I think they would see that it already follows that both the souls return to bodies, and they receive such bodies as those in which they might live blessedly and immortally.

For according to Plato even holy souls will return to human bodies; according to Porphyry holy souls will not return to the evils of this world. Let Porphyry, then, say with Plato, *they will return to bodies*; let Plato say with Porphyry, *they will not return to evils*: and they will agree that they return to those bodies in which they suffer no evils. And these, then, will be none other than those which God promises—that blessed souls will live forever with their own eternal flesh. For this, as I judge, both would now easily grant us: that, since they confessed that the souls of the saints would return to immortal bodies, they should allow them to return to their own bodies, in which they endured the evils of this age, in which they devoutly and faithfully worshiped God, that they might be free of these evils.

**28.** Some of our people, loving Plato on account of a certain most excellent manner of speaking and on account of certain things which he truly perceived, say that he perceived something similar to us even about the resurrection of the dead[^126]. Which indeed Tully touches upon in his books *On the Republic* in such a way that he means that Plato was playing rather than affirming this to be true[^127]. For he brings in a man who revived and narrated certain things which accorded with the Platonic disputations[^128]. Labeo too says that two men died on one day and met each other at a certain crossroads, then were ordered to return to their bodies and resolved between themselves that they would live as friends, and that it so came to pass, until they later died[^129]. But these authors narrated that such a resurrection of the body had taken place as was that of those whom we know to have risen again and to have been restored indeed to this life, but not in such a way that they would not die thereafter. But Marcus Varro sets down something more marvelous in the books which he wrote *On the Race of the Roman People*, whose very words I have thought should be set down. *Certain astrologers wrote*, he says, *that there is in the rebirth of men what the Greeks call* palingenesia; *by this they wrote that it is brought about, in the number of four hundred and forty years, that the same body and the same soul, which were at some time joined together in a man, return again into the same conjunction*[^130].

This Varro, indeed, or those astrologers—I know not who (for he did not disclose the names of those whose opinion he recorded)—said something which, although it is false (for, once souls have returned to the same bodies which they bore, they will never afterward leave them), nevertheless tears apart and destroys many arguments of that impossibility by which those men babble against us. For those who hold this, or held it, did not think it could not happen that corpses dissolved into the air, into dust, into ash, into liquids, into the bodies of beasts feeding on them, or even of men themselves, should return again to that which they were. Wherefore Plato and Porphyry, or rather whoever love them and are still alive, if they agree with us that holy souls too will return to bodies, as Plato says, yet not return to any evils, as Porphyry says—so that from these there follows what the Christian faith preaches, that they will receive such bodies as those in which they may live forever happily without any evil—let them also take this from Varro, that they return to the same bodies in which they were before, and with them the whole question about the resurrection of the flesh unto eternity will be solved.

## How we shall see God.

**29. 1.** Now let us see, as far as the Lord deigns to help, what the saints will do in their immortal and spiritual bodies, their flesh now living not carnally but spiritually. And indeed what that action, or rather rest and leisure, will be, if I wish to say the truth, I do not know. For I have never seen this through the senses of the body. But if I should say that I have seen it with the mind, that is, with the understanding, how great or what is our understanding compared to that excellence? For there is the *peace of God which*, as the Apostle says, *surpasses all understanding*[^131]; whose, if not ours, or perhaps even that of the holy angels? for surely not God's.

If, then, the saints are to live in the peace of God, surely they are to live in that peace which surpasses all understanding. That it surpasses ours, there is no doubt; but if it surpasses also that of the angels, so that he who said *all understanding* seems not to have excepted even them, then we ought to take it to be said in this sense: that the peace of God, by which God himself is at peace, as God knows it, we cannot know in that way, nor can any angels. It *surpasses*, therefore, *all understanding*—no doubt except his own.

But because we too, in our measure, made partakers of his peace, know the highest peace within us and among us and with him, as far as is our highest: in this way, in their measure, the holy angels know it; but men now far below, however much they may excel by advance of mind. For we must consider how great a man it was who said, *we know in part and we prophesy in part, until that which is perfect comes*[^132]; and, *we see now through a mirror in an enigma, but then face to face*[^133].

Thus already the holy angels see, who have also been called our angels, because, rescued from the power of darkness and, having received the pledge of the Spirit, translated to the kingdom of Christ[^134], we have begun to belong to those angels with whom we shall share the holy and most sweet city of God, that very city which is common to us, about which we have already written so many books. So, then, those are our angels who are the angels of God, just as Christ, who is God's Christ, is ours. They are God's, because they have not forsaken God; they are ours, because they have begun to have us as their fellow citizens. And the Lord Jesus said, *see that you do not despise one of these little ones. For I say to you that their angels in heaven always see the face of my Father who is in heaven*[^135]. As, then, they see, so we too shall see; but we do not yet see thus. On account of which the Apostle says what I said a little before: *we see now through a mirror in an enigma, but then face to face*. As the reward of faith, then, this vision is reserved for us, of which the apostle John also, speaking, says: *when he appears, we shall be like him, for we shall see him as he is*[^136]. But the face of God is to be understood as his manifestation, not some such member as we have in the body and call by that name.

## What that vision itself will be.

**29. 2.** Wherefore, when it is asked of me what the saints will do in that spiritual body, I do not say what I already see, but I say what I believe, according to that which I read in the Psalm: *I have believed, on which account I have spoken*[^137]. I say, then: they will see God in the body itself; but whether through it, as now through the body we see the sun, moon, stars, sea, and earth and the things in it, is no small question. For it is hard to say that the saints will then have such bodies that they cannot close and open their eyes when they will; but harder, that there anyone who closes his eyes will not see God.

For if the prophet Elisha, absent in body, saw his servant Gehazi receiving the gifts which Naaman the Syrian—whom the said Prophet had freed from the deformity of leprosy—gave him, which the wicked servant thought he had done secretly, with his master not seeing[^138]: how much more in that spiritual body will the saints see all things, not only if they close their eyes, but even those things from which they are absent in body! For then will be perfect that of which the Apostle, speaking, says: *we know in part and we prophesy in part; but when that which is perfect has come, that which is in part will be done away*[^139]. Then, that by some likeness he might show how far this life—not of just any men, but even of those endowed here with surpassing holiness—is distant from that which is to come: *when I was*, he says, *a little child, I understood as a little child, I spoke as a little child, I thought as a little child; but when I became a man, I did away with the things that were of a little child. We see now through a mirror in an enigma, but then face to face. Now I know in part, but then I shall know even as I have been known*[^140].

If, then, in this life, where the prophecy of wonderful men is to be compared to that life as a little child to a young man, Elisha nevertheless saw his servant receiving the gifts where he himself was not: will the saints then, when that which is perfect has come and the corruptible body no longer weighs down the soul, but the incorruptible one hinders nothing, need eyes for seeing those things which are to be seen with bodily eyes—eyes which Elisha, absent, did not need for seeing his servant? For according to the Septuagint translators these are the words of the Prophet to Gehazi: *was not my heart with you, when the man turned from his chariot to meet you and you took the money?*[^141] and the rest; but as the presbyter Jerome translated from the Hebrew: *was not my heart*, he says, *present, when the man turned back from his chariot to meet you?*

With his heart, then, the Prophet said that he saw this, marvelously helped, with no one doubting, divinely. But how much more will all then abound in this gift, when *God will be all in all*[^142]! Yet those bodily eyes too will have their own office and will be in their place, and the spirit will use them through the spiritual body. For not even that Prophet, because he did not need them to see one absent, did not use them for seeing things present; which, nevertheless, he could see with the spirit, even if he closed them, just as he saw absent things, where he himself was not with them. Far be it, then, that we should say that those saints in that life will not see, with closed eyes, God, whom they will always see with the spirit.

**29. 3.** But whether they will see also through the eyes of the body, when they have them open, is the question. For if even the spiritual eyes themselves, in the spiritual body, will be able to do only as much as these eyes of the sort we now have can: then beyond doubt God will not be able to be seen through them. They will, therefore, be of a far different power, if through them that incorporeal nature is to be seen, which is not contained in a place, but is everywhere whole. For not because we say that God is both in heaven and on earth (for he himself says through the Prophet, *I fill heaven and earth*[^143]) are we going to say that he has one part in heaven and another on earth; but he is whole in heaven, whole on earth, not at alternating times, but both at once, which no corporeal nature can do. The power, then, of those eyes will be more prevailing, not that they may see more keenly than certain serpents or eagles are reported to see (for with however great keenness of discerning, those same animals can see nothing other than bodies), but that they may see even incorporeal things.

And perhaps this great power of discerning was given for a time even in this mortal body to the eyes of the holy man Job, when he says to God, *by the hearing of the ear I heard you before, but now my eye sees you; therefore I have despised myself and wasted away, and have esteemed myself earth and ash*[^144]; although nothing here forbids the eye of the heart to be understood, of which eyes the Apostle says, that we have *the eyes of your heart* enlightened[^145]. But that God will be seen with those eyes, when he is seen, no Christian doubts who faithfully accepts what that divine Teacher says: *blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God*[^146]. But whether he is seen there also with bodily eyes, this we are treating in this question.

**29. 4.** For that which is written, *and all flesh shall see the salvation of God*[^147], can be understood, without the knot of any difficulty, as if it had been said, "and every man shall see the Christ of God," who was surely seen in the body and will be seen in the body, when he judges the living and the dead. But that he himself is the salvation of God, there are many other testimonies of the Scriptures; but more evidently the words of that venerable old man Simeon declare it, who, when he had received the infant Christ into his hands, said: *now you dismiss your servant, Lord, according to your word, in peace, for my eyes have seen your salvation*[^148]. That too which the above-mentioned Job says, as it is found in the copies that are from the Hebrew, *and in my flesh I shall see God*[^149], prophesied indeed the resurrection of the flesh without doubt, yet he did not say, "through my flesh."

Which indeed, if he had said it, *God* could be understood as Christ, who through the flesh, in the flesh, will be seen; but now it can also be taken thus: *in my flesh I shall see God*, as if he had said, "in my flesh I shall be when I see God." And that which the Apostle says, *face to face*[^150], does not compel us to believe that we shall see through this corporeal face, where the corporeal eyes are, him whom we shall see with the spirit without intermission.

For unless there were also a face of the inner man, the same Apostle would not say, *but we, with unveiled face beholding the glory of the Lord, are transformed into the same image, from glory to glory, as by the Spirit of the Lord*[^151]; nor do we understand otherwise also what is sung in the Psalm, *draw near to him and be enlightened, and your faces will not be ashamed*[^152]. For it is by faith that one draws near to God, which faith is, it is agreed, of the heart, not of the body. But because we do not know how great accesses the spiritual body will have (for we are speaking of a thing not experienced), where the authority of the divine Scriptures—any that cannot be understood otherwise—neither meets nor helps us, it is necessary that there befall in us what is read in the book of *Wisdom: the thoughts of mortals are timid and our foresights uncertain*[^153].

**29. 5.** For that reasoning of the philosophers, by which they argue that intelligible things are so seen by the gaze of the mind and sensible things, that is, corporeal things, by the sense of the body, that the mind can intuit neither intelligible things through the body nor corporeal things through itself—if it could be most certain to us, surely it would be certain that God can in no way be seen through the eyes even of a spiritual body. But both true reason and prophetic authority mock this reasoning. For who would be so averse to the truth as to dare to say that God does not know these corporeal things? Has he, then, a body, through whose eyes he can learn them? Next, what we said a little before about the prophet Elisha[^154], does it not sufficiently indicate that corporeal things can be discerned even by the spirit, not through the body? For when that servant received the gifts, it was surely done corporeally; which, nevertheless, the Prophet saw not through the body but through the spirit.

As, then, it is agreed that bodies are seen by the spirit, what if so great will be the power of the spiritual body that even spirit is seen by the body? For *God is a spirit*. Next, each one knows his own life, indeed, by which he now lives in the body and quickens these earthly members and makes them living, by an inner sense, not through corporeal eyes; but the lives of others, since they are invisible, he sees through the body. For whence do we distinguish living bodies from non-living ones, except that we see at once bodies and lives, which we cannot see except through the body? But lives without bodies we do not see with corporeal eyes.

**29. 6.** Wherefore it can come to pass, and is very credible, that we shall then so see the worldly bodies of the new heaven and new earth, that we shall see God, everywhere present and governing all things, even corporeal, with most clear perspicuity, through the bodies we shall bear and which we shall behold, wherever we direct our eyes,

not as now the invisible things of God are beheld, understood through the things that have been made, through a mirror in an enigma[^155] and in part, where the faith by which we believe avails more in us than the appearance of corporeal things which we discern through corporeal eyes.

But just as men, among whom, living and exercising vital motions, we live, as soon as we look at them, we do not believe to live, but see it, although we cannot see their life without bodies, which life nevertheless we behold in them through their bodies, with all ambiguity removed; so, whatever spiritual lights of our bodies we shall carry about, we shall behold the incorporeal God ruling all things, even through bodies.

Either, then, God will be seen through those eyes in such a way that they have something, in so great an excellence, similar to the mind, by which even the incorporeal nature is discerned—which it is either difficult or impossible to show by any examples or testimonies of the divine Scriptures—or, what is easier to understand, God will be so known and visible to us that he is seen by the spirit, by each of us in each of us, seen by one in another, seen in himself, seen in the new heaven and new earth and in every creature that shall then be, seen also through bodies in every body, wherever the keenness of the spiritual body's eyes, directed, shall reach. Our thoughts too will be open to one another. For then will be fulfilled what the Apostle, when he had said, *do not judge anything before the time*, soon added: *until the Lord comes, and he will illumine the hidden things of darkness and make manifest the thoughts of the heart, and then there will be praise for each from God*[^156].

## The supreme happiness in eternal life . . .

**30. 1.** How great will be that happiness, where there will be no evil, no good will lie hidden, and there will be leisure for the praises of God, who will be all in all[^157]! For what else is done, where there will be no ceasing through any idleness and no laboring through any want, I do not know. I am admonished also by the holy Canticle, where I read or hear: *blessed are they who dwell in your house, Lord; forever and ever they will praise you*[^158].

All the members and inwards of the incorruptible body, which we now see distributed to the various uses of necessity, since then there will be not necessity itself, but full, certain, secure, everlasting happiness, will avail for the praises of God. For all those numbers of bodily harmony of which I have already spoken[^159], which now lie hidden, will not lie hidden, arranged within and without through all the parts of the body; and along with the other things which will there be seen, great and marvelous, they will kindle the rational minds to the praise of so great an Artist with the delight of reasonable beauty.

What the motions of such bodies will there be, I do not rashly dare to define, since I am unable to conceive it; nevertheless both the motion and the rest, like the appearance itself, will be becoming, whatever it will be, where what will not be becoming will not be. Certainly, where the spirit will, there the body will be at once; nor will the spirit will anything which could become neither the spirit nor the body. There will be true glory, where no one will be praised by the error or the flattery of one who praises; true honor, which will be denied to no one worthy, deferred to no one unworthy; but neither will any unworthy one aspire to it, where no one will be permitted to be except the worthy; true peace, where no one will suffer anything adverse, either from himself or from any other.

The reward of virtue will be he himself who gave virtue and promised himself to it, than whom nothing can be better or greater. For what else is it that he said through the Prophet, *I will be their God, and they will be my people*[^160], if not, "I will be that from which they are satisfied, I will be whatever is honorably desired by men—both life and health and food and abundance and glory and honor and peace and all good things"? For so too that is rightly understood which the Apostle says: *that God may be all in all*[^161]. He will be the end of our desires, who will be seen without end, loved without weariness, praised without fatigue. This gift, this affection, this act will surely be common to all, as is eternal life itself.

**30. 2.** But what the degrees of honors and glories will be, according to the merits of rewards, who is fit to think, much less to say? That they will be, however, is not to be doubted. And this too that blessed city will see as a great good in itself: that no inferior will envy any superior, just as now the other angels do not envy the archangels; and each will so be unwilling to be what he did not receive—though bound to the one who received it by a most peaceful bond of concord—as the eye, in the body, does not wish to be the finger, since the peaceful framework of the whole body contains both members[^162]. So, then, one will have a gift less than another in such a way that he has also this gift, of not wishing for more.

**30. 3.** Nor will they on that account not have free choice, because sins will not be able to delight them. For it will rather be more free, freed from the delight of sinning unto an unswerving delight in not sinning. For the first free choice, which was given to man when he was first created upright, was able not to sin, but was also able to sin; but this last one will be the more powerful in that it will not be able to sin—yet this too by the gift of God, not by the possibility of its own nature. For it is one thing to be God, another to be a partaker of God. God by nature cannot sin; but the partaker of God received from him that he cannot sin.

But the degrees of the divine gift had to be preserved, so that first there should be given a free choice by which man was able not to sin, and last one by which he was not able to sin; and that the former should pertain to acquiring merit, the latter to receiving reward.

But because this nature sinned when it was able to sin, by a more bountiful grace it is freed, that it may be brought to that liberty in which it is not able to sin. For just as the first immortality, which Adam lost by sinning, was to be able not to die, and the last will be not to be able to die: so the first free choice was to be able not to sin, the last not to be able to sin. For the will of devotion and equity will be as incapable of being lost as is that of happiness. For surely by sinning we kept neither devotion nor happiness, but the will of happiness we did not lose even with happiness lost. Certainly God himself—is he then to be denied to have free choice, because he cannot sin?

**30. 4.** There will be, then, of that city a will both one in all and inseparable in each, free, freed from all evil and filled with all good, enjoying unfailingly the pleasantness of eternal joys, forgetful of faults, forgetful of punishments; nor yet on that account forgetful of its own liberation, so that it should be ungrateful to its liberator. As far, then, as pertains to rational knowledge, it is mindful of its past evils too; but as far as pertains to the sense of one experiencing them, utterly unmindful. For even the most skilled physician knows nearly all the diseases of the body as they are known by the art; but as they are felt in the body, he knows very many not, which he himself has not suffered.

As, then, there are two kinds of knowledge of evils—one by which they do not lie hidden from the power of the mind, another by which they cling to the senses of one experiencing them (for all vices are known one way through the doctrine of wisdom, another way through the most wicked life of a fool)—so there are also two kinds of forgetting of evils.

For the educated and learned man forgets them one way, the one who experienced and suffered them another way; the former, if he neglects his skill, the latter, if he is free of misery. According to this forgetting, which I have set in the latter place, the saints will not be mindful of their past evils; for they will be free of all of them, so that they are utterly blotted out from their senses.

Yet by that power of knowledge, which will be great in them, not only their own past evils but also the everlasting misery of the damned will not lie hidden from them. Otherwise, if they are not going to know that they were wretched, how, as the Psalm says, will they *sing the mercies of the Lord forever*[^163]? Than which song, surely, nothing will be more pleasant to that city, for the glory of the grace of Christ, by whose blood we have been freed.

There will be perfected, *be still and see that I am God*[^164]; which will be truly the greatest Sabbath, having no evening, which the Lord commended in the first works of the world, where it is read: *and God rested on the seventh day from all his works which he made, and God blessed the seventh day and sanctified it, because in it he rested from all his works which God began to do*[^165]. For we ourselves too shall be the seventh day, when we shall be full of his blessing and sanctification and made new.

There, being still, we shall see that he himself is God; which we wished to be ourselves when we fell away from him, listening to the seducer, *you will be like gods*[^166], and withdrawing from the true God, by whose doing we would be gods by participation in him, not by deserting him. For what did we do without him, except that in his anger we failed? And by him, made new and perfected by a greater grace, we shall be still for eternity, seeing that he himself is God, of whom we shall be full when he himself will be all in all[^167].

For even our own good works themselves, when they are understood to be rather his than ours, are then reckoned to us for the attaining of this Sabbath; because if we attribute them to ourselves, they will be servile, since of the Sabbath it is said, *you shall do no servile work*[^168]; on account of which it is also said through the prophet Ezekiel, *and I gave them my Sabbaths as a sign between me and between them, that they might know that I am the Lord, who sanctify them*[^169]. This we shall then know perfectly, when we are perfectly still, and shall perfectly see that he himself is God.

**30. 5.** The very number of the ages, too, as it were of days, if it be computed according to those articles of time which seem to be expressed in the Scriptures, this Sabbath-rest will appear more evidently, since it is found to be the seventh; so that the first age, as it were the first day, is from Adam to the flood, the second from then to Abraham—not by equality of times, but by the number of generations; for they are found to have ten each. From here on, as the evangelist Matthew determines, three ages follow up to the coming of Christ, which are each unfolded in fourteen generations: from Abraham to David one, another from then to the migration into Babylon, the third from then to the carnal nativity of Christ[^170]. There come to be, then, five in all. The sixth is now being passed, to be measured by no number of generations, on account of what was said: *it is not yours to know the times which the Father has placed in his own power*[^171]. After this, as on the seventh day, God will rest, when he will make that same seventh day, which we shall be, rest in himself, God. But to dispute now carefully about these individual ages is a long matter; this seventh, however, will be our Sabbath, whose end will be not an evening, but the Lord's day, as it were an eternal eighth, which has been consecrated by the resurrection of Christ, prefiguring the eternal rest not only of the spirit but also of the body. There we shall be still and see, we shall see and love, we shall love and praise. Behold what will be in the end without end. For what else is our end but to come to the kingdom of which there is no end?

It would take too long now to discuss each of these ages in detail. This seventh age, however, will be our Sabbath, whose end will not be evening but the Lord’s day, an eternal eighth day, as it were, hallowed by Christ’s Resurrection and prefiguring the eternal rest not only of the spirit but also of the body. There we shall rest and see, see and love, love and praise. Behold what will be in the end without end. For what other end do we have than to reach the kingdom that has no end?

**30. 6.** I seem to myself, with the Lord's help, to have paid the debt of this huge work. Let those for whom it is too little, or those for whom it is too much, pardon me; but let those for whom it is enough give thanks, not to me, but to God along with me, congratulating one another. Amen. Amen.
