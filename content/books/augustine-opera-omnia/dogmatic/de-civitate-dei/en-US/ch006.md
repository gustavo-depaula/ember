# Book IV: The many gods availed nothing toward enlarging the empire.

**1.** Having undertaken to speak of the City of God, I thought I should first answer its enemies, who, pursuing earthly joys and gaping after fleeting things, blame the Christian religion—which alone is the wholesome and true religion—for whatever sorrow they suffer in them, sorrow that comes rather from God's mercy admonishing them than from his severity punishing them.

And since, there being among them also the uninstructed common crowd, they are more grievously stirred up to hatred of us as if by the authority of the learned—the ignorant supposing that the things which befell unusually in their own times were not wont to befall in other times gone by, and the learned, who know this opinion to be false, confirming it by a feigned dissimulation of their own knowledge, so that they may seem to have just grounds for murmuring against us:

it was necessary to demonstrate, from the books which their own authors committed to memory for the knowledge of the history of past times, that the matter is far otherwise than they suppose; and at the same time to teach that the false gods, whom they worshipped openly or still worship in secret, are most unclean spirits and most malignant and most deceitful demons—so much so that they take delight in their own crimes, whether real or even fictitious, which they wished to have celebrated for themselves through their festivals,

so that human weakness cannot be called back from perpetrating damnable deeds, while authority is offered, as though divine, for imitating these things.

These things we proved not from our own conjecture, but partly from recent memory, because we ourselves saw such things performed, and to such deities, and partly from the writings of those who left these things recorded for posterity not as though in reproach, but as though in honor of their own gods,

so that Varro, a most learned man among them and of the gravest authority, when he made his books on human and divine matters distinct—distributing some to human matters, others to divine, according to the dignity of each subject—placed the theatrical games not even among human matters but among divine matters[^1]; whereas surely, if only good and honorable men were in the city, the theatrical games ought not to have existed even among human matters. This indeed he did not on his own authority, but because, born and reared at Rome, he found them among divine matters.

And since at the end of the first book we briefly set down what was to be said thereafter, and have said some of these things in the two following books, we recognize that what remains must be rendered to the expectation of our readers.

**2.** We had promised, then, that we would say certain things against those who refer the disasters of the Roman republic to our religion, and that we would recount whatever and however great evils could have occurred to us, or seemed sufficient, which that city, or the provinces belonging to its empire, endured before their sacrifices had been forbidden—all of which they would without doubt ascribe to us, if by then either our religion shone forth to them or so forbade them their sacrilegious rites.

These things we have, I think, sufficiently completed in the second and third books—treating in the second of the evils of morals, which are to be reckoned the only, or the greatest, evils, and in the third of those evils which fools alone shudder to endure, namely those of the body and of external things, which for the most part good men too suffer; but those evils by which they themselves become bad, they hold not, I do not say patiently, but gladly.

And how little I have said of that one city alone and its empire! And not even all of that down to Caesar Augustus. What if I had wished to recount and pile up those evils which men do not do to one another—such as the devastations and overthrows of those who wage war—but which befall earthly things from the very elements of the world itself

(which Apuleius touches on briefly in one place, in that little book he wrote *On the World,* saying that all earthly things have their changes, reversals, and destructions; for, to use his words, *by immoderate tremors of the earth the ground has split apart and cities together with their peoples have been swallowed up; whole regions too have been washed away by bursting rains; lands that had before been continents have been turned into islands by foreign and alien floods, and others made passable to travelers on foot by the withdrawal of the sea; cities have been overthrown by winds and storms; fires have flashed out of the clouds, by which the regions of the East were consumed and perished, and in the regions of the West certain gushing springs and floods have wrought the same destruction; so once, when the craters poured forth from the peaks of Etna, rivers of flame ran down the slopes like a torrent by a divine fire*)[^2].

If I had wished to gather these things and the like, which history contains, from wherever I could, when should I have finished? These are the things that befell in those times, before the name of Christ suppressed any of those vanities of theirs that are ruinous to true salvation.

I had also promised that I would show what their morals were, and for what cause the true God, in whose power all kingdoms are, deigned to help them in enlarging their empire, and how those whom they think gods helped them in nothing, but rather how much they harmed them by deceiving and beguiling them. Whence I see that I must now speak, and rather of the growth of the Roman empire. For about the harmful deceit of the demons, whom they worshipped as gods, and how much evil it brought upon their morals, not a little has already been said, especially in the second book. But throughout all three completed books, where it seemed opportune, we have shown how much consolation God, through the name of Christ—to whom the barbarians paid so much honor, contrary to the custom of wars—conferred upon good and bad alike even amid the evils of war, in the way of him *who makes his sun rise on the good and the bad and sends rain on the just and the unjust*[^3].

**3.** Now, then, let us see what sort of thing it is that they dare to ascribe so great a breadth and long duration of the Roman empire to those gods, whom they contend they worshipped honorably even through the obsequies of base games and the ministries of base men.

And yet I would first inquire a little what reason, what prudence there is, when you cannot show the felicity of men—men always engaged in the disasters of war and in blood, whether of citizens or of enemies, but human nonetheless, men living amid dark fear and bloody desire—so that a glassy gladness is acquired, brittle in its brilliance, for which there is the more horrible fear lest it suddenly be shattered, to wish to glory in the breadth and greatness of empire.

That this may be more easily judged, let us not be carried away with empty windiness, nor blunt the keenness of our attention with high-sounding names of things, when we hear "peoples," "kingdoms," "provinces"; but let us set up two men (for each single man, just as a single letter in speech, is, as it were, the element of a city and a kingdom, however widely spread over its occupation of lands), and of these two men let us think of one as poor, or rather of middling means, and the other as exceedingly rich;

but the rich man anxious with fears, wasting away with griefs, burning with desire, never secure, always restless, panting with the perpetual contentions of enmities, augmenting indeed by these miseries his patrimony to an immense degree, but heaping up with those increases the most bitter cares as well;

while that man of middling means is sufficient to himself with a small and modest estate, most dear to his own, rejoicing in the sweetest peace with kinsmen, neighbors, and friends, devout in piety, kindly in mind, sound in body, frugal in life, chaste in morals, secure in conscience. I do not know whether anyone is so foolish as to dare to doubt which of the two he would prefer. As, then, in these two men, so in two households, so in two peoples, so in two kingdoms, the rule of fairness follows; and if our attention is corrected by it, vigilantly applied, we shall most easily see where vanity dwells and where felicity.

Wherefore, if the true God is worshipped and is served with true rites and good morals, it is useful that the good should reign far and wide and long; nor is this so useful to themselves as to those over whom they reign. For as far as concerns themselves, their piety and probity—which are great gifts of God—suffice for their true felicity, by which both this life is well lived and afterward eternal life is received. On this earth, then, the reign of the good is bestowed not so much on themselves as on human affairs; but the reign of the bad harms more the rulers themselves, who devastate their own souls with a greater license for crimes; while to those who, by serving them, are made subject, it does no harm except their own iniquity. For whatever of evils is inflicted on the just by unjust masters is not the punishment of crime, but the testing of virtue. Accordingly the good man, even if he is a slave, is free; but the bad man, even if he reigns, is a slave—and not of one man, but, what is graver, of as many masters as he has vices. Of these vices, when Holy Scripture treated, it said: *for by whatever a man is overcome, to this he is also given over as a slave*[^4].

**4.** Therefore, with justice removed, what are kingdoms but great bands of robbers? *For* what are bands of robbers but little kingdoms? A band too is a company of men, ruled by the command of a prince, bound together by a compact of fellowship, and the booty divided by the law agreed upon. If this evil grows, by the accession of abandoned men, to such a point that it holds places, establishes seats, occupies cities, and subdues peoples, it more openly assumes the name of a kingdom—a name now conferred on it openly not by the removal of greed, but by the addition of impunity. For elegantly and truly did a captured pirate answer that famous Alexander the Great. When the king asked the man what he meant by infesting the sea, he replied with free defiance: *the same as you mean by infesting the whole world; but because I do it with a small vessel, I am called a robber; because you do it with a great fleet, an emperor*[^5].

**5.** Accordingly I forbear to ask what sort of men Romulus gathered together, since much provision was made for them, so that, the fellowship of the city being given them, they might cease, after that former life, to ponder the penalties due—the fear of which was driving them on to greater crimes—so that thenceforth they might be more peaceable members of human society.

This I say: that the Roman empire itself, already great with many nations subdued and terrible to the rest, felt bitterly, feared grievously, and crushed with no small effort, to avoid an immense disaster, the moment when a very few gladiators, fleeing from a training school in Campania, raised a great army, had three leaders, and most widely and most cruelly laid waste Italy[^6]. Let them say what God helped these men, so that from a small and contemptible band of robbers they came to a kingdom that was by then to be feared even by so great Roman forces and citadels.

Or because they did not last long, will they on that account be denied to have been helped by the gods? As though the life of any man whatever were long-lasting. On this principle, then, the gods help no one to reign, since each one dies quickly; nor is it to be reckoned a benefit which, in a short time, in each man—and through this, individually, in all alike—vanishes away like vapor. For what does it matter to those who worshipped the gods under Romulus and died long ago, that after their death the Roman empire grew so great, when they are pleading their own causes among the dead? Whether good or bad, that does not pertain to the present matter.

But this is to be understood of all who, through that empire (although, as mortals die off and others succeed, it is stretched over long periods), have passed swiftly and hurriedly through the few days of their own life, bearing the burdens of their own deeds.

But if even the benefits of the briefest time are to be ascribed to the help of the gods, those gladiators were helped not a little: they broke the chains of their servile condition, fled, escaped, gathered a great and very brave army, and—obeying the counsels and commands of their kings—were much to be feared by Roman loftiness, were unconquerable to several Roman commanders, captured many things, won very many victories, used what pleasures they wished, did what lust suggested, and at last, until they were conquered (which was done with the greatest difficulty), lived exalted and reigning. But let us come to greater matters.

## Trogus and Justin are witnesses that Ninus first subdued his neighbors.

**6.** Justin, who, following Trogus Pompeius—a Greek, or rather a foreign, historian—wrote history not only in Latin, as Trogus did, but also briefly, begins the work of his books thus: *In the beginning of the affairs of peoples and nations, dominion lay with kings, whom not popular ambition but moderation approved among good men advanced to the summit of this majesty. The peoples were held by no laws; it was the custom to guard the bounds of empire rather than to extend them; each man's kingdom was confined within his own homeland.*

*First of all, Ninus, king of the Assyrians, changed by a new lust for empire the old and as it were ancestral custom of the nations. He first made war on his neighbors and, subduing peoples as yet untrained to resist, brought them under his yoke as far as the bounds of Libya.* And a little after, he says: *Ninus made firm the greatness of the dominion he had sought by continuous possession. So, the nearest peoples being subdued, when with the accession of forces he passed, stronger, to others, and each nearest victory was the instrument of the next, he subjugated the peoples of the whole East*[^7].

But with whatever fidelity to fact either Justin or Trogus may have written (for that they lied in certain things, other more trustworthy writings show), it is nevertheless agreed even among other writers that the kingdom of the Assyrians was extended far and wide by King Ninus[^8]. And it endured so long that the Roman empire is not yet of its age. For, as those who have pursued chronological history write, this kingdom lasted twelve hundred and forty years from the first year in which Ninus began to reign, until it was transferred to the Medes[^9]. But to make war on one's neighbors, and to proceed thence against the rest, and to grind down and subdue peoples who give no trouble, out of mere lust for empire—what else is this to be called than great robbery?

**7.** If, then, this kingdom was so great and so long-lasting with no help of the gods, why is the Roman kingdom, ample in places and long-lasting in time, ascribed to the Roman gods? For whatever the cause of the one is, the same is also the cause of the other. But if they contend that the former too is to be ascribed to the help of the gods, I ask, of which gods? For the other nations, which Ninus subdued and subjugated, did not at that time worship other gods. Or if the Assyrians had their own gods, as more skilled craftsmen, so to speak, for constructing and preserving an empire—did they then die, when these too lost their empire,

or, their pay not being rendered to them, or a greater one being promised, did they prefer to cross over to the Medes, and thence again to the Persians, at the invitation of Cyrus and the promise of something more advantageous? Which nation, after the kingdom of Alexander of Macedon—great in places but very brief in time—endures still even now in its own kingdom, within no narrow bounds of the East.

If this is so, then either the gods are unfaithful, who desert their own and go over to the enemy (which not even the man Camillus did, when, the victor and stormer of a most hostile city, he found Rome, for which he had conquered, ungrateful—Rome which nevertheless, afterward forgetting the injury and mindful of his homeland, he again freed from the Gauls),

or they are not so strong as it befits gods to be strong, since they can be conquered by human counsels or forces; or, if, when they war among themselves, the gods are conquered not by men but by other gods—the gods, that is, proper to particular cities—then they too have enmities among themselves, which each takes up on behalf of his own side. A city, then, ought not to have worshipped its own gods rather than others, by whom its own gods might be helped.

Finally, in whatever way this crossing over, or flight, or migration, or failing in battle of the gods is to be understood, the name of Christ had not yet been preached in those times and in those parts of the world, when those kingdoms were lost and transferred through immense disasters of war.

For if, after twelve hundred years and more, when the kingdom was taken from the Assyrians, the Christian religion had there been preaching another, eternal kingdom and forbidding the sacrilegious worship of false gods, what else would the vain men of that nation say but that the kingdom, which had been preserved so long, could have perished for no other cause than that its own religions were abandoned and that one received? In which voice of vanity, such as it could have been, let these men look upon their own mirror, and, if there is any shame in them, let them blush to complain of like things.

Although the Roman empire was afflicted rather than changed—which befell it at other times too, before the name of Christ, and from which affliction it recovered—a thing not to be despaired of in these times either. For who knows the will of God concerning this matter?

## How many gods there are for preserving the things of nature.

**8.** Next let us ask, if it please, out of so great a throng of gods which the Romans worshipped, which one above all, or which gods, they believe enlarged and preserved that empire. For surely they do not dare to assign any part in this so glorious a work, full of so great dignity, to the goddess Cloacina, or to Volupia, who is named from pleasure (*voluptas*), or to Lubentina, whose name is from lust (*libido*), or to Vaticanus, who presides over the wailing of infants, or to Cunina, who looks after their cradles. But how can all the names of gods and goddesses be recounted in one place of this book, which they could scarcely include in great volumes, distributing to single things the proper offices of the deities? They did not even think the charge of the fields was to be committed to some one god, but set the goddess Rusina over the countryside, the god Iugatinus over the mountain ridges; over the hills they put the goddess Collatina, over the valleys Vallonia.

Nor could they even find a single Segetia of such a kind, to whom they might once for all commend the standing crops, but they wished the sown grain, as long as it was under the earth, to have the goddess Seia set over it; but when it was now above ground and forming a crop, the goddess Segetia; and the grain, once gathered and stored, that it might be safely kept, they set the goddess Tutilina over. To whom would that Segetia not seem to suffice, so long as the crop passed from its first grassy beginnings to the dry ears? Yet it was not enough for men who love a multitude of gods, that the wretched soul should be prostituted to a throng of demons, having disdained the chaste embrace of the one true God.

They set, then, Proserpina over the sprouting grain, the god Nodutus over the knots and joints of the stalks, the goddess Volutina over the wrappings of the husks; when the husks open, that the ear may come forth, the goddess Patelana; when the crops are made even with new ears—because the ancients called "to make even" *hostire*—the goddess Hostilina; over the flowering grain the goddess Flora, over the milky grain the god Lacturnus, over the ripening grain the goddess Matuta; when they are weeded, that is, taken from the earth, the goddess Runcina[^10].

Nor do I recount them all, because it irks me to mention what does not shame them. But these very few things I have said for this reason, that it might be understood that they in no way dare to say that these deities established, enlarged, and preserved the Roman empire—deities who were each so assigned to their own offices that nothing as a whole was entrusted to any one of them. When, then, would Segetia have cared for the empire, who was not allowed to take care of crops and trees at the same time? When would Cunina have thought about arms, whose charge was not permitted to go beyond the cradles of little ones? When would Nodutus help in war, who pertained not even to the husk of the ear, but only to the knot of the joint?

Each man sets a single doorkeeper for his house, and, because he is a man, that is entirely enough; but these men set three gods: Forculus for the doors, Cardea for the hinge, Limentinus for the threshold[^11]. So Forculus could not at the same time guard the hinge and the threshold.

## What is to be said about Jove.

**9.** Leaving aside, then, this throng of petty gods, or setting it aside for a while, we must inquire into the office of the greater gods, by which Rome was made so great that it ruled so many nations for so long. No doubt, then, this is the work of Jove. For they would have him the king of all gods and goddesses: this his scepter indicates, this the Capitol on the high hill. Of this god they cite, most fittingly, a saying of the poet: *all things are full of Jove*[^12]. Varro believes that he is worshipped even by those who worship one God alone without an image, but called by another name[^13]. If this is so, why was he so badly treated at Rome—as indeed among the other nations too—that an image was made for him? This displeases Varro himself so much that, though pressed by the perverse custom of so great a city, he yet did not at all hesitate to say and to write that those who set up images for peoples have both taken away fear and added error[^14].

**10.** Why is the wife Juno also joined to him, who is called *sister and consort*[^15]? Because, they say, we take Jove to be in the ether, Juno in the air, and these two elements are joined, the one higher, the other lower. He, then, of whom it was said *all things are full of Jove,* is not the one who fills all things, if Juno too fills some part. Or does each fill both, and are both these consorts present, both in these two elements and in each one at once? Why, then, is the ether given to Jove, the air to Juno? Finally, these two themselves would be enough: how is it that the sea is assigned to Neptune, the earth to Pluto? And lest these too should remain without consorts, Salacia is added to Neptune, Proserpina to Pluto. For just as Juno holds the lower part of the sky, that is, the air, as they say[^16], so Salacia holds the lower part of the sea and Proserpina the lower part of the earth.

They seek how to patch up their fables, and do not find it. For if these things were so, their ancients would have declared that there are three elements of the world rather than four[^17], so that the several marriages of the gods might be divided among the several elements. But as it is, they have in every way affirmed that the ether is one thing, the air another. But water, whether higher or lower, is certainly water; suppose it is unlike: is it so unlike as not to be water? And what else can the lower earth be than earth, distinguished by however great a difference?

Then, behold, the corporeal world is now wholly completed in these four, or three, elements: where will Minerva be? What will she hold? What will she fill? For she is established together with these in the Capitol, though she is not the daughter of both. Or if they say that Minerva holds the higher part of the ether, and that on this occasion the poets feign that she was born from the head of Jove[^18]: why, then, is she not rather reckoned the queen of the gods, since she is higher than Jove? Or because it was unworthy to set a daughter above her father?

Why was that justice not preserved on the part of Jove himself toward Saturn? Or because he was conquered? Did they fight, then? Far be it, they say[^19]; that is the chatter of fables. Behold, let the fables not be believed, and let better things be thought of the gods: why, then, was there not given to the father of Jove a seat of honor, if not higher, then at least equal? Because Saturn, they say[^20], is length of time.

Time, then, do they worship who worship Saturn, and Jove, the king of the gods, is insinuated to have been born of time. For what unworthy thing is said, when Jove and Juno are said to be born of time, if he is heaven and she earth, since heaven and earth were certainly made? For their learned and wise men have this too in their books[^21].

And it was not from poetic figments, but from the books of the philosophers, that Virgil said:  
*then the almighty father, the ether, descends in fruitful showers into the lap of his joyful consort*[^22]—that is, into the lap of the ground or the earth; for here too they wish there to be certain differences, and within the earth itself they think one thing Terra, another Tellus, another Tellumo, and they hold all these as gods called by their own names, distinguished by their own offices, venerated with their own altars and rites[^23]. The same Earth they also call the mother of the gods[^24], so that the poets now feign more tolerable things, if, according to their books not of poets but of sacred rites, Juno is not only the *sister and consort,* but even the mother of Jove. The same Earth they would have to be Ceres, and the same to be Vesta[^25], though they more often declare Vesta to be nothing but the fire belonging to the hearths, without which a city cannot exist, and that for this reason virgins are wont to serve her, because, as nothing is born from a virgin, so nothing is born from fire. All which vanity it certainly behooved to be abolished and extinguished by him who was born of a virgin.

For who could bear it, that, when they have ascribed so much honor and, as it were, chastity to fire, they sometimes do not blush even to call Vesta Venus, so that the honored virginity vanishes in her handmaids? For if Vesta is Venus, how have virgins rightly served her by abstaining from the works of Venus? Or are there two Venuses, one a virgin, the other a woman? Or rather three, one of virgins, who is also Vesta, another of the married, another of harlots? To whom the Phoenicians too used to give a gift from the prostitution of their daughters, before they joined them to husbands. Which of these is the matron of Vulcan? Not the virgin, surely, since she has a husband. But far be it that she should be the harlot, lest we seem to do injury to the son of Juno and the fellow worker of Minerva. She is understood, then, to pertain to the married women; but we do not wish them to imitate her in what she did with Mars.

"Again," they say, "you return to the fables." What sort of justice is this—to be angry with us because we say such things about their gods, and not to be angry with themselves, who most gladly watch in the theaters these crimes of their own gods? And (what would be incredible, were it not most attestedly proved) these very theatrical crimes of the gods were instituted in honor of those same gods.

## . . . on the worship of Jove and the gods assigned to morals and to boys.

**11.** Whatever, then, they may assert by physical reasonings and disputations[^26]: now let Jove be the soul of this corporeal world, who fills and moves that whole mass constructed and compacted of four elements—or of however many they please—now let him yield his own parts from it to his sister and brothers; now let him be the ether, so that from above he embraces Juno, the air, spread beneath him; now let him be the whole sky together with the air, and let him impregnate the earth—as his consort and likewise his mother (because this is not base among the gods)—with fruitful showers and seeds;

and now (lest it be necessary to run through all of them) let him be the one god, of whom many think the most noble poet said:  
*for God goes through all lands and the tracts of the sea and the deep heaven*[^27]; let him be Jove in the ether, the same Juno in the air, the same Neptune in the sea, the same Salacia in the lower parts of the sea too, Pluto in the earth, Proserpina in the lower earth, Vesta in the domestic hearths, Vulcan in the smiths' furnace, the sun and the moon and the stars among the heavenly bodies, Apollo among diviners, Mercury in merchandise, Janus the beginner, Terminus the ender, Saturn in time, Mars and Bellona in wars, Liber in the vineyards, Ceres in the grain, Diana in the woods, Minerva in talents[^28];

let him be, finally, even in that throng of, as it were, plebeian gods; let him preside, under the name of Liber, over the seeds of males, and under the name of Libera over those of females; let him be Diespater, who brings the offspring forth to the day; let him be the goddess Mena, whom they set over the menstrual flows of women; let him be Lucina, who is invoked by women in labor;

let him bring help to those being born by receiving them in the lap of the earth, and be called Ops; let him open the mouth in wailing and be called the god Vaticanus; let him lift from the earth and be called the goddess Levana; let him guard the cradles and be called the goddess Cunina; let no other, but he himself, be in those goddesses who sing the fates to the newborn and are called the Carmentes;

let him preside over chance events and be called Fortune; in the goddess Rumina let him give the breast to the little one—because the ancients called the breast *ruma*—in the goddess Potina let him minister drink, in the goddess Educa offer food; from the fright of infants let him be named Paventia, from hope that comes, Venilia, from pleasure, Volupia, from action, Agenoria;

from the goads by which a man is driven to excessive action let him be named the goddess Stimula; let him be the goddess Strenia by making men vigorous, Numeria who teaches counting, Camena who teaches singing; let him be both the god Consus by furnishing counsel and the goddess Sentia by inspiring opinions;

let him be the goddess Iuventas, who receives the beginnings of the youthful age after the boy's toga; let him be also bearded Fortune, who clothes the grown men with a beard (whom they were unwilling to honor so far as at least to name this deity, such as it is, a male god, calling it either Barbatus from the beard, as Nodutus from the knots, or at any rate not Fortuna but, because it has beards, Fortunius);

let him in the god Iugatinus join consorts, and when the girdle is loosed for the virgin wife, let him be invoked and be called the goddess Virginensis; let him be Mutunus, or Tutunus, who among the Greeks is Priapus[^29]:

if it does not shame them, let all these things that I have said, and whatever I have not said (for I have not thought everything ought to be said), all these gods and goddesses be the one Jove—whether, as some would have it[^30], all these are his parts, or his powers, as it seems to those to whom it is pleasing that he should be the soul of the world[^31]—which opinion is, as it were, that of great and very learned men.

If these things are so (and what sort of thing this may be I do not yet for the moment inquire), what would they lose if they worshipped one God by a more prudent shortcut? For what part of him would be despised, when he himself were worshipped? But if it had to be feared lest his parts, passed over or neglected, should grow angry, then this is not, as they will have it, the whole single life of one living being which contains all the gods as its own powers or members or parts; but each part has its own life separate from the rest, if one part can grow angry apart from another, and one be appeased, another stirred up.

But if it is said that all together—that is, the whole of Jove himself—could have been offended, if his parts were not also worshipped one by one and bit by bit, it is foolishly said. For none of them would be passed over, when he himself, the one who has all things, were worshipped. For, to omit other things, which are countless, when they say that all the stars are parts of Jove, and that all live and have rational souls, and are therefore without controversy gods[^32], they do not see how many they do not worship, to how many they build no temples, set up no altars—which they nevertheless thought should be set up for very few of the stars, and that to these sacrifice should be made one by one.

If, then, those grow angry who are not worshipped one by one, do they not fear, with a few appeased, to live with the whole heaven angry? But if they worship all the stars for the reason that they are in Jove, whom they worship, by this shortcut they could supplicate them all in that one (for thus no one would grow angry, since in that one no one would be despised), rather than that, certain ones being worshipped, a just cause of anger be furnished to those who were passed over, who are far more numerous—especially when, before those gleaming from the lofty seat, Priapus, distended in shameful nakedness, would be set.

## Whether Jove is the world itself or its soul . . .

**12.** What? Ought not this to move sharp men—or men of any sort whatever (for excellence of talent is not needed for this)—that, laying aside the zeal for contention, they should attend, if the soul of the world is God and the world is to that soul as a body, so that there is one living being consisting of soul and body, and this God contains all things in himself in a certain bosom of nature, so that from his own soul, by which this whole mass is given life, the lives and souls of all living things are taken according to each one's lot of being born, then nothing at all remains that is not a part of God? But if this is so, who does not see how great an impiety and irreligion follows—that whatever anyone treads on, he treads on a part of God, and in killing every living thing a part of God is slaughtered? I do not wish to say all the things that can occur to those who ponder, but they cannot be said without shame.

**13.** But if they contend that only rational living beings, such as men are, are parts of God, I do not indeed see, if the whole world is God, how they separate the beasts from his parts; but what need is there to wrangle? Of the rational living being itself, that is, man, what more unhappy thing can be believed than that a part of God is thrashed, when a boy is thrashed? And then who could bear that parts of God should become wanton, unjust, impious, and altogether damnable, unless one who is utterly insane? Finally, why is he angry with those by whom he is not worshipped, when he is not worshipped by his own parts?

It remains, then, for them to say that all the gods have their own lives, that each lives for himself, that none of them is a part of any other, but that all are to be worshipped who can be known and worshipped—since they are so many that all cannot be.

Of whom, because Jove presides as king, I believe they think that he himself established or enlarged the Roman kingdom. For if he himself did not do this, what other god could they believe was able to undertake so great a work, since all are occupied with their own offices and works, nor does one break in upon another's? By the king of the gods, then, the kingdom of men could be propagated and enlarged.

## Why, if many gods favor the enlarging of the empire, Victory should favor it.

**14.** Here first I ask, why is not the kingdom itself also some god? For why should it not be so, if Victory is a goddess? Or what need is there of Jove himself in this matter, if Victory favors and is propitious and always goes to those whom she wishes to be victors? With this goddess favoring and propitious, even with Jove at leisure or doing something else, what nations would remain unsubdued? What kingdoms would not yield?

Or does it perhaps displease good men to fight with most iniquitous wickedness, and to provoke quiet neighbors who do no injury to a war undertaken of one's own accord, in order to extend the kingdom? Plainly, if they feel thus, I approve and praise it.

**15.** Let them see, then, whether perhaps it does not pertain to good men to rejoice over the breadth of the kingdom. For the iniquity of those with whom just wars were waged helped the kingdom to grow,

which would certainly be small if the quiet and justice of the neighbors provoked, by no injury, no war to be waged against them; and thus, with human affairs happier, all kingdoms would be small, rejoicing in concordant neighborliness, and there would be very many kingdoms of nations in the world, as there are very many houses of citizens in a city.

Accordingly, to wage war and, with nations subdued, to extend the kingdom seems felicity to the bad, necessity to the good. But because it would be worse for the injurious to lord it over the more just, therefore this too is not unfittingly called felicity. But without doubt the greater felicity is to have a good neighbor at peace than to subjugate a bad neighbor by war. They are evil wishes, to wish to have one whom you may hate or fear, so that there may be one whom you can conquer.

If, then, by waging just wars—not impious, not iniquitous—the Romans were able to acquire so great an empire, is even the iniquity of others to be worshipped by them as a kind of goddess? For we see that it cooperated much toward this breadth of empire, the iniquity which made men injurious, so that there might be those with whom just wars could be waged and the empire enlarged. But why should not iniquity, even of foreign nations, be a goddess, if Fear and Pallor and Fever deserved to be Roman gods? By these two, then—that is, by the iniquity of others and the goddess Victory—while iniquity stirs up the causes of wars and the same Victory brings those wars to a happy end, the empire grew, even with Jove on holiday.

For what part would Jove have here, when the things that could be thought his benefits are held to be gods, are called gods, are worshipped as gods, and are themselves invoked for their own parts?

But he too would have some part here, if he himself were also called Kingdom, just as that goddess is called Victory. Or if the kingdom is the gift of Jove, why is not victory too held to be his gift? Which assuredly would be so held, if not a stone in the Capitol, but the true *King of kings and Lord of lords*[^33] were acknowledged and worshipped.

**16.** I wonder most of all that, when they assigned single gods to single things and almost to single movements—calling the goddess Agenoria, who roused to action; the goddess Stimula, who goaded to action beyond measure; the goddess Murcia, who would not move a man beyond measure and would make him, as Pomponius says[^34], *murcidus,* that is, too slothful and inactive; the goddess Strenia, who would make a man vigorous—they undertook to perform public rites for all these gods and goddesses, but "Quiet," who would make a man quiet, although she had a temple outside the Colline gate[^35], they were unwilling to receive publicly. Was this an indication of an unquiet mind, or was it rather thus signified that whoever should persist in worshipping that throng—plainly not of gods but of demons—could not have quiet? To which the true Physician calls, saying: *learn from me, for I am gentle and lowly in heart, and you will find rest for your souls*[^36].

**17.** Or do they perhaps say that Jove sends the goddess Victory, and that she, obeying him as the king of the gods, comes to those to whom he commands and takes her seat on their side? This is truly said, not of that Jove whom they feign, in their own opinion, to be the king of the gods, but of that true King of the ages: that he sends not Victory, who is no substance, but his angel, and makes whom he will to conquer—whose counsel can be hidden, but cannot be iniquitous.

For if Victory is a goddess, why is not Triumph also a god, and joined to Victory either as husband or brother or son? For these men held such opinions about the gods as, if the poets had feigned them and we had attacked them, they would answer were laughable figments of the poets, not to be attributed to true deities; and yet they did not laugh at themselves, when they read such ravings not in the poets but worshipped them in the temples. Let them, then, ask Jove for all things, supplicate him alone. For Victory, if she is a goddess and is under that king, could not, wherever he had sent her, dare to resist him and rather do her own will.

## Whether Felicity and Fortune are goddesses.

**18.** What of the fact that Felicity too is a goddess? She received a temple, deserved an altar, and fitting rites were paid her. She, then, alone should be worshipped. For where she herself was, what good would not be there? But what does it mean, that Fortune too is thought a goddess and worshipped? Is felicity one thing, fortune another? Because fortune can also be bad; but felicity, if it were bad, would not be felicity. Certainly we ought to esteem all the gods of either sex (if they even have a sex) to be none but good. This Plato says[^37], this other philosophers, this the excellent rulers of the republic and of peoples.

How, then, is the goddess Fortune sometimes good, sometimes bad? Or is it perhaps that, when she is bad, she is not a goddess, but is suddenly turned into a malignant demon? How many, then, are these goddesses? Assuredly as many as there are fortunate men, that is, men of good fortune. For since there are also very many others at the same time—that is, at one time—of bad fortune, would she, if she were the one goddess, be at the same time both good and bad, this to these, that to those? Or is that one who is a goddess always good? She, then, is Felicity herself: why are different names applied?

But this is to be borne; for one thing is wont to be called by two names. What of the different temples, the different altars, the different rites? There is a reason, they say[^38]: because felicity is that which the good have through preceding merits; but fortune, which is called good, befalls men, both good and bad, by chance, without any weighing of merits—whence it is even named Fortune. How, then, is that good which comes to both the good and the bad without any judgment?

But to what end is she worshipped who is so blind, running everywhere upon whomever she meets, that for the most part she passes over her own worshippers and clings to her despisers? Or if her worshippers profit anything, so that they are seen and loved by her, then she follows merits and does not come by chance. Where is that definition of Fortune? Where is the fact that she received her very name from chance events? For it does no good to worship her, if she is fortune. But if she discerns her own worshippers, so as to profit them, she is not fortune.

Or does Jove send her too wherever he wishes? Let him, then, alone be worshipped; for Fortune cannot resist him when he commands and sends her wherever he wishes. Or at any rate let the bad worship her, who do not wish to have the merits by which the goddess Felicity could be invited.

**19.** So much, indeed, do they ascribe to this thing they call Fortune, as if to a deity, that they committed to memory that her image—which was dedicated by the matrons and called Womanly Fortune—even spoke, and said not once but twice, that the matrons had duly dedicated her[^39]. If indeed this is true, we ought not to wonder. For it is not so hard even thus for malignant demons to deceive,

whose arts and cunning they ought rather to have noticed from this: that she spoke who is a goddess that befalls by chance, not she who comes by merits. For Fortune was talkative and Felicity mute—to what end, except that men should not care to live rightly, having won over to themselves Fortune, who would make them fortunate without any good merits?

And certainly, if Fortune speaks, let her speak not at least as a woman but rather as a man, so that the very women who dedicated the image might not be thought to have feigned so great a miracle by womanly talkativeness.

## What goddesses Virtue and Faith are.

**20.** Virtue too they made a goddess; and indeed, if she were a goddess, she would have had to be preferred to many. And now, because she is not a goddess but a gift of God, let her be obtained from him by whom alone she can be given, and the whole throng of false gods will vanish.

But why was Faith too believed a goddess and herself also given a temple and an altar? Whoever prudently acknowledges her makes himself her dwelling place. But how do they know what faith is, whose first and greatest office is that the true God be believed in? But why was Virtue not enough? Is not Faith there too? Since indeed they saw that virtue is to be distributed into four kinds—prudence, justice, fortitude, temperance[^40]; and since these single kinds too have their own parts, faith is among the parts of justice and holds the greatest place with us, whoever we are who know what it is that *the just man lives by faith*[^41].

But I wonder at those seekers of a multitude of gods, why, if Faith is a goddess, they did injury to so many other goddesses by passing them over—goddesses to whom they could likewise have dedicated temples and altars. Why did temperance not deserve to be a goddess, when by her name some Roman princes acquired no small glory? Why, finally, is fortitude not a goddess, who was present with Mucius when he held out his right hand into the flames; who was present with Curtius when he hurled himself headlong for his homeland into the broken earth; who was present with Decius the father and Decius the son when they vowed themselves for the army? If indeed true fortitude was in all these[^42]—of which there is now no question. Why did prudence, why did wisdom deserve no places among the deities?

Or is it because they are all worshipped under the general name of virtue itself? In that way, then, one God could also be worshipped, of whom the rest of the gods are thought to be parts. But in that one virtue there are both faith and chastity, which nevertheless deserved altars outside, in their own temples.

## Felicity is especially to be invoked, because she is from God himself.

**21.** These goddesses are made not by truth but by vanity; for these are the gifts of the true God, not themselves goddesses. Yet where there are virtue and felicity, what else is sought? What is enough for him for whom virtue and felicity are not enough? For virtue embraces all that is to be done, felicity all that is to be wished. If Jove was worshipped in order to give these—because, if the breadth and long duration of a kingdom is anything good, it pertains to that same felicity—why was it not understood that these are gifts of God, not goddesses?

But if they were thought goddesses, at least so great a throng of other gods would not be sought. For when the offices of all the gods and goddesses have been considered, which they feigned as they pleased according to their own opinion, let them find, if they can, anything that could be furnished by any god to a man who has virtue and has felicity. What learning would have to be sought either from Mercury or from Minerva, when virtue had all things with itself? For the art itself of living well and rightly was defined by the ancients as virtue[^43]. Whence they thought that from that which in Greek is called ἀρετή, "virtue," the Latins derived the word "art" (*ars*)[^44]. But if virtue could come only to the talented, what need was there of the god Catius the father, who would make men *cati,* that is, sharp, when felicity could confer this?

The ancients defined virtue as the very art of living well and rightly[^45]. Hence they thought that the Latins derived their word for “art” from the Greek ἀρετή, meaning “virtue”[^46]. But if virtue could come only to someone intelligent, what need was there of the god Father Catius to make people cati, that is, sharp-witted, when happiness could bestow this?

For to be born talented belongs to felicity; whence, even though the goddess Felicity could not be worshipped by one not yet born, so as to grant him this when won over, she would confer this upon his parents, her worshippers, so that talented children might be born to them.

What need was there for women in labor to invoke Lucina, when, if Felicity were present, they would not only bear well, but bear good children? What necessity was there to commend the newborn to the goddess Ops, the wailing to the god Vaticanus, those lying down to the goddess Cunina, those sucking to the goddess Rumina, those standing to the god Statilinus, those approaching to the goddess Adeona, those departing to Abeona;

to the goddess Mind, that they might have a good mind; to the god Volumnus and the goddess Volumna, that they might will good things; to the nuptial gods, that they might be well married; to the rural gods, that they might take most abundant fruits, and especially to the goddess Fructesea herself; to Mars and Bellona, that they might war well; to the goddess Victory, that they might conquer; to the god Honor, that they might be honored; to the goddess Money, that they might be moneyed; to the god Aesculanus and his son Argentinus, that they might have bronze and silver money?

For they made Aesculanus the father of Argentinus for this reason: because bronze money first came into use, afterward silver. But I wonder that Argentinus did not beget Aurinus, since gold money too followed. If these men had such a god, then, as they set Jove above Saturn, so they would set Aurinus above his father Argentinus and his grandfather Aesculanus.

What need was there, then, on account of these goods—whether of the mind or of the body or external—to worship and invoke so great a throng of gods (whom I have neither all recounted, nor could they themselves provide petty and single gods for all human goods parceled out bit by bit and one by one), when by a great and easy shortcut the one goddess Felicity could confer all things, and no other be sought, either for gaining goods or even for driving away evils?

For why should the goddess Fessona be invoked on account of the weary, the goddess Pellonia for driving away enemies, a physician—whether Apollo or Aesculapius or both together—on account of the sick, when there was great danger? Nor would the god Spiniensis be asked, to root the thorns out of the fields; nor the goddess Robigo, that she might not come near: with Felicity alone present and protecting, either no evils would arise, or they would be most easily driven away.

Finally, since we are treating of these two goddesses, Virtue and Felicity: if felicity is the reward of virtue, it is not a goddess but a gift of God; but if she is a goddess, why may she not be said to confer virtue too, since indeed to attain virtue is itself a great felicity?

## Varro is a witness that something belongs to each god.

**22.** What is it, then, that Varro boasts of furnishing his fellow citizens as an immense benefit, namely that he not only recounts the gods whom the Romans ought to worship, but also says what pertains to each one? *Since it does no good,* he says, *to know the name and form of some man, a physician, and to be ignorant that he is a physician: so, he says, it does no good to know that Aesculapius is a god, if you do not know that he aids in health, and so are ignorant why you ought to supplicate him.* He affirms this also by another comparison, saying that *no one can not only live well, but live at all, if he is ignorant who is a smith, who a baker, who a plasterer, from whom he can seek what utensil, whom he may take as a helper, whom as a guide, whom as a teacher*; asserting that in this way it is doubtful to no one that the knowledge of the gods is thus useful, if it is known what power and capacity and authority each god has over each thing. *For from this,* he says, *we shall be able to know what god we ought to call upon and invoke for the sake of each thing, lest we do as the mimes are wont, and ask water from Liber, wine from the Lymphs*[^47].

A great usefulness, to be sure. Who would not give him thanks, if he showed true things, and if he taught men to worship the one true God, from whom all good things come?

## Felicity a recent goddess . . .

**23.** 1. But (and this is the present question) if their books and rites are true and Felicity is a goddess, why was not she alone appointed to be worshipped, who could confer all things and make a man happy by a shortcut? For who wishes anything for any other reason than that he may become happy? Why, finally, so late, after so many Roman princes, did Lucullus set up a temple to this so great goddess? Why did Romulus himself, desiring to found a happy city, not build a temple to her above all, nor supplicate the other gods for anything, when nothing would be lacking if she were present? For he himself would have been neither king first, nor, as they think, a god afterward, if he had not had this goddess propitious.

To what end, then, did he establish for the Romans the gods Janus, Jove, Mars, Picus, Faunus, Tiberinus, Hercules, and any others? To what end did Titus Tatius add Saturn, Ops, Sol, Luna, Vulcan, Lux, and whatever others he added, among whom even the goddess Cloacina, while Felicity was neglected? To what end did Numa establish so many gods and so many goddesses without her? Or could he perhaps not see her in so great a throng? King Hostilius certainly would not have introduced new gods of his own, Fear and Pallor, to be propitiated, if he had known or worshipped this goddess. For with Felicity present, all fear and pallor would depart, not propitiated, but driven off and put to flight.

**23.** 2. Then what is this, that the Roman empire was already growing far and wide, and as yet no one worshipped felicity? Was the empire on that account greater than it was happier? For how could there be true felicity there, where there was no true piety? For piety is the truthful worship of the true God, not the worship of false gods, as many as there are demons. But even afterward, when Felicity had now been received into the number of the gods, a great unhappiness of civil wars followed. Or was Felicity perhaps justly indignant that she was invited both so late and not to honor but rather to insult—so that, along with her, Priapus and Cloacina and Fear and Pallor and Fever and the rest were worshipped, not the deities of the worshippers but the crimes of those who worshipped?

**23.** 3. Finally, if so great a goddess seemed fit to be worshipped along with that most unworthy throng, why was she not at least worshipped more illustriously than the rest? For who could bear that Felicity was placed neither among the Consenting gods, whom they say are taken into the council of Jove[^48], nor among the gods whom they call the "Select"[^49]? Some temple should have been made for her that surpassed the rest both in loftiness of place and in dignity of work. For why not something better than for Jove himself?

For what gave even Jove his kingdom but Felicity? If indeed he was happy while he reigned. And felicity is preferable to a kingdom. For no one doubts that a man can easily be found who would fear to be made a king; but none is found who would not wish to be happy. The gods themselves, then, if men think they can be consulted by auguries or in any way whatever, should be consulted on this matter: whether they would be willing to yield place to Felicity, if perhaps a place had already been occupied by the temples or altars of others where a temple greater and loftier might be built for Felicity; even Jove himself would yield, so that Felicity rather might hold the very summit of the Capitoline hill. For no one would resist Felicity, unless—what cannot be—one who wished to be unhappy.

In no way at all, if he were consulted, would Jove do what three gods did to him—Mars, Terminus, and Iuventas[^50]—who were in no way willing to yield place to their greater and their king. For, as their writings have it, when King Tarquinius wished the Capitol to be built, and saw that the place which seemed to him more worthy and more fitting was preoccupied by other gods, not daring to do anything against their will, and believing that they would yield by their own will to so great a deity and their own prince,

because there were many of them there where the Capitol was established, he inquired by augury whether they would be willing to concede the place to Jove; and they all wished to yield thence except those whom I mentioned, Mars, Terminus, and Iuventas; and therefore the Capitol was so constructed that these three too were inside, with signs so obscure that scarcely the most learned men knew it.

In no way, then, would Jove himself despise Felicity, as he was despised by Terminus, Mars, and Iuventas. But even those who had not yielded to Jove would surely yield to Felicity, who had made Jove king for them. Or if they did not yield, they would do so not out of contempt for her, but because they would prefer to be obscure in the house of Felicity than to be eminent in their own places without her.

**23.** 4. Thus, with the goddess Felicity established in the most ample and most lofty place, the citizens would learn whence the help of every good wish was to be sought; and so, nature itself persuading, the superfluous multitude of other gods being abandoned, Felicity alone would be worshipped, she alone supplicated,

her temple alone frequented by the citizens who wished to be happy—of whom there would be no one who would not—and so she herself would be sought from herself, who was sought by all.

For who would wish to receive anything from any god except felicity, or what he judges pertains to felicity? Accordingly, if felicity has in her power with what man she is (and she has, if she is a goddess): what folly, after all, is it to seek her from some god, whom you could obtain from herself?

This goddess, then, they ought to have honored above the other gods even by the dignity of her place. For, as is read among them[^51], the ancient Romans worshipped a certain Summanus, to whom they assigned the nocturnal thunderbolts, more than Jove, to whom the diurnal thunderbolts pertained. But after a notable and lofty temple was built for Jove, the multitude flocked to him on account of the dignity of the temple, so that one can scarcely be found who remembers that he has at least read the name of Summanus, which he can no longer hear.

But if felicity is not a goddess, since—what is true—she is a gift of God: let that God be sought who can give her, and let the harmful multitude of false gods be abandoned, which the vain multitude of foolish men pursues, making for itself gods out of the gifts of God and offending him whose gifts they are by the obstinacy of a proud will. For thus he cannot lack unhappiness who worships felicity as a goddess and abandons God the giver of felicity, just as he cannot lack hunger who licks a painted loaf of bread and does not ask it of a man who has a real one.

## A physical explanation is set forth, that the gods are the gifts of the one God . . .

**24.** But it is worthwhile to consider their reasonings. Is it to be believed, they say, that our ancestors were so unwise as not to know that these things were divine gifts, not gods[^52]? But since they knew that such things were granted to no one except by the bounty of some god, they called by the names of those things the gods whom they did not find named, gods whom they felt to be the givers of the things they perceived to be given by them,

inflecting some words from them—as from war (*bellum*) they named Bellona, not Bellum; as from cradles (*cunae*) Cunina, not Cuna; as from crops (*segetes*) Segetia, not Seges; as from fruits (*poma*) Pomona, not Pomum; as from oxen (*boves*) Bubona, not Bos: or else with no inflection of the word, just as the things themselves are named, as Pecunia (Money) was called a goddess who gives money, money itself not being thought a goddess at all; so Virtue, who gives virtue, Honor, who gives honor, Concord, who gives concord, Victory, who gives victory. So, they say, when felicity is called a goddess, it is not the felicity itself that is given that is meant, but that deity by whom felicity is given.

**25.** This explanation having been rendered us, we shall perhaps much more easily persuade those whose heart has not become too hardened of what we wish. For if human weakness has now felt that felicity cannot be given except by some god, and if men felt this who worshipped so many gods, among them their very king Jove: because they were ignorant of the name of him by whom felicity was given, they wished to call him by the name of the very thing which they believed was given by him; they sufficiently indicated, then, that felicity could not be given even by Jove himself, whom they were already worshipping, but certainly by him whom they judged ought to be worshipped under the name of felicity itself.

I affirm outright that they believed felicity to be given by a certain god whom they did not know: let him, then, be sought, let him be worshipped, and it is enough. Let the din of innumerable demons be repudiated; let this god not be enough for the man for whom his gift is not enough. Let not God the giver of felicity, I say, be enough to worship for him for whom felicity itself is not enough to receive. But for him for whom she is enough (for a man has nothing more that he ought to wish), let him serve the one God, the giver of felicity. He is not the one whom they name Jove.

For if they acknowledged him as the giver of felicity, they would surely not seek another god or goddess, by whom felicity might be given, under the name of Felicity itself, nor would they think Jove himself ought to be worshipped with so great outrages. For he is called the adulterer of others' wives, the shameless lover and ravisher of a beautiful boy.

## The gods demand that theatrical games be assigned to them.

**26.** But *Homer feigned these things,* says Cicero, *and transferred human things to the gods: I would rather he transferred divine things to us*[^53]. Deservedly did the poet, the fabricator of divine crimes, displease a serious man. Why, then, are the theatrical games, where these things are recited, sung, and acted, exhibited for the honors of the gods, and written down among divine matters by the most learned men?

Here let Cicero cry out, not against the figments of the poets, but against the institutions of the ancestors—or let those ancestors cry out too: *What have we done! The gods themselves demanded that these things be exhibited for their honors, fiercely commanded them, foretold disaster unless it were done, most severely avenged it because something was neglected, and showed that they were appeased because what had been neglected was done.*

Among their virtues and marvelous deeds is recounted what I shall now relate. To Titus Latinius, a Roman countryman and father of a family, it was said in his sleep that he should announce to the senate that the Roman games be renewed, because on the first day of them, in a certain criminal who, while the people watched, had been ordered to be led to punishment, the sad command had displeased the deities, who, namely, were seeking merriment from the games.

When, then, that man who had been admonished by the dream did not dare to do what was commanded the next day, on the second night this same thing was commanded again more severely: he lost his son, because he did not do it. On the third night it was said to the man that a greater punishment threatened him if he did not do it. When even thus he did not dare, he fell into a sharp and horrible illness. Then indeed, on the advice of his friends, he laid the matter before the magistrates and was brought into the senate in a litter; and when he had told his dream, his health was immediately restored, and he departed sound on his own feet. Stupefied by so great a miracle, the senate decreed that the games be renewed with quadrupled money[^54].

Who that has sound understanding does not see that men subject to malignant demons—from whose domination nothing frees us but the grace of God through Jesus Christ our Lord[^55]—were compelled by force to exhibit to such gods things that could by right counsel be judged base?

In those games, certainly, the poetic crimes of the deities are frequented—those games which, the deities compelling, were renewed by command of the senate. In those games the most base actors sang, acted, and gave pleasure with Jove the corruptor of chastity.

If that was feigned, he would be angry; but if he delighted even in his crimes, fictitious though they were, then how could he be worshipped, unless the devil were served? Would he, then, found, extend, and preserve the Roman empire—a being more abject than any Roman man to whom such things were displeasing? Would he give felicity, who was worshipped so unhappily, and, unless he were so worshipped, grew angry more unhappily still?

## Scaevola, rejecting images, says there are three kinds of gods.

**27.** It has been committed to writing that the most learned pontiff Scaevola disputed that three kinds of gods have been handed down: one by the poets, another by the philosophers, the third by the leaders of the state. The first kind he says is trifling, because many unworthy things are feigned about the gods; the second does not suit cities, because it has some things that are superfluous, some too that it harms the peoples to know. Of the superfluous there is no great concern; for it is also wont to be said by the jurists: *superfluous things do no harm*[^56].

But what are those things which, brought out to the multitude, do harm? *That these,* he says, *are not gods—Hercules, Aesculapius, Castor, Pollux; for it is reported by the learned that they were men and ended by the human condition.* What else? *That the cities do not have true images of those who are gods, because the true God has neither sex nor age nor defined members of a body*[^57]. These things the pontiff does not wish the peoples to know; for he does not think them false. He judges, therefore, that it is expedient for cities to be deceived in religion. This Varro himself too does not hesitate to say in his books *On Divine Matters*[^58]. A splendid religion, to which the weak man should flee to be freed—and when he seeks the truth by which he may be freed, it is believed expedient for him that he be deceived!

Why Scaevola rejects the poetic kind of gods is not passed over in silence in the same writings: namely because they so deform the gods that they are not even comparable to good men,

when they make one steal, another commit adultery, and likewise one say or do something else basely and ineptly; that three goddesses contended among themselves for the prize of beauty, and the two conquered by Venus overthrew Troy; that Jove himself was turned into a bull or a swan, in order to lie with some woman; that a goddess married a man, that Saturn devoured his children: in short, that no monstrous deeds or vices can be feigned that are not found there and that are far removed from the nature of the gods.

O Scaevola, supreme pontiff, abolish the games, if you can; bid the peoples not to offer such honors to the immortal gods, where it is pleasing to admire the crimes of the gods and pleasing to imitate what can be done.

But if the people answer you: "You pontiffs brought these things in upon us": ask the gods themselves, at whose instigation you commanded these things, that they not bid such things to be exhibited to themselves. And if these things are evil and therefore in no way to be believed of the majesty of the gods, the greater is the injury done to the gods, about whom they are feigned with impunity.

But they do not hear you, they are demons, they teach depraved things, they rejoice in base things: not only do they not reckon it an injury if such things are feigned about them, but they rather cannot bear the injury if these things are not enacted at their solemnities. And now, if against them you appeal to Jove—especially for this cause, that more of his crimes are enacted in the theatrical games: even though you call Jove God, by whom this whole world is ruled and administered, is not the greatest injury done him by you in this, that you think he ought to be worshipped along with these, and declare him to be their king?

## The gods could not enlarge the empire.

**28.** In no way, then, could such gods—who are appeased, or rather accused, by such honors, so that it is a greater crime that they are delighted by false things about them than if true things were said—have enlarged and preserved the Roman empire. For if they could do this, they would rather confer so great a gift on the Greeks, who in divine matters of this kind, that is, theatrical games, worshipped them more honorably and more worthily, since they both did not withdraw themselves from the bites of the poets, by whom they saw the gods torn apart—giving them license to treat badly whatever men they pleased—and did not judge the actors themselves base, but held them worthy even of illustrious honors[^59].

But just as the Romans could have gold money, although they did not worship the god Aurinus: so they could have silver and bronze money too, if they worshipped neither Argentinus nor his father Aesculanus, and likewise all the things which it irks me to recount again. So, then, a kingdom too they could in no way have had with the true God unwilling; but with these false and many gods unknown or despised, and that one God known and worshipped with sincere faith and good morals, they would both have a kingdom here the better, however great a one they had, and afterward receive an everlasting one—whether they had one here or not.

## The auspices falsely prophesied that the Romans would never yield.

**29.** For what sort of thing is that which they said was a most beautiful auspice, which I recounted a little before—that Mars and Terminus and Iuventas were unwilling to yield place even to Jove, the king of the gods? For thus, they say, it was signified that the Martian nation, that is, the Roman, would give to no one the place it held; that no one too would move the Roman boundaries, on account of the god Terminus; that the Roman youth too, on account of the goddess Iuventas, would yield to no one. Let them see, then, in what manner they hold this king of their gods and giver of their kingdom, that they set these auspices against him as against an adversary, to whom it would be beautiful not to yield.

And yet, if these things are true, they have nothing at all to fear. For they will not confess that the gods yielded to Christ, who were unwilling to yield to Jove; for, with the bounds of the empire safe, they could yield to Christ both from the seats of their places and especially from the heart of believers.

But before Christ had come in the flesh, before, finally, these things were written which we bring forward from their books, yet after that auspice was made under King Tarquinius, several times the Roman army was routed, that is, turned to flight, and showed the auspice false by which that Iuventas had not yielded to Jove; and the Martian nation was crushed, with the Gauls overcoming and bursting into the very City, and the bounds of the empire, with many states going over to Hannibal, had been confined within a narrow space. Thus the beauty of the auspices was emptied out, and there remained against Jove a defiance, not of gods but of demons. For it is one thing not to have yielded, another to have returned to a place from which you had yielded.

And yet afterward too, in the eastern parts, the bounds of the Roman empire were changed by the will of Hadrian. For he ceded three noble provinces—Armenia, Mesopotamia, Assyria—to the empire of the Persians[^60], so that that god Terminus, who according to these men guarded the Roman boundaries and through that most beautiful auspice had not yielded place to Jove, seems to have feared Hadrian, the king of men, more than the king of the gods. And when the aforesaid provinces had been recovered at another time, Terminus retreated backward almost within our own memory, when Julian, given over to the oracles of those gods, with immoderate daring ordered the ships to be burned in which provisions were carried; and the army, deprived of these, soon, with Julian himself too slain by an enemy's wound, was reduced to such want that none could have escaped from there—with the enemy attacking on every side a soldiery thrown into confusion by the emperor's death—unless by a pact of peace the bounds of the empire were there established where they persist even today, not indeed at so great a loss as Hadrian had conceded, but yet fixed by a middle settlement.

By a vain augury, then, the god Terminus did not yield to Jove, who yet yielded to the will of Hadrian, yielded too to the rashness of Julian and the necessity of Jovian[^61]. The more intelligent and more serious Romans saw these things; but against the custom of the city, which had been bound by demonic rites, they availed little, because they themselves too, even if they felt those things to be vain, yet thought that religious worship, which is owed to God, ought to be offered to the nature of things established under the rule and command of the one true God: *serving,* as the Apostle says, *the creature rather than the Creator, who is blessed forever*[^62]. The help of this true God was necessary, by whom holy men, truly pious, might be sent, to die for the true religion, so that the false religions might be taken away from the living.

## Cicero is indignant at the fables and the images.

**30.** Cicero the augur mocks the auguries and mocks men who order the counsels of their life by the voices of the raven and the crow[^63]. But this Academic, who contends that all things are uncertain, is unworthy to have any authority in these matters. In his work Quintus Lucilius Balbus disputes in the second book *On the Nature of the Gods,* and although he himself inserts the superstitions as, so to speak, physical and philosophical, derived from the nature of things, he is nevertheless indignant at the establishment of images and at the fabulous opinions, speaking thus: *Do you see, then, how reason has been drawn from physical things, well and usefully discovered, to fictitious and feigned gods? This thing has begotten false opinions and turbulent errors and superstitions almost old-wifely. For the forms of the gods, and their ages, and their clothing and adornment are known to us, their families besides, their marriages, their kinships, and all things translated to the likeness of human weakness.*

*For they are even introduced with disturbed minds; for we have received accounts of the gods' desires, ailments, and fits of anger. Nor indeed, as the fables relate, did the gods lack wars and battles; and not only, as in Homer, when other gods defended two opposing armies from opposite sides, but they even waged their own proper wars (as with the Titans or with the Giants). These things are both said and believed most foolishly, and are full of vanity and the utmost frivolity*[^64]. Behold, for the moment, what they confess who defend the gods of the gentiles.

Then, since he says that these things pertain to superstition, but to religion, which he himself seems to teach according to the Stoics: *for not only the philosophers,* he says, *but also our ancestors separated superstition from religion; for those who prayed whole days and made sacrifices that their children might survive them were called superstitious*[^65]. Who does not understand that he is trying, while he fears the custom of the city, to praise the religion of the ancestors and to wish to separate it from superstition, but does not find how he can do it?

For if those were called superstitious by the ancestors who prayed whole days and made sacrifices, then are not those too superstitious who established (what he reproaches) the images of the gods, distinguished by different age and dress, and the families, marriages, and kinships of the gods? When these things are blamed as superstitious, this blame implicates the ancestors who established and worshipped such images; it implicates him too, who, however much by his eloquence he strives to extricate himself into freedom, was obliged to venerate these things; nor would he dare to mutter in the assembly of the people what he loudly proclaims, eloquent, in this disputation.

Let us Christians, then, give thanks to the Lord our God—not to heaven and earth, as this man disputes, but to him who made heaven and earth—who has overthrown these superstitions, which this Balbus, as it were babbling (*balbutiens*), scarcely reproaches, through the most exalted humility of Christ, through the preaching of the Apostles, through the faith of the martyrs who die for the truth and live with the truth—overthrown them not only in religious hearts but even in superstitious temples, by the free service of his own.

**31.** 1. What of Varro himself, whom we grieve to have placed the theatrical games among divine matters—though not by his own judgment—when in many places he exhorts to the worship of the gods as though religious? Does he not so confess that he does not follow by his own judgment those things which he recounts the Roman state to have instituted, that he does not hesitate to confess that, if he were founding that state anew, he would have dedicated the gods and their names rather according to the rule of nature? But since he was now among an old people, he says he ought to keep the history of their names and surnames received from the ancients as it was handed down, and to write and search out those things to this end, that the common people should rather wish to worship the gods more than to despise them.

By which words a most acute man sufficiently indicates that he does not disclose everything—things which would be objects of contempt not only to himself, but would seem to the common people themselves things to be despised, unless they were passed over in silence.

I should be thought to be conjecturing these things, were it not that he himself, in another place, speaking of religions, evidently says that there are many true things which it is not useful for the common people to know, and also that, although they are false, it is expedient for the people to think otherwise; and that for this reason the Greeks shut up their rites and mysteries in silence and within walls.

Here certainly he betrayed the whole plan, as it were, of the wise men by whom the cities and peoples were ruled. Yet by this deceit the malignant demons are delighted in marvelous ways, who possess both the deceivers and the deceived alike—from whose domination nothing frees us but the grace of God through Jesus Christ our Lord.

**31.** 2. The same most acute and most learned author also says that those alone seem to him to have perceived what God is, who believed him to be a soul governing the world by motion and reason; and through this, although he did not yet hold what the truth holds (for the true God is not a soul, but the maker and founder of the soul too), nevertheless, if he could have been free against the prejudices of custom, he would have confessed and urged that one God ought to be worshipped, governing the world by motion and reason—so that this question about that matter would remain with him: that he called him a soul, rather than also the Creator of the soul.

He also says that the ancient Romans worshipped the gods without an image for more than a hundred and seventy years. *And if this had continued,* he says, *the gods would be observed more chastely*[^66]. As a witness to this opinion of his he adduces, among others, the Jewish nation too; nor does he hesitate to conclude that passage by saying that those who first set up images of the gods for the peoples both took away fear from their cities and added error, prudently judging that the gods could easily be despised in the dullness of images.

But because he does not say *they handed down error,* but *they added* it: surely he wishes it to be understood that there was error even without images. Wherefore, when he says that those alone perceived what God is who believed him to be a soul governing the world, and judges that religion is more chastely observed without images, who does not see how near he came to the truth?

For if he could have availed anything against the antiquity of so great an error, he would assuredly have judged both that one God, by whom he believed the world to be governed, ought to be worshipped, and that without an image; and, found so near, he might perhaps easily have been admonished of the mutability of the soul, so as to perceive that the true God is rather an immutable nature, which had founded the soul itself as well.

Since these things are so, whatever mockeries of the many gods such men set down in their writings, they were compelled to confess them by the hidden will of God rather than tried to persuade them. If, then, any testimonies are brought forward by us from there, they are brought forward to refute those who refuse to consider from how great and how malignant a power of demons the singular sacrifice of so holy a blood poured out, and the gift of the Spirit imparted, frees us.

**32.** He says also, concerning the generations of the gods, that the peoples were more inclined to the poets than to the natural philosophers, and that for this reason their ancestors, that is, the ancient Romans, believed in both the sex and the generations of the gods and established their marriages. Which surely seems to have been done for no other cause than that it was the business of men, as though prudent and wise, to deceive the people in religions, and in that very thing not only to worship but even to imitate the demons, whose greatest desire is to deceive. For just as demons cannot possess any but those whom they have deceived by deceiving, so men who are leaders—not indeed just, but like the demons—urged upon the peoples, under the name of religion, the things they knew to be vain, as though they were true, in this way binding them, as it were, the more fittingly to civil society, that they might likewise possess them as subjects. But who, weak and unlearned, could escape both the deceitful leaders of the city and the demons together?

## The most high God is true felicity.

**33.** That God, then, the author and giver of felicity, because he alone is the true God, himself gives earthly kingdoms both to the good and to the bad—and this not rashly and as if by chance, because he is God, not fortune, but according to the order of things and times, hidden from us but most known to himself; to which order of times, however, he is not made subject and a slave, but he himself rules it as lord and disposes it as governor: but felicity he gives only to the good. For this both slaves can lack and have, and rulers can lack and have; yet it will be full in that life where no one will any longer be a slave.

And therefore earthly kingdoms are given by him both to the good and to the bad, lest his worshippers, still little ones in the advancement of their mind, should covet these gifts from him as though something great.

And this is the mystery of the Old Testament, where the New was hidden—that there the promises and gifts are earthly, even to those who, then too, were spiritual, understanding, although not yet preaching it openly, both the eternity that was signified by those temporal things, and in what gifts of God true felicity lay.

**34.** And so, that it might be known that even those earthly goods, after which alone they gape who cannot think of better things, are placed in the power of the one God himself, not of the many false gods whom the Romans formerly believed ought to be worshipped, he multiplied his own people in Egypt from the very fewest and thence freed them by marvelous signs. Nor did those women invoke Lucina, when he himself preserved their offspring—that they might be multiplied in marvelous ways and that nation grow incredibly—from the hands of the Egyptians who pursued them and wished to kill all the infants.

Without the goddess Rumina they suckled, without Cunina they lay in cradles, without Educa and Potina they took food and drink, without so many childhood gods they were reared, without the marriage gods they were married, without the worship of Priapus they were joined to their spouses; without the invocation of Neptune the sea, divided, lay open to those crossing it and overwhelmed their pursuing enemies with the waves returning upon themselves. Nor did they consecrate any goddess Mannia, when they took manna from heaven; nor, when for the thirsty the struck rock poured forth water, did they worship the Nymphs and the Lymphs. Without the insane rites of Mars and Bellona they waged wars, and indeed they did not conquer without victory, yet they held it not as a goddess but as the gift of their God. Without Segetia they had crops, without Bubona oxen, honey without Mellona, fruits without Pomona, and absolutely all things for which the Romans thought so great a throng of false gods had to be supplicated, they received far more happily from the one true God.

And if they had not sinned against him—seduced by impious curiosity as though by magic arts, slipping away to strange gods and to idols, and at last by killing Christ—they would have remained in the same kingdom, even if not more spacious, yet happier. And now the fact that they are dispersed through almost all lands and nations is the providence of that one true God, so that, when everywhere the images, altars, groves, and temples of the false gods are overthrown and their sacrifices forbidden, it may be proved from their own books how this was prophesied so long before; lest perhaps, when it was read in ours, it should be thought by us to have been fabricated.
