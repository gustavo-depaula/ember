# Book IX: What Has Already Been Inquired Concerning the Good Gods and the Demons

**1.** Some have supposed that there are both good and evil gods; but others, thinking better things of the gods, attributed to them so much honor and praise that they did not dare to believe any of the gods evil. But those who said that some gods are good, some evil[^1], called the demons too by the name of gods, and the gods, though more rarely, by the name of demons, so that they admit that Jupiter himself, whom they would have to be king and chief of the rest, was called a demon by Homer[^2].

But those who assert that all the gods are none but good, and far more excellent than those men who are reported to be good, are rightly moved by the deeds of the demons, which they cannot deny, and, thinking that these can in no way be committed by the gods, whom they will all to be good, are compelled to apply a distinction between gods and demons, so that whatever rightly displeases them in depraved works or affections, by which the hidden spirits manifest their power, they believe to be of the demons, not of the gods.

But because they think these same demons are set as intermediaries between men and gods, in such a way that no god mingles with man, so that they carry desires hence and bring back things obtained thence—and the Platonists, the chief and most noble of the philosophers, hold this, with whom, as with the more excellent, it pleased us to examine this question, whether the worship of many gods profits for attaining the blessed life that is to be after death:

we inquired in the preceding book in what way the demons, who rejoice in such things as good and prudent men turn from and condemn—that is, the sacrilegious, shameful, and criminal figments of the poets, not about any man, but about the gods themselves, and the wicked and punishable violence of the magic arts—could, as nearer and more friendly to the gods, reconcile good men to the good gods; and it was found that this can by no reasoning be done.

**2.** Accordingly this book, as we promised at the end of that one, ought to contain a disputation about the difference (if they wish there to be any)—not of the gods among themselves, whom they all call good, nor about the difference of gods and demons, of whom they separate the former far and high from men and place the latter between gods and men—but about the difference of the demons themselves, so far as pertains to the present question.

For among very many it is customary to say that some demons are good, others evil; and whether this be the opinion of the Platonists or of any others, by no means is its discussion to be neglected,

lest anyone think that there are, as it were, good demons whom he ought to follow, through whom, as through intermediaries to the gods whom he believes all good, while he strives and is eager to be reconciled, that he may, as it were, be able to be with them after death,

he should, ensnared and deceived by the deceit of the malignant spirits, stray far from the true God, with whom alone and in whom alone and concerning whom alone the human soul, that is, the rational and intellectual soul, is blessed.

## The Demons Are Disturbed in Soul

**3.** What, then, is the difference between good and evil demons? Since indeed the Platonist Apuleius, discoursing of them in general and speaking so much about their airy bodies, kept silent about the virtues of soul with which they would be endowed, if they were good.

He kept silent, therefore, about the cause of blessedness, but the indication of wretchedness he could not keep silent, confessing that their mind, by which he reported them to be rational, is not even imbued and fortified with virtue so as in no way to yield to the irrational passions of the soul, but is itself also, as is the manner of foolish minds, agitated by, as it were, stormy disturbances.

For his words on this matter are these: *from this number of demons, roughly,* he says, *the poets are wont, not at all far from the truth, to feign gods as haters and lovers of certain men; these they prosper and exalt, those, on the contrary, they oppose and afflict;*

*therefore they both pity and are indignant, are anguished and are glad, and suffer every aspect of the human soul, tossed by a like motion of the heart and surge of the mind through all the tides of their thoughts. All which disturbances and storms are exiled far from the tranquility of the heavenly gods*[^3].

Is there in these words any doubt that he said not some lower parts of their souls, but the very minds of the demons, by which they are rational animals, are disturbed by the tempest of the passions, like a stormy sea?

So that they are not even to be compared with wise men, who, with an undisturbed mind, resist disturbances of soul of this kind—from which human weakness is not immune—even when they suffer them by the condition of this life, not yielding to them so as to approve or perpetrate anything that strays from the path of wisdom and the law of justice;

but, like foolish and unjust mortals, similar to them not in their bodies but in their morals (not to say worse, in that they are older and, by their due punishment, incurable),

they too toss with the surge, as he called it, of the very mind, and stand fast in no part of the soul in truth and virtue, by which turbulent and depraved affections are resisted.

## What the Philosophers Have Taught Concerning the Disturbances

**4.1.** There are two opinions of the philosophers about these motions of the soul, which the Greeks call πάθη, but some of ours, such as Cicero[^4], call "disturbances," some "affections" or "affects"[^5], and some, like this man, more expressly from the Greek, call "passions"[^6].

These disturbances, then, or affections, or passions, some philosophers say befall even the wise man, but moderated and subjected to reason, so that the dominion of the mind imposes upon them laws, as it were, by which they are reduced to a necessary measure.

Those who hold this are Platonists or Aristotelians, since Aristotle was a disciple of Plato, who founded the Peripatetic sect. But to others, such as the Stoics, it does not seem that any passions of this kind at all befall the wise man. These, however, that is, the Stoics, Cicero, in his books *On the Ends of Goods and Evils*, convicts of contending against the Platonists or Peripatetics by words rather than by realities[^7];

since the Stoics are unwilling to call the things of the body and external things "goods," but "advantages," because they will that there be no good of man besides virtue, as it were the art of living well, which is only in the soul.

These things, however, the others call simply, and from the common usage of speech, "goods"; but, in comparison with the virtue by which one lives rightly, small and slight. From which it comes about that, by whatever name they are called by either side, whether goods or advantages, they are nevertheless weighed at an equal estimation, and in this question the Stoics delight in nothing but the novelty of words.

It seems to me, therefore, that in this too, where it is asked whether the passions of the soul befall the wise man, or whether he is wholly a stranger to them, they make a controversy about words rather than an examination of realities. For I think that they themselves hold nothing other on this point than the Platonists and Peripatetics, as far as the force of the realities is concerned, not the sound of the words.

**4.2.** For—to pass over other things by which I might show this, lest I be long—I will say one thing which is most evident. In the books entitled *Attic Nights*, Aulus Gellius, a man of most elegant eloquence and of much knowledge gathered from everywhere, writes that he once sailed with a certain noble Stoic philosopher.

This philosopher, as Aulus Gellius narrates more broadly and fully, which I will touch on briefly[^8], when that ship was tossed most perilously by a dreadful sky and sea, grew pale by the force of fear. This was noticed by those who were present, though, in the nearness of death, most curiously attentive whether or not the philosopher was disturbed in soul.

Then, the storm having passed, as soon as security afforded room for conversing or even chattering, a certain one of those whom that ship carried, a rich and luxurious Asiatic, accosts the philosopher, mocking him for having been afraid and grown pale, while he himself had remained intrepid in the destruction that was threatening.

But the philosopher returned the answer of Aristippus the Socratic, who, when in a like situation he had heard the same words from a like man, answered *that the other had rightly not been anxious for the soul of a most worthless rascal, but that he himself had had to fear for the soul of Aristippus.* Having driven off the rich man with this answer, Aulus Gellius afterward asked the philosopher, not in a mind to provoke, but to learn, what was that reason for his fear.

And he, to teach a man earnestly kindled with the zeal of knowing, at once brought forth from his little bag a book of the Stoic Epictetus, in which were written things that accorded with the decrees of Zeno and Chrysippus, whom we know to have been the chiefs of the Stoics.

In that book Aulus Gellius says he read that the Stoics held this: that the impressions of the soul, which they call phantasiai, and over which it is not in one's power whether and when they fall upon the soul,

when they come from terrible and fearful things, necessarily move even the soul of the wise man, so that for a little while he either grows frightened with fear or is drawn together with sadness, as though these passions forestalled the office of the mind and reason;

nor yet, for all that, is an opinion of evil formed in the mind, nor are these things approved or consented to. For this, they will, is in one's power, and they judge this to be the difference between the soul of the wise man and the fool: that the soul of the fool yields to these same passions and accommodates the assent of the mind; but the soul of the wise man, though it suffers them by necessity, nevertheless retains, with an unshaken mind, a true and stable judgment concerning the things it ought reasonably to seek or to shun.

These things I have set forth as I could—not indeed more conveniently than Aulus Gellius, but certainly more briefly and, as I think, more plainly—which he records that he read in the book of Epictetus, that Epictetus said and held from the decrees of the Stoics.

**4.3.** If these things are so, then either nothing or almost nothing differs between the opinion of the Stoics and of other philosophers about the passions and disturbances of souls; for both defend the mind and reason of the wise man from their dominion.

And therefore perhaps the Stoics say that these do not befall the wise man, because they in no way becloud with any error or overthrow by any stain that wisdom of his, by which he is surely wise. But they do befall the soul of the wise man, with the serenity of wisdom preserved, on account of those things which they call advantages or disadvantages, although they are unwilling to call them goods or evils[^9].

For surely, if that philosopher set at naught those things which he felt he would lose by shipwreck, such as this life and the safety of the body, he would not so shudder at that peril as to be betrayed even by the testimony of his pallor. Yet nevertheless he could both suffer that emotion and hold fixed in his mind the judgment that that life and the safety of the body, whose loss the violence of the storm threatened, are not goods which make those in whom they are present good, as justice does.

But that they say these are not to be called goods, but advantages: this is to be assigned to a contest about words, not to an examination of realities.

For what does it matter whether they are more aptly called goods or advantages, when nonetheless, lest he be deprived of them, the Stoic no less than the Peripatetic grows frightened and pale, naming them not equally, but esteeming them equally?

Both indeed, if they are pressed, by the peril of these goods or advantages, toward shameful deed or crime, so that they cannot otherwise retain them, say that they would rather lose these things, by which the nature of the body is kept safe and sound, than commit those things by which justice is violated.

Thus the mind, where this judgment is fixed, permits no disturbances, even if they befall the lower parts of the soul, to prevail in it against reason; nay rather, it itself dominates them, and by not consenting to them but rather resisting them, exercises the reign of virtue. Such a one Virgil too describes Aeneas to be, where he says:  
*his mind remains unmoved, the tears roll down in vain*[^10].

## What Christian Teaching Holds About Moderation, and the Stoic About Mercy

**5.** It is not now necessary to show copiously and diligently what divine Scripture, in which Christian teaching is contained, teaches about these passions.

For it subjects the very mind to God to be ruled and helped, and the passions to the mind, to be so moderated and bridled that they are turned to the use of justice.

In short, in our discipline it is not so much asked whether a pious soul is angry, but why it is angry[^11]; nor whether it is sad, but from what it is sad[^12]; nor whether it is afraid, but what it fears[^13]. For to be angry with one who sins, that he may be corrected, to be saddened over one who is afflicted, that he may be freed, to fear for one in peril, lest he perish—I do not know whether anyone would reprehend these with sound consideration.

For it is the way of the Stoics to be wont to blame mercy too[^14]; but how much more honorably would that Stoic have been disturbed by mercy in freeing a man than by the fear of shipwreck.

Far better and more humanly and more suited to the sentiments of the pious did Cicero speak in the praise of Caesar, where he says: *of all your virtues none is more admirable or more pleasing than your mercy*[^15].

But what is mercy except a certain compassion in our heart for another's misery, by which we are surely compelled to come to his aid if we can? This motion serves reason when mercy is so shown that justice is preserved, whether when something is given to one in need, or when one who repents is pardoned.

This Cicero, an outstanding speaker, did not hesitate to call a virtue, which the Stoics are not ashamed to number among the vices—who nevertheless, as the book of Epictetus, a most noble Stoic, taught, from the decrees of Zeno and Chrysippus, who held the first place in this sect, admit passions of this kind into the soul of the wise man, whom they will to be free from all vices.

Whence it follows that they do not think these very things vices, when they so befall the wise man that they can do nothing against the virtue of the mind and reason, and that one and the same opinion belongs to the Peripatetics, or even the Platonists, and to the Stoics themselves; but, as Tully says, *a controversy of words has long now tormented those Greeklings, more desirous of contention than of truth*[^16].

But it can still rightly be asked whether it pertains to the weakness of the present life to suffer affections of this kind even in any good offices,

And the holy angels punish, yet without anger, those whom they receive to be punished under God's eternal law; they come to the aid of the wretched, yet without sharing in their misery; they rescue those they love when in peril, yet without fear;

and yet the names of these emotions are applied even to the angels by the custom of human speech, on account of a certain likeness in their works, not because of any weakness in their dispositions, just as God himself, according to the Scriptures, grows angry and yet is troubled by no emotion. For that word expresses the effect of vengeance, not the turbulent disposition of one who takes it.

## Moderation is absent from the mind of the demons.

**6.** Setting aside for now this question about the holy angels, let us consider in what way the Platonists say that the demons, set in the middle between gods and men, are tossed about by these surging emotions.

For if their mind were free from these emotions and held sway over them, and they nonetheless suffered movements of this kind, Apuleius would not say that they are *tossed by a like motion of the heart and a like swell of the mind through every surge of thought*[^17].

Therefore their very mind, that is, the higher part of the soul by which they are rational, in which their virtue and wisdom would reside if they had any, and which would rule and moderate the turbulent emotions of the lower parts of the soul—their very mind, I say, as this Platonist admits, is tossed on a swell of disturbances.

The mind of the demons, then, is subject to the emotions of lust, fear, anger, and the rest of this kind. What part in them, therefore, is free and possessed of wisdom, by which they might please the gods and counsel men toward a likeness of good morals, when their mind is subjugated and oppressed by the vices of the emotions, and bends whatever reason it naturally has toward deceiving and beguiling, all the more keenly the more the desire to do harm possesses it?

## The gods too are driven by emotions.

**7.** But if anyone says that those whom the poets, not far from the truth, invent as gods who hate and love certain men belong not to the whole number of demons but to the number of evil ones—for it was of these that Apuleius said that they are tossed on a swell of the mind through every surge of thought—

how shall we be able to understand this, when, in saying it, he was describing the intermediate position not of certain demons, that is, the evil ones, but of all demons on account of their aerial bodies, set between gods and men?

For he says that the poets invent this, namely, that out of the number of these demons they make gods and give them the names of gods, and from among them assign friends and enemies to whatever men they please, with the unpunished license of feigned song, while he maintains that the gods are removed from these morals of demons, set in a heavenly place and in the abundance of blessedness.

This, then, is the poets' fiction: to call gods those who are not gods, and to make them contend among themselves on behalf of men, whom out of partisan zeal they love or hate. But he says this fiction is not far from the truth, because, though called by the names of gods, those who are not gods are nonetheless described as the demons actually are.

Finally, he says that the Homeric Minerva is of this sort, *who intervened to restrain Achilles in the midst of the assembly of the Greeks*[^18]. That this was Minerva, then, he holds to be a poetic invention, since he thinks Minerva a goddess and sets her among the gods—all of whom he believes to be good and blessed—in a lofty ethereal seat, far from the company of mortals;

but that some demon favoring the Greeks and opposed to the Trojans, just as another, an ally of the Trojans against the Greeks, whom the same poet records under the name of Venus or Mars—gods whom he, since they do no such things, places in heavenly dwellings—that these demons contended among themselves on behalf of those they loved against those they hated: this he confessed the poets said not far from the truth.

For they said these things about beings who, he testifies, are tossed about with a like motion of the heart and a like swell of the mind through every surge of thought,

so that they can exercise their loves and hatreds not according to justice, but as a populace like them does over hunters and charioteers, taking the side of some against others according to the zeal of their factions[^19].

For this seems to have been the Platonist philosopher's concern: that, when these things were sung by the poets, they should not be believed to be done by the gods themselves—whose names the poets place in their fictions[^20]—but by the intermediate demons.

**8.** What of this? Is that very definition of demons too little to be examined—the one in which he certainly embraced them all in his determination—where he says that demons are, in genus, living beings; in soul, subject to emotion; in mind, rational; in body, aerial; in time, eternal?[^21]

Among these five things he mentioned, he said nothing at all by which demons might seem to have in common with even good men anything that was not also in evil men. For when he embraced men themselves in a somewhat fuller description, speaking of them in their own place as of beings lowest and earthly—having first spoken of the heavenly gods, so that, with the two parts at the highest and lowest extremes commended, he might in the third place speak of the intermediate demons:

*Men, then*, he says, *rejoicing in reason, powerful in speech, with immortal souls and dying limbs, with fickle and anxious minds, with dull and burdensome bodies, of unlike characters but like errors, of stubborn boldness and obstinate hope, of fruitless toil and fickle fortune, mortal one by one yet as a whole race everlasting, changeable through the supply of offspring in turn, with fleeting time, slow wisdom, swift death, and a complaining life, inhabit the earth*[^22].

When he said here so many things that pertain to most men, did he by any chance pass over in silence what he knew belonged to few, when he says *slow wisdom*? Had he omitted this, he could in no way have rightly defined the human race with so attentive a care in his description. But when he commended the excellence of the gods, he affirmed that this very blessedness, to which men wish to attain through wisdom, is preeminent in them.

Accordingly, if he had wished some demons to be understood as good, he would have placed something even in their own description by which they might be thought to share some portion of blessedness with the gods, or some wisdom, of whatever kind, with men.

As it is, however, he mentioned no good in them by which the good are distinguished from the wicked.

Although he also refrained from expressing their wickedness more freely, not so much to avoid offending the demons themselves as to avoid offending their worshipers, among whom he was speaking:

he nonetheless indicated to the prudent what they ought to think about them,

since indeed the gods, whom he wished all to be believed good and blessed, he separated in every way from the emotions and, as he himself says, the disturbances of the demons, joining them only in the eternity of their bodies,

while most plainly impressing it upon us that the demons are like in soul not to the gods but to men; and this not by the good of wisdom, of which men too can be partakers,

but by the disturbance of the emotions, which holds sway over the foolish and the wicked, but is so ruled by the wise and the good that they would rather not have it than conquer it.

For if he had wished the demons to be understood as having in common with the gods not the eternity of bodies but of souls, he would certainly not have separated men from a share in this, since beyond doubt he holds, as a Platonist, that men too have eternal souls.

Therefore, when he described this kind of living being, he said that men have immortal souls and dying limbs. And by this, if men do not have eternity in common with the gods because they are mortal in body, then the demons have it because they are immortal in body.

## If a demon is conceived as intermediate, it stands head downward.

**9.** What sort of mediators, then, between men and gods are these, through whom men should court the friendship of the gods, who have in common with men the worse element, which is the better part in a living being, that is, the soul, but have in common with the gods the better element, which is the worse part in a living being, that is, the body?

For since a living being—that is, an animal—consists of soul and body, of which two the soul is certainly the better part of the body, even if it is vicious and weak, it is surely better than even the soundest and strongest body, because its nature is more excellent and is not ranked below the body by the corruption of its vices, just as gold, even when soiled, is valued more highly than silver or lead, however pure:

these mediators of gods and men, through whose interposition human things are joined to divine, have in common with the gods an eternal body, but with men a vicious soul—as though the religion by which they would have men joined to the gods through demons were established in the body and not in the soul.

What wickedness or punishment, then, has hung these false and deceitful mediators up, as it were, head downward, so that they have the lower part of a living being, that is, the body, in common with the higher beings, but the higher part, that is, the soul, in common with the lower; and, joined to the heavenly gods in the part that serves, are wretched along with earthly men in the part that rules?

For the body is indeed a servant, as Sallust too says: *we make more use of the soul's command, the body's service*. And he added: *the one we have in common with the gods, the other with the beasts*[^23],

since he was speaking of men, who have a mortal body as the beasts do. But these beings, whom the philosophers have provided for us as mediators between us and the gods, can indeed say of soul and body, "the one we have in common with the gods, the other with men;"

but, as I have said, being, as it were, bound and hung up upside down, they have a servile body in common with the blessed gods, and a ruling soul in common with wretched men,

exalted in their lower part, cast down in their higher.

Hence, even if anyone should suppose, on this account, that they have eternity in common with the gods because their souls are not, like those of earthly animals, released from the body by any death, this body must not be considered an eternal vehicle of honored beings, but an eternal chain of the damned.

**10.** Plotinus certainly, who lived in times near our own memory, is praised for having understood Plato more excellently than the rest[^24]. When he was treating of human souls, he says, *the merciful Father made their bonds mortal*[^25]. Thus he judged that this very fact, that men are mortal in body, pertains to the mercy of God the Father, lest they be held forever in the misery of this life.

The iniquity of the demons was judged unworthy of this mercy, which, in the misery of a soul subject to emotion, received not a mortal body like men, but an eternal one.

For they would indeed be happier than men if they had a mortal body in common with them and a blessed soul in common with the gods.

And they would be the equals of men if, along with a wretched soul, they had deserved at least to have a mortal body in common with them—provided, that is, that they acquired some measure of piety, so that they might rest from their hardships at least in death.

As it is, however, not only are they not happier than men, given their wretched soul, but they are even more wretched in the perpetual chain of the body. For he did not wish it to be understood that, by some discipline of piety and wisdom and by making progress, gods are made out of demons, since he most plainly said that demons are eternal.

**11.** He says, indeed, that the souls of men too are demons, and that from men come lares, if they are of good desert; lemures or larvae, if evil; but they are called manes-gods, if it is uncertain whether they are of good or evil desert[^26]. In this opinion, what a great abyss they open up for the pursuit of depraved morals, who does not see, if he gives even a little attention? For however worthless men may have been, while they suppose either that they become larvae, or that they become manes-gods, they grow so much the worse the more eager they are to do harm, so that they even suppose themselves to be invited by certain sacrifices, as by divine honors after death, in order to do harm. For he says that larvae are harmful demons made out of men. But there is another question here. He maintains, moreover, that the blessed are for this reason called in Greek *eudaimones*, because they are of a good soul, that is, good demons[^27], thereby confirming that the souls of men too are demons.

## Three things set the gods apart from men.

**12.** But now we are dealing with those whom, in their proper nature, he described, between gods and men, as in genus living beings, in mind rational, in soul subject to emotion, in body aerial, in time eternal.

For when he had first set the gods in the lofty heaven and men on the lowest earth, separated by place and by the dignity of their nature, he concluded thus: *you have, then*, he says, *for the present two kinds of living beings: the gods, differing greatly from men in the loftiness of their place, the perpetuity of their life, the perfection of their nature,*

*with no close communion between them, since so great an interval of height separates the highest dwellings from the lowest, and there life is eternal and unfailing, here transient and brief, and those natures are exalted to blessedness, these brought low to miseries*[^28].

Here I see three contraries mentioned, drawn from the two extreme parts of nature, that is, the highest and the lowest. For the three praiseworthy things he set down concerning the gods, these same he repeated, in other words indeed, so as to assign to them, drawn from men, three other things opposed.

These three things belong to the gods: loftiness of place, perpetuity of life, perfection of nature. These he repeated in other words, so as to set against them three contraries of the human condition.

*Since so great an interval of height separates the highest dwellings from the lowest*, he says, because he had spoken of loftiness of place;

*and there life is eternal and unfailing, here transient and brief*, he says, because he had spoken of perpetuity of life;

*and those natures are exalted to blessedness, these brought low to miseries*, he says, because he had spoken of perfection of nature.

Three things, then, are set down by him as belonging to the gods, that is, a lofty place, eternity, and blessedness; and three contrary to these as belonging to men, that is, a lowly place, mortality, and misery.

**13.** Since, among these three pairs belonging to gods and men, he placed the demons in the middle, about place there is no dispute; for a place between a lofty one and a lowly one is most aptly held and called intermediate.

The other two pairs remain, to which closer attention must be given as to how they are shown to be foreign to the demons, or else so distributed to them as their intermediate position seems to demand. But they cannot be foreign to them.

For we cannot rightly say of the demons, just as we say of an intermediate place that it is neither the highest nor the lowest, that—being rational living beings—they are neither blessed nor wretched, as shrubs or cattle are, which are devoid of sense or reason.

For those in whose minds reason is present must necessarily be either wretched or blessed. Likewise we cannot rightly say that the demons are neither mortal nor eternal. For all living things either live forever or end by death what they live. And indeed he said that the demons are eternal in time.

What, then, remains, except that these intermediate beings have one of the two highest things and the other of the two lowest? For if they have both from the lowest things or both from the highest, they will not be intermediate, but will spring up to one side or sink down to the other.

Since, therefore, as has been shown, they cannot lack both members of these two pairs, by taking one from each side they will hold the middle.

And by this, since they cannot have eternity from the lowest things, where it is not found, this one thing they have from the highest; and therefore there is nothing else, to complete their intermediate position, that they may have from the lowest, except misery.

And so, according to the Platonists, the lofty gods have either blessed eternity or eternal blessedness[^29]; the lowest men have either mortal misery or wretched mortality; and the intermediate demons have either wretched eternity or eternal misery.

For even by those five things which he set down in the definition of demons, he did not show them to be intermediate, as he promised;

for he said that they have three things in common with us—that they are living beings in genus, rational in mind, and subject to emotion in soul—but one thing with the gods, that they are eternal in time; and one thing proper to themselves, that they are aerial in body.

How, then, are they intermediate, when they have one thing in common with the highest and three with the lowest? Who does not see how far, leaving the middle behind, they incline and are pressed down toward the lowest?

But plainly, even here they can be found to be intermediate in this way,

that they have one thing proper to themselves, which is an aerial body, just as those at the highest and lowest extremes each have something proper—the gods an ethereal body and men an earthly one;

while two things are common to all, namely, that they are living beings in genus and rational in mind.

For he himself, when he was speaking of gods and men, says, *you have two kinds of living beings*, and these men are not accustomed to represent the gods as anything but rational in mind[^30].

Two things remain, that they are subject to emotion in soul and eternal in time; of these they have one in common with the lowest, the other with the highest, so that the middle, balanced by a proportionate ratio, is neither raised up to the highest nor pressed down to the lowest.

And this very thing is that wretched eternity of the demons, or eternal misery. For he who said *subject to emotion in soul* would have said "wretched" too, had he not blushed before their worshipers.

Moreover, since the world is governed by the providence of the most high God, as they themselves also admit, and not by chance recklessness, the demons would never have eternal misery unless they had great wickedness.

## . . . therefore not intermediate between gods and men.

If, then, the blessed are rightly called "eudaimones," then the demons are not "eudaimones," whom these men have placed in the middle between men and gods. What place, then, is there for good demons, who above men and below the gods might furnish aid to the former and ministry to the latter? For if they are good and eternal, they are surely blessed as well. But eternal blessedness does not allow them to be intermediate, since it sets them on a level with the gods in great measure and separates them far from men.

Hence in vain will these men try to show how good demons, if they are both immortal and blessed, are rightly established as intermediate between the gods, who are immortal and blessed, and men, who are mortal and wretched. For since they have both these things in common with the gods, namely, both blessedness and immortality, but none of these in common with men, who are both wretched and mortal, how are they not rather removed from men and joined to the gods than established as intermediate between the two?

For they would then be intermediate if they themselves too had two things of their own, in common not with both members of either pair but with one member of each; just as man is something intermediate, but between cattle and angels,

so that, since cattle are irrational and mortal living beings, while an angel is rational and immortal, man is intermediate, but lower than the angels and higher than the cattle, having mortality in common with the cattle and reason in common with the angels—a rational, mortal living being. So, then, when we look for what is intermediate between the blessed immortals and the wretched mortals, we ought to find this: something that is either mortal and blessed, or immortal and wretched.

**14.** Whether a man can be both blessed and mortal is a great question among men. For some have looked upon their condition more humbly and have denied that man can be capable of blessedness so long as he lives in a mortal state. But others have exalted themselves and have dared to say that mortals who possess wisdom can be blessed.

But if this is so, why are not these very men rather established as intermediate between the wretched mortals and the blessed immortals, having blessedness in common with the blessed immortals and mortality in common with the wretched mortals? For surely, if they are blessed, they envy no one (for what is more wretched than envy?), and therefore they counsel the wretched mortals, as far as they can, toward attaining blessedness, so that these may be able to be immortal even after death and to be joined to the immortal and blessed angels.

## Christ is mediator because he is man and God.

**15.** But if, as is argued with much greater credibility and probability, all men, so long as they are mortal, must also necessarily be wretched, then a mediator must be sought who is not only man but also God, so that by the blessed mortality of this mediator, intervening, men may be led from mortal misery to blessed immortality; one who ought neither not to have become mortal, nor to have remained mortal.

He became mortal, indeed, not by any weakening of the divinity of the Word, but by taking on the weakness of flesh; yet he did not remain mortal in that very flesh, which he raised from the dead; for this is the very fruit of his mediation, that those for whose deliverance he became mediator should not remain in perpetual death even of the flesh.

Accordingly, the mediator between us and God had to have a passing mortality and an abiding blessedness, so that through what passes away he might be in keeping with those who must die, and to what abides he might carry them over from the dead.

The good angels, then, cannot be intermediate between the wretched mortals and the blessed immortals, because they too are both blessed and immortal;

but the evil angels can be intermediate, because they are immortal along with the former and wretched along with the latter.

Contrary to these is the good mediator, who, against their immortality and misery, willed both to be mortal for a time and was able to abide blessed in eternity; and so, by the humility of his death and the kindness of his blessedness, he destroyed in them, the proud immortals and the harmful wretched ones—lest by the boasting of their immortality they should seduce men to misery—those whose hearts, purifying them through faith in him, he set free from the most unclean dominion of those beings.

What intermediary, then, should mortal and wretched man, far sundered from the immortal and blessed, choose, through which he may be united to immortality and blessedness? Whatever might give delight in the immortality of the demons is wretched; whatever might give offense in the mortality of Christ no longer exists.

There, then, everlasting misery is to be guarded against; here, death is not to be feared, which could not be everlasting, and everlasting blessedness is to be loved. For the immortal and wretched intermediary interposes himself for this end: that he may not allow passage to blessed immortality, since what hinders, namely, misery itself, abides;

but the mortal and blessed mediator interposed himself for this end: that, his mortality being passed through, he might make men immortal out of the dead—which he showed in himself by rising again—and blessed out of the wretched, from which blessedness he himself never departed.

There is, then, one evil intermediary who separates friends, and another, the good one, who reconciles enemies. And therefore the intermediaries who separate are many, because the multitude that is blessed is made blessed by participation in the one God; and through the privation of this participation the wretched multitude of evil angels,

which sets itself in the way rather as a hindrance than interposes itself as a help toward blessedness, by its very multitude also clamors, as it were, against us, so that we cannot attain to that one beatifying [good], to which, that we might be led, there was need not of many but of one mediator, and that one the very being by participation in whom we are blessed, that is, the Word of God, not made, through whom all things were made[^31].

Yet he is not mediator on this account, that he is the Word; for the Word, supremely immortal and supremely blessed, is far from wretched mortals;

but he is mediator through that by which he is man[^32], by this very thing surely showing that, to attain to that good which is not only blessed but also beatifying, no other mediators are to be sought through whom we might suppose that steps to our arrival are to be contrived,

because the blessed and beatifying God, having become a partaker of our humanity, has afforded a short way to participation in his divinity.

For in freeing us from mortality and misery, he does not lead us to the immortal and blessed angels in such a way that by participation in them we too may be immortal and blessed;

but he leads us to that Trinity, by participation in which the angels too are blessed.

Therefore, when in the form of a servant[^33], that he might be mediator, he willed to be below the angels, he remained, in the form of God, above the angels;

the same one being, in the lower things, the way of life, who in the higher is life itself[^34].

## It is absurd for anyone to teach that the gods are defiled.

**16.** For it is not true, what the same Platonist says Plato said: *no god mingles with man*; and this he says is the chief mark of their sublimity, that they are defiled by no contact with men.

He admits, then, that the demons are defiled, and therefore they cannot purify those by whom they are defiled[^35], and both alike become unclean—the demons by contact with men and men by the worship of demons.

Or if the demons can be touched and mingle with men and yet not be defiled, then they are surely better than the gods, since the gods, if they mingled, would be defiled.

For this is said to be the chief thing about the gods, that, sublimely set apart, human contact cannot defile them[^36].

He asserts, indeed, that the most high God, the creator of all things, whom we call the true God, is so proclaimed by Plato that he alone cannot, in the poverty of human speech, be comprehended by any discourse even in slight measure; that scarcely even to wise men, when by the vigor of the mind they have withdrawn themselves as far as they could from the body, does the understanding of this God come, and that too only at times, like a bright light flashing through in the deepest darkness with the swiftest gleam[^37].

If, then, the truly most high God above all things, by a certain intelligible and ineffable presence—even if at times, even if like a bright light flashing through with the swiftest gleam—is nonetheless present to the minds of the wise when they have withdrawn themselves as far as they could from the body, and cannot be defiled by them, what is the reason that these gods are set far off in a lofty place, lest they be defiled by human contact?

As though it were anything else than enough to see those ethereal bodies, by whose light the earth is illumined as much as is sufficient. Furthermore, if the stars are not defiled when they are seen—and he says all the visible gods are stars[^38]—then neither are the demons defiled by the gaze of men, even though they are seen from close at hand.

Or are they perhaps defiled by human voices, who are not defiled by the keenness of human eyes, and therefore have the demons as intermediaries, through whom the voices of men may be reported to them, from whom they are far removed, so that they may remain utterly undefiled?

What now shall I say of the other senses? For neither could the gods, if they were present, be defiled by smelling, nor can the demons, when they are present, be defiled by the vapors of living human bodies, if they are not defiled by the corpse-like reeks of so many sacrifices.

In the sense of taste, moreover, they are pressed by no necessity of restoring a mortal nature, so that, driven by hunger, they should seek food from men. As for touch, it is within their power. For although "contact" seems to be named chiefly from that sense, yet they would mingle with men only so far—if they wished—as to see and be seen, to hear and be heard.

But what need is there of touching? For neither would men dare to desire this, when they were enjoying the sight or conversation of the gods or of good demons; and if curiosity went so far that they wished it, in what way could anyone touch against his will a god or a demon, who cannot touch a sparrow unless he has caught it?

By seeing, then, and by offering themselves to be seen, and by speaking and hearing, the gods could mingle bodily with men. But if the demons mingle in this way, as I have said, and are not defiled, while the gods would be defiled if they mingled, then they call the demons incapable of defilement and the gods capable of it.

But if the demons too are defiled, what do they confer on men toward a blessed life after death, whom, being defiled, they cannot purify, so as to join them, purified, to the undefiled gods, between whom and men they have been set as intermediaries?

Or if they confer no such benefit on them, what does the friendly mediation of the demons profit men?

Is it that, after death, men should pass not to the gods through the demons, but that both should live together defiled, and on this account neither blessed?

Unless perhaps someone should say that, after the manner of sponges or things of this kind, the demons cleanse their friends, in such a way that they themselves become as much the filthier as men, by their wiping, as it were, become the cleaner.

But if this is so, the gods mingle with demons all the more defiled, who, lest they be defiled, have shunned nearness to men and contact with them.

Or can the gods perhaps purify the demons defiled by men, and not be defiled by them, while in that same way they could not also purify men? Who would think such things, except one whom the most deceitful demons have deceived?

What of this? If being seen and seeing defiles, the gods are seen by men, whom he calls visible[^39]—*the most brilliant lights of the world*[^40] and the other stars—and the demons are safer from this defilement by men, since they cannot be seen unless they will it?

Or if not being seen, but seeing, defiles, let them deny that men are seen by those *most brilliant lights of the world*, which they suppose to be gods, since they extend their rays all the way to the earth.

And yet their rays, spread out through all manner of unclean things, are not defiled, while the gods would be defiled if they mingled with men, even if contact were necessary in coming to their aid? For the earth is touched by the rays of the sun and moon, and does not defile that light.

**17.** But I wonder very much that such learned men, who judged that all corporeal and sensible things are to be ranked below the incorporeal and intelligible, should, when the blessed life is in question, make mention of corporeal contacts.

Where is that saying of Plotinus, where he says: *we must flee, then, to our dearest fatherland, and there is the Father, and there are all things. What fleet, then*, he says, *or flight? To become like God*[^41]. If, then, the more like God one becomes, the nearer one becomes to him, there is no remoteness from him other than unlikeness to him. And the soul of man is the more unlike that incorporeal, eternal, and unchangeable being the more it desires temporal and changeable things.

That this may be healed—since the things that are at the bottom, mortal and unclean, cannot agree with the immortal purity that is at the summit—there is indeed need of a mediator; not, however, such a one as has, indeed, an immortal body near to the highest, but a diseased soul like the lowest

(by which disease he would rather envy us, lest we be healed, than help us to be healed); but such a one as, fitted to us who are lowest by the mortality of his body, yet by the immortal righteousness of his spirit—by which he remained among the highest not by distance of place but by the excellence of likeness—may furnish a truly divine help for our purification and deliverance.

And far be it that this God, who cannot be defiled, should fear defilement from the man with whom he was clothed, or from the men among whom in that man he lived.

For these two things, which he showed for our salvation by his incarnation, are no small matters: that true divinity cannot be defiled by flesh, and that the demons are not therefore to be thought better than we because they do not have flesh. This is, as holy Scripture proclaims him, *the mediator of God and men, the man Christ Jesus*[^42], of whose divinity, by which he is always equal to the Father, and humanity, by which he was made like to us, this is not the place to speak fittingly according to our ability.

**18.** But those false and deceitful mediators, the demons, who—though by the uncleanness of their spirit they are wretched and malignant, and show it by many effects—nonetheless, through the intervals of corporeal places and through the lightness of their aerial bodies, strive to call us away and turn us from the advancement of our souls, do not furnish a way to God, but rather hinder us, lest the way be held.

For even in that very corporeal way (which is most false and most full of error, the way by which righteousness does not journey; since we ought to ascend to God not by corporeal height, but by spiritual, that is, incorporeal, likeness)—yet in that very corporeal way, which the demons' friends arrange by the gradations of the elements, with the aerial demons set in the middle between the ethereal gods and the earthly men, they suppose the gods to have this chief privilege, that on account of this interval of places they are not defiled by human contact.

Thus they believe that the demons are defiled by men rather than that men are purified by the demons, and that the gods themselves could have been defiled, had they not been protected by the height of their place[^43].

Who is so unhappy as to suppose himself purified by this way, where men are proclaimed as defiling, demons as defiled, and gods as capable of defilement;

and not rather choose the way where the defiling demons are instead avoided, and men are purified from defilement by the God who cannot be defiled[^44], so as to enter into the fellowship of the undefiled angels?

## The angels are not good demons.

**19.** But lest we too seem to be contending merely about words, since some of these worshipers of demons, so to speak—among whom is Labeo—maintain that those whom they themselves call demons are by others called angels, I see that I must now say something about the good angels, whom these men do not deny to exist, but whom they prefer to call good demons rather than angels.

But we, as Scripture speaks—the Scripture according to which we are Christians—do indeed read of angels, some good and some evil, but never of good demons; rather, wherever in those Writings this word is found, whether they be called "demons" or "demonia," none but malignant spirits are signified.

And the peoples everywhere have followed this usage of speech to such a degree that, even among those who are called pagans and who contend that many gods and demons are to be worshiped, there is scarcely anyone so lettered and learned as to dare to say in praise even to his slave, *you have a demon*[^45]; but whoever has wished to say this to anyone cannot doubt that he meant it as nothing other than a curse. What reason, then, compels us, after offending so many ears—now almost everyone's—which are accustomed to hear this word only in a bad sense, to be forced to explain what we have said, when we could, by using the name "angels," avoid the same offense that might be caused by the name "demons"?

## The demon belongs to a knowledge that puffs up.

**20.** And yet the very origin of this name, if we look to the divine books, brings to light something most worthy of recognition. For demons (since the word is Greek) are so named from knowledge[^46]. But the Apostle, speaking by the Holy Spirit, says: *knowledge puffs up, but love builds up*[^47]; which is rightly understood in no other way than that knowledge profits then, when love is present[^48];

but without it, knowledge puffs up, that is, exalts one into the pride of a most empty windiness, as it were. There is, then, in the demons knowledge without love, and therefore they are so puffed up, that is, so proud, that they have taken pains to have the divine honors and the service of religion, which they know is owed to the true God, displayed to themselves, and they still do so as far as they can and among those they can.

Furthermore, against the pride of the demons, by which, according to its deserts, the human race was held in possession, how great a power the humility of God, which appeared in Christ, possesses, the souls of men do not know, being puffed up with the uncleanness of self-exaltation, like the demons in pride, though not in knowledge.

**21.** But the demons themselves know even this so well that they said to the same Lord, clothed in the weakness of flesh: *what have we to do with you, Jesus of Nazareth? Have you come to destroy us?*[^49] It is clear in these words that in them there was both such great knowledge and no love.

For they dreaded their own punishment from him; they did not love the righteousness in him. And he made himself known to them only as much as he willed; and he willed only as much as was fitting.

But he made himself known not as he does to the holy angels, who enjoy him, in that he is the Word of God, by a shared eternity[^50], but as he had to be made known to them in order to terrify them—out of whose tyrannical power, in a manner of speaking, he was about to deliver those predestined to his kingdom and to a glory ever true and truly everlasting.

He made himself known to the demons, then, not through that which is eternal life and the unchangeable light which illumines the devout, by seeing which through the faith that is in him their hearts are purified,

but through certain temporal effects of his power and signs of his most hidden presence, which could be discernible to the angelic senses even of the malignant spirits rather than to the weakness of men.

Finally, when he judged that these should be suppressed a little and lay hidden somewhat more deeply, the prince of the demons doubted concerning him and tempted him, probing whether he was the Christ,

as far as he himself permitted himself to be tempted, that he might fashion the man whom he bore into an example for our imitation.

But after that temptation, when the angels, as it is written, ministered to him[^51]—good, surely, and holy, and on this account to be feared and dreaded by the unclean spirits—

it became more and more known to the demons how great he was, so that, although the weakness of the flesh in him seemed contemptible, none dared resist him when he commanded.

**22.** To these good angels, then, all knowledge of corporeal and temporal things, by which the demons are puffed up, is cheap; not that they are ignorant of these things, but that the love of God, by which they are sanctified, is dear to them.

In comparison with his beauty—not only incorporeal, but also unchangeable and ineffable, with holy love of which they burn—all things that are below it, and that are not what it is, including themselves among those things, they hold cheap, so that with the whole of what makes them good they may enjoy that good from which they are good.

And therefore they know these temporal and changeable things more surely too, because they behold their principal causes in the Word of God, through whom the world was made—the causes by which some things are approved, some rejected, and all are ordered.

The demons, however, do not contemplate the eternal and, in a manner, pivotal causes of times in the Wisdom of God, but, by greater experience of certain signs hidden from us, foresee far more future things than men do; sometimes they also foretell their own arrangements.

Finally, the demons are often deceived, the angels never at all. For it is one thing to conjecture temporal things from temporal and changeable things from changeable, and to insert into them the temporal and changeable measure of one's own will and capacity, which is permitted to the demons by a fixed reason;

but it is another thing to foresee, in the eternal and unchangeable laws of God which live in his Wisdom, the changes of times, and by participation in his Spirit to know the will of God, which is the most certain as well as the most powerful of all—which has been granted to the holy angels by a right discernment. And so they are not only eternal but also blessed. The good by which they are blessed is God, by whom they were created. For they enjoy his participation and contemplation without deviation.

## What the name of gods is in the Scriptures.

**23.** If the Platonists prefer to call these gods rather than demons, and to number them among those whom Plato, their author and master, writes were made gods by the most high God[^52], let them say what they wish; for there is no need to labor with them over a controversy about words.

For if they say that they are immortal in such a way as yet to have been made by the most high God, and that they are blessed not through themselves but by cleaving to him by whom they were made, they say what we say, by whatever name they call them.

And that this is the opinion of the Platonists, whether of all or of the better among them, can be found in their writings. For even about the very name by which they call such immortal and blessed creatures gods, there is therefore almost no dissent between us and them,

because in our own sacred Writings too it is read: *the God of gods, the Lord, has spoken*[^53], and elsewhere: *give thanks to the God of gods*[^54], and elsewhere: *a great King above all gods*[^55].

But why it was said, in that place where it is written, *he is terrible above all gods*, is shown next. For it follows: *for all the gods of the nations are demons, but the Lord made the heavens*[^56].

He said, then, *above all gods*, but of *the nations*, that is, those whom the nations hold for gods, which are *demons*; therefore *terrible*, under which terror they said to the Lord, *have you come to destroy us?*[^57]

But that passage where it is said, *the God of gods*, cannot be understood as the god of demons; and far be it that *a great King above all gods* should be taken to mean "a great king above all demons." But the same Scripture calls men too, among the people of God, gods. *I said*, he says, *you are gods, and all of you sons of the Most High*[^58]. And so he can be understood as the God of these gods, who is called *the God of gods*, and a great King above these gods, who is called *a great King above all gods*.

Yet when it is asked of us, "If men have been called gods because they are among the people of God, whom God addresses through angels or through men, how much more are the immortals worthy of that name, who enjoy that blessedness to which men, by worshiping God, desire to attain?"—what shall we answer, except that it was not in vain that in the holy Scriptures men were more expressly called gods than those immortal and blessed beings, to whom we are promised that we shall be equal in the resurrection,

lest, namely, on account of the excellence of those beings, unbelieving weakness should dare to set up some one of them as a god for us? In the case of a man this is easy to avoid. And men in the people of God ought to have been called gods more evidently, so that they might be made certain and confident that he is their God who is called *the God of gods*[^59];

because, although those immortal and blessed beings who are in the heavens are called gods, yet they were not called gods of gods, that is, gods of men established among the people of God, to whom it was said, *I said, you are gods, and all of you sons of the Most High*[^60].

Hence it is that the Apostle says: *for though there be those that are called gods, whether in heaven or on earth, as indeed there are many gods and many lords, yet for us there is one God, the Father, from whom are all things and we in him, and one Lord Jesus Christ, through whom are all things and we through him*[^61].

There is no need, then, to dispute much about the name, since the matter itself is so clear as to be free from any scruple of doubt. But the point that we say angels were sent from the number of those immortal and blessed beings to announce the will of God to men does not please them,

because they believe that this ministry is performed not through those whom they call gods, that is, immortal and blessed beings, but through demons, whom they dare to call only immortal, not also blessed—or at least so immortal and blessed as nonetheless to be good demons, not gods sublimely placed and removed from human contact. And although it may seem a controversy about a name, yet the name of demons is so detestable that we ought in every way to keep ourselves, by this name, apart from the holy angels.

Now, then, let this book be closed in such a way that we may know that the immortal and blessed beings, by whatever name they are called, who are nonetheless made and created, are not intermediaries for leading the wretched mortals to immortal blessedness, since they are separated from them by both differences.

But those who are intermediaries, having immortality in common with the higher beings and misery in common with the lower—since they are wretched by the desert of wickedness—can rather envy us the blessedness they do not have than bestow it.

Hence the friends of the demons have nothing worthy to bring forward to show why we ought to worship them as helpers, whom we ought rather to avoid as deceivers. But as for those whom they think good and therefore not only immortal but also blessed, and to be worshiped under the name of gods with rites and sacrifices for the sake of attaining a blessed life after death,

of whatever sort they may be and worthy of whatever name, that they do not wish, through such homage of religion, that anyone be worshiped except the one God, by whom they were created and by participation in whom they are blessed—this, with his help, we shall discuss more carefully in the following book.
