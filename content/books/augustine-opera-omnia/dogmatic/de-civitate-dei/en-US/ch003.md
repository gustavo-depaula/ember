# Book I: Whether the calamities of the times are governed by the providence of God

## Preface

The most glorious city of God—whether in this course of the times, while it journeys as a pilgrim among the impious, living by faith[^1], or in that stability of the eternal seat, which it now awaits through patience[^2], *until righteousness is turned into judgment*[^3], and thereafter shall attain by excellence the final victory and perfect peace—I have undertaken to defend in this work, begun[^4] and owed by my promise to you, against those who prefer their own gods to its founder, dearest son Marcellinus. A great and arduous work, but *God is our helper*[^5].

For I know what strength is needed to persuade the proud how great is the power of humility, by which it comes about that one transcends all earthly heights, swaying with their temporal instability, by a loftiness that is not usurped by human pride but bestowed by divine grace. For the King and founder of this city, of which we have set out to speak, has opened to his people in their Scripture the verdict of the divine law, where it is said: *God resists the proud, but gives grace to the humble*[^6]. But this, which belongs to God, the puffed-up spirit of the proud soul also aspires to, and loves to have said of itself in its praises: *to spare the conquered and to subdue the proud*[^7].

And so concerning the earthly city as well—which, when it strives for mastery, though peoples serve it, is itself mastered by its own lust for mastery—we must not pass over in silence whatever the plan of the work we have undertaken requires us to say, if the means is granted.

**1**. For from this city arise the enemies against whom the city of God must be defended—though many of them, correcting the error of their impiety, become quite worthy citizens within it; but many burn against it with such fires of hatred and are so ungrateful for the manifest benefits of its Redeemer that today they would not move their tongues against it had they not, fleeing the enemy's sword, found in its consecrated places the life of which they are proud. Are not even those Romans hostile to the name of Christ whom the barbarians spared for Christ's sake?

The shrines of the martyrs and the basilicas of the apostles bear witness to this, for in that devastation of the City they received both their own and strangers who fled to them[^8]. Up to this point the bloodthirsty enemy raged; there the fury of the slayer met its limit; thither were led by pitying foes those whom they had spared even outside those places, lest they fall upon men who did not have a like mercy.

Yet these same men, who elsewhere raged fiercely and in the manner of enemies, once they came to those places where what would have been permitted elsewhere by the law of war had been forbidden, restrained the whole monstrousness of their striking, and their lust for taking captives was broken.

So escaped many who now disparage Christian times and lay to Christ's charge the evils that city endured; but the good things done to them, that they might live, for the honor of Christ, they lay not to the charge of our Christ but to their own fate—when rather, if they had any sound sense, they ought to attribute the harsh and hard things they suffered at the enemy's hands to divine providence, which is wont to amend and grind down the corrupt morals of men by wars, and likewise to exercise by such afflictions a just and praiseworthy human life, and either, when it is proved, to transfer it to better things, or to keep it still on this earth for other purposes;

but as for that mercy by which the fierce barbarians, contrary to the custom of wars, spared them, whether anywhere for the name of Christ or in places most dedicated to the name of Christ and most spacious, chosen for a more generous mercy to hold the multitude—this they ought to attribute to Christian times, for this to give thanks to God, for this to run truly to his name in order to escape the punishments of eternal fire, the name that many of them falsely took up in order to escape the punishments of present destruction.

For of those whom you see wantonly and insolently insulting the servants of Christ, there are very many among them who would not have escaped that destruction and slaughter had they not pretended to be servants of Christ. And now with ungrateful pride and most impious madness they resist his name with a perverse heart, so that they may be punished with everlasting darkness, the name to which they fled with a lip even cunning, that they might enjoy the temporal light.

**2**. So many wars have been waged and recorded, whether before Rome was founded or from its rise and rule: let them read and bring forward instances either that some city was so taken by foreigners that the enemies who had taken it spared those whom they had found to have fled to the temples of their gods, or that some barbarian leader commanded that, when a town was stormed, no one should be struck who had been found in this or that temple.

Did not Aeneas see Priam *defiling with his blood the fires that he himself had consecrated upon the altars*[^9]?

Did not Diomedes and Ulysses, *having slain the guards of the citadel's height, seize the sacred image and dare with bloody hands to touch the virgin fillets of the goddess?*

And yet what follows is not true: *from that moment the hopes of the Greeks ebbed away and, slipping back, were carried backward*[^10].

For afterward they conquered, afterward they destroyed Troy with sword and fire, afterward they cut down Priam as he fled to the altars[^11].

Nor did Troy perish because it lost Minerva. For what had Minerva herself first lost, that she should perish? Her own guards, perhaps? This indeed is true; for when they were slain, she could be carried off. For it was not the men who were guarded by the image, but the image that was guarded by the men. How then was she worshiped, that she might guard the fatherland and its citizens, when she could not guard her own guards?

**3**. Behold the kind of gods to whom the Romans rejoiced that they had entrusted their city to be guarded! O error all too pitiable! And they are angry with us when we say such things about their gods; yet they are not angry with their own authors, whom they paid a fee to learn, and besides held the teachers themselves most worthy of a public salary and of honors. Indeed in Virgil—whom for this very reason little children read, namely that the great poet, the most illustrious and best of all, drunk in by tender minds, may not easily be blotted out by forgetfulness, according to that line of Horace: *the jar will long keep the odor with which once when new it was steeped*[^12]—in this Virgil, then, Juno is brought in, hostile to the Trojans, saying to Aeolus the king of the winds, to stir him up against them: *a race hateful to me sails the Tyrrhenian sea, carrying Ilium into Italy and its conquered household gods*[^13].

Was it then to these *conquered household gods* that they ought prudently to have entrusted Rome, that it might not be conquered? But Juno was saying these things like an angry woman, not knowing what she was speaking. What of Aeneas himself, so often called *pious*? Does he not relate thus: *Panthus, son of Othrys, priest of the citadel and of Phoebus, himself drags along in his hand the sacred things and the conquered gods and his little grandson, and frantic makes for the threshold at a run*[^14]? Does he not present the very gods, whom he does not hesitate to call conquered, as entrusted to him rather than himself to them, when it is said to him: *To you Troy commends her sacred things and her household gods*[^15]?

If, therefore, Virgil calls such gods both conquered and entrusted to a man so that, even though conquered, they might somehow escape, what madness is it to suppose that Rome was wisely committed to these guardians, and that it could not have been laid waste unless it had lost them? Indeed, to worship conquered gods as protectors and defenders—what is this but to hold not good divinities, but bad names?

For how much more wisely is it believed, not that Rome would not have come to that disaster unless they had first perished, but rather that they would long ago have perished unless Rome had preserved them as far as it could! For who, when he considers it, does not see with what great vanity it was presumed that Rome could not be conquered under conquered defenders, and that it perished because it lost its guardian gods—when in fact the one possible cause of its perishing might have been its wanting to have guardians who themselves would perish? And so, when those things about conquered gods were being written and sung, it was not that the poets took pleasure in lying, but that the truth compelled men of sense to confess it.

But these matters are to be treated carefully and at length in another place more suitable to them. For now I will, as best I can, briefly deal with what I had set out to say about ungrateful men, who blasphemously lay to Christ's charge the evils they deservedly suffer for the perversity of their own morals; while the fact that even such men are spared for Christ's sake they do not deign to notice, and they exercise against his name, in the madness of a sacrilegious perversity, the very tongues with which they falsely took up that name in order to live, or which in his consecrated places they restrained out of fear, so that, safe and protected there, where for his sake they had been unharmed by the enemy, they might from that very place leap out against him with the curses of enemies.

## What Christ grants in the sparing of the conquered, and what Juno grants.

**4**. Troy itself, as I said, the mother of the Roman people, in the consecrated places of its own gods could not protect its citizens from the fires and the sword of the Greeks, who worshiped those very same gods. On the contrary, *in the sanctuary of Juno chosen guards, Phoenix and dread Ulysses, kept watch over the plunder; here from all sides the treasure of Troy, snatched from the burning shrines, and the tables of the gods, and bowls of solid gold, and captured raiment were heaped up. Boys and trembling mothers in a long line stood round about*[^16].

A place consecrated to so great a goddess was chosen, you see, not as one from which it was not permitted to lead out captives, but as one in which it was permitted to shut captives in. Compare now that sanctuary—not of any common god or one from the crowd of the rabble, but of Jupiter's own *sister and wife* and the queen of all the gods—with the memorial shrines of our apostles. To the one were carried the spoils snatched from the burning temples and gods, not to be given to the conquered but to be divided among the conquerors; to the other, even what was found to belong elsewhere to such places was carried back with honor and most reverent observance.

There liberty was lost, here preserved; there captivity was enclosed, here forbidden; there men were pressed to be possessed by mastering enemies, here they were led to be set free by pitying ones. In short, that temple of Juno had been chosen by the avarice and pride of frivolous little Greeks; these basilicas of Christ by the mercy and humility even of monstrous barbarians.

Unless perhaps the Greeks, in that victory of theirs, did indeed spare the temples of the gods they held in common, and did not dare to strike down or take captive the wretched and conquered Trojans who fled there, and Virgil, in the manner of poets, lied about these things. On the contrary, he described the custom of enemies who overthrow cities.

**5**. This custom even Cato, as Sallust writes, a historian of noble truthfulness, does not fail to mention in the opinion he delivered in the senate concerning the conspirators: *maidens and boys are seized, children are torn from their parents' embrace, mothers of households suffer whatever has pleased the victors, temples and homes are despoiled, slaughter and burnings are carried out; in short, everything is filled with arms, corpses, blood, and grief*[^17].

Here, if he had been silent about the temples, we would suppose that enemies were accustomed to spare the seats of the gods. And these things the Roman temples feared not from foreign enemies but from Catiline and his associates, most noble senators and Roman citizens. But these, of course, were ruined men and parricides of their fatherland.

**6**. Why, then, should our discourse range through the many nations that have waged wars among themselves and nowhere spared the conquered in the seats of their gods? Let us look at the Romans themselves; the Romans themselves, I say, let us recall and consider, of whose chief praise it was said: *to spare the conquered and to subdue the proud*[^18], and that *when a wrong had been received they preferred to forgive rather than to pursue it*[^19]: when they stormed and captured and overthrew so many and so great cities in order to extend their dominion far and wide, let us be shown what temples they were accustomed to exempt, so that whoever fled to them was set free. Or did they do this, and the writers of those very deeds kept silent about it? In that case, men who especially sought out things to praise would have passed over these most illustrious tokens of piety according to their own standard?

The distinguished bearer of the Roman name, Marcus Marcellus, who took the most beautifully adorned city of Syracuse, is reported first to have wept for it as it was about to fall, and to have shed his own tears for it before its blood. He also took care that chastity should be preserved even in the enemy. For before the victor ordered the town to be invaded, he established by edict that no one should violate a free person[^20]. Yet the city was overthrown after the manner of wars, and nowhere is it read that this so chaste and merciful commander gave an order that whoever fled to this or that temple should be held unharmed[^21]. This would surely have in no way been passed over, since neither his weeping nor what he had decreed for the inviolability of chastity could be left unmentioned. Fabius, the overthrower of the city of Tarentum, is praised for having abstained from the plundering of images. For when his secretary suggested to him what he wished done about the statues of the gods, of which many had been captured, he seasoned his self-restraint even with a joke. For he asked of what kind they were, and when it was reported to him that not only were there many great ones, but even some armed, he said, *let us leave the Tarentines their angry gods*[^22]. Since, then, the writers of Roman deeds could not be silent either about the one's weeping or the other's laughter, neither about the one's chaste mercy nor the other's witty restraint, how could it have been passed over, had they spared any men in honor of some one of their gods so as to forbid slaughter or captivity in any temple?

**7**. Whatever, then, of devastation, slaughter, plundering, burning, and affliction was committed in that most recent Roman disaster, this the custom of wars brought about. But what was done in a new way—that, with an unaccustomed appearance of things, the barbarian monstrousness showed itself so mild that the most spacious basilicas were chosen and decreed to be filled with the people who were to be spared, where no one was struck, from where no one was seized, to where many were led to be set free by pitying enemies, from where none who were to be taken captive were led away even by cruel enemies—this is to be attributed to the name of Christ, this to Christian times. Whoever does not see this is blind; whoever sees it and does not praise it is ungrateful; whoever resists the one who praises it is mad. Far be it that any prudent person should lay this to the ferocity of the barbarians. The most savage and ferocious minds he terrified, he reined in, he wondrously tempered, who through the prophet said so long before: *I will visit their iniquities with the rod and their sins with scourges; but my mercy I will not scatter from them*[^23].

## By calamities the wicked are corrected and the good instructed, by the most just ordering of God.

**8**. Someone will say: "Why, then, did this divine mercy reach even the impious and the ungrateful?" Why do we think, except because it was extended by him who daily *makes his sun rise on the good and the evil and sends rain on the just and the unjust*[^24]? For although some of them, thinking on these things, correct themselves from their impiety by repenting, while others, as the Apostle says, despising *the riches of God's goodness and forbearance*, *according to their hardness of heart and impenitent heart store up for themselves wrath on the day of wrath and of the revelation of the just judgment of God, who will render to each according to his works*[^25]: nevertheless the patience of God invites the wicked to repentance, just as the scourge of God instructs the good in patience; and likewise the mercy of God embraces the good to cherish them, just as the severity of God corrects the wicked to punish them.

For it pleased divine providence to prepare for the just goods in the world to come that the unjust will not enjoy, and for the impious evils with which the good will not be tormented. But these temporal goods and evils it willed to be common to both, so that the goods might not be too eagerly sought, which the wicked too are seen to have, nor the evils basely avoided, with which the good as well are often afflicted.

But it matters very greatly what use is made either of those things called prosperous or of those called adverse. For the good man is neither lifted up by temporal goods nor broken by evils; the wicked man, however, is punished by this kind of unhappiness for the very reason that he is corrupted by happiness.

Yet God often shows his working more evidently even in the distribution of these things. For if every sin were now struck with manifest punishment, it would be thought that nothing was reserved for the last judgment; on the other hand, if the divinity now punished no sin openly, it would be believed that there was no divine providence. Likewise in favorable things: if God did not grant them to certain people who ask, with the most evident generosity, we would say that these things did not pertain to him; and likewise, if he gave them to all who ask, we would suppose that he was to be served only for the sake of such rewards, and such service would make us not pious but rather greedy and covetous.

Since these things are so, whoever, good and wicked alike, were afflicted together, they are not therefore themselves undistinguished because what both suffered was undistinguished. For the unlikeness of those who suffer remains even in the likeness of their sufferings, and though under the same torment, virtue and vice are not the same.

For just as under one fire gold gleams red and chaff smokes, and under the same threshing sledge the stalks are crushed and the grain is cleansed, nor are the dregs mixed with the oil because they are pressed out by the same weight of the press: so one and the same force, bearing down, proves, purifies, and refines the good; condemns, lays waste, and destroys the wicked. Hence in the same affliction the wicked detest and blaspheme God, while the good pray and praise him. So much does it matter, not what things one suffers, but what sort of person suffers. For stirred up by the same motion, filth gives off a horrible stench, and ointment a sweet fragrance.

## Even what Christians suffered in the enemy's devastation did not escape God's providence, since even the good have need of correction.

**9**. What, then, did Christians suffer in that devastation of things that would not rather have availed for their advancement, if they considered it faithfully? First, that humbly reflecting on the very sins on account of which God, in his indignation, filled the world with such great calamities—though they are far from the criminal, the shameful, and the impious—they nevertheless do not reckon themselves so free from offenses as to judge themselves unworthy of suffering temporal evils for them.

For setting aside the fact that everyone, however praiseworthily he lives, yields in certain matters to carnal desire—if not to the monstrousness of crimes and the whirlpool of shameful deeds and the abomination of impiety, yet to some sins, either rare or, the smaller they are, the more frequent: setting this aside, then, who, after all, is easily found who holds those very people, on account of whose horrible pride and luxury and avarice and execrable iniquities and impieties God, as he foretold in his threats, grinds down the lands[^26], as they ought to be held? who lives with them as one ought to live with such people?

For very often we wrongly look the other way from teaching and admonishing them, sometimes even from rebuking and correcting them, whether because the labor is irksome, or because we are ashamed to offend their face, or because we shun enmities, lest they hinder and harm us in those temporal things which either our desire still strives to obtain or our weakness dreads to lose; so that, although the life of the wicked displeases the good, and therefore they do not fall with them into that condemnation which is prepared after this life for such people,

nevertheless, because they spare the damnable sins of the wicked—while they fear them in their own, though light and venial—they are justly scourged with them in temporal matters, although they are not punished with them for eternity; justly do they feel this life bitter, when they are afflicted by God along with them, the sweetness of which life, by loving it, they were unwilling to make bitter to those who were sinning.

For if someone refrains from rebuking and correcting those who do wrong because he is looking for a more suitable time, or fears for those very people, lest they be made worse by it, or lest they hinder others, weak ones who are to be instructed in a good and pious life, and press them down and turn them away from the faith: this does not seem to be an occasion of desire, but a counsel of love.

What is blameworthy is this: that those who live differently and shrink from the deeds of the wicked nevertheless spare the sins of others, which they ought to unteach or rebuke, while they avoid giving offense to them, lest they harm themselves in those things which the good lawfully and innocently use, but more greedily than was fitting for those who are pilgrims in this world and bear before themselves the hope of the heavenly fatherland.

For not only do the weaker, who lead the married life, who have children or seek to have them, who possess homes and households—whom the Apostle addresses in the churches, teaching and admonishing how they ought to live, both wives with husbands and husbands with wives, and children with parents and parents with children, and slaves with masters and masters with slaves[^27]—gladly obtain many temporal, many earthly things, and lose them with distress, on account of which they do not dare to offend men whose most contaminated and most wicked life displeases them; but even those who hold a higher grade of life, and are not ensnared by the bonds of marriage, and make use of little food and clothing, very often, while they fear the snares and assaults of the wicked against their own reputation and safety, refrain from reproving them.

And although they do not fear them so much as to yield to any of their terrors and wickednesses so as to commit similar deeds, yet those very things which they do not commit with them they often refuse to correct, when perhaps by correcting they might set some right; lest, if they could not, their own safety and reputation should come into danger and ruin. And this they do not by that consideration in which they see their reputation and safety to be necessary for the benefit of the men to be instructed, but rather by that weakness in which the flattering tongue and the human day[^28] give pleasure, and the judgment of the crowd and the torture or destruction of the flesh are dreaded; that is, on account of certain bonds of desire, not on account of the duties of love.

It does not therefore seem to me a small reason why the good too are scourged along with the wicked, when it pleases God to punish ruined morals even by the affliction of temporal penalties. For they are scourged together, not because they lead an evil life together, but because they love the temporal life together—not indeed equally, yet together—which the good ought to despise, so that the others, corrected and set right, might attain the eternal life; and if they should be unwilling to be companions in attaining it, they should be borne with and loved as enemies, since, as long as they live, it is always uncertain whether they will change their will for the better.

In this matter, those certainly do not have an equal but a far graver cause to whom it is said through the prophet: *he indeed will die in his own sin, but his blood I will require at the watchman's hand*[^29]. For watchmen—that is, the overseers of the peoples—have been appointed in the churches for this very purpose: that they should not spare sins by reproving them.

Nor yet is anyone wholly free from this kind of fault who, though he be not an overseer, nevertheless knows in those with whom the necessity of this life joins him many things to be admonished or reproved, and neglects them, avoiding their offense on account of those things which in this life he uses not undeservedly, but takes more delight in than he ought.

Then the good have another cause why they are afflicted by temporal evils, such as Job had: that the human mind itself may be proved and known to itself, with how great a strength of piety it loves God freely.

## To the faithful and pious every calamity is turned to good, whether the loss of riches . . .

**10**. These things rightly considered and discerned, consider whether anything evil befell the faithful and pious that was not turned to their good—unless perhaps that apostolic sentence is to be thought void, where he says: *we know that for those who love God all things work together for good*[^30]. They lost all that they had. Surely not faith? Surely not piety? Surely not the goods of the inner man, who is rich before God[^31]? These are the riches of Christians, in which the Apostle, abounding, said: *there is great gain in godliness with contentment. For we brought nothing into this world, and we cannot take anything out of it. But having food and clothing, with these let us be content. For those who desire to be rich fall into temptation and a snare and into many senseless and harmful desires that plunge men into ruin and destruction. For the love of money is the root of all evils; it is through this craving that some have wandered away from the faith and pierced themselves with many pains*[^32].

Those, then, whose earthly riches perished in that devastation, if they held them in the way they had heard from that man poor outwardly, rich inwardly—that is, if they used the world as though not using it[^33]—were able to say what that man said who was grievously tempted and not at all overcome: *naked I came forth from my mother's womb, naked shall I return to the earth. The Lord gave, the Lord has taken away; as it pleased the Lord, so has it been done; blessed be the name of the Lord*[^34]; so that the good servant might hold his Lord's very will as his great wealth, and, attending it in mind, grow rich, and not be saddened at being left, while living, without those things which, dying, he would soon have left behind.

But those weaker ones who, though they did not prefer these earthly goods to Christ, nevertheless clung to them with some little desire, felt by losing them how much they had sinned in loving them. For they grieved as much as they *had pierced themselves with pains*, as I recalled above that the Apostle said. For it was needful that the discipline of experience be added to them as well, the discipline of words having been so long neglected by them.

For when the Apostle said, *those who desire to be rich fall into temptation*, and the rest, he certainly reproved desire in riches, not the possession; for elsewhere he gave a command, saying: *charge the rich of this world not to be haughty, nor to set their hope on the uncertainty of riches, but on the living God, who furnishes us with everything richly to enjoy: let them do good, be rich in good works, give readily, share, and store up for themselves a good foundation for the future, so that they may take hold of the true life*[^35]. Those who did these things with their riches were consoled for light losses by great gains, and rejoiced more in those things which by readily giving they more safely kept, than they grieved over those which by timidly retaining they the more easily lost.

For what could perish on earth was what it irked them to transfer from there. For those who received the counsel of their Lord, who says: *do not lay up for yourselves treasures on earth, where moth and rust consume and where thieves dig through and steal; but lay up for yourselves treasure in heaven, where no thief approaches nor moth corrupts; for where your treasure is, there will your heart be also*[^36], proved in the time of tribulation how rightly they had been wise in not despising the most truthful teacher and the most faithful and invincible guardian of their treasure. For if many rejoiced that they had their riches there where it chanced that the enemy did not come, how much more surely and securely could those rejoice who at the warning of their God migrated to that place where he could not come at all!

Hence our Paulinus, bishop of Nola, who from a most opulent rich man became by his own will most poor and most abundantly holy, when the barbarians laid waste Nola itself and he was held by them, prayed thus in his heart, as we afterward learned from him: *Lord, let me not be tortured for gold and silver; for where all my goods are, you know*. For he had all his goods there where he who had foretold that these evils would come upon the world had shown him to store and treasure them up.

And through this, those who had obeyed their Lord admonishing them where and how they ought to treasure up did not lose even their earthly riches when the barbarians attacked. But those who repented of not having obeyed learned what should be done with such things—if not by foregoing wisdom, then certainly by subsequent experience.

## . . . or torments and tortures and famine.

But, it is said, certain good Christians too were tortured to make them give up their goods to the enemy. Yet they could neither give up nor lose the good by which they themselves were good. But if they preferred to be tortured rather than to give up the mammon of iniquity, they were not good.

But those who suffered so much for gold ought to have been admonished how much was to be endured for Christ, so that they might rather learn to love him who enriches with eternal happiness those who suffer for him, not gold and silver, for which to suffer was most wretched, whether it was hidden by lying or given up by telling the truth. For amid the torments no one lost Christ by confessing him, no one kept gold except by denying it. Wherefore the torments which taught that an incorruptible good was to be loved were perhaps more useful than those goods which, without any useful fruit, tortured their owners by love of them.

But some, even though they had nothing to give up, were tortured because it was not believed. And these perhaps desired to have it and were not poor by a holy will; to whom it had to be shown that not their possessions but their very desires were worthy of such tortures. But if, by their resolve of a better life, they did not have gold and silver stored away, I do not indeed know whether it happened to any such person that he was tortured because he was believed to have it; yet even if it did happen, surely the one who amid those torments confessed his holy poverty confessed Christ. Wherefore, even if he did not deserve to be believed by the enemy, the confessor of holy poverty nevertheless could not be tortured without a heavenly reward.

Many Christians too, they say, a long-lasting famine laid waste. This also the good faithful turned to their own use by piously enduring it. For those whom the famine killed it snatched away from the evils of this life, as bodily sickness does; but those whom it did not kill it taught to live more sparingly, taught to fast more lengthily.

## There is no cause to dread that the pious were killed or lacked burial, which even philosophers despised.

**11**. But indeed many Christians too were killed, many consumed by the foul variety of many kinds of death. If this is to be borne with grief, it is certainly common to all who have been brought forth into this life. This I know: that no one has died who was not at some time going to die. But the end of life makes a life as long as it does a short one to be one and the same thing. For there is not one thing better and another worse, or one greater and another shorter, of a thing that now equally is not. And what does it matter by what kind of death this life is ended, when he to whom it is ended is not compelled to die again? But since, under the daily chances of this life, innumerable deaths in a manner threaten every mortal, as long as it is uncertain which of them is going to come, I ask whether it is better to suffer one by dying or to fear them all by living. Nor am I unaware how much more readily one would choose to live long under the fear of so many deaths than, by dying once, thereafter to dread none. But it is one thing that the weakly fearful sense of the flesh shrinks from, and another that the reason of the mind, carefully sifted out, establishes.

That death is not to be thought evil which a good life has preceded. For nothing makes death evil except what follows death. They, therefore, who must necessarily die need not be much concerned by what chance it comes about that they die, but whither dying they are compelled to go. Since, then, Christians know that the death of the religious poor man amid the tongues of licking dogs was far better than that of the impious rich man in his purple and fine linen[^37], how did those dreadful kinds of death harm the dead who had lived well?

**12**. But, it is said, in so great a heap of corpses they could not even be buried[^38]. But pious faith does not too greatly dread this, holding it foretold that not even devouring beasts will harm bodies that are to rise again, of which not a hair of the head will perish[^39]. The Truth would in no way say, *do not fear those who kill the body but cannot kill the soul*[^40], if whatever enemies wished to do with the bodies of the slain could harm the life to come.

Unless perhaps someone is so absurd as to contend that those who kill the body are not to be feared before death, lest they kill the body, and are to be feared after death, lest they not allow the slain body to be buried. False, then, is what Christ says: *those who kill the body and afterward have nothing they can do*[^41], if they have so many things they can do with the corpses. Far be it that what the Truth said should be false. For it was said that they do something when they kill, because there is sensation in the body in the killing; but afterward they have nothing they can do, because there is no sensation in the slain body.

And so the earth did not cover many bodies of Christians; but no one separated any of them from heaven and earth, all of which the presence of him fills who knows whence to raise up again what he created. It is indeed said in the psalm: *they have set the dead bodies of your servants as food for the birds of heaven, the flesh of your saints for the beasts of the earth; they have poured out their blood like water round about Jerusalem, and there was none to bury them*[^42], but rather to magnify the cruelty of those who did these things, not the unhappiness of those who suffered them. For although these things seem hard and dire in the sight of men, yet *precious in the sight of the Lord is the death of his saints*[^43].

Accordingly, all these things—that is, the care of the funeral, the manner of burial, the pomp of the obsequies—are more the consolations of the living than helps for the dead[^44]. If a costly burial does any good to the impious, a cheap one or none will harm the pious. Distinguished obsequies in the sight of men were furnished to that purple-clad rich man by a crowd of servants; but far more distinguished ones in the sight of the Lord were rendered to that ulcerous poor man by the ministry of angels, who did not carry him out to a marble tomb but bore him up into Abraham's bosom[^45].

These things they laugh at, against whom we have undertaken to defend the city of God. Yet even their own philosophers despised the care of burial[^46]. And often whole armies, while they died for their earthly fatherland, took no care where they would afterward lie or for what beasts they would become food, and it was permitted to the poets to say plausibly on this matter: *he who has no urn is covered by the sky*[^47]. How much less ought they to insult Christians over unburied bodies, to whom it is promised that the very reformation of the flesh and of all its members will be restored and made whole, not only from the earth but even from the most secret recess of the other elements, into which the dissolved corpses have departed, in a moment of time[^48].

**13**. Nevertheless, the bodies of the dead are not therefore to be despised and cast away, and most of all of the just and faithful, of which the Spirit holily made use as instruments and vessels for all good works. For if a father's garment and ring, and anything of this kind, is the dearer to descendants the greater their affection toward their parents, then in no way are the bodies themselves to be spurned, which surely we wear far more intimately and closely joined than any garments. For these belong not to ornament or aid that is applied from without, but to the very nature of man. Hence the funerals of the just men of old were tended with dutiful piety, the obsequies celebrated, and burial provided, and they themselves, while they lived, gave commands to their sons about burying or even transferring their bodies[^49]; and Tobit is commended, by the testimony of the angel, for having merited God's favor by burying the dead[^50]. The Lord himself too, who was to rise on the third day, proclaims the good work of the religious woman and commends it to be proclaimed, that she poured precious ointment over his members and did this for his burial[^51]. And those are commemorated with praise in the Gospel who took his body down from the cross and took care that it should be carefully and honorably wrapped and buried[^52].

But these authorities do not admonish us of this, that there is any sensation in corpses, but signify that the bodies of the dead too pertain to the providence of God, to whom even such offices of piety are pleasing, for the sake of establishing the faith of the resurrection. Here too that is wholesomely learned: how great can be the recompense for the alms which we render to the living and sentient, if not even this perishes before God, that is paid out in office and care to the lifeless members of men. There are indeed other things which the holy patriarchs wished to be understood as said in a prophetic spirit about the embalming or transferring of their bodies[^53]; but this is not the place to treat them at length, since what we have said suffices.

But if those things which are necessary for sustaining the living—such as food and clothing—do not, even when they are lacking with grievous affliction, break in the good the virtue of bearing and enduring, nor uproot piety from the soul, but make it more fruitful by exercise: how much more do the things which are wont to be applied to the care of funerals and the embalming of the bodies of the dead, when they are lacking, not make wretched those already at rest in the hidden dwellings of the pious! And through this, however much these things were lacking to the corpses of Christians in that devastation of the great city or even of other towns, it is neither the fault of the living, who could not furnish them, nor a punishment of the dead, who cannot feel them.

## Even captivity is not without good.

**14**. But many, they say, Christians too, were led away captive. This indeed is most wretched, if they could be led anywhere where they did not find their God. There are in the holy Scriptures great consolations even for this disaster. There were in captivity the three young men, there was Daniel, there were other prophets[^54]; nor was God lacking as a consoler. So, then, he did not desert his faithful under the dominion of a nation, barbarous indeed yet human, who did not desert the prophet even in the belly of the beast[^55]. These things too those with whom we are dealing prefer to mock rather than to believe—though they believe in their own writings that Arion of Methymna, the most noble lyre-player, when he had been cast from a ship, was taken up on a dolphin's back and carried to land[^56]. But that account of ours about the prophet Jonah is more incredible. Plainly more incredible because more wondrous, and more wondrous because it shows greater power.

## Regulus's unconquered fortitude points us toward true liberty.

**15**. These people have, however, even concerning captivity to be voluntarily endured for the sake of religion, a most noble example among their own illustrious men. Marcus Regulus, a commander of the Roman people, was a captive among the Carthaginians[^57]. When they preferred to have their own men returned to them by the Romans rather than to keep the Romans' captives, they sent this very Regulus in particular, with their envoys, to Rome to obtain this, first binding him by an oath to return to Carthage if he should not accomplish what they wished. He went, and in the senate persuaded the opposite, since he did not judge it useful to the Roman commonwealth to exchange captives.

Nor after this persuasion was he compelled by his own people to return to the enemy; but because he had sworn, he fulfilled it of his own accord. And they killed him with contrived and horrible tortures. For they shut him up in a narrow box, where he was forced to stand, fixed all around with very sharp nails, so that he could lean on no part of it without the most atrocious pains, and put him to death even by keeping him awake.

They rightly, certainly, praise a virtue greater than so great a misfortune[^58]. And he had sworn by the gods, by the prohibition of whose worship these people suppose that these disasters are inflicted on the human race.

Those gods, then, who were worshiped for this purpose, that they might make this life prosperous—if they either willed or permitted these penalties to be imposed on one who swore truly, what worse could they in their anger do to a perjurer?

But why should I not rather bring my reasoning to a conclusion on both sides? He certainly worshiped the gods in such a way that, for the sake of fidelity to his oath, he would neither remain in his fatherland nor go from there anywhere else, but did not in the least hesitate to return to his most bitter enemies. If he thought this useful for this life, of which he merited so horrible an end, he was beyond doubt deceived. For by his own example he taught that the gods are of no avail to their worshipers for this temporal happiness, since indeed that man, devoted to their worship, was both conquered and led away captive, and, because he was unwilling to act otherwise than as he had sworn through them, was killed, tortured by a new and previously unheard-of and exceedingly horrible kind of punishment.

But if the worship of the gods renders happiness as a reward after this life, why do they slander Christian times, saying that that calamity befell the City because it ceased to worship its gods, when even one who worshiped them most diligently could become as unhappy as that Regulus was? Unless perhaps, against the clearest truth, someone strives with so great a madness of wondrous blindness as to dare to contend that a whole city worshiping the gods cannot be unhappy, but one man can—because, namely, the power of their gods is fit to preserve many rather than individuals, when the multitude is composed of individuals.

But if they say that Marcus Regulus, even in that captivity and those tortures of the body, could be blessed by virtue of soul, then let rather the true virtue be sought by which a city too can be blessed. For a city is not blessed from one source and a man from another, since a city is nothing other than a harmonious multitude of men. Wherefore for now I do not yet dispute what sort of virtue there was in Regulus; it suffices now that by this most noble example they are compelled to confess that the gods are not to be worshiped for the sake of bodily goods or of those things which befall a man from without, since indeed that man preferred to lack all these rather than to offend the gods by whom he had sworn.

But what shall we do with men who boast that they had such a citizen as they fear to have a city? And if they do not fear it, let them confess, then, that something such as befell Regulus could befall even a city worshiping the gods as diligently as he did, and let them not slander Christian times. But since the question arose about those Christians who were also taken captive, let those look at this and be silent who from this matter shamelessly and imprudently mock the most wholesome religion. For if it was no disgrace to their gods that their most attentive worshiper, while he kept fidelity to his oath toward them, lacked a fatherland, since he had no other, and as a captive among enemies was killed by a long death with the punishment of a new cruelty, much less is the Christian name to be charged because of the captivity of its consecrated ones, who, awaiting the heavenly fatherland with truthful faith, knew themselves to be pilgrims even in their own dwellings[^59].

## True chastity does not suffer defilement.

**16**. They think, indeed, that they bring a great charge against Christians when, exaggerating their captivity, they add even the rapes committed, not only on other men's wives and on virgins about to marry, but even on certain consecrated women. Here, in truth, it is not faith, not piety, not the very virtue called chastity, but rather our discussion that is hemmed in within certain straits between modesty and reason.

Nor do we here care so much to give an answer to others as to give consolation to our own people. Let it therefore first of all be laid down and established that the virtue by which one lives rightly commands from its seat in the soul the members of the body, and that the body is made holy by the use of a holy will, and that, while this remains unshaken and steadfast, whatever anyone else does with the body or in the body which cannot be avoided without one's own sin is beyond the fault of the one who suffers it.

But because not only what pertains to pain but also what pertains to lust can be perpetrated on another's body: whatever such thing has been done, even if it does not shake out the chastity retained with a most steadfast soul, nevertheless inflicts shame, lest it be believed that what perhaps could not be done without some pleasure of the flesh was done with the will of the mind as well.

**17**. And through this, even those who killed themselves lest they suffer anything of this kind—what human feeling would not wish them to be pardoned? And those who were unwilling to kill themselves, lest they avoid another's shameful deed by their own crime—whoever lays this to their charge will not himself avoid the charge of folly.

For surely if it is not permitted by private authority to kill a man, even a guilty one, whom no law grants license to kill, then assuredly one who kills himself too is a murderer, and he becomes the more guilty in killing himself the more innocent he was in the cause for which he thought he ought to be killed. For if we deservedly detest the deed of Judas, and the Truth judges that, when he hanged himself with a noose, he increased rather than expiated the guilt of that wicked betrayal[^60]—since, repenting unto destruction by despairing of God's mercy, he left himself no room for wholesome repentance: how much more ought he to abstain from his own slaying who has nothing in himself to punish by such a penalty! For Judas, when he killed himself, killed a wicked man, and yet he ended this life guilty not only of Christ's death but also of his own, since, though killed for his own crime, he was killed by another crime of his own. Why, then, should a man who has done no evil do evil to himself, and by killing himself kill an innocent man, lest he suffer another guilty man, and perpetrate in himself his own sin, lest another's sin be perpetrated in him?

## Defilement in oppressed flesh does not diminish the holiness of the soul.

**18**. But it is feared that another's lust may defile us. It will not defile, if it is another's; but if it defiles, it will not be another's. But since chastity is a virtue of the soul and has fortitude as its companion, by which it resolves to endure any evils rather than consent to evil, and since no high-souled and chaste person has it in his power what is done with his flesh, but only what he assents to or refuses with his mind: who, with that same sound mind, would think that he loses his chastity if perchance a lust not his own is exercised and sated upon his seized and oppressed flesh? For if chastity perishes in this way, then surely chastity will not be a virtue of the soul, nor will it belong to those goods by which one lives well, but will be numbered among the goods of the body, such as strength, beauty, sound health, and anything of this kind; which goods, even if they are diminished, do not at all diminish a good and just life. But if chastity is something of this kind, why is there labor for it, lest it be lost, even at the peril of the body? But if it is a good of the soul, it is not lost even when the body is oppressed. Indeed, the good of holy continence, when it does not yield to the uncleanness of carnal desires, sanctifies the body itself; and therefore, when it persists with unshaken intention in not yielding to them, the holiness does not perish even from the body itself, because the will to use it holily perseveres, and, so far as is in it, even the capacity.

For the body is not holy because its members are intact, or because they are touched by no contact, since by various accidents they can, even when wounded, suffer violence, and physicians, sometimes aiding health, do things there which the sight shrinks from. A midwife, examining as it were with her hand the integrity of a certain virgin—whether through malice, ignorance, or accident—destroyed it while inspecting it. I do not suppose that anyone is so foolishly wise as to think that anything was thereby lost even of the holiness of the body itself, although the integrity of that member was now lost. Wherefore, while the resolve of the soul remains—through which the body too merited to be sanctified—the violence of another's lust does not take away holiness even from the body itself, which the perseverance of one's own continence preserves.

Or rather, if some woman, corrupted in mind and having violated the resolve that she had vowed to God, goes off to be defiled by her seducer—do we call her, as she goes off to this, holy even in body, that holiness of soul through which the body was being sanctified having been lost and destroyed? Far be this error, and from it let us rather be admonished that the holiness of the body is not lost while the holiness of the soul remains, even when the body is oppressed, just as the holiness of the body too is lost when the holiness of the soul is violated, even when the body is untouched.

Wherefore a woman violently oppressed without any consent of her own and crushed by another's sin has nothing in herself to punish by a voluntary death; how much less before this is done! lest a certain murder be committed when the shameful deed itself, though another's, still hangs uncertain.

## Lucretia, who was oppressed against her will, unjustly killed herself—if indeed she was chaste.

**19**. But will those perhaps dare to contradict this clear reasoning—by which we say that when the body is oppressed, the resolve of chastity in no way changed by any consent to evil, the shameful deed is only his who oppressing has lain with her, not hers who, oppressed, has by no will consented to him who lay with her—against whom we defend not only the minds but also the bodies of Christian women oppressed in captivity as holy? Lucretia, certainly, a noble Roman matron of old, they extol with great praises of chastity. When the son of King Tarquin had lustfully gained possession of her body by violently oppressing it, she disclosed the crime of that most wicked youth to her husband Collatinus and her kinsman Brutus, men most illustrious and brave, and bound them to vengeance. Then, sick at and impatient of the foul deed committed against her, she put herself to death[^61].

What shall we say? Is she to be judged an adulteress or chaste? Who would think there was any need to labor over this question? Excellently and truly someone declaiming on this says: *wondrous to say, there were two, and one committed adultery*[^62]. Splendidly and most truly. For looking in the union of two bodies at the most defiled desire of the one and the most chaste will of the other, and attending not to what was done by the joining of members but to what was done by the diversity of souls, he says, *there were two, and one committed adultery*.

But what is this, that vengeance is taken more severely on her who did not commit adultery? For he was driven with his father from the fatherland, while she was slain with the supreme penalty. If that is not unchastity by which one is oppressed unwilling, this is not justice by which the chaste one is punished. I call upon you, laws and judges of Rome. Surely, after crimes have been committed, you did not wish even any wicked man to be killed uncondemned and with impunity. If, therefore, anyone brought this charge before your judgment and it were proved to you that a woman not only uncondemned but even chaste and innocent had been killed, would you not punish the one who had done it with fitting severity?

This is what that Lucretia did; that, that Lucretia so celebrated, killed besides the innocent, chaste Lucretia who had suffered violence. Pronounce sentence. But if you cannot do so for this reason, that she is not present whom you might punish, why do you praise with such celebration the killer of an innocent and chaste woman?

Whom certainly before the judges of the underworld—even such as are sung in the songs of your poets—you defend by no reasoning, placed as she is, namely, among those *who, guiltless, dealt themselves death by their own hand, and, loathing the light, cast away their souls;* for whom, desiring to return to the world above, *the divine law stands in the way, and the sad marsh of the unlovely water binds them fast*[^63].

Or is she perhaps not there because she killed herself not guiltless, but ill conscious of guilt? For what if (which she alone could know) she consented to the youth violently rushing upon her, enticed even by her own lust, and, punishing this in herself, so grieved that she thought it had to be expiated by death? Although not even so ought she to have killed herself, if she could have done fruitful repentance before the false gods.

But nevertheless, if it is perhaps so, and that saying is false, *that there were two, and one committed adultery*, but rather both committed adultery, the one by manifest assault, the other by hidden consent: then she did not kill herself guiltless, and therefore it can be said by her learned defenders that she is not among those in the underworld *who, guiltless, dealt themselves death by their own hand*. But thus this case is hemmed in on both sides, so that, if the murder is extenuated, the adultery is confirmed; if the adultery is cleared, the murder is heaped up; nor is any way out found at all, where it is said: "if she was an adulteress, why praised? if chaste, why killed?"

For us, however, in this so noble example of this woman, for refuting those who insult Christian women crushed in captivity, being strangers to every thought of holiness, it suffices what was said in her illustrious praises: *there were two, and one committed adultery*. For such a Lucretia was rather believed by them, one who could not stain herself by any adulterous consent. That she killed herself, then, not being an adulteress, because she had endured an adulterer, is not the love of chastity but the weakness of shame.

For she was ashamed of another's baseness committed upon her, even if not with her, and, a Roman woman too greedy for praise, she feared lest it be thought that what she had suffered by violence while living she had willingly suffered if she lived. Hence she thought she ought to apply that penalty before the eyes of men as a witness of her mind, to whom she could not demonstrate her conscience. For she blushed to be believed an associate in the deed if she bore patiently what another had basely done in her.

This Christian women did not do, who, having suffered similar things, nevertheless live and did not avenge another's crime upon themselves, lest they add their own to the crimes of others, if, because enemies in lusting had committed rapes on them, they should out of shame commit murders on themselves. For they have within the glory of chastity, the testimony of conscience; and they have it before the eyes of their God, and they seek nothing further, where they have nothing further to do rightly, lest they swerve from the authority of the divine law while they wrongly avoid the offense of human suspicion.

**20**. For it is not in vain that nowhere in the holy canonical books can it be found divinely commanded or permitted to us that we should inflict death upon ourselves, whether for the sake of attaining immortality itself or of avoiding or being rid of any evil. For we are to understand that we are forbidden this also where the law says: *you shall not kill*, especially since it did not add "your neighbor," as when forbidding false testimony it said: *you shall not bear false witness against your neighbor*[^64]. Nor yet, if anyone bears false witness against himself, should he therefore think himself free from this charge, since the one who loves received the rule of loving his neighbor from himself, since indeed it is written: *you shall love your neighbor as yourself*[^65].

Furthermore, if one who confesses falsely about himself is no less guilty of false testimony than if he did this against his neighbor—since in that commandment by which false testimony is forbidden, it is forbidden against one's neighbor, and it might seem to those not understanding rightly that it is not forbidden that anyone stand as a false witness against himself: how much more is it to be understood that it is not permitted for a man to kill himself, since in what is written, *you shall not kill*, nothing being added thereafter, no one—not even, surely, the very one to whom it is commanded—is understood to be excepted!

Hence some try to extend this commandment even to beasts and cattle, so that from this it would not be permitted to kill any of them either[^66]. Why then not also herbs and whatever is nourished and fixed by roots in the soil? For this kind of thing too, though it does not sense, is said to live, and through this it can also die, and accordingly, when force is applied, be killed. Hence the Apostle too, when he spoke of seeds of this kind, says: *what you sow is not brought to life unless it dies*[^67]; and in the psalm it is written: *he killed their vines with hail*[^68]. Are we, then, on this account, when we hear *you shall not kill*, to count it wrong to pull up a shrub, and most insanely acquiesce in the error of the Manichees?

These ravings, then, set aside, when we read *you shall not kill*, if we do not take this to have been said of shrubs, because there is no sensation in them, nor of irrational living things—flying, swimming, walking, creeping—because they are joined to us by no reason, which it has not been given them to have in common with us (whence by the most just ordering of the Creator both their life and their death are subjected to our uses): it remains that we understand of man what was said, *you shall not kill*—therefore neither another nor yourself. For one who kills himself kills nothing other than a man.

**21**. But certain exceptions that same divine authority itself has made, so that a man may not be killed.

But with these excepted whom God commands to be killed, whether by a law given or by a command expressed for the occasion to a particular person—(but he himself does not kill who owes service to the one commanding, as a sword is an aid to the one using it;

and therefore those in no way acted against this commandment by which it was said, *you shall not kill*, who with God as author waged wars, or, bearing the person of public authority, punished the wicked with death according to his laws, that is, by the rule of the most just reason;

and Abraham was not only not blamed for the crime of cruelty, but even praised for the name of piety, because he was willing to kill his son not at all wickedly, but obediently[^69]; and it is rightly asked whether it is to be held as done by God's command that Jephthah killed his daughter, who met her father, when he had vowed to sacrifice to God whatever first met him as he returned victorious from the battle[^70]; nor is Samson otherwise excused for crushing himself along with his enemies by the ruin of the house[^71], except that the Spirit, who through him worked miracles, had secretly commanded this.

With these excepted, then, whom either a just law generally or God himself, the very fount of justice, specially commands to be killed, whoever kills a man—whether himself or any other—is bound by the charge of murder.

**22**. And whoever have perpetrated this upon themselves are perhaps to be wondered at for greatness of soul, but not to be praised for soundness of wisdom. Although, if you consult reason more carefully, not even greatness of soul will rightly be the name for it, where anyone, not being strong enough to endure either hardships or the sins of others, has killed himself. For a mind is rather caught out as weak which cannot bear either the harsh servitude of its own body or the foolish opinion of the crowd; and that soul is deservedly to be called greater which can bear a wretched life rather than flee it, and which can despise, before the light and purity of conscience, the human judgment—and most of all the common judgment, which is for the most part wrapped in the fog of error.

Wherefore, if it is to be thought that it is done with greatness of soul when a man brings death upon himself, that Theombrotus[^72] is rather found to have this greatness of soul, who, they say, after reading Plato's book where he discussed the immortality of the soul, threw himself headlong from a wall and so departed from this life to that which he believed to be better. For no calamity or charge, true or false, pressed upon him such that, being unable to bear it, he should take himself off; but greatness of soul alone was present to seize death and to break the sweet bonds of this life. Yet that this was done greatly rather than well, Plato himself, whom he had read, could be a witness for him[^73], who surely would have done this above all and most of all, or even commanded it, had he not judged, by that mind with which he saw the immortality of the soul, that it was by no means to be done, but rather to be forbidden.

But many, it is said, killed themselves lest they fall into the hands of enemies. We do not now ask whether it was done, but whether it ought to have been done. For sound reason is to be set even before examples, with which indeed examples too accord—but those which are the worthier of imitation the more they excel in piety. The patriarchs did not do it, nor the prophets, nor the apostles, since the Lord Christ himself, when he admonished them, if they should suffer persecution, to flee from city to city[^74], could have admonished them to lay hands on themselves lest they fall into the hands of their persecutors. Further, if he did not command or advise this—that they should in this way depart from this life, for whom departing he promised that he would prepare eternal dwellings[^75]—whatever examples the nations that do not know God may set forth, it is manifest that this is not permitted to those who worship the one true God.

## Cato the Younger killed himself unwisely.

**23**. But nevertheless even they, besides Lucretia, about whom we have said enough above as it seemed, do not easily find anyone on whose authority they may take their stand, except that Cato who killed himself at Utica[^76]; not because he alone did it, but because he was held to be a learned and upright man, so that it may deservedly be thought that what he did could have been or could be done rightly.

What shall I say above all about his deed, except that his friends, even certain learned men, who more prudently dissuaded this from being done, judged it the deed of a weaker rather than a braver soul, by which was shown not an honorableness guarding against base things, but a weakness not sustaining adversities?

Cato himself judged this in his own dearest son. For if it was base to live under Caesar's victory, why was he the author of this baseness to his son, whom he charged to hope for everything from Caesar's kindness[^77]? Why did he not force him too to die with him? For if Torquatus killed even his victorious son who had fought against an enemy contrary to orders, and was praised for it[^78], why did the conquered Cato spare his conquered son, who did not spare himself? Was it baser to be a victor against orders than to be a victor against honor?

In no way, then, did Cato judge it base to live under the victor Caesar; otherwise he would have freed his son from this baseness by his paternal sword. What then, *except that, as much as he loved his son, for whom he both hoped and wished that he be spared by Caesar, so much did he envy Caesar the glory of sparing him too*, as Caesar himself is said to have said[^79]—or, to say something milder, "he blushed at it"?

**24**. But those against whom we are arguing do not wish us to prefer to Cato the holy man Job, who chose to suffer such horrible evils in his flesh rather than, by inflicting death on himself, to be rid of all torments, or other holy men from our writings, most lofty in supreme authority and most worthy of faith, who chose to bear the captivity and dominion of enemies rather than to inflict death on themselves; but from their own writings I will prefer to that Marcus Cato Marcus Regulus.

For Cato had never conquered Caesar, to whom, when conquered, he disdained to be subjected, and, lest he be subjected, chose to be killed by himself: but Regulus had already conquered the Carthaginians, and as a Roman commander had brought back to the Roman dominion a victory not to be mourned over fellow citizens but to be praised over enemies; yet, afterward conquered by them, he preferred to bear them by serving rather than to be rid of them by dying.

Accordingly he preserved both patience under the dominion of the Carthaginians and constancy in his love of the Romans, taking neither his conquered body away from the enemy nor his unconquered soul away from his fellow citizens. Nor was it from love of this life that he was unwilling to kill himself. This he proved when, for the sake of his promise and oath, he returned without any hesitation to those same enemies whom he had offended more grievously in the senate with words than in war with arms. So great a despiser of this life, then, since he chose, with the enemies raging, to end it through any penalties rather than to destroy himself, beyond doubt judged it a great crime for a man to destroy himself.

Among all their praiseworthy men, illustrious for the marks of virtue, the Romans bring forward none better, whom neither prosperity corrupted—for in so great a victory he remained most poor[^80]—nor misfortune broke, for he returned undaunted to such destruction.

Further, if the bravest and most illustrious men, defenders of an earthly fatherland and worshipers of gods—false indeed, yet not false but most truthful worshipers, even in their swearing—who could strike enemies conquered by the custom and law of war, these, when conquered by enemies, were unwilling to strike themselves, and, though they did not in the least dread death, nevertheless preferred to bear their conquering masters rather than to inflict it on themselves: how much more will Christians, worshiping the true God and longing for the heavenly fatherland, refrain from this deed, if the divine arrangement should for a time subject them, to be proved or amended, to enemies—those whom he who came so humbly for their sake, the Most High, does not desert in that humility—especially those whom the rights of no military power or such service bind to strike even a conquered enemy.

What error so evil, then, creeps in, that a man should kill himself, either because an enemy has sinned against him, or lest the enemy sin against him, when he would not dare to kill that very enemy who has sinned or is about to sin?

## The fear of sinning does not excuse one who kills himself, the exceptions being what God or the law have commanded.

**25**. But it is to be feared and guarded against, it is said, lest the body, subjected to lust, by a most enticing pleasure allure the soul to consent to sin. Accordingly, they say, not now on account of another's but on account of one's own sin, before anyone commits this, he ought to kill himself. In no way indeed will the soul do this—consent to the lust of its own flesh aroused by another's lust—which is subject rather to God and his wisdom than to the body and its desire. But nevertheless, if it is a detestable deed and a damnable crime to kill even oneself, as the manifest truth proclaims, who would be so foolish as to say: "let us now sin, lest afterward perhaps we sin; let us now perpetrate murder, lest afterward perhaps we fall into adultery"? Surely, if iniquity so dominates that not innocence but rather sins are chosen, is it not better to have an adultery uncertain about the future than a murder certain in the present? Is it not better to commit a shameful deed which may be healed by repentance than such a deed where no room for wholesome repentance is left?

I have said these things on account of those men or women who think that violence by which they may die ought to be inflicted on themselves, not for the sake of avoiding another's sin, but their own, lest, aroused under another's lust, they perhaps consent to their own. But far be it from the Christian mind, which trusts in its God and, with hope placed in him, relies on his help—far be it, I say, that such a mind should yield to any pleasures of the flesh to consent to baseness. But if that disobedience of desire, which still dwells in our dying members, is moved, contrary to the law of our will, as it were by its own law, how much more is it without fault in the body of one not consenting, if it is without fault in the body of one sleeping!

**26**. But certain holy women, it is said, in the time of persecution, in order to escape those who pursued their chastity, threw themselves into a river that would seize and kill them, and in that way died, and their martyrdoms are celebrated with most frequent veneration in the Catholic Church. About these I dare to judge nothing rashly. For whether the divine authority persuaded the Church by certain trustworthy testimonies[^81] to honor their memory thus, I do not know; and it may be that it is so. For what if they did this, not deceived in a human way but commanded by God, not erring but obeying? As about Samson it is not lawful for us to believe otherwise. But when God commands and intimates without any ambiguities that he commands, who would call obedience a crime? Who would accuse the compliance of piety?

But not on that account does whoever has resolved to sacrifice his son to God act without crime, because Abraham did this even with praise. For when a soldier too, obeying the authority under which he is lawfully placed, kills a man, by no law of his city is he guilty of murder; on the contrary, unless he did it, he is guilty of deserting and despising the command; but if he had done it of his own accord and on his own authority, he would have fallen into the crime of shed human blood. And so the thing for which he is punished if he did it uncommanded is the thing for which he will be punished unless he does it when commanded.

But if it is so when a commander commands, how much more when the Creator commands! Whoever, then, hears that he is not permitted to kill himself, let him do it if he has been commanded by one whose commands it is not permitted to despise; only let him see whether the divine command wavers with no uncertainty.

We reach the conscience through the ear; we do not usurp for ourselves the judgment of hidden things. *No one knows what is going on in a man except the spirit of the man, which is in him*[^82]. This we say, this we assert, this in every way we approve: that no one ought to inflict a voluntary death on himself, as if by fleeing temporal troubles, lest he fall into perpetual ones; no one on account of another's sins, lest by this very act he begin to have a most grievous one of his own, whom another's did not defile; no one on account of his own past sins, on account of which this life is the more needed, that they may be able to be healed by repentance; no one as if from a desire for the better life which is hoped for after death, because the better life after death does not receive those guilty of their own death.

**27**. There remains one cause, about which I had begun to speak, on account of which it is thought useful that anyone should kill himself, namely, lest he rush into sin either by flattering pleasure or by raging pain. If we are willing to admit this cause, it will go so far as to bring it about that men are to be urged rather to kill themselves then, when, washed by the laver of holy regeneration, they have received the remission of all their sins. For that is the time for guarding against all future sins, when all past ones have been blotted out. But if a voluntary death is rightly done, why is it not done then most of all? Why does each baptized person spare himself? Why does he again insert his freed head into so many perils of this life, when it is of the easiest power, by inflicting death on himself, to avoid them all—and it is written: *he who loves danger will fall into it*[^83]?

Why, then, are so many and so great perils loved—or, certainly, even if they are not loved, undertaken—when one remains in this life, from which it is permitted to depart? Or does so insipid a perversity overturn the heart and turn it away from the consideration of truth, that, if anyone ought to kill himself lest by the dominion of one captor he fall into sin, he should think he must live so as to endure the world itself, full through all its hours of temptations—both such as is feared under one master and innumerable others, without which this life is not led? What cause is there, then, why we should consume our time in those exhortations by which, addressing the baptized, we strive to kindle them either to virginal integrity or to the continence of widowhood or to the very fidelity of the marriage bed[^84], when we have better and, from all perils of sinning, more removed shortcuts—so that, whomever we could persuade, after the most recent remission of sins, to seize and inflict death on themselves, we might send them to the Lord sounder and purer?

Further, if whoever thinks this is to be attempted and urged is—I do not say foolish, but mad: with what face, after all, does he say to a man, "kill yourself, lest to your small sins you add a graver one while you live under a master of barbarous and shameless morals," who can only say most wickedly, "kill yourself, all your sins being absolved, lest you again commit such or even worse, while you live in a world enticing with so many impure pleasures, raging with so many unspeakable cruelties, hostile with so many errors and terrors"? Because it is wrong to say this, it is surely wrong to kill oneself.

For if any just cause could exist for doing this of one's own accord, beyond doubt there would be none more just than this. But since not even this is just, therefore there is none.

**28**. Let not your life, then, O faithful of Christ, be a weariness to you, if your chastity was made a mockery by enemies. You have a great and true consolation, if you retain a faithful conscience that you did not consent to the sins of those who were permitted to sin against you. But if perhaps you ask why they were permitted, the providence of the Creator and Ruler of the world is indeed deep, *and his judgments are unsearchable and his ways inscrutable*[^85];

but nevertheless question your souls faithfully, lest perhaps you have lifted yourselves up too inflatedly over this good of integrity and continence or chastity, and, delighted with human praises, have even envied others in this. I do not accuse what I do not know, nor do I hear what your hearts, questioned, answer you. Yet if they answer that it is so, do not wonder that you have lost that from which you were eager to please men, and that there has remained to you that which cannot be shown to men. If you did not consent to those sinning, divine help came to the divine grace, that it might not be lost; human reproach succeeded to human glory, that it might not be loved. Be consoled in both, faint-hearted ones—on the one side proved, on the other chastised; on the one side justified, on the other amended.

But those whose hearts, questioned, answer that they never prided themselves on the good of virginity or widowhood or marital chastity, but, consenting with the humble, exulted with trembling over the gift of God[^86], and did not envy anyone an equal excellence of holiness and chastity, but, setting human praise (which is wont to be paid the greater the rarer the good that demands praise) below, wished rather that their number should be larger than that they themselves should stand out more in fewness: let not these, who are such, even if barbarian lust crushed some of them too, complain that this was permitted, nor on that account believe that God neglects such things because he permits what no one commits with impunity. For certain weights, as it were, of evil desires are both relaxed by a present hidden divine judgment and reserved for a manifest final one.

But perhaps those who are well conscious to themselves that they did not lift up an inflated heart over this good of chastity, and yet suffered the enemy's violence in the flesh, had something of hidden weakness which could have been lifted up into the haughtiness of pride, had they escaped this humiliation in that devastation. As, then, some were snatched away by death, lest malice change their understanding[^87], so something was snatched from these by force, lest prosperity change their modesty.

For both, then—those who were already proud over their flesh, that it had suffered the foul contact of no one, or perhaps could have become proud had it not been touched by the violence of enemies—chastity was not taken away, but humility was persuaded; the swelling of the one was relieved as it stood within, the swelling of the other was met as it threatened.

Although that too is not to be left unsaid, that to some who suffered these things the good of continence could seem to be reckoned among bodily goods and to remain only if the body were touched by no one's lust; and not to be placed in the divinely aided strength of the will alone, so that both body and spirit may be holy; nor to be such a good as cannot be taken away against the will of the soul; which error was perhaps taken from them. For when they consider with what conscience they served God, and with unshaken faith do not think of him that he could in any way desert those serving him thus and calling upon him, and cannot doubt how much chastity pleases him, they see it follows that he would in no way have permitted these things to befall his holy ones, if in that way the holiness could perish which he conferred on them and loves in them.

**29**. And so the whole household of the supreme and true God has its own consolation, not a deceptive one nor placed in the hope of wavering or slipping things, and a temporal life itself in no way to be regretted, in which it is schooled for the eternal one, and uses earthly goods as a pilgrim and is not captivated by them, while by evils it is either proved or amended. But those who insult its uprightness and say to it, when perchance it has come into some temporal evils, *where is your God*?[^88]—let them themselves say where their gods are, when they suffer such things as those for whose avoidance they either worship them or contend that they are to be worshiped.

For it answers thus: my God is present everywhere, everywhere whole, enclosed nowhere, who can be present in secret, be absent without being moved; he, when he stirs me up with adverse things, either examines my merits or chastises my sins, and reserves for me an eternal reward for temporal evils piously endured; but who are you, with whom it is worthwhile even to speak about your gods, much less about my God, who *is terrible above all gods, since all the gods of the nations are demons, but the Lord made the heavens*[^89]?

## What evils follow the abundance of riches.

**30**. If that Scipio Nasica of yours, once your pontiff, whom, under the terror of the Punic War, when the best man was being sought to receive the Phrygian sacred rites, the whole senate chose[^90]—whose face perhaps you would not dare to look upon—were alive, he himself would restrain you from this shamelessness. For why, when afflicted by adverse things, do you complain about Christian times, except because you desire to have your luxury secure and, with all the harshness of troubles removed, to dissolve in the most ruined morals? For you do not desire to have peace and to abound in every kind of resources in order to use these goods honorably—that is, modestly, soberly, temperately, piously—but in order that an infinite variety of pleasures may be sought out in mad extravagances, and that from prosperity may arise those evils in morals which are worse than raging enemies.

But that Scipio, your supreme pontiff, that man whom by the judgment of the whole senate was judged best, fearing this calamity for you, did not wish Carthage, then the rival of the Roman empire, to be destroyed, and contradicted Cato when he was deciding that it should be destroyed[^91], fearing security for weak souls as an enemy, and seeing that terror was a necessary and suitable guardian for the citizens as if for wards.

Nor did his opinion deceive him: by the event itself it was proved how truly he spoke. For when Carthage was destroyed—a great terror of the Roman commonwealth being thus driven away and extinguished—so many evils arising from prosperous things immediately followed, that, with concord corrupted and broken, first by savage and bloody seditions, then soon by a chain of evil causes even by civil wars, such great slaughters were produced, so much blood was shed, monstrousness raged with such great desire for proscriptions and plunderings, that those Romans who, in a more upright life, feared evils from enemies, with the integrity of life lost, suffered crueler ones from fellow citizens; and that very lust for mastery, which among the other vices of the human race was present in a less diluted form in the whole Roman people, after it had conquered in a few more powerful men, oppressed the rest, worn down and wearied, even with the yoke of slavery.

**31**. For when would that lust rest in most proud minds, until by continuous honors it arrived at royal power? But the means of continuing honors would not exist unless ambition prevailed. And ambition would in no way prevail unless in a people corrupted by avarice and luxury.

But a greedy and luxurious people was produced by prosperous things, which that Nasica most providently judged were to be guarded against, when he was unwilling that the greatest, strongest, and most opulent city of the enemy be taken away, so that lust might be pressed down by fear, and lust pressed down might not run riot, and luxury checked, so that avarice too might not run rampant; and, with these vices barred out, virtue useful to the city might flourish and grow, and a liberty fitting to that virtue might remain to it.

Hence too it was, and from this most provident love of the fatherland it came, that this same supreme pontiff of yours, chosen (which must often be said) without any disagreement of opinions by the senate of that time as the best man, restrained the senate, when it was striving to construct an enclosure for a theater, from this plan and desire, and persuaded it by a most weighty speech not to allow Greek luxury to creep in upon the manly morals of the fatherland and not to consent to a foreign wickedness for the weakening and enervating of Roman virtue; and he prevailed so much by his authority that, the senatorial providence being moved by his words, it thereafter forbade even the benches, heaped up for the hour, which the city had already begun to use at the spectacle of the games, to be set in place[^92].

With how great zeal would this man have removed from the city of Rome the very stage-shows themselves, if he had dared to resist the authority of those whom he thought to be gods, whom he did not understand to be harmful demons—or, if he understood it, thought rather that they too were to be placated than despised! For the heavenly teaching had not yet been declared to the nations, which, cleansing the heart by faith, would change human affection by humble piety toward laying hold of heavenly or supercelestial things and free it from the dominion of proud demons.

**32**. But nevertheless know, you who are ignorant of these things, and take note, you who pretend not to know them; you who murmur against the liberator from such masters, attend: stage-shows, spectacles of baseness and the license of vanities, were instituted at Rome not by the vices of men but by the commands of your gods. More tolerably would you offer divine honors to that Scipio than worship gods of this kind. For those gods were not better than their own pontiff. Behold, attend, if a mind so long drunk with quaffed errors permits you to think anything sound! The gods commanded stage-shows to be exhibited to them for the quelling of a pestilence of bodies[^93]; but the pontiff, for the guarding against a pestilence of souls, forbade the very stage to be set up. If by any light of mind you set the soul before the body, choose whom you will worship!

For not even did that pestilence of bodies subside because the delicate madness of stage-shows crept in upon a warlike people, before accustomed only to the games of the circus; but the cunning of unspeakable spirits, foreseeing that the pestilence would now cease at its due end, took care, from this occasion, to send in another far graver one, in which it greatly rejoices—not upon bodies but upon morals—which so blinded the souls of the wretched with such great darkness, and so defiled them with such great deformity, that even now (which will perhaps be incredible if it is heard by our descendants), with the city of Rome laid waste, those whom this pestilence possessed and who, fleeing from there, were able to reach Carthage, raged in the theaters daily, vying in their madness for the actors.

**33**. O senseless minds! What is this so great—not error, but frenzy—that, while the eastern peoples bewailed your ruin, as we have heard, and the greatest cities in the most remote lands kept public mourning and grief, you should seek out theaters, enter them, fill them, and do things far more insane than they had been before? This blight and pestilence of souls, this overthrow of uprightness and honor, that Scipio feared for you when he forbade theaters to be constructed, when he discerned that you could easily be corrupted and overthrown by prosperous things, when he was unwilling that you be secure from the enemy's terror. For he did not judge that commonwealth happy whose walls stood while its morals fell. But in you what the impious demons seduced prevailed more than what provident men guarded against.

Hence it is that you do not wish the evils which you do to be imputed to you, but the evils which you suffer you impute to Christian times. For in your security you seek not a peaceful commonwealth but an unpunished luxury, you who, depraved by prosperous things, could not be corrected even by adverse ones. That Scipio wished you to be terrified by the enemy, lest you flow into luxury; but, not even crushed by the enemy, you did not repress your luxury, you lost the usefulness of calamity, and you became most wretched and remained most wicked.

**34**. And yet that you live is God's doing, who, by sparing you, admonishes that you be corrected by repenting; who granted even to you ungrateful ones that you might escape the hostile hands, whether under the name of his servants or in the places of his martyrs. Romulus and Remus are said to have established a sanctuary, where whoever fled should be free from all liability, seeking to increase the multitude of the city to be founded. A wondrous example preceded, in honor of Christ. The overthrowers of the city established what its founders had established before. But what is great if they did this so that the number of their citizens might be filled up, which these did so that the multitude of their enemies might be preserved?

## In each city the elect are mingled with the reprobate.

**35**. These things, and others if it can more abundantly and fittingly, let the redeemed household of the Lord Christ and the pilgrim city of King Christ answer to its enemies. Let it indeed remember that among those very enemies are hidden future citizens, lest it think it fruitless that, until it reaches those who confess, it bears those who are hostile; just as, from their number too, the city of God has joined to it, as long as it is a pilgrim in the world, by the communion of the sacraments, those who will not be with it in the eternal lot of the saints—who are partly in secret, partly in the open, who do not hesitate to murmur even with those very enemies against God, whose sacrament they bear, now filling the theaters with them, now the churches with us.

But concerning the correction of certain even of such people, there is much less to be despaired of, if among the most open adversaries predestined friends lie hidden, still unknown even to themselves.

For these two cities are entangled in this age and mutually mingled, until they are separated at the last judgment; concerning whose rise and course and appointed ends I will set forth what I think must be said, as far as I shall be divinely aided, for the sake of the glory of the city of God, which, with the contrary things compared by way of contrast, will stand out the more clearly.

## What must be said besides.

**36**. But there are still certain things I must say against those who lay the disasters of the Roman commonwealth to the charge of our religion, by which they are forbidden to sacrifice to their gods. For there must be recalled the evils, and how great, that could occur to mind, or will seem enough, which that city suffered, or the provinces belonging to its empire, before their sacrifices had been forbidden; all of which they would beyond doubt attribute to us, if our religion had then either been clear to them, or had thus forbidden them the sacrilegious rites.

Then it must be shown what morals of theirs, and for what cause, the true God, in whose power are all kingdoms, deigned to aid for the increase of their empire, and how those whom they think gods aided them in nothing, and rather how much they harmed them by deceiving and deluding.

Finally it will be said against those who, refuted and convicted by the most manifest proofs, try to assert that the gods are to be worshiped not for the sake of the usefulness of the present life, but for the sake of that which will come after death. This question, unless I am mistaken, will be much more laborious and worthy of a subtler discussion, so that in it the matter may be argued even against the philosophers—not just any, but those who among them are renowned with the most excellent glory and agree with us on many points, both about the immortality of the soul and that the true God founded the world and about his providence, by which he rules the universe that he founded.

But since they too must be refuted in those things in which they think against us, we ought not to fail in this office, so that, the impious contradictions having been refuted to the best of our strength, which God will impart, we may assert the city of God and true piety and the worship of God, in which alone everlasting blessedness is truly promised. Let this, then, be the limit of this volume, so that next we may take up the matters arranged from another beginning.
