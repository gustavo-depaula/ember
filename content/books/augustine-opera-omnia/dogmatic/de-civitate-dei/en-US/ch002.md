# Revisions, Book II

## Chapter 43 (70): The City of God, in twenty-two books

**1**. Meanwhile Rome was overthrown by an inrush of the Goths acting under their king Alaric and by the onslaught of a great disaster. The worshipers of false and many gods, whom by the customary name we call pagans, trying to lay its overthrow to the charge of the Christian religion, began to blaspheme the true God more bitterly and harshly than usual. And so I, *burning with zeal for the house of God*[^1], set out to write the books of *The City of God* against their blasphemies and errors. This work occupied me for several years, because many other things kept intervening that ought not to be put off and engaged me first for their settling. But this great work, *The City of God*, was at last completed in twenty-two books.

The first five of these refute those who want human affairs to prosper in such a way that they judge the worship of the many gods, which the pagans have been accustomed to render, to be necessary for this, and who contend that because it is forbidden, these evils arise and abound.

The next five speak against those who admit that these evils have never been wanting to mortals and never will be, and that they vary—now great, now small—according to places, times, and persons, but who argue that the worship of the many gods, by which sacrifice is offered to them, is useful for the life to come after death.

In these ten books, then, those two empty opinions opposed to the Christian religion are refuted.

**2**. But lest anyone reproach us for having only refuted the views of others without asserting our own, the other part of this work, which is contained in twelve books, does this—although, where it is needed, in the first ten we also assert what is ours, and in the last twelve refute what is opposed.

Of the twelve following books, then, the first four contain the rise of the two cities, of which one is God's and the other this world's; the second four their course or progress; and the third, which are also the last, their appointed ends.

So all twenty-two books, though they are written about both cities, have nevertheless taken their title from the better, so that they are called rather *The City of God*.

In the tenth of these books I ought not to have set down as a miracle, in Abraham's sacrifice, that a flame made from heaven ran among the divided victims[^2], since this was shown to him in a vision.

In the seventeenth book, what was said of Samuel, that *he was not of the sons of Aaron*[^3], ought rather to have been said: "he was not the son of a priest." For it was more the lawful custom that the sons of priests should succeed priests when they died. Now Samuel's father is found among the sons of Aaron, but he was not a priest, nor was he among the sons in such a way that Aaron himself begot him, but in the way that all of that people are called the sons of Israel.

This work begins thus: *The most glorious city of God*.

[^1]: Ps 69:9 (68:10); Jn 2:17.
[^2]: *De civ. Dei* 10.8.
[^3]: *De civ. Dei* 17.5.
