# Book XIX: What the end of goods and evils is

## [The ends of goods and evils of the cities]

**1.** **1.** Since I see that I must next discuss the due ends of each city—the earthly, namely, and the heavenly—the arguments of mortals must first be set forth, as much as the plan of finishing this work allows, by which they have striven to make blessedness for themselves in the unhappiness of this life, so that it may become clear how our hope, which God has given us, differs from their vain notions, and the very thing—that is, the true blessedness which he will give—not only by divine authority, but also by applying reason, such reason as we can apply for the sake of unbelievers.

For concerning the ends of goods and evils the philosophers have disputed among themselves much and in manifold ways; turning over this question with the greatest intensity, they have tried to find what makes a man blessed[^1]. For that is the end of our good for the sake of which other things are to be sought, but which is itself sought for its own sake; and that is the end of evil for the sake of which other things are to be avoided, but which is itself avoided for its own sake.

We now call it the "end" of good, then, not in the sense that it is consumed so as not to be, but that it is perfected so as to be complete;

and the "end" of evil, not in the sense that it ceases to be, but in the sense of how far it leads by harming. These "ends," accordingly, are the highest good and the highest evil.

About finding these, and about attaining the highest good and avoiding the highest evil in this life, those who professed the pursuit of wisdom in the vanity of this world labored much, as I have said; yet the boundary of nature did not permit them, though erring in diverse ways, to deviate so far from the path of truth that they did not, some in the mind, some in the body, some in both, place the ends of goods and evils.

From this threefold distribution, as it were, of the general sects, Marcus Varro, in his book *On Philosophy*, having diligently and subtly scrutinized so great a variety of teachings, noted that, by applying certain differences, he could most easily arrive at two hundred and eighty-eight sects—not such as already existed, but such as could exist.

## [Varro offers positions concerning the good]

**1.** **2.** To show this briefly, I must begin from what he himself noted and set down in the said book: that there are four things which men, without a teacher, without any support of doctrine, without that diligence or art of living which is called virtue and which is without doubt learned, seek as it were naturally—either pleasure, by which the bodily sense is delightfully moved, or rest, by which it comes about that one suffers no trouble of the body, or both, which Epicurus, however, calls by the one name of pleasure[^2], or universally the primary goods of nature, in which both these and other things are, whether in the body—such as the integrity, health, and soundness of the members—or in the mind, such as are those endowments, whether small or great, found in men's natures.

These four, then—that is: pleasure, rest, both, the primary goods of nature—are so in us that either virtue, which doctrine afterward implants, is to be sought for the sake of these, or these for the sake of virtue, or both for their own sake; and on this account there arise hence twelve sects; for by this reasoning each one is tripled;

and when I have demonstrated this in one, it will not be difficult to find it in the rest. When, therefore, bodily pleasure is either subordinated to, or preferred to, or joined with the virtue of the mind, it is varied by a threefold diversity of sects. It is subordinated to virtue when it is taken up for the use of virtue. For it pertains to the office of virtue both to live for one's country and to beget children for one's country's sake, neither of which can be done without bodily pleasure; for without it neither food and drink are taken, that one may live, nor is there intercourse, that generation may be propagated.

But when it is preferred to virtue, it is sought for its own sake, and virtue is believed to be taken up for its sake—that is, so that virtue does nothing except to attain or preserve bodily pleasure; which life is indeed deformed, since there virtue serves pleasure as its mistress (although this is by no means to be called virtue); but yet even this horrible baseness has had certain philosophers as its patrons and defenders[^3]. Pleasure, moreover, is joined with virtue when neither of them is sought for the sake of the other, but both are sought for their own sake.

Wherefore, just as pleasure, whether subordinated to, preferred to, or joined with virtue, makes three sects, so rest, so both, so the primary goods of nature are found to produce three each. For according to the variety of human opinions they are sometimes subordinated to virtue, sometimes preferred to it, sometimes joined with it, and thus one arrives at the number of twelve sects.

But this number too is doubled by applying one difference, namely that of the social life; since whoever follows any one of these twelve sects assuredly does so either for himself alone, or also for the sake of a companion, for whom he ought to will what he wills for himself. Wherefore there are twelve of those who think each one should be held for themselves alone, and another twelve of those who decide that one should philosophize in this way or that not only for themselves but also for others, whose good they seek as their own. But these twenty-four sects are again doubled by the addition of the difference drawn from the new Academics, and become forty-eight.

For each one of those twenty-four sects a man can so hold and defend as certain, as the Stoics defended that the good of man, by which he was blessed, consisted only in the virtue of the mind; another can hold it as uncertain, as the new Academics defended what seemed to them, although not certain, yet verisimilar.

Twenty-four, then, come about through those who hold them as certain on account of truth, and another twenty-four through those who think the same should be followed, although uncertain, on account of verisimilitude. Again, because each one of these forty-eight sects a man can follow in the dress of the rest of the philosophers, and likewise another can follow it in the dress of the Cynics, from this difference too they are doubled and become ninety-six.

Then, because men can so maintain and follow each single one of them, that they either love a life of leisure, like those who wished and were able to devote themselves only to the pursuits of learning, or a life of business, like those who, although they philosophized, were nevertheless most occupied with the administration of the commonwealth and the governing of human affairs, or a life tempered from both kinds, like those who assigned the times of their life partly to learned leisure and partly to necessary business—on account of these differences this number of sects too can be tripled and brought to two hundred and eighty-eight.

## What the Academics and the Cynics thought about this matter.

**1.** **3.** These things from Varro's book I have set down as briefly and clearly as I could, explaining his views in my own words. But how, refuting the rest, he chooses one which he wishes to be that of the old Academics (whom, instituted by Plato down to Polemo, who held his school—called the *Academy*—as the fourth from him, he wishes to be seen to have had certain teachings, and on this account distinguishes them from the new Academics, to whom all things are uncertain, which kind of philosophy began with Arcesilaus, successor of Polemo[^4]), and how he judges that sect—that is, of the old Academics—to be free, as from doubt, so from all error, it is long to demonstrate throughout; yet the matter is not to be omitted in every part.

He removes, then, first all those differences which multiplied the number of the sects, which he thinks should be removed because the end of good is not in them. For he does not think that any sect of philosophy is to be called such which does not differ from the rest in this, that it has different ends of goods and evils.

Since indeed there is no cause for a man to philosophize except that he may be blessed; but what makes him blessed is itself the end of good; there is therefore no cause for philosophizing except the end of good: wherefore that which follows no end of good is to be called no sect of philosophy.

When, therefore, it is asked about the social life, whether it is to be held by the wise man—that is, whether he should so will and care for the highest good, by which a man becomes blessed, for his friend as for himself, or do whatever he does for the sake of his own blessedness alone—the question is not about the highest good itself, but about taking or not taking a companion to share in this good, not for his own sake, but for the sake of that same companion, so that he may rejoice in his good as he rejoices in his own.

Likewise, when it is asked about the new Academics, to whom all things are uncertain, whether the things about which one must philosophize are to be held thus, or whether, as it pleased other philosophers, we ought to hold them as certain—it is not asked what is to be followed in the end of good, but about the truth of the good itself, which seems to be followed, whether it is to be doubted or not;

that is, to say it more plainly, whether it is to be followed in such a way that he who follows it says it is true, or in such a way that he who follows it says it seems true to him—even if perhaps it is false—yet each follows one and the same good.

In that difference too which is applied from the dress and custom of the Cynics, it is not asked what the end of good is, but whether in that dress and custom he ought to live who follows the true good, whatever may seem to him to be true and to be followed. Finally, there were those who, although they followed different final goods—some virtue, others pleasure—yet held the same dress and custom, from which they were called Cynics.

Thus that, whatever it is, by which the Cynic philosophers are distinguished from the rest, availed nothing at all for choosing and holding the good by which they might become blessed. For if it made any difference to this, surely the same dress would compel them to follow the same end, and a different dress would not allow them to follow the same end.

**2.** In those three kinds of life too—the one, namely, leisured not in sloth but in the contemplation or inquiry of truth; the second, busied in the conducting of human affairs; the third, tempered from both kinds—when it is asked which of these is rather to be chosen, the end of good has no controversy; but which of these three brings difficulty or ease to attaining or retaining the end of good, that is what is at issue in this question. For the end of good, when one has reached it, immediately makes him blessed; but in lettered leisure, or in public business, or when both are done by turns, one is not forthwith blessed. For many can live in any of these three and err in seeking the end of good, by which a man becomes blessed.

There is, then, one question about the ends of goods and evils, which makes each sect of the philosophers, and other questions about the social life, about the hesitation of the Academics, about the clothing and food of the Cynics, about the three kinds of life—the leisured, the active, and that moderated from both; of which there is none in which there is dispute about the ends of goods and evils.

Accordingly, since Marcus Varro, applying these four differences—that is, from the social life, from the new Academics, from the Cynics, from that threefold kind of life—arrived at two hundred and eighty-eight sects, and any others that can similarly be added; having removed all these, because they introduce no question about following the highest good and therefore are not, and are not to be called, sects, he returns to those twelve in which it is asked what is the good of man, on attaining which he becomes blessed, in order to show that one of them is true, the rest false.

For when that threefold kind of life is removed, two parts of this number are subtracted and ninety-six sects remain. But when the difference added from the Cynics is removed, they are reduced to half and become forty-eight. Let us take away also what was applied from the new Academics; again the half remains, that is, twenty-four. Let what had accrued from the social life likewise be taken away: twelve remain, which that difference had doubled so that they became twenty-four.

About these twelve, then, nothing can be said as to why they should not be held sects. For nothing else is sought in them than the end of goods and evils. But when the ends of goods are found, the ends of evils are surely the contrary. Now, for these to become twelve sects, those four are tripled: pleasure, rest, both, and the primary goods of nature, which Varro calls the "primal" goods.

For these four, while they are individually sometimes subordinated to virtue, so that they seem to be sought not for their own sake but for the office of virtue; sometimes preferred to it, so that virtue is thought necessary not for its own sake but for attaining or preserving these; sometimes joined with it, so that both virtue and these are believed to be sought for their own sake—make the number four triple and arrive at twelve sects.

But of those four things Varro removes three—namely pleasure and rest and both—not because he disapproves of them, but because those primal goods of nature have in themselves both pleasure and rest. What need is there, then, to make of these two three things, that is, two when pleasure or rest is sought individually, and a third when both together, since indeed the primary goods of nature contain both these and, besides these, many other things?

About three sects, then, it pleases him that diligent treatment be given, as to which should rather be chosen. For true reason does not permit there to be more than one true sect, whether it be among these three or somewhere else, which we shall see afterward. Meanwhile, let us discuss, as briefly and plainly as we can, how Varro chooses one of these three. These three sects, of course, come about thus, when either the primary goods of nature are to be sought for the sake of virtue, or virtue for the sake of the primary goods of nature, or both—that is, both virtue and the primary goods of nature—for their own sake.

**3.** **1.** Which, then, of these three is true and to be followed, he tries to persuade us in this way. First, because the highest good in philosophy is sought not of a tree, not of a beast, not of God, but of man, he thinks it must be asked what man himself is. For he perceives that in his nature there are two things, body and soul, and of these two he in no way doubts that the soul is the better and far the more excellent;

but whether the soul alone is man, so that the body is to it as a horse is to a horseman (for the horseman is not the man and the horse, but the man alone; yet he is called a horseman because he stands in some relation to the horse), or whether the body alone is man, standing in some relation to the soul, as a cup to the drink (for the goblet and the drink which the goblet contains are not together called a cup, but the goblet alone; yet for this reason, that it is suited to containing the drink), or whether indeed neither the soul alone nor the body alone, but both together are man, of which one part is either the soul or the body, but the whole man consists of both, so that he is man (just as we call two yoked horses a chariot-pair, of which either the right or the left is a part of the pair, but one of them, however it stands in relation to the other, we do not call a chariot-pair, but both together).

Of these three he chose this third, and judges that man is neither the soul alone nor the body alone, but soul and body together. Accordingly, he says that the highest good of man, by which he becomes blessed, consists of the goods of both things, that is, of the soul and of the body. And on this account he thinks that those primary goods of nature are to be sought for their own sake, and virtue itself, which doctrine implants as it were the art of living, which is the most excellent good among the goods of the soul.

Wherefore the same virtue—that is, the art of conducting life—when it has received the primary goods of nature, which were there without it, but yet were there even when doctrine was still lacking to them, seeks all things for their own sake and at the same time itself too, and uses all things together with itself, to this end, that it may delight in and fully enjoy all things, more and less, as each is greater or lesser among themselves, yet rejoicing in all, and despising certain lesser things, if necessity demands it, for the sake of attaining or holding the greater.

But of all goods, whether of the mind or of the body, virtue sets nothing at all before itself. For it uses well both itself and the other goods which make a man blessed. But where it is not present, however many goods there may be, they are not for the good of him whose they are, and on this account they are not even to be called "goods" of him to whom, since he uses them ill, they cannot be useful.

This life of man, then, which enjoys virtue and the other goods of mind and body, without which virtue cannot exist, is said to be blessed; but if it enjoys also other goods, without which virtue can exist, whether any or many, it is more blessed; and if absolutely all, so that no good at all of mind or body is lacking, it is most blessed. For life is not the same as virtue, since not every life, but the wise life, is virtue; and yet life of whatever kind can exist without any virtue; but virtue cannot exist without any life.

This I would say also of memory and reason, and of any other such thing there is in man. For these exist even before doctrine, but without them there can be no doctrine, and on this account no virtue either, which is certainly learned. But to run well, to be beautiful in body, to prevail with enormous strength, and other things of this kind are such that both virtue can exist without them and they without virtue; yet they are goods, and, according to these philosophers, virtue loves even them for their own sake, and uses and enjoys them, as befits virtue[^5].

**3.** **2.** This blessed life they also declare to be social, which loves the goods of friends for their own sake as its own and wills for them, for their own sake, what it wills for itself;

whether they are in the household, such as spouse and children and all the members of the household, or in the place where his house is, such as a city, as are those who are called citizens, or in the whole world, as are the nations whom human society joins to him, or in the universe itself, which is reckoned by the name of heaven and earth, as they say the gods are, whom they wish to be friends to the wise man, whom we more familiarly call angels.

But about the ends of goods, and on the contrary of evils, they deny that there is any way to doubt, and they affirm that this is the distance between themselves and the new Academics; nor does it matter to them at all whether anyone philosophizes in the dress and food of the Cynics or of any other kind, within these ends which they think true.

Moreover, of those three kinds of life—the leisured, the active, and that which is composed of both—they assert that this third pleases them. That the old Academics thought and taught these things, Varro asserts on the authority of Antiochus, the teacher of Cicero and of himself, whom indeed Cicero wishes to be seen, in several respects, to have been more a Stoic than an old Academic. But what is that to us, who ought rather to judge of the matters themselves than to think it a great thing to know what each man thought about men?

## The end of goods is in God through faith.

**4.** **1.** If, then, it should be asked of us what the city of God, questioned about these several matters, answers, and first what it thinks about the ends of goods and evils: it will answer that eternal life is the highest good, and eternal death the highest evil; and that therefore, in order to attain the one and to avoid the other, we must live rightly. On account of which it is written: *the just man lives by faith*[^6]; since we do not yet see our good, for which reason we must seek it by believing, nor is the very living rightly in our power from ourselves, unless he who gave also the very faith—by which we believe that we are to be helped by him—helps us as we believe and pray.

## [Blessedness, the end of goods, is not in this life]

But those who thought that the ends of goods and evils are in this life, placing the highest good whether in the body or in the mind or in both, and—to say it more explicitly—whether in pleasure or in virtue or in both, whether in rest or in virtue or in both, whether in pleasure and rest together or in virtue or in both, whether in the primary goods of nature or in virtue or in both, wished, by a wondrous vanity, to be blessed here and to be made blessed by themselves. The Truth derided these through the Prophet saying: *the Lord knows the thoughts of men*[^7], or, as the apostle Paul set down this testimony: *the Lord knows the thoughts of the wise, that they are vain*[^8].

**4.** **2.** For who is able, with however great a flood of eloquence, to unfold the miseries of this life? Cicero lamented it in his *Consolation* on the death of his daughter, as well as he could; but how much is what he could do? For those things which are called the primary goods of nature, when, where, how can they be so well off in this life that they do not toss about under uncertain chances? For what pain contrary to pleasure, what disquiet contrary to rest, cannot fall upon the body of the wise man? The amputation or disabling of the members surely assails a man's soundness, deformity his beauty, weakness his health, weariness his strength, torpor or slowness his mobility; and which of these is there that cannot rush upon the flesh of the wise man? The postures too and movements of the body, when they are seemly and fitting, are numbered among the primary goods of nature; but what if some bad health shakes the members with trembling? what if the spine of the back is so bent that the hands are placed on the ground and the man is made in a manner four-footed? Will it not pervert every form and grace of standing and moving the body?

What of the goods of the mind itself which are called primal, where they place two first—sense and intellect—for the comprehension and perception of truth? But of what kind and how great does sense remain if, to be silent about other things, a man becomes deaf and blind? Whither will reason and intelligence withdraw, where will they be lulled to sleep, if by some disease he is made insane? When the frenzied say or do many absurd things, for the most part alien to their good purpose and character—indeed contrary to their good purpose and character—whether we reflect on these things or see them, if we consider them worthily, we can scarcely hold back our tears, or perhaps cannot.

What shall I say of those who suffer the assaults of demons? Where do they have their intelligence hidden or buried, when the malignant spirit uses both their soul and their body according to his own will? And who is confident that this evil cannot befall the wise man in this life? Then, what or how great is the perception of truth in this flesh, when, as we read in the truthful book of *Wisdom*: *the corruptible body weighs down the soul, and the earthly dwelling presses down the mind that ponders many things*[^9]? Moreover, the impulse or appetite for action—if this is rightly so called in Latin, what the Greeks call *horme*, since they too reckon it among the primary goods of nature—is it not the very thing by which even those pitiable movements and deeds of the insane are carried out, which we shudder at, when sense is perverted and reason lulled to sleep?

## What things are opposed to temperance in relation to the end.

**4.** **3.** Moreover, virtue itself, which is not among the primary goods of nature—since it comes upon them afterward, doctrine introducing it—when it claims for itself the summit of human goods, what does it do here but wage perpetual wars with vices, and not exterior but interior ones, not others' but plainly our own and proper ones, especially that virtue which in Greek is called *sophrosyne*, in Latin "temperance," by which carnal lusts are bridled, lest they drag the consenting mind into any disgraceful deeds[^10]?

For it is not the case that there is no vice, when, as the Apostle says: *the flesh lusts against the spirit*; to which vice virtue is contrary, when, as the same one says: *the spirit lusts against the flesh. For these*, he says, *are opposed to each other, so that you do not do the things that you wish*[^11].

But what do we wish to do, when we wish to be perfected by the end of the highest good, except that the flesh should not lust against the spirit, and that there should not be in us this vice against which the spirit lusts? Since in this life, although we wish it, we are not able to do this, we do at least this with the help of God: that we do not, by the spirit succumbing to the flesh lusting against the spirit, fall and be dragged by our own consent to the perpetrating of sin. Far be it, then, that, as long as we are in this internal war, we should believe that we have already attained that blessedness to which, by conquering, we wish to come. And who is so wise as to have no conflict at all against the lusts?

**4.** **4.** What of that virtue which is called prudence—does it not with all its vigilance distinguish goods from evils, so that no error may creep in, in seeking the former and avoiding the latter, and on this account does it not itself too testify that we are among evils, or that evils are in us? For it teaches that it is evil to consent to sinning, and good not to consent to lust unto sinning. Yet that evil, to which prudence teaches us not to consent, temperance restrains; neither prudence nor temperance removes it from this life.

What of justice, whose office is to render to each his own (whence there arises in man himself a certain just order of nature, that the soul be subject to God, and the flesh to the soul, and on this account both soul and flesh to God)—does it not show that it still labors in this work rather than already rests in the end of this work? For the soul is so much the less subject to God as it conceives God less in its very thoughts; and the flesh is so much the less subject to the soul as it lusts the more against the spirit. As long, therefore, as this infirmity, this plague, this languor is in us, how shall we dare to call ourselves already saved, and, if not yet saved, how blessed with that final blessedness?

## [Fortitude and suicide]

But now that virtue whose name is fortitude, in however great wisdom it may be, is a most evident witness of human evils, which it is compelled to endure with patience. These evils I marvel with what face the Stoic philosophers contend are not evils, when they confess that, if they are so great that the wise man either cannot or ought not to endure them, he is compelled to inflict death upon himself and to depart from this life.

But so great is the stupor of pride in these men, who think that the end of good is here and that they are made blessed by themselves, that their wise man—that is, such as they describe with wondrous vanity—even if he is blinded, deafened, struck dumb, disabled in his members, tortured with pains, and falls into any other such evil that can be told or thought, whereby he is compelled to inflict death upon himself, is not ashamed to call this life, set among these evils, blessed. O blessed life, which seeks the help of death that it may be ended! If it is blessed, let one remain in it.

How are those things not evils which overcome the good of fortitude and compel that same fortitude not only to yield to them but even to rave, so that it both calls the same life blessed and persuades us that it is to be fled? Who is so blind as not to see that, if it were blessed, it would not be to be fled? But by the open voice of infirmity they confess that it is to be fled.

What reason is there, then, why they do not, with the neck of pride broken, confess it to be also wretched? Was it, I beseech you, by patience or rather by impatience that the famous Cato killed himself? For he would not have done this had he not borne impatiently the victory of Caesar. Where is fortitude? Surely it yielded, surely it succumbed, surely it was so far overcome that it abandoned, deserted, fled the "blessed life." Or was it now no longer blessed? Then it was wretched. How, then, were those things not evils which made life wretched and to be fled?

**4.** **5.** Wherefore even those very men who have confessed these to be evils—such as the Peripatetics, such as the old Academics, whose sect Varro defends—speak indeed more tolerably; but their error too is strange, in that they contend that life is nevertheless blessed amid these evils, even if they are so grave that, by death inflicted on himself, they must be fled by the one who suffers them. *The torments and tortures of the body are evils*, he says, *and they are so much the worse the greater they can be; that you may be rid of them, you must flee from this life*. From what life, I beseech you? *From this*, he says, *which is weighed down with such great evils*[^12]. So then, is it blessed amid those very evils on account of which you say it must be fled? Or do you call it blessed because you are permitted to depart from these evils by death?

What, then, if by some divine judgment you were held among them and were not permitted to die nor ever allowed to be without them? Surely then at least you would call such a life wretched. It is not, therefore, not wretched because it is quickly left. Since indeed, if it were everlasting, it is judged wretched even by you yourself; not, therefore, because it is brief should it seem no misery, or—what is more absurd—because it is a brief misery, should it on that account even be called blessedness.

Great is the force in those evils which compel a man, even a wise one according to these philosophers, to take away from himself that which he is, a man; since they say—and say truly—that this is in a manner the first and greatest voice of nature, that a man should be reconciled to himself and therefore naturally flee death, being so much his own friend that he vehemently wishes and seeks to be a living being and to live in this conjunction of body and soul[^13].

Great is the force in those evils by which that sense of nature is overcome, by which death is avoided in every way with all strength and effort, and is so overcome that what was being avoided is chosen and sought, and, if it cannot come about from elsewhere, is inflicted by the man himself upon himself.

Great is the force in those evils which make fortitude a murderer; if, that is, that is still to be called fortitude which is so overcome by these evils that it not only cannot, by patience, guard the man whom, as a virtue, it undertook to govern and protect, but is itself moreover compelled to slay him. The wise man ought indeed to bear death too patiently, but death that befalls from elsewhere. But according to these philosophers, if he is compelled to inflict it upon himself, surely it must be confessed by them that the things which compel him to perpetrate this are not only evils, but even intolerable evils.

The life, therefore, which is either pressed by the burdens or subjected to the chances of these so great and so grave evils,

would by no means be called blessed, if the men who say this would, just as, overcome by worsening evils, when they bring death upon themselves they yield to unhappiness, so, overcome by sure reasonings, when they seek the blessed life, deign to yield to the truth, and not think that they should rejoice, in this mortality, in the end of the highest good—where the very virtues, than which nothing better or more useful is found here in man, the greater the helps they are against the force of dangers, labors, and pains, are the more faithful witnesses of miseries.

For if they are true virtues—which cannot exist except in those in whom there is true piety—they do not profess that they can bring it about that the men in whom they are suffer no miseries (for true virtues are not lying, that they should profess this), but that human life, which is compelled to be wretched by so many and so great evils of this world, may be blessed by the hope of the world to come, just as it is also saved. For how is that blessed which is not yet saved? Whence also the apostle Paul, not of imprudent, impatient, intemperate, and unjust men, but of those who lived according to true piety and therefore had the virtues they had as true, says: *for in hope we have been saved. But hope that is seen is not hope. For what a man sees, why does he also hope for? But if we hope for what we do not see, we wait for it with patience*[^14].

As, therefore, we have been made saved in hope, so in hope we have been made blessed; and as we do not now hold salvation present but await it future, so too blessedness, and this *through patience*; because we are among evils which we must tolerate patiently, until we come to those goods where there will be all things in which we may ineffably delight, but nothing that we shall any longer have to tolerate.

Such salvation, which will be in the world to come, will itself be also the final blessedness. Which blessedness these philosophers, because, not seeing it, they refuse to believe it, try to fabricate for themselves here as a most false one, with a virtue the more lying the prouder it is.

## Certain things conflict within the household.

**5.** But that they wish the life of the wise man to be social, we approve much more fully. For whence would this city of God, of which, behold, we now have in hand the nineteenth book of this work, either be begun in its rise, or proceed in its course, or attain its due ends, if the life of the saints were not social? But in the hardship of this mortality, with how many and how great evils human society abounds, who is able to enumerate? who is sufficient to estimate?

Let them hear in their own comic writers a man saying, with the sense and consent of all men: *I took a wife; what misery I saw there! Children were born—another care*[^15]. What likewise of those vices in love which the same Terence recalls: *injuries, suspicions, enmities, war, and then peace again*[^16]; have they not filled human affairs everywhere? do they not for the most part befall even in the honorable loves of friends? are not human affairs everywhere full of these, where we feel injuries, suspicions, enmities, war to be sure evils; but peace an uncertain good, since we do not know the hearts of those with whom we wish to keep it, and even if we could know them today, what they would be tomorrow we should certainly not know?

Who, moreover, are wont, or ought, to be more friendly among themselves than those who are even contained within one household? And yet who is secure on that account, since such great evils have often arisen from their hidden plots—so much the more bitter as the peace was the sweeter, which was thought true, when it was most cunningly feigned? On account of which that saying of Tully so touches the breasts of all that it compels them to a groan: *there are no more hidden plots than those which lie concealed in the pretense of duty or in some name of close relationship. For one who is openly an adversary you can easily avoid by being on guard; but this hidden, internal, and domestic evil not only arises, but even overwhelms you, before you can foresee and examine it*[^17].

On account of which that divine voice too: *and a man's enemies are those of his own household*[^18] is heard with great sorrow of heart; because, even if anyone is so brave as to bear it with an even mind, or so vigilant as, by provident counsel, to guard against what a feigned friendship contrives against him, yet by the evil of those perfidious men, when he finds them to be utterly wicked—if he himself is good—he must of necessity be grievously tormented, whether they were always evil and feigned themselves good, or were changed into this malice from goodness.

If, then, the household, the common refuge in these evils of the human race, is not safe, what of the city, which, the greater it is, the more its forum is full of lawsuits both civil and criminal, even if there be at rest not only turbulent but more often even bloody seditions and civil wars, from whose events cities are sometimes free, but from whose dangers never?

**6.** What of those very judgments of men about men, which cannot be lacking to cities, however great the peace in which they abide—of what kind do we think they are, how wretched, how lamentable? Since indeed those judge who cannot see into the consciences of those whom they judge. Whence they are often compelled, by the tortures of innocent witnesses pertaining to another's cause, to seek the truth.

What of when, in his own cause, a man is tortured, and, when it is asked whether he is guilty, is racked, and an innocent man pays most certain penalties for an uncertain crime—not because it is detected that he committed it, but because it is not known that he did not commit it? And on this account the ignorance of the judge is for the most part the calamity of the innocent.

And what is more intolerable and more to be bewailed and to be watered, if it could be, with fountains of tears—when the judge tortures the accused for this reason, lest he unknowingly kill an innocent man, it comes about, through the misery of ignorance, that he kills both tortured and innocent the one whom he had tortured lest he kill him innocent.

For if, according to the wisdom of these philosophers, he chooses to flee from this life rather than to endure those torments any longer, he says that he committed what he did not commit.

And when he has been condemned and killed, the judge still does not know whether he has killed a guilty or an innocent man—the one whom he tortured lest he unknowingly kill him innocent; and on this account he both tortured the innocent man that he might know, and, while he did not know, killed him.

In these darknesses of social life, will that wise judge sit, or will he not dare? He will plainly sit. For human society, which he reckons it wickedness to desert, binds him and drags him to this office. For he does not reckon it wickedness that innocent witnesses are tortured in others' cases; that those who are accused, for the most part overcome by the force of pain and confessing falsely about themselves, are even punished as innocents, when they have already been tortured as innocents;

that, even if they are not punished with death, they for the most part die amid or from the very torments; that sometimes even the accusers themselves, desiring perhaps to be of profit to human society, lest crimes go unpunished, and, with the witnesses lying and the defendant himself holding out monstrously against the torments and not confessing, being unable to prove what they charge, although they charged true things, are condemned by an unknowing judge.

These so many and so great evils he does not reckon to be sins; for the wise judge does not do these things by a will to harm, but by the necessity of not knowing, and yet, because human society compels him, by the necessity also of judging.

This, then, is what we call the misery, at least, of man, even if not the malice of the wise man. Or does he, by the necessity of not knowing and of judging, torture the innocent, punish the innocent, and is it too little for him that he is not guilty, if he is not moreover blessed as well?

How much more considerately, and in a manner more worthy of a man, does he recognize the misery in this necessity, and hate it in himself, and, if he is piously wise, cry out to God: *deliver me from my necessities!*[^19]

## The world is wretched through the diversity of tongues and through wars.

**7.** After the city or town there follows the world, in which they place the third grade of human society, beginning from the household and thence advancing to the city, then coming to the world; which, of course, like a gathering of waters, the greater it is, the fuller of dangers. In it, first, the diversity of tongues estranges man from man. For if two men meet each other and are compelled by some necessity not to pass by but to be together, neither of whom knows the other's language: more easily are mute animals, even of different kinds, associated together than they, though both are men. For when they cannot communicate among themselves the things they perceive, on account of the mere diversity of tongue so great a likeness of nature avails nothing for associating men, so that a man is more gladly with his own dog than with a foreign man. But, it is said, care has been taken that the imperious city should impose on subdued nations not only its yoke but also its tongue, through the peace of society, by which there should not be lacking—indeed there should even abound—a supply of interpreters[^20]. True; but by how many and how great wars, by what slaughter of men, by what shedding of human blood was this procured?

When these are over, the misery of those same evils is nevertheless not ended. For although there have not been, and are not, lacking enemies among foreign nations, against whom wars have always been and are waged; yet even the very breadth of empire has begotten wars of a worse kind—social, namely, and civil—by which the human race is shaken more pitiably, whether when war is being waged that they may sometime be at rest, or when it is feared lest they rise up again. The many and manifold disasters of these evils, the hard and dire necessities, if I should wish to set forth as is fitting—though I could by no means do so as the matter demands—what limit would there be to a lengthy discussion?

But, they say, the wise man will wage just wars. As if he would not, if he remembers that he is a man, much rather grieve that the necessity of just wars has arisen for him, since, unless they were just, he would not have to wage them, and on this account there would be no wars for the wise man. For it is the iniquity of the opposing party that imposes on the wise man just wars to be waged; which iniquity surely a man ought to grieve over, because it is the iniquity of men, even if no necessity of warring were to arise from it.

Whoever, then, considers with grief these evils so great, so horrible, so savage, let him confess that this is misery; but whoever either suffers them or thinks of them without grief of mind, surely thinks himself blessed all the more wretchedly, because he has also lost human feeling.

**8.** But even if there does not befall a certain ignorance like madness—which, however, in the wretched condition of this life often befalls—whereby one is believed a friend who is an enemy, or an enemy who is a friend; what consoles us in this human society, most full of errors and hardships, but the unfeigned faith and mutual love of true and good friends? The more of these we have, and in the more places, the more widely and far we fear lest some evil befall them from the so great heaps of evils of this world. For we are anxious not only lest they be afflicted by famine, by wars, by diseases, by captivities, lest in that same servitude they suffer such things as we are not able even to conceive; but also—where the fear is much more bitter—lest they be changed into perfidy, malice, and wickedness. And when these things befall (the more, of course, the more of them there are) and are brought to our notice, by what scourges our heart is burned, who can perceive but he who feels such things? For we would rather hear them dead, although even this we cannot hear without grief.

For those whose life delighted us by the solaces of friendship—how can it come about that their death brings us no sadness? Whoever forbids this, let him forbid, if he can, friendly conversations, let him interdict friendly affection or cut it off, let him burst the bonds of all human relationships by a hard insensibility of mind, or judge that they are to be so used that no sweetness from them suffuses the soul. But if this can in no way be done, how shall it come about that the death of one is not bitter to us, whose life is sweet? For hence too is grief, like a wound or sore of a heart not inhuman, to whose healing the dutiful consolations are applied. For it is not that there is nothing to be healed because the better the soul is, the more quickly and easily it is healed in it.

Since, therefore, the life of mortals is afflicted, now more gently, now more harshly, even by the deaths of those most dear—especially of those whose offices are necessary to human society—yet we would rather hear or see those whom we love dead than fallen from faith or from good morals, that is, dead in the very soul.

With this vast material of evils the earth is full, on account of which it is written: *is not the life of man upon earth a temptation?*[^21] and on account of which the Lord himself says: *woe to the world because of scandals*[^22]; and again: *because iniquity has abounded*, he says, *the love of many shall grow cold*[^23]. Whence it comes about that we congratulate good friends when they are dead and, although their death saddens us, the very death more surely consoles us, since they have been spared the evils by which, in this life, even good men are either ground down or depraved or endangered in both.

## The demons too trouble us.

**9.** But in the society of the holy angels, which those philosophers who wished gods to be our friends placed in the fourth grade, as it were coming to the universe from the world, so as in a manner to embrace heaven too, we by no means fear lest such friends sadden us either by their death or by their depravation.

But because they do not mingle with us by that familiarity with which men do (which itself too belongs to the hardships of this life), and because sometimes Satan, as we read, transfigures himself as an angel of light[^24] to tempt those whom it is either necessary should be instructed or just should be deceived; great is the mercy of God needed, lest anyone, when he thinks he has good angels as friends, have evil demons as feigned friends, and suffer them, so much the more harmful as they are the craftier and more deceitful, as enemies.

And for whom is this great mercy of God necessary but for great human misery, which is so pressed by ignorance that it is easily deceived by their pretense? And it is most certain that those philosophers in the impious city, who said that gods were friends to them, fell in among the malignant demons, to whom that whole city is subject, destined to have eternal punishment with them.

For from their sacred rites, or rather sacrileges, by which they thought they should be worshiped, and from their most unclean games, where their crimes are celebrated, by which they thought they should be appeased—those same beings being the authors and exactors of such great disgraces—it is sufficiently clear by whom they are worshiped.

**10.** But neither are the holy and faithful worshipers of the one true and supreme God secure from their deceits and manifold temptation. For in this place of weakness and in these malignant days even this anxiety is not useless, that that security, where there is fullest and most certain peace, may be sought with a more fervent longing. For there will be the gifts of nature—that is, those which are given to our nature by the Creator of all natures—not only good but also everlasting, not only in the mind, which is healed by wisdom, but also in the body, which will be renewed by resurrection; there will be virtues, not contending against any vices or any evils whatever, but having as the reward of victory eternal peace, which no adversary shall disturb. For this is the final blessedness itself, the very end of perfection, which has no end that consumes it. But here we are indeed called blessed when we have peace, however little can be had here in a good life; but this blessedness, compared with that which we call final, is found to be sheer misery. This peace, then, such as it can be here, we mortal men, when we have it among mortal things, if we live rightly, virtue rightly uses its goods; but when we do not have it, virtue uses well even the evils that a man suffers. But then is virtue true, when it refers both all the goods which it uses well, and whatever it does in the good use of goods and evils, and itself, to that end where there will be for us such and so great a peace that none better and greater can exist.

## The end of goods is peace and eternal life.

**11.** Wherefore we could say that the end of our goods is peace, just as we said it is eternal life, especially because to the very city of God, about which this most laborious discussion of ours is concerned, it is said in the holy Psalm: *praise the Lord, O Jerusalem; praise your God, O Zion; for he has strengthened the bars of your gates, he has blessed your children within you, who has set your borders peace*[^25]. For when the bars of its gates have been strengthened, then no one shall enter into it nor any go out from it. And on this account we ought to understand its borders here to be that peace which we wish to demonstrate to be final. For the mystical name of the city itself—that is, Jerusalem, which we have also said before—is interpreted "vision of peace."

But because the name of "peace" is frequently used even in these mortal things, where there is surely no eternal life, on this account we preferred to call the end of this city, where its highest good will be, eternal life rather than peace. Of which end the Apostle says: *but now, being freed from sin and made servants to God, you have your fruit unto sanctification, and the end, eternal life*[^26].

But again, because eternal life can be taken, by those who do not have familiarity with the holy Scriptures, even as the life of the wicked, whether according to certain philosophers on account of the immortality of the soul, or even according to our own faith on account of the unending punishments of the impious, who surely could not be tortured eternally unless they also lived eternally: assuredly the end of this city, in which it will have its highest good, must be called either "peace in eternal life" or "eternal life in peace," that it may more easily be understood by all. For so great is the good of peace that even in earthly and mortal things nothing is wont to be heard more gratefully, nothing desired more longingly, nothing, finally, can be found better. About which, if we should wish to speak somewhat longer, we shall not, I think, be burdensome to readers, both on account of the end of this city, of which our discourse treats, and on account of the very sweetness of peace, which is dear to all.

## All desire to have peace.

**12.** **1.** This whoever observes human affairs and our common nature, in any way, recognizes along with me; for just as there is no one who would not wish to rejoice, so there is no one who would not wish to have peace. Since indeed even those who wish for wars wish for nothing else than to conquer; therefore they desire, by warring, to arrive at a glorious peace. For what is victory but the subjection of those who resist? and when this has been done, there will be peace. Wars, then, are waged with the intention of peace, even by those who are eager to exercise warlike valor by commanding and fighting.

Whence it is established that peace is the desired end of war. For every man, even by warring, seeks peace; but no one seeks war by making peace. For even those who wish to disturb the peace in which they are do not hate peace, but desire it changed according to their own judgment. They do not, then, wish that there be no peace, but that it be the one they wish. Finally, even if by sedition they have separated themselves from others, unless they keep some semblance of peace with those very fellow conspirators or confederates of theirs, they do not accomplish what they intend. Accordingly, robbers themselves, in order to be more vehemently and safely hostile to the peace of others, wish to have peace among their associates.

But even if one be so preeminent in strength and so wary of accomplices that he entrusts himself to no associate and, plotting and prevailing alone, drives off booty when he has oppressed and slain whomever he could, yet with those whom he cannot kill and from whom he wishes to hide what he does, he keeps some shadow of peace.

But in his own house, with his wife and children, and any others he has there, he assuredly strives to be at peace; for he is without doubt delighted when they obey him at his nod.

For if it does not happen, he is indignant, he rebukes, he punishes, and the peace of his house he composes, if it be so necessary, even by raging—a peace which he perceives cannot exist unless the others in that same domestic society are subject to some head, which he himself is in his own house.

And therefore, if the servitude of many—whether of a city or of a nation—were offered to him, so that they should serve him as he wished to be served in his own house: he would no longer hide himself a robber in lurking places, but would exalt himself a conspicuous king, though the same cupidity and malice remained in him. All, then, desire to have peace with their own, whom they wish to live according to their own judgment. For even with those with whom they wage war, they wish, if they can, to make them their own, and, having subjected them, to impose on them the laws of their own peace.

**12.** **2.** But let us suppose someone such as the poetic and fabulous narrative sings of, whom perhaps, on account of his very unsociable savagery, they preferred to call a half-man rather than a man[^27]. Although, then, his kingdom was the solitude of a dread cave, and his malice so singular that from it a name was found for him (for in Greek "evil" is called *kakos*, which is what he was called),

no wife brought and returned to him fond speech, he neither played with any little children nor commanded any grown-up ones, he enjoyed the conversation of no friend, not even of his father Vulcan—in which one respect alone he was not a little happier, because he himself did not beget such a monster;

he gave nothing to anyone, but from anyone he could, he took away whatever he wished and, when he could, whomever he wished: yet in that very solitary cave of his, *the ground of which*, as it is described, *was always warm with fresh slaughter*, he wished nothing else than peace, in which no one should be troublesome to him, nor should the force or terror of anyone disturb his quiet.

Finally, he desired to have peace with his own body, and as much as he had, so much was well with him. Since indeed he commanded his obedient members, and, in order that he might pacify his mortality—rebelling against him out of need and stirring up the sedition of hunger to dissociate and shut out the soul from the body—with as much haste as he could, he snatched, killed, devoured; and although huge and savage, he yet hugely and ferociously took counsel for the peace of his own life and safety; and on this account, if he had wished to have also with others the peace which he was at sufficient pains to have in his cave and in himself, he would be called neither evil nor a monster nor a half-man. Or if the form of his body and the vomiting of black fires deterred human society from him, perhaps he raged not from a cupidity of harming, but from the necessity of living.

But indeed he either was not, or—what is more to be believed—was not such as he is described with poetic vanity; for unless Cacus were too much accused, Hercules would be too little praised. Such a man, then, or rather half-man, is better believed, as I have said, not to have existed, like many of the poets' fictions.

For even the most savage beasts, from which he had a part of his savagery (for he was called half-beast too[^28]), guard their own kind by a certain peace, by mating, begetting, bearing, cherishing and nourishing their young, although most of them are unsociable and solitary-roaming—not, of course, like sheep, deer, doves, starlings, bees, but like lions, wolves, foxes, eagles, owls. For what tigress does not gently purr to her cubs and, laying aside her savagery, caress them? What kite, however solitary as it circles about for prey, does not couple in marriage, build a nest, warm the eggs, feed the chicks, and, as with the mother of his family, preserve domestic fellowship with as much peace as he can?

How much more is man borne, in a manner, by the laws of his own nature, to enter into fellowship and to maintain peace, as far as in him lies, with all men, since even the wicked make war for the peace of their own and wish, if they could, to make all men theirs, so that all and everything might serve one—by what compact, unless they consent to his peace either by loving or by fearing?

For thus pride perversely imitates God. For it hates equality with its associates under him, but wishes to impose its own dominion on its associates in his place. It hates, therefore, the just peace of God and loves its own unjust peace. Yet it cannot in any way not love some peace. For no one's vice is so contrary to nature as to destroy even the last traces of nature.

**12.** **3.** And so he who knows how to prefer the right to the crooked and the ordered to the perverse sees that the peace of the unjust, in comparison with the peace of the just, is not even to be called peace. But that which is perverse must also necessarily be at peace in some part, and from some part, and with some part of the things in which it is, or of which it consists; otherwise it would be nothing at all.

As, for example, if anyone hang head downward, the position of the body and the order of the members is surely perverse, because that which nature requires to be above is below, and that which nature wishes to be below has been made above; this perversity has disturbed the peace of the flesh and therefore is troublesome: but nevertheless the soul is at peace with its body and busies itself for its safety, and therefore there is one who grieves; which soul, if, driven out by its troubles, it departs, then, as long as the framework of the members remains, what remains is not without a certain peace of the parts, and therefore there is still one who hangs.

And in that the earthly body strives toward the earth and strains against the bond by which it is suspended, it tends toward the order of its own peace and in a manner demands, by the voice of its weight, a place where it may rest; and now lifeless and without any sensation, it nevertheless does not depart from the natural peace of its own order, either when it holds it or when it is borne toward it. For if medicaments and treatment are applied that do not allow the form of the cadaver to be dissolved and to fall apart, still a certain peace joins parts to parts and applies the whole mass to an earthly and suitable and therefore peaceful place.

But if no care of embalming is applied, but it is left to its natural course, it is, as it were, in tumult, with exhalations disagreeing and unsuited to our sense (for that is what is perceived in the stench), until it agrees with the elements of the world and departs into their peace little by little, particle by particle. Yet in no way is anything thence withdrawn from the laws of that highest Creator and Orderer, by whom the peace of the universe is administered; for even if from the cadaver of a larger animal tiny living things are born, by that same law of the Creator all these little bodies serve their own little souls in the peace of safety; and even if the flesh of the dead is devoured by other animals, they find the same laws, diffused throughout all things for the safety of every kind of mortal beings, pacifying fitting things to fitting, in whatever direction they are drawn and to whatever things they are joined and into whatever things they are converted and changed.

## Peace is in the tranquillity of order.

**13.** **1.** The peace of the body, then, is the ordered proportion of its parts; the peace of the irrational soul is the ordered rest of the appetites; the peace of the rational soul is the ordered agreement of cognition and action; the peace of body and soul is the ordered life and health of the living being; the peace of mortal man and God is the ordered obedience, in faith, under the eternal law; the peace of men is ordered concord; the peace of a household is the ordered concord of those dwelling together in commanding and obeying; the peace of a city is the ordered concord of the citizens in commanding and obeying;

the peace of the heavenly city is the most ordered and most concordant fellowship of enjoying God and one another in God; the peace of all things is the tranquillity of order. Order is the disposition of equal and unequal things, assigning to each its own place.

Accordingly, the wretched, because, insofar as they are wretched, they are surely not in peace, lack indeed the tranquillity of order, where there is no disturbance; yet because they are deservedly and justly wretched, they cannot, even in that very misery of theirs, be outside order; not indeed joined to the blessed, but yet separated from them by the law of order. These, when they are without disturbance, are fitted, with whatever congruity, to the things in which they are; and on this account there is in them some tranquillity of order, and therefore some peace.

But they are wretched for this reason: that, although in some security they do not grieve, yet they are not there where they ought to be secure and not to grieve; and more wretched, if there is no peace for them with the very law by which the natural order is administered. But when they grieve, in that part where they grieve, a disturbance of peace has occurred; yet there is still peace in that part where neither does pain burn nor is the framework itself dissolved.

As, therefore, there is a certain life without pain, but pain cannot exist without some life: so there is a certain peace without any war, but war cannot exist without some peace; not according to that by which it is war, but according to that by which it is waged by, or in, things that are some natures; which would in no way exist unless they subsisted by some peace.

**13.** **2.** Wherefore there is a nature in which there is no evil, or even in which there can be no evil; but there cannot be a nature in which there is no good. Accordingly, not even the nature of the devil himself, insofar as it is a nature, is evil; but perversity makes it evil. And so he did not stand in the truth[^29], but did not escape the judgment of the truth; he did not remain in the tranquillity of order, nor yet on that account did he flee from the power of the Orderer. The good of God, which is in him by nature, does not withdraw him from the justice of God, by which he is ordered in punishment; nor does God there pursue the good which he created, but the evil which he committed.

For he does not take away the whole that he gave to nature, but he takes away something, leaves something, that there may be one to grieve over what he took away. And the very grief is a testimony of the good taken away and of the good left. For unless a good were left, one could not grieve over the good lost. For he who sins is worse if he rejoices in the loss of equity; but he who is tortured, if he gains thence no good, grieves over the loss of safety.

And since both equity and safety are good, and over the loss of a good one ought to grieve rather than rejoice (provided there is no compensation of a better; but the equity of the mind is better than the health of the body); surely it is more fitting that the unjust man grieve in punishment than that he rejoiced in transgression.

As, therefore, the joy in the good deserted in sin is a witness of an evil will, so the grief over the good lost in punishment is a witness of a good nature. For he who grieves over the lost peace of his nature, grieves over it from some remnants of peace, by which it comes about that his nature is friendly to itself. And this rightly comes about in the final punishment, that the unjust and the impious bewail in their torments the losses of their natural goods, perceiving that the one who took them away is the most just God, whom they despised as the most kind bestower.

God, therefore, the most wise Founder and most just Orderer of all natures, who established the mortal human race as the greatest ornament of earthly things, gave to men certain goods suited to this life, that is, temporal peace, according to the measure of mortal life, in the very safety and soundness and society of their kind, and whatever things are necessary either for guarding or recovering this peace (such as those things which fittingly and suitably are adjacent to the senses—light, voice, breathable airs, drinkable waters, and whatever is suited to nourishing, covering, healing, and adorning the body),

by this most equitable compact: that whoever, being mortal, has rightly used such goods suited to the peace of mortals, should receive ampler and better ones—namely the very peace of immortality, and the glory and honor befitting it, in eternal life, for the enjoyment of God and of one's neighbor in God; but whoever has used them wrongly, should not receive those and should lose these.

## Peace is loved both in itself and with others.

**14.** All use of temporal things, then, is referred to the fruit of earthly peace in the earthly city; but in the heavenly city it is referred to the fruit of eternal peace. Wherefore, if we were irrational living beings, we would seek nothing beyond the ordered proportion of the parts of the body and the rest of the appetites; nothing, therefore, beyond the quiet of the flesh and an abundance of pleasures, so that the peace of the body might profit the peace of the soul. For if the peace of the body is lacking, the peace of the irrational soul too is hindered, because it cannot attain the rest of the appetites. But both together profit that peace which the soul and body have between themselves, that is, of ordered life and health.

For as living beings show that they love the peace of the body when they flee pain, and the peace of the soul when, in order to satisfy the wants of the appetites, they pursue pleasure: so by fleeing death they sufficiently indicate how much they love that peace by which soul and body are reconciled to one another.

But because in man there is a rational soul, he subjects all this, which he has in common with the beasts, to the peace of the rational soul, that he may contemplate something with the mind and act according to this, so that there may be for him an ordered agreement of cognition and action, which we had called the peace of the rational soul. For to this end he ought to wish neither to be troubled by pain nor disturbed by desire nor dissolved by death, that he may know something useful and according to that knowledge compose his life and morals.

But lest, by the very zeal for knowledge, on account of the weakness of the human mind, he run into the plague of some error, he has need of divine teaching, which he may surely obey, and of help, that he may freely obey. And since, as long as he is in this mortal body, he is on pilgrimage away from the Lord: he walks by faith, not by sight[^30]; and on this account he refers all peace, whether of body or of soul or of both body and soul together, to that peace which mortal man has with the immortal God, that there may be for him an ordered obedience, in faith, under the eternal law.

But now, because God the teacher teaches two chief precepts, that is, the love of God and the love of neighbor[^31], in which a man finds three things to love—God, himself, and his neighbor—and he who loves God does not err in loving himself: it follows that he should also take counsel for his neighbor to love God, whom he is commanded to love as himself (so for his wife, so for his children, so for the members of his household, so for the other men whom he can), and to this end should wish counsel to be taken for himself by his neighbor, if perhaps he is in need; and on this account he will be at peace, as far as in him lies, with every man, in the peace of men, that is, in ordered concord, whose order here is, first, that he harm no one, then, that he even profit whom he can.

First, then, there is in him the care of his own; for to them he has a more opportune and easier access of taking counsel, either by the order of nature or by that of human society itself. Whence the Apostle says: *but whoever does not provide for his own, and especially for those of his household, has denied the faith and is worse than an unbeliever*[^32].

Hence, then, also domestic peace arises, that is, the ordered concord of those dwelling together in commanding and obeying. For those command who take counsel; as the husband for the wife, parents for children, masters for servants. But those obey for whom counsel is taken; as wives for husbands, children for parents, servants for masters.

But in the household of the just man living by faith[^33] and still on pilgrimage from that heavenly city[^34], even those who command serve those whom they seem to command. For they command not from a cupidity of domination, but from the office of taking counsel, not from the pride of ruling, but from the mercy of providing.

## There is peace even in the condition of slavery.

**15.** This the natural order prescribes; thus God created man. For: *let him have dominion*, he says, *over the fish of the sea and the birds of the air and all the creeping things that creep upon the earth*[^35]. Made rational in his own image, he willed him to have dominion only over the irrational; not man over man, but man over the beast. Hence the first just men were established as shepherds of flocks rather than as kings of men[^36], that even thus God might intimate what the order of creatures requires, what the deserving of sins demands.

For the condition of slavery is rightly understood to have been imposed on the sinner. Accordingly, nowhere in the Scriptures do we read "slave" before the just Noah punished the sin of his son with this word[^37]. So this name guilt deserved, not nature. But the origin of the word "slaves" (servi) in the Latin tongue is believed to be drawn from this: that those who by the law of war could be killed, when they were saved (servabantur) by the victors, became slaves, named from saving (servando)[^38]; which itself too is not without the deserving of sin.

For even when a just war is waged, the fighting is on the contrary side for sin; and every victory, when it comes even to the wicked, humbles the conquered by divine judgment, either amending sins or punishing them. Witness is the man of God Daniel, who, set in captivity, confesses his own sins and the sins of his people to God and testifies with pious grief that this is the cause of that captivity[^39]. The first cause of slavery, then, is sin, that man should be subjected to man by the bond of condition; which does not happen except by God's judging, with whom there is no iniquity and who knows how to distribute diverse punishments according to the deserts of offenders.

But as the Lord on high says: *everyone who commits sin is the slave of sin*[^40], and on this account many indeed who are religious serve masters who are unjust, yet not free: *for by whom a man is overcome, to him also is he assigned as a slave*[^41]. And surely it is more fortunate to serve a man than a lust, since the lust of domination itself, to omit others, lays waste the hearts of mortals with a most savage rule. But among men, by that order of peace in which some are subject to others, as humility profits those who serve, so pride harms those who dominate.

And surely it is happier to serve a man than a disordered desire, since—to pass over the others—the very desire to rule devastates mortal hearts with the cruelest domination. In that order of peace by which some human beings are subject to others, humility benefits those who serve, while pride harms those who rule.

But no one is by nature, in which God first created man, a slave of man or of sin. Yet even penal slavery is ordered by that law which commands the natural order to be preserved and forbids it to be disturbed; for if nothing had been done against that law, there would be nothing to be restrained by penal slavery. And therefore the Apostle admonishes even slaves to be subject to their masters and to serve them from the heart with good will[^42]; so that, namely, if they cannot be made free by their masters, they may themselves in a manner make their own slavery free, by serving not with crafty fear but with faithful love, until iniquity passes away and every human principality and power is made void[^43] and God is all in all[^44].

**16.** Wherefore, even if our just fathers had slaves, they so administered domestic peace that, according to these temporal goods, they distinguished the lot of children from the condition of slaves; but for worshiping God, in whom eternal goods are to be hoped for[^45], they took counsel with equal love for all the members of their household. The natural order so prescribes this, that the name "fathers of families" arose from this and was so widely spread that even those who dominate unjustly rejoice to be called by it[^46].

But those who are true fathers of families take counsel for all in their family, as for children, in worshiping and gaining the favor of God, desiring and wishing to come to the heavenly home, where the office of commanding mortals will not be necessary, because the office of taking counsel will not be necessary for those now happy in that immortality; until which is reached, fathers ought to endure that they dominate rather than slaves that they serve.

But if anyone in the household, through disobedience, opposes the domestic peace, he is corrected, whether by word or by blow or by any other kind of penalty just and lawful, as much as human society allows, for the benefit of him who is corrected, that he may be fitted again to the peace from which he had broken away. For just as it is not beneficence, by helping, to bring it about that a greater good be lost; so it is not innocence, by sparing, to allow one to fall into a graver evil. It pertains, then, to the office of the innocent not only to inflict evil on no one, but also to restrain from sin or to punish sin; that either he himself who is chastised may be corrected by the experience, or others may be terrified by the example.

Since, therefore, a man's household ought to be the beginning or a particle of the city, and every beginning is referred to some end of its own kind and every part to the integrity of the whole of which it is a part, it sufficiently appears to be consequent that domestic peace be referred to civic peace, that is, that the ordered concord of those dwelling together in commanding and obeying be referred to the ordered concord of citizens in commanding and obeying. Thus it comes about that the father of a family ought to take precepts from the law of the city, by which he so governs his household that it be suited to the peace of the city.

## What the peace, the order, the piety is in each city.

**17.** But the household of men who do not live by faith pursues earthly peace from the things and advantages of this temporal life; whereas the household of men living by faith awaits those things which are promised as eternal in the future, and uses earthly and temporal things as a pilgrim—not so as to be captivated by them and turned aside from whither it tends toward God, but so as to be supported by them for more easily bearing, and in no way increasing, the burdens of the corruptible body, which weighs down the soul[^47]. Therefore the use of things necessary to this mortal life is common to both kinds of men and to both households; but the end of using is, for each, its own proper one and much different.

So too the earthly city, which does not live by faith, seeks earthly peace, and fixes the concord of citizens in commanding and obeying upon this: that there be among them a certain composition of human wills concerning the things pertaining to mortal life. But the heavenly city, or rather that part of it which is on pilgrimage in this mortality and lives by faith, must of necessity use this peace too, until that very mortality, for which such peace is necessary, passes away; and on this account, while it leads, as it were, a captive life of its pilgrimage among the earthly city—having already received the promise of redemption and the spiritual gift as a kind of pledge—it does not hesitate to obey the laws of the earthly city by which those things are administered which are suited to the sustaining of mortal life, so that, since mortality itself is common, concord may be preserved between both cities in the things pertaining to it.

But because the earthly city had certain wise men of its own, whom the divine teaching disapproves, who, either suspecting or deceived by the demons, believed that many gods were to be conciliated to human affairs, and that to their diverse offices, in a manner, diverse things were subject and pertained—to one the body, to another the mind, and in the body itself to one the head, to another the neck, and the several other parts to several gods;

likewise in the mind, to one natural ability, to another learning, to another anger, to another desire; and in the very things adjacent to life, to one cattle, to another wheat, to another wine, to another oil, to another the woods, to another money, to another navigation, to another wars and victories, to another marriages, to another childbirth and fecundity, and to others the other things;

but the heavenly city, since it knew that one God alone is to be worshiped, and judged with faithful piety that he alone is to be served with that service which in Greek is called *latreia* and is owed to none but God; it came about that it could not have its laws of religion in common with the earthly city, and on account of these had to dissent from it and to be a burden to those who thought differently, and to bear their angers and hatreds and the assaults of persecutions, except when it repelled the minds of its adversaries sometimes by the terror of its multitude, and always by divine help.

This heavenly city, then, while it is on pilgrimage on earth, calls forth citizens from all nations and gathers a society of pilgrims in all tongues, caring nothing about whatever is diverse in morals, laws, and institutions by which earthly peace is either sought or maintained—rescinding or destroying none of them, indeed even preserving and following them—since, although it is diverse in diverse nations, it is yet directed to one and the same end of earthly peace, provided it does not hinder the religion by which the one supreme and true God is taught to be worshiped.

Therefore the heavenly city too, in this its pilgrimage, uses earthly peace and—as far as is conceded while piety and religion are preserved—protects and seeks the composition of human wills concerning the things pertaining to the mortal nature of men, and refers that earthly peace to the heavenly peace, which is so truly peace that, for the rational creature at least, it alone is to be held and called peace, namely the most ordered and most concordant fellowship of enjoying God and one another in God;

and when it shall have come thither, there will be no mortal life, but plainly and surely a vital one, nor an animal body which, while it is corrupted, weighs down the soul, but a spiritual one without any want, subject in every part to the will. This peace it has, while it is on pilgrimage, in faith, and by this faith it lives justly, when it refers to the attaining of that peace whatever good actions it does toward God and neighbor, since the life of the city is assuredly social.

## Against the Academics: what doubt, evidence, and opinion are.

**18.** But as concerns that difference which Varro applied from the new Academics, to whom all things are uncertain[^48], the city of God altogether detests such doubt as madness, having, about the things which it comprehends by mind and reason, even if a small comprehension on account of the corruptible body which weighs down the soul (since, as the Apostle says: *we know in part*[^49]), yet most certain knowledge; and it believes the senses, in the evidence of each thing, which the mind uses through the body, since he is more wretchedly deceived who thinks they are never to be believed[^50]; it believes also the holy Scriptures, both old and new, which we call "canonical," from which the very faith was conceived by which the just man lives[^51]; by which we walk without doubt, as long as we are on pilgrimage from the Lord[^52]; and while this faith remains safe and certain, about certain things which we have perceived neither by sense nor by reason, nor have they become clear to us through the canonical Scripture, nor have they come to our notice through witnesses whom it is absurd not to believe, we doubt without just reproach.

**19.** It pertains nothing, indeed, to this city in what dress or manner of living, if it is not against the divine precepts, anyone follows this faith by which one comes to God; whence, when the philosophers themselves become Christians, it does not compel them to change their dress or custom of food, which in no way hinders religion, but their false teachings. Whence that difference which Varro applied from the Cynics[^53], if one does nothing basely and intemperately, it cares about not at all.

But of those three kinds of life—the leisured, the active, and that composed of both—although, with faith preserved, anyone can lead his life in whichever of them and arrive at the everlasting rewards, yet it matters what he holds by love of the truth, what he expends in the office of love. No one ought to be so leisured that in that very leisure he does not think of the usefulness of his neighbor, nor so active that he does not seek the contemplation of God. In leisure it ought not to be an idle vacancy that delights, but either the inquiry or the discovery of truth, that each may make progress in it and not begrudge to another what he has found.

But in action, honor in this life or power is not to be loved, since all things are vanity under the sun[^54], but the work itself which is done through that same honor or power, if it is done rightly and usefully, that is, so that it may avail toward that safety of the subjects which is according to God; whence we have already disputed above[^55]. On account of which the Apostle says: *he who desires the episcopate desires a good work*[^56].

He wished to set forth what the episcopate is, because it is the name of a work, not of an honor. For it is a Greek word, and the term is drawn from this: that he who is set over those over whom he is set superintends them, namely bearing care of them; for *skopos* is "intention"; therefore *episkopein*, if we wish, we can say in Latin "to superintend," so that one may understand that he is not a bishop who has loved to be in charge rather than to be of profit. And so no one is forbidden from the zeal of knowing the truth, which pertains to praiseworthy leisure; but the higher place, without which a people cannot be governed, even if it be so held and administered as is fitting, is yet unfittingly sought.

Wherefore the love of truth seeks holy leisure; the necessity of love undertakes just business. If no one imposes this burden, one should give oneself to perceiving and beholding the truth; but if it is imposed, it is to be undertaken on account of the necessity of love; yet not even so is the delight of truth to be in every way deserted, lest that sweetness be withdrawn and this necessity oppress.

**20.** Wherefore, since the highest good of the city of God is eternal and perfect peace—not that through which mortals pass by being born and dying, but that in which immortals abide, suffering nothing adverse at all—who is there who would either deny that life to be most blessed, or, in comparison with it, not judge this life, which is led here, however full it be of goods of mind and body and external things, to be most wretched?

Yet whoever so holds this life that he refers its use to that end which he most ardently loves and most faithfully hopes for, can not absurdly be called blessed even now, by that hope rather than by this reality. But this reality without that hope is a false blessedness and a great misery; for it does not use the true goods of the mind, since that is not true wisdom which, in those things which it prudently discerns, bravely accomplishes, temperately restrains, and justly distributes, does not direct its intention to that end *where God will be all in all*[^57], in sure eternity and perfect peace.

## A people, the affair of a people, right, and justice cohere.

**21.** **1.** Wherefore now is the place, that I may unfold as briefly and clearly as I can what I promised in the second book of this work that I would demonstrate[^58]: that, according to the definitions which Scipio uses in Cicero's books *On the Republic*, there never was a Roman commonwealth. For he briefly defines a commonwealth to be *the affair of a people*[^59]. If this definition is true, there never was a Roman commonwealth, because there never was an *affair of a people*, which definition he wished to be that of a commonwealth. For he defined a *people* to be *an assembly of a multitude associated by a consent of right and a community of interest*[^60]. But what he means by a *consent of right* he explains by discussing, showing thereby that a commonwealth cannot be conducted without justice; where, therefore, there is no true justice, there can be no right. For what is done by right is surely done justly; but what is done unjustly cannot be done by right. For the unjust constitutions of men are not to be called or thought rights, since even they themselves say that that is right which has flowed from the fountain of justice, and that it is false—what is wont to be said by certain men who do not think rightly—that that is right which is useful to the one who is more powerful[^61].

Wherefore, where there is no true justice, there cannot be *an assembly of men associated by a consent of right*, and therefore no people according to that definition of Scipio or of Cicero; and if no people, then no *affair of a people*, but of some multitude or other which is not worthy of the name of *people*. And on this account, if a commonwealth is *the affair of a people*, and there is no people which is not *associated by a consent of right*, but there is no right where there is no justice: it is without doubt concluded that where there is no justice, there is no commonwealth.

Justice, moreover, is that virtue which distributes to each his own. What justice, then, is it of man, which takes man himself away from the true God and subjects him to unclean demons? Is this to distribute to each his own? Or is he who takes away an estate from the one from whom it was bought, and hands it over to one who has no right in it, unjust; and is he who takes himself away from the God who rules him, by whom he was made, and serves malignant spirits, just?

**21.** **2.** It is certainly disputed most keenly and most strongly in those very books *On the Republic*, against injustice for justice. And since, when it was first argued for the side of injustice against justice, and it was said that a commonwealth cannot stand or be conducted except through injustice, this had been laid down as it were most valid, that it is unjust that men should serve men who dominate them; which injustice, however, unless an imperious city follows—a city to which a great commonwealth belongs—it cannot command provinces:

it was answered on the side of justice that it is just for this reason, that to such men servitude is useful, and that it is done for their benefit, when it is rightly done, that is, when license of injuries is taken away from the wicked, and the subdued will be better off, because as unsubdued they were worse off; and there was added, that this reasoning might be confirmed, a noble example taken as it were from nature, and it was said: *why, then, does God command man, the mind the body, reason the lust and the other vicious parts of the mind?* Plainly by this example it was sufficiently taught that servitude is useful to some, and that to serve God is useful to all.

But the mind serving God rightly commands the body, and in the mind itself reason, subject to the Lord God, rightly commands lust and the other vices. Wherefore, where man does not serve God, what is to be thought there is of justice? Since indeed, not serving God, in no way can the mind justly command the body or human reason the vices. And if in such a man there is no justice at all, without doubt there is none either in an assembly of men which consists of such men. Here, then, there is not that *consent of right* which makes a multitude of men a people, whose affair is said to be a commonwealth.

For what shall I say of interest, by a *community* of which too an assembly of men, as this definition has it, is named a people? For although, if you attend diligently, there is no interest at all for those living who live impiously, as everyone lives who does not serve God and serves demons—so much the more impious as, since they are most unclean spirits, they wish to be sacrificed to as gods: yet what we have said about the *consent of right* I think is enough, whence it appears, by this definition, that that is not a people whose affair is said to be a commonwealth, in which there is no justice.

## [Against Porphyry: justice and the true God]

For if they say that the Romans served, in their commonwealth, not unclean spirits but good and holy gods: must the same things be so often repeated which we have already said enough, indeed more than enough? For who has come to this place through the earlier books of this work who could still doubt that the Romans served evil and impure demons, unless he is either too stupid or most shamelessly contentious? But to be silent about what kind they are whom they worshiped with sacrifices: in the law of the true God it is written: *he who sacrifices to gods shall be rooted out, save to the Lord alone*[^62]. Neither to good gods, then, nor to evil ones did he will sacrifice to be made, who commanded this with so great a threat.

**22.** But it can be answered: "Who is this God, or whence is he proved worthy that the Romans ought to have obeyed him, so as to worship none of the gods besides him with sacrifices?" It is a sign of great blindness still to ask who this God is. He is the God whose prophets foretold these things which we behold. He is the God from whom Abraham received the answer: *in your seed shall all the nations be blessed*[^63]. That this is being accomplished in Christ, who according to the flesh sprang from that seed, even those very men who have remained enemies of this name, whether they will or not, recognize.

He is the God whose divine Spirit spoke through those whose predictions, fulfilled through the Church which we see diffused throughout the whole world, I have set down in the earlier books. He is the God whom Varro, the most learned of the Romans, thinks to be Jupiter, although not knowing what he says; which, however, I thought should be mentioned, since a man of such great knowledge could neither think this God to be no God nor to be a vile one. For he believed him to be the one whom he thought the supreme god[^64]. Finally, he is the God whom the most learned of the philosophers, although a most keen enemy of the Christians, even through the oracles of those whom he thinks gods, confesses to be a great god—Porphyry[^65].

**23.** **1.** For in the books which he entitles *From the Philosophy of Oracles*, in which he sets forth and records, as it were, divine responses on matters pertaining to philosophy, to set down his very words, as they have been translated from the Greek tongue into Latin: *to one asking*, he says, *by what god he might recall his wife from Christianity, Apollo says these things in verses*. Then the words, as it were of Apollo, are these: *perhaps you could more easily write with letters impressed upon water, or, inflating light wings, fly through the air a bird, than recall the sense of your polluted, impious wife. Let her go as she wishes, persevering in empty fallacies and lamenting in fallacies, singing of a dead God, whom, condemned by judges thinking rightly, a most foul death, bound with iron amid fair things, slew*. Then after these verses of Apollo, which were translated into Latin without keeping the meter, he subjoined and said: *in these, indeed, he manifested the incurableness of their opinion, saying that the Jews receive God more than these.*

Behold, where, discrediting Christ, he preferred the Jews to the Christians, confessing that the Jews receive God. For thus he expounded the verses of Apollo, where he says Christ was slain by judges thinking rightly, as though, they judging justly, he was deservedly punished. Let them see what the lying prophet of Apollo said about Christ and what this man believed, or perhaps this man himself fabricated that the prophet said what he did not say. How consistent he is with himself, or how he makes the oracles themselves agree among themselves, we shall see afterward;

here, however, he says that the Jews, as receivers of God, judged rightly about Christ, in that they decreed that he should be tortured with a most foul death. The God of the Jews, then, to whom he bears witness, ought to have been heard, saying: *he who sacrifices to gods shall be rooted out, save to the Lord alone*[^66]. But let us come to clearer matters and hear how great a God he says the God of the Jews is. Likewise, to what he asked of Apollo—which is better, the word or reason, or the law: *he answered*, he says, *saying these things in verses*.

And then he subjoins some verses of Apollo, among which are these, that I may cull from them as much as is enough: *but to God the begetter and the king before all things, at whom heaven and earth and sea and the hidden places of the underworld tremble and the very deities shudder; whose law is the Father, whom the holy Hebrews greatly honor*[^67]. By such an oracle of his god Apollo, Porphyry called the God of the Hebrews so great that the deities themselves shudder at him.

Since, then, this God has said, *he who sacrifices to gods shall be rooted out*, I am surprised that Porphyry himself did not shudder, and was not afraid of being rooted out by sacrificing to the gods.

**23. 2.** This philosopher also says good things about Christ, as though he had forgotten that insult of his of which we spoke a little before, or as though his gods had cursed Christ in their sleep and, on waking, had recognized that he was good and praised him as he deserved. In short, as if about to bring forth something marvelous and incredible, he says: *what we are going to say will surely seem to some to be contrary to expectation. For the gods have declared Christ to be most devout and to have been made immortal, and they make mention of him with kindly commendation; but the Christians*, he says, *they declare to be polluted and contaminated and entangled in error, and they use many such blasphemies against them*. Then he subjoins, as it were, oracles of the gods blaspheming the Christians; and after these: *but to those who ask about Christ whether he is God*, he says, *Hecate replies: 'that the soul is immortal after the body, as it goes onward, you know; but cut off from wisdom it ever wanders. That soul belongs to a man preeminent in piety; this soul they worship, themselves being estranged from the truth.'*

Then, weaving in his own words after the words of this oracle, as it were, he says: *he therefore called him a most devout man, and said that his soul, like the souls of other devout men, was after death deemed worthy of immortality, and that the Christians worship this soul in ignorance*. *And to those who ask*, he says, *why then was he condemned? the goddess replied by an oracle: 'the body indeed is ever exposed to weakening torments; but the soul of the devout dwells in a heavenly abode. That soul, however, fatefully gave to other souls, to whom the fates did not grant to obtain the gifts of the gods nor to have knowledge of immortal Jove, that they should be entangled in error.'*

*For this reason, then, they are hateful to the gods: because, to those for whom it was not fated to know God nor to receive gifts from the gods, this soul fatefully gave that they should be entangled in error. But he himself was devout and passed into heaven, as the devout do. Therefore you will not blaspheme him, but you will pity the madness of men, on account of him in them ready and headlong danger*[^68].

**23. 3.** Who is so foolish that he does not understand that these oracles were either fabricated by a cunning man, and one most hostile to the Christians, or were given as responses by impure demons with a like design: namely, that because they praise Christ, they may on that account be believed to be truthful in disparaging the Christians, and so, if they can, may block the way of eternal salvation, on which one becomes a Christian? For they perceive that it is not contrary to their thousandfold cunning in doing harm if they are believed when they praise Christ, provided that they are also believed when they disparage the Christians; so that the man who has believed both they may make into such a praiser of Christ that he will not wish to be a Christian, and thus, though praised by him, Christ may nevertheless not free him from the dominion of these demons—especially because they so praise Christ that whoever believes in him as he is proclaimed by them is not a true Christian but a Photinian heretic, who knows Christ only as a man and not also as God, and therefore cannot be saved through him nor avoid or loose the snares of these lying demons.

But we can approve neither Apollo disparaging Christ nor Hecate praising him. For the one wishes Christ to be believed wicked, since he says he was put to death by judges who judged rightly; the other says he was a most devout man, but only a man. Yet the intention of both is one and the same: that they do not wish men to be Christians, because, unless they are Christians, they cannot be rescued from their power.

But let this philosopher, or rather those who believe such would-be oracles against the Christians, first bring it about, if they can, that Hecate and Apollo agree among themselves about Christ himself and either both condemn him or both praise him together. And if they could do this, we would none the less avoid the deceitful demons who are both the disparagers and the praisers of Christ. But since their god and goddess disagree among themselves about Christ, the one by disparaging, the other by praising, surely men do not believe them when they blaspheme the Christians, if they themselves judge rightly.

**23. 4.** Indeed, when Porphyry or Hecate, in praising Christ, says that he himself fatefully gave to the Christians that they should be entangled in error, he nevertheless lays open the causes, as he supposes, of that same error. Before I set them out from his words, I first ask: if Christ fatefully gave the Christians their entanglement in error, did he give it willingly or unwillingly? If willingly, how is he just? If unwillingly, how is he blessed?

But let us now hear the causes of the error themselves. *There are*, he says, *certain least earthly spirits, subjected in a particular place to the power of evil demons. From these the wise men of the Hebrews (one of whom was also this Jesus, as you have heard the divine sayings of Apollo, which were spoken above)—from these demons, then, the most evil and lesser spirits, the Hebrews forbade religious men and prohibited them from being occupied with them; but rather to venerate the heavenly gods, and still more to venerate God the Father. This, however*, he says, *the gods too command, and we have shown above how they admonish men to turn their attention to God, and everywhere bid them worship him. But the unlearned and impious by nature, to whom truly fate did not grant to obtain the gifts of the gods nor to have the notion of immortal Jove, not heeding either the gods or divine men, refused indeed all the gods, but did not hate the forbidden demons, and even revered them. While pretending to worship God, they do not do the very things by which God is adored. For God indeed, as the Father of all, needs nothing; but it is well with us when we adore him through justice and chastity and the other virtues, making our very life a prayer to him through imitation of and inquiry concerning him. For inquiry*, he says, *purifies, and imitation deifies, working our affection toward him*[^69].

He has indeed proclaimed God the Father well, and has said by what habits he is to be worshiped; with which precepts the prophetic books of the Hebrews are full, when the life of the saints is either commanded or praised. But concerning the Christians he errs, or rather slanders, as much as the demons wish whom he supposes to be gods—as though it were difficult for anyone to recall what shameful and disgraceful things were done in the theaters and temples in homage to the gods, and to attend to what is read, said, and heard in the churches, or what is offered to the true God, and from this to understand where there is the building up and where the ruin of morals. But who told this man, or inspired him, except a diabolical spirit, with so empty and patent a lie: that the Christians revere rather than hate the demons whom the Hebrews forbade to be worshiped?

But that God whom the wise men of the Hebrews worshiped forbids sacrifice to be offered even to the holy angels of heaven and the powers of God, whom we venerate and love as most blessed citizens during this our mortal pilgrimage, thundering in his law, which he gave to his Hebrew people, and saying very threateningly: *he who sacrifices to gods shall be rooted out*[^70]. And lest anyone should suppose that it was commanded only that there be no sacrifice to the most evil demons and earthly spirits, whom this man calls least or lesser[^71] (because they too are called gods in the holy Scriptures, not the gods of the Hebrews but of the gentiles, which the Septuagint translators clearly set down in the Psalm, saying: *for all the gods of the gentiles are demons*[^72])—lest, then, anyone should suppose that it was forbidden to sacrifice to these demons, but permitted to sacrifice either to all or to some of the heavenly ones, he immediately added: *except to the Lord alone*, that is, except to the Lord only; lest perhaps, in what he says, *to the Lord alone*, someone should believe that the Lord is the sun, to whom he supposes sacrifice should be made—which is most easily found in the Greek Scriptures not to be understood so.

**23. 5.** The God of the Hebrews, then, to whom even so great a philosopher as this bears so great a testimony, gave his Hebrew people a law written in the Hebrew tongue—not obscure and unknown, but already published abroad among all the nations—in which law it is written: *he who sacrifices to gods shall be rooted out, except to the Lord only*[^73]. What need is there in this law of his and in his prophets to search out many things on this matter—nay, not to search out, for they are not hidden or rare, but to gather what is open and frequent and to set it down in this argument of mine—by which it appears clearer than light that the true and supreme God willed sacrifice to be offered to none whatsoever but only to himself?

See, let this one thing, briefly—nay, grandly, threateningly, but truly—spoken by that God whom their most learned men so excellently proclaim, be heard, feared, and fulfilled, lest a rooting-out follow upon the disobedient. *He who sacrifices*, he says, *to gods shall be rooted out, except to the Lord only*—not because he needs anything, but because it is to our advantage that we be his. For to him it is sung in the sacred letters of the Hebrews: *I said to the Lord: you are my God, for you have no need of my goods*[^74]. And his most excellent and best sacrifice is we ourselves, that is, his city, the mystery of which we celebrate in our offerings, which are known to the faithful, as we argued in the preceding books[^75]. For the divine oracles cried out through the Hebrew prophets that the victims which the Jews offered in the shadow of what was to come would cease, and that from the rising of the sun to its setting the nations would offer one sacrifice[^76], as we now perceive being done; and of these we have brought forward as much as seemed enough and have already sprinkled into this work[^77].

Wherefore, where this justice is not—that the one and supreme God, according to his grace, rules an obedient city so that it sacrifices to none but himself only, and that through this, in all men belonging to that same city and obedient to God, the mind also faithfully rules the body and reason rules the vices in lawful order; so that, as the one just man, so the assembly and people of the just live by faith, which works through love, by which a man loves God as God ought to be loved and his neighbor as himself—where, then, this justice is not, surely there is no assembly of men associated by a common agreement as to right and by a community of interest. And if there is none, then there is certainly no people, if this definition of a people is true. Therefore neither is there a commonwealth, because there is no concern of the people where there is no people itself.

## The Romans and the other peoples and commonwealths did exist.

**24.** But if a people is defined not in this way but in some other—as if it should be said, "a people is an assembly of a rational multitude associated by a harmonious community in the things it loves"—then surely, in order to see what sort each people is, those things must be examined which it loves. Yet whatever it loves, if it is an assembly of a multitude not of beasts but of rational creatures, and is associated by a harmonious community in the things it loves, it is not absurdly called a people; and it is by so much the better, the better the things in which it is in harmony, and by so much the worse, the worse the things in which it is in harmony.

According to this definition of ours, the Roman people is a people, and its concern is without doubt a commonwealth. But what that people loved in its earliest times and in those that followed, and by what morals, coming to most bloody seditions and thence to social and civil wars, it broke and corrupted that very harmony which is, in a certain way, the welfare of a people, history attests; of which we have set down many things in the preceding books[^78]. Nor would I therefore say either that it is not a people or that its concern is not a commonwealth, as long as there remains some assembly of a rational multitude, associated by a harmonious community in the things it loves. But what I have said of this people and of this commonwealth, let me be understood to have said and judged of the Athenians or of any of the Greeks, of the Egyptians, of that earlier Babylon of the Assyrians, when in their commonwealths they held empires whether small or great, and of any other of the other nations. For generally a city of the impious, which God does not rule as obedient to him—so that it offers sacrifice to no one but himself only, and through this both the mind rules the body and reason the vices rightly and faithfully within it—lacks the truth of justice.

**25.** For however praiseworthily the mind may seem to rule the body and reason the vices, if the mind and reason itself do not serve God as God himself has commanded that he be served, in no way does it rightly rule the body and the vices. For what sort of mistress of the body and the vices can a mind be that does not know the true God and is not made subject to his command, but is prostituted to most vicious demons that corrupt it? And so the virtues which it seems to itself to have, by which it rules the body and the vices, if it refers them to anything to be attained or held except to God, are themselves vices rather than virtues.

For although by some they are then thought to be true and honorable virtues, when they are referred to themselves and are not sought for the sake of anything else, even then they are puffed up and proud, and therefore are to be judged not virtues but vices. For just as it is not from the flesh but above the flesh that the flesh is made to live, so it is not from man but above man that man is made to live blessedly—and not only man, but also any heavenly power and virtue whatever.

## In time there is peace between the two cities.

**26.** Wherefore, as the soul is the life of the flesh, so God is the blessed life of man, of whom the sacred letters of the Hebrews say: *blessed is the people whose God is the Lord*[^79]. Wretched, therefore, is the people estranged from this God. Yet it too loves a certain peace of its own, not to be condemned, which indeed it will not have in the end, because it does not use it well before the end.

But that it may have this peace meanwhile in this life is also in our interest; since, as long as the two cities are mingled together, we too make use of the peace of Babylon, from which the people of God is so freed by faith that meanwhile it sojourns as a pilgrim within it.

For this reason the Apostle also admonished the Church to pray for kings and those in high place, adding and saying: *that we may lead a quiet and tranquil life with all piety and love*[^80]; and the prophet Jeremiah, when he foretold captivity to the ancient people of God and by divine command bade them go obediently into Babylon, serving their God by this very patience, himself also admonished that prayer be made for it, saying: *for in its peace is your peace*[^81]—the temporal peace, of course, which meanwhile is common to the good and the bad.

and the prophet Jeremiah, when he foretold captivity to God’s people of old and commanded them by divine authority to go obediently to Babylon, serving their God by this endurance too, likewise urged them to pray for that city, saying, *for in its peace is your peace*[^82]: for the present, of course, temporal peace, which the good and the evil share.

**27.** But our own peace is both here with God through faith and will be eternal with him through sight[^83]. But here, whether the common peace or our own, such peace is a solace of misery rather than the joy of blessedness. Our very justice too, though it is true because of the true end of the good to which it is referred, is nevertheless in this life only so great that it consists in the remission of sins rather than in the perfection of virtues. Witness is the prayer of the whole city of God which sojourns on earth. For through all its members it cries out to God: *forgive us our debts, as we also forgive our debtors*[^84]. And this prayer is not effective for those whose faith without works is dead[^85], but for those whose faith works through love[^86].

For because, though subject to God, yet in this mortal condition and corruptible body, which weighs down the soul[^87], reason does not perfectly rule the vices, such a prayer is necessary for the just. For surely, although it does rule, it by no means rules the vices without conflict; and certainly something of weakness creeps in at this point even for one who fights well or who rules over such enemies once conquered and subdued, so that, if not by easy action, at least by a slip of speech or a flitting thought, sin is committed.

And therefore, as long as the vices are being ruled, the peace is not full, because both those which resist are subdued in a perilous battle, and those which have been conquered are not yet triumphed over in secure ease, but are still held down by an anxious command. In these temptations, then, of all of which it is briefly said in the divine words, *is not human life upon earth a temptation?*[^88]

who would presume to live in such a way that he had no need to say to God, *forgive us our debts*, except a man puffed up?—and one not great but inflated and swollen, whom he who bestows grace on the humble resists by justice. Wherefore it is written: *God resists the proud, but gives grace to the humble*[^89].

And so here the justice in each one is this: that God rules the obedient man, the mind the body, and reason the vices—even resisting ones—whether by subduing or by resisting them; and that from God himself both the grace of merits be sought and pardon of offenses, and for the goods received that thanksgiving be rendered.

But in that final peace, to which this justice is to be referred and for the sake of attaining which it is to be held, since our nature, healed by immortality and incorruption, will have no vices, and nothing will resist any one of us either from another or from himself, there will be no need for reason to rule the vices, which will be none;

but God will rule man, and the mind the body, and there will be there as great a sweetness and ease in obeying as there is felicity in living and reigning. And this there, in all and in each, will be eternal, and it will be certain that it is eternal; and therefore the peace of this blessedness, or the blessedness of this peace, will be the supreme good.

## For the reprobate, war and grief in eternity.

**28.** But of those who do not belong to this city of God there will be, on the contrary, everlasting misery, which is also called the second death[^90], because neither the soul there can be said to live, being estranged from the life of God, nor the body, which will be subject to eternal pains; and through this the second death will be the harder, because it cannot be ended by death. But since, as misery seems to be contrary to blessedness and death to life, so war seems to be contrary to peace, it is rightly asked—just as peace has been proclaimed and praised among the ends of the good—what or what sort of war, on the contrary, can be understood among the ends of the wicked.

But whoever asks this, let him attend to what in war is harmful and ruinous, and he will see that it is nothing other than the mutual opposition and conflict of things. What war, then, can be thought of as graver and more bitter than where the will is so opposed to passion and passion to the will that by the victory of neither are such enmities ended, and where the force of pain so contends with the very nature of the body that neither yields to the other? For here, when this conflict occurs, either pain conquers and death takes away sensation, or nature conquers and health removes the pain. But there both pain remains so as to afflict, and nature endures so as to feel; and neither fails, lest the punishment should fail.

What war, then, can be imagined more grievous and bitter than one in which the will so opposes suffering and suffering so opposes the will that neither can end such hostility by victory, and in which the force of pain so contends with the body’s very nature that neither yields to the other?

For when this conflict occurs here, either pain conquers and death takes away sensation, or nature conquers and health removes the pain. But there pain remains to afflict, and nature endures to feel it; for neither fails, so that the punishment may not fail.

But concerning these ends of the good and the wicked—the former to be sought, the latter to be shunned—since the good will pass through judgment to the former and the wicked to the latter, I shall, as much as God shall grant, argue concerning this judgment in the volume that follows.
