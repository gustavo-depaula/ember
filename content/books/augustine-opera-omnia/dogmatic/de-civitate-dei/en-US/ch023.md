# Book XXI: The Punishment Must Be Discussed First

**1.** Since, through our Lord Jesus Christ, the judge of the living and the dead, both cities—of which one is God's, the other the devil's—will have come to their due ends, of what sort the punishment of the devil and of all who belong to him will be, must be discussed by us in this book more diligently, as far as we shall be able by divine help. But I have preferred to keep this order, and afterward to treat of the felicity of the saints, because both will be with bodies, and it seems more incredible that bodies should endure in eternal torments than that they should remain without any pain in eternal blessedness; and through this, when I have shown that that punishment ought not to be incredible, it will help me much, so that the immortality of the body, free from all trouble, which is to be in the saints, may be believed far more easily.

Nor is this order at variance with the divine words, where sometimes indeed the blessedness of the good is set first, as is that: *those who have done good, to the resurrection of life; but those who have done evil, to the resurrection of judgment*[^1];

but sometimes also later, as is: *the Son of man will send his angels, and they will gather out of his kingdom all causes of scandal and will cast them into the burning furnace of fire; there will be weeping and gnashing of teeth; then the just will shine like the sun in the kingdom of their Father*[^2], and that: *so these will go away into eternal punishment, but the just into eternal life*[^3]; and in the Prophets, which it would be long to mention, now this, now that order is found, if anyone looks. But why I have chosen this order, I have said.

## There are animals that live in fire.

**2.** What, then, shall I bring forward, whence the unbelievers may be convinced, that human bodies, animated and living, can not only never be dissolved by death, but also endure in the torments of eternal fires? For they will not have us refer this to the power of the Almighty, but demand that they be persuaded by some example. If we answer them that there are animals surely corruptible, because mortal, which nevertheless live in the midst of fires[^4], and that a certain kind of worm too is found in the bubbling springs of hot waters, whose heat no one handles with impunity, but those creatures not only are there without any harm to themselves, but cannot be outside it[^5]—either they refuse to believe, if we are unable to show it;

For they do not wish us to appeal to the Almighty’s power, but demand to be persuaded by some example.

If we answer them that there are animals which are certainly perishable, since they are mortal, yet live in the midst of fires[^6], and that a certain kind of worm is also found in springs of hot water whose heat no one can touch without injury, yet these creatures not only remain there unharmed but cannot live outside them[^7]:

or, if we have been able either to demonstrate the things themselves to their eyes or to teach them through suitable witnesses, they will contend with the same unbelief that this is not enough for an example of the matter in question, because these animals do not live forever and live in those heats without pains; for they are quickened by those elements suited to their nature, not tormented by them—as though it were not more incredible to be quickened than to be tormented by such things.

For it is marvelous to feel pain in fires and yet to live, but more marvelous to live in fires and not to feel pain. But if this is believed, why not also that?

## Not everything that feels pain dies.

**3. 1.** But there is no body, they say, which can feel pain and cannot die. And how do we know this? For who is certain about the bodies of the demons, whether they feel pain in them, when they confess that they are afflicted with great torments? But if it is answered that there is no earthly body—solid, that is, and visible, or, to explain it rather by one name, no flesh—which can feel pain and cannot die: what else is said but what men have gathered by bodily sense and experience? For they know no flesh except mortal; and this is their whole reasoning: that what they have not experienced they think can by no means exist. For what reasoning is it to make pain an argument for death, when it is rather a token of life? For although we ask whether it can always live, yet it is certain that everything which feels pain lives, and that all pain can be only in a living thing.

It is necessary, then, that the one feeling pain live; it is not necessary that pain kill, since not even all pain kills these mortal and surely-to-die bodies, and the reason why some pain can kill is this: that the soul is so connected to this body that it yields to the highest pains and departs; since the very framework of the members and vital parts is so weak that it cannot sustain that force which makes great or highest pain. But then the soul will be connected to such a body and in such a way that, as that bond will not be loosed by any length of time, so it will not be broken by any pain.

Accordingly, even if no such flesh now exists which can suffer the sensation of pain and cannot die, yet there will then be such flesh as now is not, just as there will also be such a death as now is not. For there will be not no death, but an everlasting death, when the soul will be able neither to live by not having God nor to be free from the pains of the body by dying. The first death drives the unwilling soul from the body, the second death holds the unwilling soul in the body; by both deaths in common it is held that the soul suffers from its own body what it does not will.

**3. 2.** But these objectors attend to the fact that there is now no flesh which can suffer pain and cannot die, and do not attend that there is nevertheless something such which is greater than the body. For the mind itself, by whose presence the body lives and is ruled, can both suffer pain and cannot die. Behold, a thing has been found which, though it has the sensation of pain, is immortal. This, then, will then be in the bodies of the damned, which we now know to be in the minds of all.

But if we consider more diligently, the pain which is called of the body pertains rather to the soul. For it belongs to the soul to feel pain, not to the body, even when the cause of feeling pain comes to it from the body, when it feels pain in that place where the body is injured. As, then, we speak of bodies feeling and bodies living, when sensation and life are to the body from the soul, so we speak of bodies feeling pain too, when pain can be to the body only from the soul.

The soul, then, feels pain together with the body in that place of it where something happens that it should feel pain; and it feels pain alone, though it be in the body, when from some cause even invisible it is itself sad while the body is unharmed; it feels pain even when not established in a body; for surely that rich man in the lower regions felt pain when he said: *I am tormented in this flame*[^8].

But the body, when lifeless, does not feel pain, nor, when animated, does it feel pain without the soul. If, then, from pain an argument were rightly drawn to death, so that death could befall because pain too could befall, dying would pertain rather to the soul, to which feeling pain too pertains rather. But since that which can feel pain more cannot die, what weight is there why we should believe that those bodies, because they will be in pains, will therefore also die?

The Platonists indeed said that it is from earthly bodies and dying members that souls both fear and desire and feel pain and rejoice; whence Virgil says: *hence* (that is, from the dying members of the earthly body) *they fear and desire, feel pain and rejoice*[^9]. But we convicted them in the twelfth book of this work[^10] that, according to themselves, souls cleansed even from every stain of the body have a dire desire by which they begin again to wish to return into bodies[^11]. But where there can be desire, there surely can also be pain. For frustrated desire—whether by not reaching where it tended or by losing where it had reached—is turned into pain.

Wherefore, if the soul, which either alone or chiefly feels pain, nevertheless has a certain immortality of its own in its own measure, those bodies will not be able to die for this reason, that they will feel pain. Finally, if bodies cause souls to feel pain, why can they inflict pain on them but cannot inflict death, except because it does not follow that what causes pain causes death? Why, then, is it incredible that fires can in this way inflict pain on those bodies, not death, just as the bodies themselves cause souls to feel pain, which nevertheless they do not for that reason compel to die? Pain, then, is not a necessary argument for future death.

## There are certain marvels in nature.

**4. 1.** Wherefore, if, as those have written who have more curiously investigated the natures of animals, the salamander lives in fires[^12], and certain most famous mountains of Sicily, which for so great a length and antiquity of time both up to now and hereafter burn with flames and yet remain whole, are sufficiently fit witnesses that not everything which burns is consumed—and the soul shows that not everything which can feel pain can also die—why are examples still demanded of us, by which we may teach that it is not incredible that the bodies of men punished with everlasting punishment both do not lose their soul in fire and burn without detriment and feel pain without destruction?

For the substance of the flesh will then have that quality given by him who gave so many wondrous and various qualities to so many things we see, that, because they are many, we do not marvel at them. For who but God, the creator of all things, gave to the flesh of a dead peacock that it should not rot? When this seemed incredible to hear, it happened that at Carthage this bird was set before us cooked, from whose breast we ordered as much of the meat as seemed good to be kept; which, after so great a space of days as any other cooked flesh whatever would rot, when brought out and offered, in no way offended our smell. And likewise put away again after more than thirty days, it was found to be the same as it was, and the same after a year, except that it was somewhat drier and more shrunken in body[^13].

**4. 2.** Who gave to chaff so cold a power that it preserves snows that are buried under it, or so warm a power that it ripens unripe fruits? Of fire itself who can explain the marvels[^14], by which all things scorched grow black, though it itself is bright, and, most beautiful in color, it discolors almost everything which it surrounds and licks, and from a glowing coal makes the foulest charcoal?

And this is not, as it were, defined by rule; for, on the contrary, stones thoroughly baked by glowing fire themselves too become white, and although the fire is rather red, they grow white; yet what is white agrees with light, as what is black agrees with darkness. Thus, although fire burns in wood so as to bake stones, it has contrary effects, not in contrary things. For although wood and stones are diverse, they are nevertheless not contrary, as white and black are, one of which it makes in stones, the other in wood, that clear thing clarifying the one, darkening the other, since in the one it would fail unless it lived in the other.

And in charcoal, is not so great a weakness to be marveled at, that it is broken by the lightest blow, crushed by the easiest pressure—and so great a firmness, that it is corrupted by no moisture, conquered by no age—to such a degree that those who fix boundaries are accustomed to lay them underneath, to convict any litigant who, after however long a time, shall come forward and contend that a fixed stone is not a boundary? Who has made it possible that they, buried in moist earth, where wood would rot, can last so long incorruptibly, except that corrupter of things, fire?

**4. 3.** Let us consider too the marvel of lime. Besides that of which we have already said enough—that it grows white with fire, by which other things are made foul—it also most secretly conceives fire from fire, and, now a cold lump to those who touch it, it keeps it so latently that it does not at all appear to any sense of ours, but, ascertained by experiment, even while it does not appear, is known to be present, lying dormant. For which reason we call it "living lime," as though the latent fire were the invisible soul of a visible body.

But how marvelous is it that, when it is extinguished, then it is kindled! For that it may be rid of the hidden fire, it is poured into water or sprinkled with water, and, when it was cold before, from that it grows hot, by which all hot things grow cold.

As though, then, the lump expires, the departing fire which lay hidden appears, and thereafter, as though by death, it is so cold that, though water be added, it will not burn; and what we called "living" lime, we call "extinguished."

What can seem capable of being added to this marvel? And yet it is added. For if you apply not water but oil, which is rather fuel for fire, it grows hot by no pouring over or pouring in of it.

If we read or heard of this marvel concerning some Indian stone, and it could not come into our experience[^15], surely we would either think it a lie or certainly marvel greatly. But of those things whose daily proofs are before our eyes, not less marvelous in kind, but by their very constancy they grow cheap, so that even from India itself, which is a region of the world remote from us, we have ceased to marvel at certain things which could be brought to us as wonders.

**4. 4.** Many among us have the diamond stone, and especially goldsmiths and engravers of gems, which stone is said to be conquered neither by iron nor by fire nor by any other force except the blood of a goat[^16]. But do those who have it and know it marvel as much as those to whom its power is first shown? But those to whom it is not shown perhaps do not even believe it; or if they believe, they marvel at it as unproved; and if they happen to prove it, they marvel still indeed at what is unusual, but the constancy of proving gradually withdraws the incitement to wonder.

We know the magnet stone is a marvelous snatcher of iron; which, when I first saw it, I shuddered greatly. For I saw an iron ring snatched up and suspended by the stone; then, as though it had given its own power to the iron which it had snatched and made it common, that same ring was brought near another and suspended it, and just as that first ring clung to the stone, so the second ring clung to the first; a third was added in the same way, a fourth was added; and now there hung, as it were, a chain of rings, joined by mutual circles, not interlinked within but clinging without. Who would not be astonished at this power of the stone, which not only was in it but also passed through so many suspended rings and bound them with invisible bonds?

But far more marvelous is what I learned of this stone from my brother and fellow-bishop Severus of Milevis. For he related that he himself saw how Bathanarius, once count of Africa, when the bishop was dining with him, brought out this same stone and held it under a silver plate and placed iron upon the silver; then, as he moved his hand below, with which he held the stone, so the iron above was moved, and, the silver in the middle suffering nothing, by a most rapid running and re-running the stone below was moved by the man, the iron above was snatched by the stone.

I have told what I myself saw; I have told what I heard from him, whom I believed as though I myself had seen it. Let me also say what I have read of this magnet. When a diamond is placed beside it, it does not snatch iron, and if it had already snatched it, when the diamond has come near, it presently lets it go[^17].

India sends these stones; but if we, once they are known, cease to marvel at them, how much more do those from whom they come, if they have them most readily, perhaps have them as we have lime, which, growing hot in a marvelous way by water, by which fire is usually extinguished, and not growing hot by oil, by which fire is usually kindled, because it is ready at hand for us, we do not marvel at.

**5. 1.** Nevertheless, unbelieving men, who, when we proclaim divine miracles whether past or future, which we are unable to show them for their own experience, demand of us a reason for those things—which, since we cannot render it (for they exceed the powers of the human mind), they think that what we say is false—ought themselves to render a reason for so many marvelous things which we either can see or do see. And if they perceive that this cannot be done by man, they must confess that not for this reason was anything either not, or not going to be, because a reason for it cannot be rendered, since there exist things of which likewise a reason cannot be rendered.

I do not, then, go through the many things which are committed to letters, not as done and past, but as remaining in various places; to which, if anyone wishes and is able to go, he will explore whether they are true; but I mention a few. They say that the salt of Agrigentum in Sicily, when it has been brought near fire, flows as it were in water[^18]; but when it is put in water itself, it crackles as it were in fire. Among the Garamantes there is a certain spring so cold by day that it is not drunk, so hot by night that it is not touched[^19]. In Epirus there is another spring in which torches, as in others, are extinguished when lit, but, unlike others, are lit when extinguished[^20]. The stone "asbestos" of Arcadia is so called for this reason, that, once kindled, it can no longer be extinguished[^21].

The wood of a certain Egyptian fig does not, like other woods, float in waters, but sinks; and—what is more marvelous—when it has been for some time at the bottom, it rises again thence to the surface of the water, when, soaked, it ought to be weighed down by the burden of moisture[^22]. In the land of Sodom fruits are indeed produced and come to the appearance of ripeness; but, tested by bite or pressure, they vanish into smoke and ash, the skin cracking open[^23]. The Persian stone pyrites burns the hand of him who holds it, if it is pressed too hard, on account of which it received its name from "fire"[^24]. In the same Persia is also produced the stone selenite, whose inner whiteness waxes and wanes with the moon[^25]. In Cappadocia mares too conceive by the wind, and these same offspring do not live more than three years[^26]. The island Tylos of India is preferred to other lands for this reason, that every tree which grows in it is never stripped of the covering of its leaves[^27].

**5. 2.** Of these and other innumerable marvels, which the history not of deeds done and past but of places remaining holds—but for me, occupied with something else, it would be too long to pursue them—let those unbelievers render a reason, if they can, who refuse to believe the divine letters; what else but because, not thinking these to be divine, on the ground that they contain incredible things, such as is this with which we are now dealing.

For no reasoning, they say, admits that flesh should burn and not be consumed, should feel pain and not die: great reasoners, namely, who can render a reason for all things which are established to be marvelous! Let them, then, render one of those few which we have set down, which without doubt, if they did not know they exist and we said they would be, they would believe much less than what now, when we say it will one day come, they refuse to believe us. For who of them would believe us, if, just as we say that the living bodies of men will be such as will ever burn and feel pain and yet never at any time die, so we said that in the future age there will be salt which fire would make flow as it were in water, and which water would make crackle as it were in fire; or that there will be a spring whose water in the cool of night so burns that it cannot be touched, but in the heats of day so chills that it cannot be drunk; or that there will be a stone, either one which by its heat would burn the hand of him who grasps it, or one which, kindled from anywhere, could not be extinguished at all, and the rest which, omitting innumerable others, I have for the present thought to mention?

If, then, we said these things would be in that age which is to come, and the unbelievers answered us, "if you want us to believe these things, render a reason for each," we would confess that we cannot, because by these and similar marvelous works of God the weak reasoning of mortals is overcome; yet that this reason is fixed in our minds, that the Almighty does not without reason do that for which the weak human mind cannot render a reason; and that in many things indeed it is uncertain to us what he wills, but that this is most certain, that none of those things is impossible to him which he shall have willed; and that we believe him who foretells, whom we can believe neither powerless nor lying.

Yet these reprovers of faith and exactors of reason—what do they answer to those things of which a reason cannot be rendered by man, and yet they exist and seem to be contrary to the very reason of nature? Which, if we said they would be, a reason would likewise be demanded of us by the unbelievers, just as of those things which we say will be.

And through this, since in such works of God the reason of the human heart and speech fails, as these things are not for that reason nonexistent, so neither will those things for that reason not be, since a reason for both cannot be rendered by man.

**6. 1.** Here perhaps they may answer: "These things do not exist at all, and we do not believe them; false things are said of them, false things written"; and they may add, reasoning and saying: "If such things are to be believed, believe also what is set down in those same letters: that there was or is a certain shrine of Venus, and there a candelabrum and in it a lamp burning under the open sky in such a way that no tempest, no rain extinguished it; whence, just as that stone, so this was called λύχνος ἄσβεστος, that is, 'the inextinguishable lamp.'"

They will be able to say this for this purpose, to press upon us the straits of answering; because, if we say it is not to be believed, we shall weaken those written accounts of miracles; but if we grant that it is to be believed, we shall confirm the deities of the pagans. But we, as I already said in the eighteenth book of this work[^28], have no need to believe all things which the history of the gentiles contains, since even the historians themselves disagree among themselves, as Varro says, on many points, as it were on purpose and by design; but we believe, if we will, those things which are not opposed to the books which we do not doubt we ought to believe. But concerning these places of miracles, for those things which we wish to persuade the unbelievers will be, let those suffice which we too can experience, and of which it is not difficult to find suitable witnesses.

But concerning that shrine of Venus and the inextinguishable lamp, not only are we forced into no straits, but rather a field of breadth is opened to us. For we add to this inextinguishable lamp many miracles both of human and of magical arts—that is, of arts demonic through men—and of the demons themselves through themselves; which, if we should wish to deny, we shall be at variance with the truth of the sacred letters themselves, which we believe. Either, then, in that lamp some mechanical device of the asbestos stone was contrived by human art, or it was made by magic art that men should marvel at it in that temple, or some demon under the name of Venus presented himself with such efficacy that this portent both appeared to men and remained for a long time. But demons are enticed to dwell through creatures, which not they themselves but God made—creatures delightful to them, diverse according to their diversity—not as animals by foods, but as spirits by signs that suit each one's delight, through various kinds of stones, herbs, woods, animals, songs, rites.

But that they may be enticed by men, first they themselves, by most cunning craftiness, seduce them, either by inspiring into their hearts a hidden poison, or by appearing in deceitful friendships, and make a few of them their disciples and teachers of very many. For it could not be learned, except by their first teaching, what each of them desires, what it shuns, by what name it is invited, by what it is compelled; whence magical arts and their practitioners arose.

But chiefly they possess the hearts of mortals—in which possession they especially glory—when they transform themselves into angels of light[^29]. Their deeds, then, are very many, which the more marvelous we confess them to be, the more cautiously we ought to avoid them; but for the purpose with which we are now dealing, even these very things profit us. For if these unclean demons can do these things, how much more powerful are the holy angels, how much more powerful than all these is God, who made even the angels themselves the workers of such great miracles!

**6. 2.** Wherefore, if so many and so great marvelous things, which they call μηχανήματα, are done by men using human arts upon God's creation, so that those who do not know them suppose them to be divine (whence it came about that in a certain temple, magnet stones being placed in proportion to the size in the floor and the ceiling, an iron image hung in the middle of that air between the two stones, those who did not know what was above and below thinking it hung as it were by the power of a deity[^30].

Something of which sort even in that lamp of Venus we have already said could have been made by the artificer from the asbestos stone); if the works of magicians, whom our Scripture calls "sorcerers" and "enchanters," could so exalt demons that a noble poet seemed to agree with the senses of men, saying of a certain woman who was powerful in such an art: *she promises by her songs to free the minds she will, but to send hard cares upon others, to stay the water in rivers and to turn the stars backward; she calls up the nocturnal shades: you will see the earth bellow under her feet and the mountain-ashes come down from the mountains*[^31].

how much more is God able to do those things which to unbelievers are incredible, but easy to his power; since he himself created both the force of stones and of other things, and the talents of men, who use them in marvelous ways, and angelic natures more powerful than all earthly living things—overcoming all marvels by a marvelous power and by a wisdom of working, commanding, and permitting, using all things as marvelously as he created them.

**7. 1.** Why, then, can God not bring it about that both the bodies of the dead rise again and the bodies of the damned be tormented with eternal fire—he who made the world full of innumerable miracles in heaven, on earth, in air, in waters, since the world itself is without doubt a miracle greater and more excellent than all those with which it is full?

But these with whom or against whom we are dealing, who both believe that there is a God by whom the world was made, and gods made by him, through whom the world is administered by him, and who either do not deny, or even besides proclaim, the worldly powers that work miracles—whether spontaneous, or obtained by any worship and rite, or even magical—when we set before them the marvelous force of other things which are neither rational animals nor spirits endowed with any reason, such as are those of which we have mentioned a few, are accustomed to answer: "this is a force of nature; their nature is so; these are the efficacies proper to their natures." So the whole reason why the Agrigentine salt is made by flame to flow, by water to crackle, is because this is its nature.

But this rather seems contrary to nature, which gave it not to fire but to water to dissolve salt, but to roast it by fire, not water. But *this*, they say, is the natural force of this salt, that it should suffer the contrary of these.

This reason, then, is rendered also of that Garamantic spring, where one vein is cold by day, hot by night, by either force troublesome to those who touch it; this too of that other, which, though it is cold to those who handle it and extinguishes a lit torch like other springs, yet, unlike others and marvelously, itself kindles an extinguished one; this too of the asbestos stone, which, though it has no fire of its own, yet, having received another's, so burns that it cannot be extinguished; this of the rest, which it irks me to recount, in which, although an unusual force may seem to be present contrary to nature, yet no other reason is rendered of them but to say that this is their nature.

A brief reason this surely is, I confess, and a sufficient answer. But since God is the author of all natures, why do they refuse that we render a stronger reason, when they refuse to believe something as it were impossible and demand of us a rendering of a reason, and we answer that this is the will of the Almighty God? who surely is for no other reason called "Almighty" except because he is able to do whatever he wills, who was able to create so many things which, unless they were shown or told today by witnesses who are to be believed, would surely be thought impossible—not only those most unknown among us, but also those most known which I have set down. For those things which [among us] have no witness except those whose books concerning them we read, and which were written by those who were not divinely taught and who could perhaps be deceived as men, it is permitted to anyone, without right reproach, not to believe.

**7. 2.** For neither do I wish all the things I have set down to be rashly believed, since not even by myself are they so believed as though there were no doubt about them in my thinking, except those which either I myself have experienced and which it is easy for anyone to experience; as about lime, that it grows hot in water and is cold in oil; about the magnet stone, that by some imperceptible drawing it does not move a straw and snatches iron; about the flesh of the peacock not rotting, when even that of Plato has rotted; about chaff so cold that it does not let snow melt, so warm that it compels fruits to ripen; about bright fire, that according to its brightness it whitens stones by baking them, and against this same brightness of its own blackens very many things by burning them. Such too is the fact that black spots are poured out from shining oil, that likewise black lines are imprinted from white silver, and from charcoals too, that by kindling fire they are so turned into the contrary that from most beautiful woods they become foul, fragile from hard, imputrescible from putrescible.

These things I myself know, some with many, some with all, and very many others which it would be long to insert in this book. But of those which I have set down not as experienced but as read, except about that spring where torches are both extinguished when burning and kindled when extinguished, and about the fruits of the land of Sodom as it were ripe without, smoky within, I have not been able to find any suitable witnesses from whom I might hear whether they were true.

And that spring indeed I have not found those who would say they had seen it in Epirus, but those who knew a like one in Gaul not far from the city of Grenoble. But of the fruits of the trees of Sodom not only do letters worthy of trust attest, but also so many men say they have experienced them, that I cannot doubt of this.

But the rest I so hold that I have determined them neither to be denied nor to be affirmed; but I have set down even these for this reason, that I read them among the historians of those against whom we are dealing, in order to show what sort of many things, and how many of them, with no reason rendered, they believe in the writings of their men of letters—men who do not deign to believe us, when we say that almighty God will do that which transcends their experience and sense, even with no reason rendered.

For what better and stronger reason is rendered of such things than when the Almighty is asserted to be able to do them and is said to be about to do those things which he is read to have foretold there where he foretold many other things which he is shown to have done? For he himself will do it, because he foretold that he would do those things which are thought impossible—he who promised and brought it about that incredible things should be believed by unbelieving nations.

## A twofold reason for believing is given.

**8. 1.** But if they answer that for this reason they do not believe the things we say of human bodies that will ever burn and never die, because we know that the nature of human bodies was instituted far otherwise—whence neither can that reason be rendered here which was rendered of those marvelous natures, so that it can be said, "This force is natural, this is the nature of this thing"—since we know that this is not the nature of human flesh: we have indeed something to answer from the sacred letters, namely that this very human flesh was instituted otherwise before sin, that is, so that it could never suffer death; but otherwise after sin, such as it became known in the hardship of this mortality, so that it cannot keep perpetual life; thus, then, otherwise than it is known to us, it will be instituted in the resurrection of the dead.

we do indeed have an answer from the sacred writings: this very human flesh was constituted differently before sin, so that it could never have to undergo death, and differently after sin, as it has become known in the misery of this mortality, so that it cannot retain unending life. Thus at the resurrection of the dead it will be constituted differently from the flesh as we know it.

But since they do not believe these letters, in which it is read of what sort man lived in paradise and how far he was alien from the necessity of death (which, if they believed, surely we would not have to deal so laboriously with them about the punishment of the damned which is to come): something must be brought forward from the letters of those who were most learned among them, by which it may appear that it can come to pass that anything is otherwise than it had been known before in things by the determination of its nature.

**8. 2.** There is in the books of Marcus Varro, whose title is *On the Race of the Roman People*, what I will also set down here in the same words in which it is read there: *in the heaven*, he says, *a marvelous portent appeared; for on the star of Venus, most noble, which Plautus calls 'Vesperugo'*[^32] *and Homer 'Hesperos'*[^33], *calling it most beautiful, Castor writes that so great a portent appeared, that it changed color, size, figure, course; which thing, so done, neither before nor afterward happened. This, Adrastus of Cyzicus and Dion of Naples, noble mathematicians, said happened in the reign of King Ogyges*[^34]. This Varro—so great an author—would surely not call a portent, unless it seemed to be contrary to nature. For we say that all portents are contrary to nature; but they are not. For how is that contrary to nature which happens by the will of God, since the will of so great a Creator is surely the nature of each created thing? A portent, then, happens not contrary to nature, but contrary to nature as it is known.

But who numbers the multitude of portents which is contained in the history of the gentiles? But now let us attend, in this one, to what pertains to the matter of which we are dealing. What is so arranged by the author of the nature of heaven and earth as the most orderly course of the stars? What is fixed by laws so sure and firm? And yet, when he willed who rules with the highest sovereignty and power what he created, a star, most known above the rest for size and splendor, changed color, size, figure, and—what is more marvelous—the order and law of its own course. It surely then disturbed, if any then existed, the canons of the astrologers, which they have written, as it were by an unerring computation, of the past and future motions of the stars; following which canons they dared to say that this which befell Lucifer befell neither before nor afterward.

But we in the divine Books read that even the sun itself both stood still, when the holy man Joshua son of Nun had asked this of the Lord God, until the battle begun should be ended in victory[^35], and went backward, so that to King Hezekiah fifteen years added for living[^36] might be signified by this prodigy too joined to God's promise. But these miracles too, which were granted to the merits of the saints, when those men believe them done, they attribute to magical arts. Whence is that which I mentioned above that Virgil said: *to stay the water in rivers and to turn the stars backward*[^37].

For that a river both stood still and flowed upward and downward, when the people of God, with the above-mentioned leader Joshua son of Nun, was taking its way[^38], and that this was done when the prophet Elijah crossed and afterward his disciple Elisha, we read in the sacred letters[^39]; and that the greatest star went backward in the reign of Hezekiah we have just mentioned. But what Varro wrote of Lucifer is not said there to have been granted to any man who asked it.

**8. 3.** Let not the unbelievers, then, make for themselves a fog out of the knowledge of natures, as though something could not divinely come to pass in any thing other than what they have known in its nature through their own human experience; although those things too which are known to all in the nature of things are no less marvelous, and would be astounding to all who consider them, if men were accustomed to marvel at marvels except when they are rare.

For who, when reason is consulted, does not see that, in the innumerable number of men and so great a likeness of nature, it is very marvelously the case that individuals have individual faces, so that, unless they were alike to one another, their species would not be distinguished from the other animals; and again, unless they were unlike to one another, individuals would not be distinguished from other men? Those, then, whom we confess alike, the same we find unlike. But the consideration of unlikeness is more marvelous, since the common nature seems more justly to demand likeness. And yet, because the things which are rare are themselves marvelous, we marvel much more when we find two so alike that, in distinguishing them, we err either always or frequently.

**8. 4.** But that which I said was written by Varro, although he is their historian and likewise most learned, perhaps they do not believe to have truly happened; or, because the other course of that same star did not last long, but it returned to its accustomed one, they are less moved by this example.

They have, then, another which can be shown even now and which I think ought to suffice them, by which they may be admonished, when they have observed something in some institution of nature and made it most known to themselves, that they ought not thence to prescribe to God, as though he could not turn and change it into something far other than it is known to them. The land of Sodom was surely not as it now is, but lay with a face like the rest, and flourished with equal or even greater fertility; for it is compared to the paradise of God in the divine words[^40]. But after it was touched from heaven, as their history too attests[^41] and as it is now beheld by those who come to those places, it is a horror with its prodigious soot, and its fruits enclose within an interior ash under a deceptive surface of ripeness.

Behold, it was not such, and it is such. Behold, by the Founder of natures its nature was turned by a marvelous change into this foulest diversity; and what happened after so long a time, it persists in for so long a time.

**8. 5.** As, then, it was not impossible for God to institute the natures he willed, so it is not impossible for him to change into whatever he wills the natures he instituted. Whence too there grows up that multitude of those miracles which are called *monstra, ostenta, portenta, prodigia*; which, if I should wish to recall and mention, what would be the end of this work?

*Monstra* indeed they say are so called from *monstrare* ("to show"), because by signifying something they demonstrate it; and *ostenta* from *ostendere* ("to display"); and *portenta* from *portendere* ("to portend"), that is, foreshowing; and *prodigia*, because they say forth (*porro dicant*), that is, predict future things.

But let their conjecturers see for themselves how, from these, they are either deceived, or by the instinct of those spirits whose care it is to entangle in the nets of harmful curiosity the minds of men worthy of such punishment.

They predict even true things, whether by saying many things they sometimes hit upon something of the truth.

For us, however, these things which happen as it were contrary to nature and are said to happen contrary to nature (in which manner of men's speech the Apostle too spoke, saying that the wild olive was grafted contrary to nature into the olive and made a partaker of the fatness of the olive[^42]) and are called *monstra, ostenta, portenta, prodigia*, ought to monstrate, ought to ostend or foreshow, ought to predict this: that God will do what he foretold he would do concerning the bodies of men, with no difficulty hindering, with no law of nature prescribing. But how he foretold it, I think I have shown sufficiently in the preceding book, by culling from the holy Scriptures, both New and Old, not indeed all the things pertaining to this, but those which I judged would suffice for this work.

## Their worm and their fire will be forever.

**9. 1.** What, then, God said through his prophet of the everlasting punishment of the damned will come, will altogether come: *their worm will not die and their fire will not be extinguished*[^43]. For to commend this more vehemently, the Lord Jesus too, when he put for those men the members which scandalize a man—members which one loves like his own right members, and bade them be amputated—said: *it is good for you to enter into life maimed than, having two hands, to go into Gehenna, into the inextinguishable fire, where their worm does not die and the fire is not extinguished*. Likewise of the foot: *it is good for you*, he says, *to enter lame into eternal life than, having two feet, to be cast into the Gehenna of inextinguishable fire, where their worm does not die and the fire is not extinguished*. Nor does he speak otherwise of the eye: *it is good for you to enter one-eyed into the kingdom of God than, having two eyes, to be cast into the Gehenna of fire, where their worm does not die and the fire is not extinguished*[^44].

It did not irk him to say the same words three times in one place. Whom would this repetition, and so vehement a commendation of that punishment by the divine mouth, not terrify?

**9. 2.** But both of these, namely the fire and the worm, those who wish them to pertain to the punishments of the soul, not of the body, say that those who have been separated from the kingdom of God are also burned by a grief of soul, repenting late and fruitlessly, and therefore contend that "fire" could not unfittingly be put for this burning grief; whence is that of the Apostle: *who is scandalized, and I am not on fire?*[^45]. The same worm too they think is to be understood. For it is written, they say: *as the moth the garment and the worm the wood, so grief torments the heart of a man*[^46].

But those who do not doubt that in that punishment there will be punishments both of soul and of body affirm that the body is burned by fire, but the soul is gnawed in a certain way by the worm of grief. Although this is said more credibly—since it is surely absurd that there pain either of body or of soul should be lacking—I nevertheless think it easier to say that both pertain to the body than that neither does, and that the grief of the soul was therefore left unsaid in those words of the divine Scripture because it is understood to be consequent, even if it is not said, that, as the body so feels pain, the soul too is tormented with barren repentance.

For it is read also in the old Scriptures: *the vengeance of the flesh of the impious is fire and worm*[^47]. It could have been said more briefly: "the vengeance of the impious." Why, then, was it said, *of the flesh of the impious*, except because both, that is, both fire and worm, will be the punishment of the flesh? Or if he wished to say the vengeance of the flesh for this reason, because in man that will be avenged whereby he lived according to the flesh (for on this account he will come into the second death, which the Apostle signified, saying: *for if you live according to the flesh, you will die*[^48]).

Let each choose what pleases him: either to attribute the fire to the body, the worm to the soul, this properly, that figuratively; or both properly to the body. For I have already sufficiently argued above[^49] that animals can live even in fires, in burning without consuming, in pain without death, by a miracle of the most almighty Creator; and whoever denies that this is possible to him is ignorant from whom comes whatever he marvels at in all natures.

For he is God, who made all the great and small miracles in this world which we have mentioned, and incomparably more which we have not mentioned, and enclosed them in this very world, itself the one and greatest miracle of all.

Let each one, then, choose one of the two, whichever pleases him: whether he thinks the worm too pertains properly to the body, or to the soul, by the term transferred from corporeal to incorporeal things. But which of these is true, the thing itself will more readily indicate, when there will be such great knowledge of the saints that for knowing those punishments experience will not be necessary, but that wisdom which will then be full and perfect will alone suffice also for knowing this (for now we know in part, until that which is perfect comes[^50]); provided that we in no way believe those bodies are to be such that they are affected by no pains from fire.

## The fire that will not be extinguished.

**10. 1.** Here it occurs to ask: if the fire will not be incorporeal, like grief of the soul, but corporeal, harmful by touch, so that bodies can be tormented by it, how will there also be in it a punishment of evil spirits? For it will be the same fire, assigned, namely, to the punishment of men and of demons, as Christ says: *depart from me, you cursed, into the eternal fire, which is prepared for the devil and his angels*[^51]. Unless because the demons too have certain bodies of their own, as it has seemed to learned men, from this dense and moist air, the impact of which, when a wind blows, is felt. Which kind of element, if it could suffer nothing from fire, would not burn when heated in baths. For that it may burn, it is first burned and acts as it suffers.

But if anyone asserts that the demons have no bodies, neither is this matter to be labored over with painstaking inquiry, nor to be contended over with contentious disputation. For why should we not say that, in marvelous yet true ways, even incorporeal spirits can be afflicted with the punishment of corporeal fire, if the spirits of men, themselves surely also incorporeal, both now could be enclosed in corporeal members and then will be able to be bound inseparably by the bonds of their own bodies?

The spirits of demons, then, if they have no bodies—nay, the spirits which are demons—though incorporeal, will cleave to corporeal fires to be tormented, not so that the fires themselves to which they will cleave may be inspirited by their joining and become animals which consist of spirit and body, but, as I said, by cleaving in marvelous and ineffable ways, receiving from the fires punishment, not giving life to the fires;

for that other mode too, by which spirits cleave to bodies and become animals, is altogether marvelous and cannot be comprehended by man—and this man himself is.

**10. 2.** I would indeed say that spirits will burn thus without any body of their own, as that rich man was burning in the lower regions when he said, *I am tormented in this flame*[^52], were it not that I perceive it can be fittingly answered that that flame was such as were the eyes which he lifted and saw Lazarus, such the tongue for which he longed that a little moisture be poured, such the finger of Lazarus from which he asked this be done for him; where nevertheless the souls were without bodies.

Thus, then, incorporeal too was both that flame with which he burned and that little drop which he begged—of the kind that are also the visions of those sleeping or of those in ecstasy beholding incorporeal things, which nevertheless have a likeness of bodies. For even man himself, when he is in such visions with his spirit, not his body, nevertheless then sees himself so like to his own body that he cannot at all distinguish.

But that Gehenna, which was also called a lake of fire and brimstone[^53], will be a corporeal fire and will torment the bodies of the damned, whether of both men and demons—solid those of men, airy those of demons—or only the bodies of men with their spirits, but the demons, spirits without bodies, cleaving to take punishment, not to impart life to corporeal fires. For one fire will be for both, as the Truth said[^54].

## The equity of punishments is not weighed by time.

**11.** But if certain of those against whom we defend the city of God think it unjust that for sins, however great, committed in a short time indeed, anyone should be condemned to eternal punishment—as though the justice of any law ever attended to this, that anyone be punished for as great a length of time as was the length of time in which he committed that for which he is punished: Tullius writes that there are eight kinds of punishments in the laws—fine, chains, stripes, retaliation, ignominy, exile, death, slavery. Which of these is so confined to a brief time, according to the swiftness of each sin, that it is avenged in as small a delay as the perpetration is detected to have taken—unless perhaps retaliation? For this brings it about that each one suffers what he did. Whence is that of the Law: *an eye for an eye, a tooth for a tooth*[^55]. For it can come to pass that someone lose an eye by the severity of vengeance in as brief a time as he himself, by the wickedness of his sin, took away another's.

Further, if it be reasonable to avenge with stripes a kiss fixed on another man's wife, is not he who did it in a moment of time beaten through an incomparable span of hours, and the sweetness of a brief pleasure punished with long-lasting pain? What of chains? Is anyone to be judged that he ought to be so long in them as he took to do that whereby he deserved to be bound? when a slave most justly pays years of punishment in fetters who, by a word or a most swift-passing blow, either provoked or struck his master.

But further, fine, ignominy, exile, slavery, when for the most part they are so inflicted that they are relaxed by no pardon, do they not, in the measure of this life, seem like to eternal punishments? For they cannot be eternal for this reason, that the very life which is punished by them is not stretched out into eternity; and yet the sins which are avenged by punishments of the longest time are perpetrated in the briefest time; nor has there ever existed anyone who would judge that the torments of the guilty ought to be ended as quickly as the homicide or adultery or sacrilege or any other crime was done—a thing to be measured not by length of time, but by the magnitude of iniquity and impiety.

But he who is punished with death for some great crime—do the laws reckon his punishment by the delay in which he is killed, which is very brief, and not by this, that they take him away forever from the society of the living? And what it is to remove men from this mortal city by the punishment of the first death, this it is to remove men from that immortal city by the punishment of the second death. For just as the laws of this city do not bring it about that one slain be recalled into it, so neither those of that city, that one condemned to the second death be recalled into eternal life.

How, then, is it true, they say, what your Christ says: *in what measure you have measured, it will be measured back to you*[^56], if a temporal sin is punished with eternal punishment? Nor do they attend that the same measure was said not on account of an equal span of time, but on account of the requital of evil—that is, that he who has done evils should suffer evils. Although this can properly be taken in that matter of which the Lord was speaking when he said this, that is, of judgings and condemnings. Accordingly he who judges and condemns unjustly, if he is judged and condemned justly, receives in the same measure, although not the same thing which he gave. For by judging he did, by judging he suffers; although he did by condemnation what is unjust, he suffers by condemnation what is just.

**12.** But eternal punishment seems hard and unjust to human senses for this reason, because in this weakness of dying senses there is lacking that sense of the highest and purest wisdom by which it could be felt how great a wickedness was committed in that first transgression. For the more man enjoyed God, the greater was the impiety with which he forsook God and became worthy of eternal evil, who destroyed in himself that good which could have been eternal.

Hence the whole mass of the human race is damned; since he who first admitted this was punished with that which had been rooted in him by his own stock, so that no one is freed from this just and due punishment except by mercy and undeserved grace; and so the human race is so apportioned that in some it is shown what merciful grace avails, in the rest what just retribution avails. For neither would both be shown in all; because, if all remained in the punishments of just damnation, in no one would merciful grace appear; and again, if all were transferred from darkness into light, in no one would the truth of retribution appear. In which there are for this reason far more than in that, that thus may be shown what was owed to all. And if it were rendered to all, no one would justly reprove the justice of the avenger; but because so many are freed from it, there is reason to give the greatest thanks for the gratuitous gift of the liberator.

## The Platonists think all punishments purgatorial.

**13.** The Platonists indeed, although they wish no sins to be unpunished, yet think all punishments are applied to amendment, whether inflicted by human laws or divine, whether in this life or after death, if either one is spared here or so punished that he is not corrected here.

Hence is that sentence of Maro, where, when he had said of the earthly bodies and dying members, that souls *hence fear and desire, feel pain and rejoice, nor look up to the breezes, shut up in darkness and a blind prison*, he went on and added, saying: *nay, even when with the last light life has left them* (that is, when this life has left them on the last day), *yet not*, he says, *does every evil, nor do all bodily plagues, depart wholly from the wretched, and it is deeply necessary that many things, long concrete, grow inveterate in marvelous ways. Therefore they are exercised with punishments and pay the penalties of their old evils; some are spread out, suspended empty to the winds, in others the infected guilt is washed away under a vast whirlpool or burned out with fire*[^57].

Those who think this wish there to be no punishments after death but purgatorial ones, so that, since the elements higher than the lands are water, air, fire, by some of these is cleansed, through expiatory punishments, what was contracted by earthly contagion. For air is taken in what he says, *suspended to the winds*; water in what he says, *under a vast whirlpool*; but fire is expressed by its own name, when he said, *or burned out with fire*.

But we indeed confess that even in this mortal life there are certain purgatorial punishments—not by which those are afflicted whose life is either not thereby made better or rather is thereby made worse; but they are purgatorial to those who, restrained by them, are corrected. All the other punishments, whether temporary or everlasting, as each one is to be dealt with by divine providence, are inflicted either for sins, whether past or in which he still lives who is punished, or for exercising and declaring virtues through men and angels, whether good or wicked. For even if anyone suffers some evil through another's wickedness or error, the man indeed sins who, either by ignorance or by injustice, does any evil to anyone; but God does not sin, who by a just, though hidden, judgment permits it to be done.

But temporary punishments some suffer in this life only, others after death, others both now and then, yet nevertheless before that most severe and last judgment. But not all come into the eternal punishments which are to be after that judgment who suffer temporal ones after death. For to some, what is not remitted in this age is remitted in the age to come[^58], that is, that they may not be punished with the eternal punishment of the age to come, as we have already said above.

## In life infants too pay punishments.

**14.** But very rare are those who pay no punishments in this life, but only after it. Yet that there have been some who, up to decrepit old age, felt not even the lightest little fever and led a quiet life, we ourselves both know and have heard; although the very life of mortals is wholly a punishment, because it is wholly a temptation, as the Sacred Letters resound, where it is written: *is not human life upon earth a temptation?*[^59]. For not a small punishment is that very foolishness or ignorance, which is so deservedly judged to be shunned, that by punishments full of pains children are compelled to learn any crafts or letters; and the very learning, to which by punishments they are driven, is so penal to them that sometimes they prefer to bear the very punishments by which they are compelled to learn rather than to learn.

But who would not shudder and choose to die, if it were proposed to him either to undergo death or again infancy? Which, indeed, in that it begins this light not from laughter but from weeping, in a certain way prophesies, unknowing, into what evils it has entered. They say that only Zoroaster laughed when he was born[^60], nor did that monstrous laughter portend any good to him. For he is said to have been the inventor of magical arts; which indeed could not avail him even for the vain felicity of the present life against his enemies; for by Ninus, king of the Assyrians, while he himself was king of the Bactrians, he was overcome in war[^61]. Surely what is written: *a heavy yoke is upon the sons of Adam, from the day of their coming out of their mother's womb until the day of their burial in the mother of all*[^62], must be fulfilled to such a degree that even infants themselves, freed by the laver of regeneration from the bond of original sin, by which alone they were held, suffer many evils and sometimes even endure the assaults of malignant spirits. Which suffering, far be it that it should harm them, if they end this life in that age even by that suffering growing heavier and shutting the soul out of the body.

**15.** Nevertheless, in the *heavy yoke* which *is laid upon the sons of Adam from the day of their coming out of their mother's womb until the day of their burial in the mother of all*, even this marvelous evil is found, that we may be sober and understand that this life was made penal for us from that all-too-wicked sin which was perpetrated in paradise, and that the whole of what is done with us through the New Testament pertains to nothing but the new inheritance of the new age, so that here, the pledge being received, we may in our own time attain that of which this is the pledge, but now walk in hope and, advancing from day to day, mortify by the spirit the deeds of the flesh[^63]. For *the Lord knows who are his*[^64]; and *as many as are led by the Spirit of God, these are sons of God*[^65], but by grace, not by nature.

For the only-begotten Son of God by nature became for our sake by mercy the son of man, that we, by nature sons of man, might become through him by grace sons of God. For he, remaining unchangeable, took our nature, in which he might take us, took from us; and, holding fast his own divinity, was made a partaker of our weakness, that we, changed for the better—what we are, sinners and mortal—might, by participation in him who is immortal and just, lose, and what he made good in our nature, fulfilled in the highest good, might preserve in the goodness of his nature. For just as through one man sinning we came into this so great evil[^66], so through one man, and that one also God, justifying, we shall come to that so sublime good. Nor ought anyone to be confident that he has passed from that one to this one, unless he be where there will be no temptation; unless he hold the peace which through many and various conflicts of this war—in which the flesh lusts against the spirit and the spirit against the flesh[^67]—he seeks.

## The war in which the flesh is against the spirit and the spirit against the flesh.

But this war would never be any, if human nature, through free choice, had stood firm in the rectitude in which it was made. But as it is, having refused to have a happy peace with God, it wars unhappy with itself; and though this is a miserable evil, it is nevertheless better than the earlier states of this life.

For it is better to struggle with vices than for them to dominate without any struggle. Better, I say, is war with the hope of eternal peace than captivity without any thought of liberation. We desire indeed to be free of this war too, and we are kindled by the fire of divine love to lay hold of that most orderly peace where the lower things may be subjected to the better with most firm stability. But if (which be far from us) there were no hope of so great a good, we ought rather to have preferred to remain in the trouble of this conflict than, by not resisting them, to permit the vices dominion over us.

**16.** But so great is the mercy of God toward the vessels of mercy[^68], which he prepared for glory, that even the first age of man, that is, infancy, which is subject to the flesh without any resistance, and the second, which is called boyhood, where reason has not yet taken up this struggle and lies almost under all vicious delights (because, although it can now speak and therefore seems to have passed infancy, the weakness of mind in it is not yet capable of a precept), if it has received the sacraments of the Mediator, even if it ends this life in those years, having been transferred, namely, from the power of darkness into the kingdom of Christ, not only is it not prepared for eternal punishments, but it does not even suffer any purgatorial torments after death.

For spiritual regeneration alone suffices, lest after death it harm what carnal generation contracted with death.

But when one has come to the age which already takes a precept and can be subjected to the command of law, war must be undertaken against the vices and waged sharply, lest it lead to damnable sins. And if indeed they are not yet strengthened by the habit of victories, they are more easily conquered and yield; but if they have been accustomed to conquer and command, they are overcome with laborious difficulty.

Nor does this come about truly and sincerely except by delight in true justice; and this is in the faith of Christ. For if the commanding law be present and the helping spirit be absent, by the very prohibition, desire growing and conquering, the guilt of transgression too is added to sin. Sometimes indeed the most open vices are conquered by other hidden vices, which are thought to be virtues, in which pride reigns and a certain ruinous loftiness of self-pleasing. Then, therefore, vices are to be reckoned conquered when they are conquered by the love of God, which God himself alone gives, nor otherwise than through the Mediator of God and men, the man Christ Jesus[^69], who was made a partaker of our mortality, that he might make us partakers of his divinity.

But very few are of such felicity that, from their earliest youth, they commit no damnable sins, whether in flagrant acts or in crimes or in the error of any abominable impiety, but by the great bounty of the spirit suppress whatever could dominate them by carnal delight. Most, however, after they have received the precept of the law, when they are first conquered by prevailing vices and made transgressors of it, then flee to the helping grace, by which—repenting more bitterly and fighting more vehemently—they become victors, their mind first subjected to God and so set over the flesh. Whoever, therefore, desires to escape everlasting punishments, let him not only be baptized but also justified in Christ, and so truly pass from the devil to Christ.

But let him suppose that there will be no purgatorial punishments except before that last and dreadful judgment. Yet by no means is it to be denied that even the eternal fire itself, according to the diversity of the merits of the wicked, will be lighter for some, heavier for others—whether its very force and heat be varied according to the punishment worthy of each, or whether it burn equally but be felt with unequal trouble.

## 1) Will the punishments not be everlasting for angels and men?

**17.** Now I see that I must deal with our merciful ones and dispute peaceably, who refuse to believe that everlasting punishment will be either for all those men whom the most just judge will judge worthy of the punishment of Gehenna, or for some of them, but think that, after the bounds of a certain time—longer or shorter according to the quantity of each one's sin—they are to be freed from it.

In this matter Origen was surely more merciful[^70], who believed that even the devil himself and his angels, after graver and longer punishments according to their merits, were to be rescued from those torments and associated with the holy angels. But him, both on account of this and on account of certain other things, and especially on account of the unceasing alternating blessednesses and miseries, and the interminable goings and returnings from these to those and from those to these at fixed intervals of ages, the Church not undeservedly reproved; because in this too, in which he seemed merciful, he lost mercy, by making for the saints true miseries in which they would pay penalties, and false blessednesses in which they would not have the true and secure—that is, certain without fear—joy of an everlasting good.

But far otherwise the mercy of these errs by human affection, who think that the miseries of men condemned in that judgment are temporal, but the felicity of all who either sooner or later are freed is eternal. Which opinion, if for this reason it is good and true because it is merciful, will be the better and truer the more merciful it is. Let the spring of this mercy, then, be extended and deepened even to the damned angels, who are to be freed at least after many and however long ages. Why does it flow over the whole human nature, and when it has come to the angelic nature, presently dry up?

They do not dare, however, to extend themselves further in pitying and to arrive at the liberation of the devil himself. But if anyone should dare, he surely surpasses these. And yet he is found to err the more deformedly and the more perversely against the right words of God, the more clement he seems to himself to feel.

**18. 1.** There are also some—such as in our conversations I myself have experienced—who, though they seem to venerate the holy Scriptures, are nevertheless to be reproved in their morals, and, pleading their own cause, attribute to God a much greater mercy toward the human race than these. For they say that of evil and unbelieving men it was indeed divinely foretold truly that they are worthy of it; but that, when it comes to the judgment, mercy will overcome. For the merciful God, they say, will grant them to the prayers and intercessions of his saints[^71]. For if they prayed for them when they suffered them as enemies, how much more when they see them lowly and prostrate as suppliants! For it is not to be believed, they say, that the saints will then lose the bowels of mercy, when they will be of fullest and most perfect holiness, so that those who, when they themselves were not without sin, prayed for their enemies, should then not pray for their suppliants, when they have begun to have no sin.

Or will God then not hear so many and such great sons of his, when in their so great holiness he will find no hindrance to their prayer? But the testimony of the Psalm both those who allow that unbelieving and impious men are at least tormented for a long time and afterward rescued from all evils—but rather these—say is for them, where it is read: *will God forget to be merciful, or will he hold back his mercies in his anger?*[^72].

His anger, they say, is that all unworthy of everlasting blessedness be punished, by his judging, with everlasting punishment. But if he should permit it to be either long or at all, surely, that this may be able to happen, he will hold back his mercies in his anger, which the Psalm says he will not do. For he does not say, "will he long hold back his mercies in his anger?" but shows that he will not hold them back at all.

**18. 2.** Thus, then, these wish the threat of God's judgment not to be lying, although he is to damn no one—just as we cannot call lying his threat by which he said he would overthrow the city of Nineveh[^73]; and yet, they say, what he foretold without any condition was not done. For he did not say, "Nineveh will be overthrown, if they do not repent and correct themselves"; but, without adding this, he foretold the future overthrow of that city.

This threat they think true for this reason, because God foretold what they were truly worthy to suffer, although he himself was not going to do it. For although he spared the penitent, they say, he surely was not ignorant that they would repent, and yet absolutely and definitely foretold that their overthrow would come. This, then, they say, was in the truth of his severity, because that is what they were worthy of; but in the reason of his compassion it was not, which he did not hold back in his anger, so as to spare the suppliants the punishment which he had threatened the obstinate. If, then, he spared at that time, they say, when he was about to sadden by sparing his holy prophet, how much more pitiably will he spare the suppliants, when, that he may spare, all his saints will pray!

But this which they themselves suspect in their hearts, they think the divine Scriptures for this reason kept silent: that many might correct themselves through fear of punishments either long or eternal, and that there might be those who could pray for those who had not corrected themselves.

Nor yet do they suppose that the divine words have altogether kept this silent. For to what does it pertain, they say, that it is written, *how great is the multitude of your sweetness, O Lord, which you have hidden for those who fear you*[^74], except that we may understand that for the sake of fear so great and secret a sweetness of the divine mercy was hidden?

They add too that the Apostle said for this reason: *for God has shut up all in unbelief, that he may have mercy on all*[^75], to signify that by him no one will be damned.

Nor yet do these, who think this, extend this opinion of theirs to the liberation, or to the no-damnation, of the devil and his angels; for they are moved by human mercy concerning men only, and chiefly plead their own cause, promising themselves, by a general mercy as it were of God toward the human race, a false impunity for their ruined morals; and through this those will surpass them in proclaiming God's mercy who promise this impunity even to the prince of demons and his satellites.

**19.** Likewise there are others who promise liberation from eternal punishment not even to all men at least, but only to those washed by the baptism of Christ, who are made partakers of his body, however they may have lived, in whatever heresy or impiety they may have been, on account of that which Jesus says: *this is the bread which came down from heaven, that if anyone eat of it he may not die. I am the living bread, who came down from heaven. If anyone eat of this bread, he will live forever*[^76]. From eternal death, then, they say, these must necessarily be rescued and at some time brought to eternal life.

**20.** Likewise there are those who promise this not to all who have the sacrament of the baptism of Christ and of his body, but only to Catholics, however ill they live, because they ate the body of Christ not only in the sacrament but in the thing itself, being established in his very body, of which the Apostle says: *one bread, one body, we many are*[^77]; so that, even if afterward they have fallen into some heresy or even into the idolatry of the gentiles, only because in the body of Christ, that is, in the Catholic Church, they received the baptism of Christ and ate the body of Christ, they do not die forever, but at some time attain eternal life; and all that impiety, however great it has been, avails them not unto eternity but unto the long duration and magnitude of punishments.

**21.** But there are those who, on account of that which is written, *he who perseveres to the end, this one will be saved*[^78], promise this only to those persevering in the Catholic Church, although living ill in it—to be saved, namely, through fire on account of the foundation, of which the Apostle says: *for no one can lay another foundation besides that which is laid, which is Christ Jesus. But if anyone builds upon the foundation gold, silver, precious stones, wood, hay, stubble: each one's work will be made manifest; for the day will declare it, since it will be revealed in fire, and the fire will test of what sort each one's work is. If anyone's work which he built upon remains, he will receive a reward. But if anyone's work burns, he will suffer loss; but he himself will be saved, yet so as through fire*[^79]. They say, then, that a Catholic Christian, of whatever life, has Christ in the foundation, which foundation no heresy, cut off from the unity of his body, has; and therefore, on account of this foundation, even if a Catholic Christian be of evil life, as one who built upon it wood, hay, stubble, they think he is saved through fire, that is, after the punishments of that fire he is freed, by which fire the wicked will be punished in the last judgment.

**22.** I have found also some who think that those only will burn with the eternity of that punishment who neglect to do worthy almsgiving for their sins, according to that of the apostle James: *but judgment without mercy to him who has not done mercy*[^80]. He who has done it, then, they say, although he has not changed his morals for the better but, amid his very almsgiving, has lived wickedly and basely, judgment will be for him with mercy, so that either he is smitten with no damnation, or after some time, whether short or long, he is freed from that damnation. Therefore they think that the judge himself of the living and the dead willed to mention nothing else that he would say either to those on his right, to whom he is to give eternal life, or to those on his left, whom he is to damn with eternal punishment[^81], except almsgiving, whether done or not done.

To this, they say, pertains also in the Lord's Prayer the daily petition: *forgive us our debts, as we also forgive our debtors*[^82]. For whoever forgives, by pardoning, the sin of him who has sinned against him, without doubt does almsgiving. Which thing the Lord himself so commended that he said: *for if you forgive men their sins, your Father will also forgive you your sins; but if you do not forgive men, neither will your Father, who is in heaven, forgive you*[^83]. Therefore to this kind of almsgiving too pertains what the apostle James says, that judgment will be without mercy to him who has not done mercy. Nor did the Lord say, they say, great or small, but: *your Father will forgive you your sins, if you too forgive men*. And through this they think that even to those who have lived in ruin until they close the last day of this life, by this prayer all their sins, of whatever kind and however many they have been, are daily forgiven, just as that very prayer is daily frequented—if only they remember to keep this alone, that when those who have injured them by some sin ask pardon of them, they forgive from the heart.

When, God granting, I have answered all these things, this book is to be closed.

## 1) In the Word of God the punishments will be eternal for the devil and his angels.

**23.** And first it must be asked and known why the Church could not bear the disputation of those men promising to the devil too, even after the greatest and longest punishments, purgation or indulgence. For not so many saints, learned in the sacred letters both old and new, begrudged cleansing and the blessedness of the kingdom of heaven, after punishments of whatever kind and however great, to angels of whatever kind and however many; but rather they saw that the divine sentence could not be emptied or weakened which the Lord foretold he would utter in the judgment and say: *depart from me, you cursed, into the eternal fire, which is prepared for the devil and his angels*[^84] (for thus he shows that the devil and his angels will burn in eternal fire);

and what is written in the Apocalypse: *the devil, who deceived them, was cast into the lake of fire and brimstone, where also the beast and the false prophet are; and they will be tormented day and night forever and ever*[^85]. What there was said *eternal*, here was said *forever and ever*; by which words divine Scripture is not accustomed to signify anything except that which has no end of time.

Wherefore absolutely no other and juster and clearer reason can be found why, by most true piety, it is held fixed and immovable that the devil and his angels will have no return to the justice and life of the saints, except that Scripture, which deceives no one, says that God did not spare them, and that they were thus in the meantime predamned by him, so that, thrust into the prisons of the gloom of hell[^86], they were handed over to be kept and to be punished in the last judgment, when the eternal fire will receive them, where they will be tormented forever and ever.

## [In the Word of eternity the punishments will be eternal]

But if this is so, how will either all men or some, after however long a time, be withdrawn from the eternity of this punishment, without the faith being at once enervated by which it is believed that the punishment of the demons will be everlasting? For if to those to whom it will be said, *depart from me, you cursed, into the eternal fire, which is prepared for the devil and his angels*, either all or any of them will not always be there, what cause is there why the devil and his angels are believed to be always going to be there? Or perhaps the sentence of God, which will be uttered upon both the wicked angels and men, will be true in the angels, false in men? It will plainly be so, if not what God said, but what men suspect, shall prevail. But because this cannot happen, those who desire to be free of everlasting punishment ought, while there is time, not to argue against God, but rather to obey the divine precept.

Then, what sort of thing is it to take *eternal punishment* for "a fire of long time" and *eternal life* to believe "without end," when Christ in this same place, in one and the same sentence, said both, comprehending them: *so these will go into eternal punishment, but the just into eternal life*[^87]? If both are eternal, surely either both must be understood as long with an end, or both as perpetual without end. For they are related as equal to equal—on this side eternal punishment, on that eternal life. But to say in this one and the same sense, "eternal life will be without end, eternal punishment will have an end," is exceedingly absurd. Whence, because the eternal life of the saints will be without end, eternal punishment too will surely, for those for whom it will be, have no end.

## [In the Word of God the saints will intercede and the Church will pray]

**24. 1.** But this also avails against those who, pleading their own causes, try to come against the words of God by a greater mercy as it were, so that, namely, those words are true for this reason, because the things which he said men would suffer they are worthy to suffer, not because they will suffer them. For he will grant them, they say, to the prayers of his saints, who will then pray for their enemies all the more, the more holy they surely are, and whose prayer is more efficacious and worthier of being heard by God, now having no sin at all.

Why, then, with that same most perfect holiness, and being able to obtain all things by their most pure and most merciful prayers, will they not pray also for the angels, for whom eternal fire is prepared, that God may mitigate his sentence and turn it to the better and make them aliens from that fire? Or will there perhaps be someone who presumes that this too will be, affirming that the holy angels, together with the holy men who will then be equal to the angels of God, will pray for the angels and men to be damned, that by mercy they may not suffer what by truth they deserve to suffer? Which no one of sound faith has said, no one will say. Otherwise there is no reason why even now the Church should not pray for the devil and his angels, since God her Master bade her pray for her enemies.

This cause, then, by which it comes about that the Church now does not pray for the evil angels, whom she knows to be her enemies, is the very same cause by which it will come about that, in that judgment then, even for men to be tormented with eternal fire, although she be of perfect holiness, she will not pray. For now she prays for those whom in the human race she has as enemies, because there is time for fruitful repentance. For what does she most pray for them, except that *God may grant them*, as the Apostle says, *repentance, and that they may recover from the snares of the devil, by whom they are held captive according to his will*[^88]?

Finally, if she were so certain of any persons that she even knew who those were who, although still established in this life, are nevertheless predestined to go into eternal fire with the devil, she would no more pray for them than for him. But because she is certain of no one, she prays for all her enemies, at least those established in this body; nor yet is she heard for all. For she is heard only for those who, even if they oppose the Church, are nevertheless so predestined that for them the Church is heard and they become sons of the Church.

But if any will have an impenitent heart up to death and will not be converted from enemies into sons, does the Church then pray for them, that is, for the spirits of such deceased persons? Why is it so, except because he is now counted in the part of the devil who, when he was in the body, was not transferred to Christ?

**24. 2.** The same cause, then, why prayer is not then made for men to be punished with eternal fire is the cause why prayer is made neither now nor then for the evil angels; which likewise is the cause why, although prayer is made for men, yet now too prayer is not made for the unbelieving and impious dead. For prayer is heard for certain of the dead, whether of the Church itself or of certain devout ones, but for those whose life, when they were regenerated in Christ, was neither so ill led in the body that they are judged unworthy of such mercy, nor so well that they are found not to need such mercy; just as, when the resurrection of the dead has taken place, there will not be lacking those to whom, after the punishments which the spirits of the dead suffer, mercy is imparted, so that they are not cast into eternal fire.

For it would not be truly said of certain men that it is not remitted to them either in this age or in the future[^89], unless there were those to whom, although not in this, yet it is remitted in the future. But when it has been said by the judge of the living and the dead, *come, blessed of my Father, possess the kingdom prepared for you from the foundation of the world*, and to others, on the contrary, *depart from me, you cursed, into the eternal fire, which is prepared for the devil and his angels*, and these have gone *into eternal punishment, but the just into eternal life*[^90]; it is of excessive presumption to say that the eternal punishment will not be for any of those of whom God said they would go into eternal punishment, and by the persuasion of this presumption to bring it about that of that eternal life too one despairs or doubts.

## The anger of God remains.

**24. 3.** Let no one, then, so understand the Psalm singing, *will God forget to be merciful, or will he hold back his mercies in his anger?*[^91], as to suppose that the sentence of God is true concerning good men, false concerning the wicked, or true concerning good men and wicked angels, but false concerning wicked men. For this which the Psalm says pertains to the vessels of mercy and to the sons of promise, of whom even the prophet himself was one, who, when he had said, *will God forget to be merciful, or will he hold back his mercies in his anger?*, at once subjoined: *and I said: now I have begun; this is the change of the right hand of the Most High*[^92]. He surely expounded what he had said, *will he hold back his mercies in his anger?* For the anger of God is even this mortal life, where man has been made like to vanity, and his days pass away like a shadow[^93].

Yet in this anger God does not forget to be merciful, making his sun rise upon the good and the wicked and raining upon the just and the unjust[^94], and so does not hold back his mercies in his anger; and most of all in this which this Psalm expressed, saying, *now I have begun; this is the change of the right hand of the Most High*, since in this very most laborious life, which is the anger of God, he changes the vessels of mercy for the better, although his anger still remains in the misery of this corruption, because not even in his very anger does he hold back his mercies.

Since, then, in this way the truth of that divine song is fulfilled, it is not necessary that it be understood also there, where those not belonging to the city of God will be punished with everlasting punishment. But to those who please to extend that sentence even to those torments of the impious, let them at least so understand it that, the anger of God remaining in them—which is foretold in the eternal punishment—he does not hold back his mercies in this his anger, and brings it about that they are tormented not with so great an atrocity of punishments as they are worthy of.

Not that they either never undergo those punishments or at some time end them, but that they suffer them milder and lighter than their merits are. So both the anger of God will remain, and in this his anger he will not hold back his mercies. This, however, I do not for this reason confirm, because I do not resist it.

**24. 4.** But for the rest, those who think it was said threateningly rather than truthfully, *depart from me, you cursed, into the eternal fire*[^95]; and: *these will go into eternal punishment*[^96]; and: *they will be tormented forever and ever*[^97]; and: *their worm will not die and the fire will not be extinguished*[^98]; and the rest of this kind—not so much I as the divine Scripture itself most plainly and most fully refutes and confounds them. For the Ninevites did penance in this life[^99] and therefore fruitfully, as those sowing in this field in which God willed it to be sown with tears, what afterward might be reaped with joy[^100]; and yet who will deny that what the Lord foretold concerning them was fulfilled, unless he attends too little how God overthrows sinners not only being angry but also being merciful? For sinners are overthrown in two ways: either as the Sodomites, that the men themselves be punished for their sins, or as the Ninevites, that the very sins of the men be destroyed by repenting. It was done, then, what God foretold: the Nineveh which was evil was overthrown, and the good which was not was built up[^101]. For, the walls and houses standing, the city was overthrown in its ruined morals. And so, although the prophet was saddened because what those men feared would come at his prophesying did not come, yet it was done what was foretold in God's foreknowledge, since he who foretold it knew how it was to be fulfilled for the better.

## There will be mercy for those who have hoped.

**24. 5.** But that these perversely merciful ones may know to what that pertains which is written, *how great is the multitude of your sweetness, O Lord, which you have hidden for those who fear you!* let them read what follows: *but you have perfected it for those who hope in you*[^102].

What is: *you have hidden for those who fear, you have perfected for those who hope*, except that to those who wish to establish their own justice which is in the law, through fear of punishments, the justice of God is not sweet[^103], because they do not know it? For they have not tasted it. For they hope in themselves, not in him, and therefore the multitude of God's sweetness is hidden from them; because they fear God indeed, but with that servile fear which is not in love, since perfect love casts out fear[^104].

Therefore for those who hope in him he perfects his sweetness, inspiring into them his love, so that with a chaste fear—not the one which love casts out, but the one which remains forever and ever—when they glory, they may glory in the Lord[^105]. For the justice of God is Christ, *who was made for us*, as the Apostle says, *wisdom from God and justice and sanctification and redemption, that, as it is written: he who glories, let him glory in the Lord*[^106]. This justice of God, which grace gives without merits, those do not know who wish to establish their own justice, and therefore they are not subject to the justice of God, which is Christ[^107]. In which justice is the great multitude of the sweetness of God, on account of which it is said in the Psalm: *taste and see how sweet is the Lord*[^108]. And tasting this in this pilgrimage, not taking it to fullness, we rather hunger and thirst for it, that afterward we may be filled with it, when we shall see him as he is[^109], and there will be fulfilled what is written: *I shall be filled when your glory is manifested*[^110]. Thus Christ perfects the great multitude of his sweetness for those who hope in him.

But further, if God hides his sweetness—which they suppose—for those who fear him, the sweetness by which he is not going to damn the impious, so that, not knowing this and fearing to be damned, they may live rightly and so there may be those who pray for those not living rightly:

how does he perfect it for those who hope in him, since indeed, as they dream, by this sweetness he is not going to damn those who do not hope in him?

Let us therefore seek that sweetness of his which he perfects for those who hope in him, not that which he is thought to perfect for those who despise and blaspheme him. In vain, then, does a man inquire after this once the body is gone, when he has neglected to obtain it for himself while he was in the body.

## ... who shall have been found worthy of mercy.

**24. 6.** That apostolic saying too, *for God has consigned all to unbelief, that he may have mercy upon all*[^111], was not said because he will damn no one, but it is clear from what comes before in what sense it was said. For when the Apostle was speaking of the Jews who would later believe, addressing the gentiles, to whom of course he was writing as already believers, he said: *just as you were once disobedient to God but now have received mercy because of their disobedience, so they have now been disobedient in order that by the mercy shown to you they also may receive mercy*[^112]. Then he added the very words by which these people flatter themselves in their error, saying: *for God has consigned all to unbelief, that he may have mercy upon all*. All of whom, if not those of whom he was speaking, as though saying, "both you and them"?

God, then, has consigned both gentiles and Jews, *whom he foreknew and predestined to be conformed to the image of his Son*[^113], all of them to unbelief, so that, confounded by repenting of the bitterness of their unbelief and turned by believing to the sweetness of God's mercy, they might cry out the words of the Psalm: *How abundant is the multitude of your sweetness, O Lord, which you have hidden for those who fear you, but have perfected*, not in themselves, but *in you!*[^114] And so he has mercy upon all the vessels of mercy. What does *all* mean? It means both those whom he predestined, called, justified, and glorified from among the gentiles and those from among the Jews—not all human beings, but of all these he will damn none.

## Neither sharing in the sacraments nor remaining within the Church outweighs an immoral life

**25. 1.** But let us now answer also those who promise deliverance from eternal fire not, like these last, to the devil and his angels, nor even to all human beings, but only to those who have been washed in the baptism of Christ and made partakers of his body and blood, however they may have lived and in whatever heresy or impiety they may have been. But the Apostle contradicts them, saying: *now the works of the flesh are plain: fornication, impurity, licentiousness, idolatry, sorcery, enmity, strife, jealousy, anger, dissension, heresy, envy, drunkenness, carousing, and the like; I warn you, as I warned you before, that those who do such things shall not possess the kingdom of God*[^115].

This apostolic statement is surely false if such people, after however long a time, will be set free and possess the kingdom of God.

But since it is not false, they will assuredly not possess the kingdom of God. And if they are never to be in possession of the kingdom of God, they will be held in eternal punishment; for there is no middle place where one would not be in punishment, if he has not been set in the kingdom.

**25. 2.** Therefore how we are to take what the Lord Jesus said—*this is the bread that came down from heaven, that if anyone eats of it he may not die. I am the living bread that came down from heaven; if anyone eats of this bread, he will live forever*[^116]—is rightly asked. And this understanding is taken away from the people whom we are now answering by those whom we must answer next; these latter are the ones who promise this deliverance not to all who have the sacrament of baptism and of the body of Christ, but only to Catholics, however badly they live, because, they say, such people have eaten the body of Christ not by the sacrament alone but in reality itself, being set within his very body—the body of which the Apostle says: *we who are many are one bread, one body*[^117].

He, then, who is in the unity of his body—that is, in the structure of Christian members, whose body's sacrament the faithful are accustomed to receive in communion from the altar—he is the one truly to be said to eat the body of Christ and to drink the blood of Christ. And for this reason heretics and schismatics, separated from the unity of this body, can receive the same sacrament, but not to their own benefit; indeed, even to their harm, so that they are judged the more gravely, or set free the more slowly. For they are not in that bond of peace which is signified by that sacrament.

**25. 3.** But again, even those who rightly understand that one is not to be said to eat the body of Christ who is not in the body of Christ do not rightly promise deliverance at some point from the punishment of eternal fire to those who slip away from the unity of that body into heresy or even into the superstition of the gentiles. First, because they ought to consider how intolerable, and how far astray from sound doctrine, it is that many—indeed almost all—who founded impious heresies by going out from the Catholic Church and became heresiarchs should have a better case than those who, never having been Catholics, fell into their snares, if it is this that frees those heresiarchs from everlasting punishment: that they were baptized in the Catholic Church and first received the sacrament of the body of Christ within the true body of Christ.

For surely the deserter of the faith, who from a deserter has become its assailant, is worse than one who never deserted what he never held. Second, because the Apostle confronts these people too with the same words, and after listing those works of the flesh foretells with the same truth: *that those who do such things shall not possess the kingdom of God*[^118].

**25. 4.** Hence even those who do persevere to the end as it were in the communion of the Catholic Church ought not to feel secure in ruinous and damnable ways of life, looking to what is said—*he who endures to the end will be saved*[^119]—while through the wickedness of their life they desert the very righteousness of life, which Christ is for them, whether by fornicating or by perpetrating in their own bodies the other defilements of disgraceful acts that the Apostle did not even wish to name, or by dissolving in the foulness of licentiousness, or by doing any other of those things of which he says: *that those who do such things shall not possess the kingdom of God*[^120]. And for this reason whoever does such things will be nowhere but in everlasting punishment, since they cannot be in the kingdom of God. For by persevering in these things to the end of this life they are by no means to be said to have persevered in Christ to the end, because to persevere in Christ is to persevere in his faith—which *faith*, as the same Apostle defines it, *works through love*[^121]; and *love*, as he himself says elsewhere, *works no evil*[^122].

These people, then, are not to be said to eat the body of Christ either, since they are not to be counted among the members of Christ. For, to pass over other matters, they cannot at one and the same time be both members of Christ and members of a harlot[^123]. Finally, he himself says: *he who eats my flesh and drinks my blood abides in me, and I in him*[^124]. He shows what it is to eat the body of Christ and drink his blood not merely by way of the sacrament but in very truth; for this is to abide in Christ, so that Christ also abides in him. For he said this as though he were saying, "He who does not abide in me, and in whom I do not abide, let him not say or suppose that he eats my body or drinks my blood." Those, therefore, do not abide in Christ who are not his members. And those are not members of Christ who make themselves members of a harlot, unless they cease to be so by repenting of that evil and return to this good through reconciliation.

## Who will be saved as though through fire

**26. 1.** But, they say, Catholic Christians have Christ in the foundation, and from his unity they have not departed, even though upon this foundation they have built a thoroughly evil life, as it were wood, hay, stubble[^125]; and so the right faith, through which Christ is the foundation, will be able at some point to save them from the perpetuity of that fire, though with loss, since the things built upon it will be burned away. Let the apostle James answer them briefly: *if someone says he has faith but has not works, can his faith save him?*[^126]. And who is it, they say, of whom the apostle Paul says: *he himself will be saved, but only as though through fire*[^127]? Let us at the same time inquire who this man is; yet that it is not this kind of person is most certain, lest we set the statements of two Apostles at odds, if one says, "Even if a man has had evil works, his faith will save him through fire," while the other says: *if he has not works, can his faith save him?*

**26. 2.** We shall find, then, who can be saved through fire if we first find what it is to have Christ in the foundation. So that we may grasp this as quickly as possible from the comparison itself: nothing in a building is placed before the foundation; whoever, then, has Christ in his heart in such a way that he does not place before him earthly and temporal things—not even those that are lawful and permitted—has Christ as his foundation. But if he does place them before him, then even though he may seem to have the faith of Christ, still Christ is not the foundation in one to whom such things are preferred; how much more so if, despising the precepts of salvation, he commits unlawful acts, he is convicted of having put Christ not before but behind, having set him in second place when he commanded or permitted, while contrary to his commands or permissions he chose to satisfy his own lust through shameful deeds!

If, then, a Christian loves a harlot and, cleaving to her, becomes one body with her[^128], he no longer has Christ in the foundation. But if a man loves his wife, and loves her according to Christ[^129], who would doubt that Christ is in his foundation? But if he loves her according to this world, in a carnal way, in the disease of concupiscence, like the gentiles who do not know God[^130], even this the Apostle—indeed, Christ through the Apostle—allows by way of concession.

Even such a man, then, can have Christ in the foundation[^131]. For if he places nothing of such affection and pleasure before him, then, though he build wood, hay, stubble, Christ is the foundation, and for this reason he will be saved through fire. For the fire of tribulation will burn away delights of this kind and earthly loves, which, on account of the marriage bond, are not indeed damnable; and to this fire belong bereavements and whatever calamities take such things away. And for this reason that building will be ruinous to the one who built it, because he will not have what he built upon it, and he will be tormented by the loss of those things in whose enjoyment he indeed rejoiced; but through this fire he will be saved by the merit of the foundation, because, even if it were put to him by a persecutor whether he would rather have that thing or Christ, that thing would not be preferred to Christ.

See, in the Apostle's words, the man building upon the foundation gold, silver, precious stones: *he who is unmarried*, he says, *thinks about the things of God, how to please God*[^132]. See another building wood, hay, stubble: *but he who is joined in marriage*, he says, *thinks about the things of the world, how to please his wife*[^133]. *The work of each one will be made manifest; for the day will declare it* (the day, that is, of tribulation), *since it will be revealed in fire*, he says[^134]. (He calls this same tribulation fire, as is read elsewhere: *the furnace tests the potter's vessels, and the trial of tribulation tests righteous men*[^135].) *And the fire will test what sort of work each one's is*[^136]. *If anyone's work remains* (for what remains is that of the one who thinks about the things of God, how to please God), *he will receive a reward for what he has built up* (that is, he will gain from what he thought about); *but if anyone's work burns up, he will suffer loss* (since he will not have what he had loved), *but he himself will be saved* (because no tribulation removed him from the stability of that foundation), *but only as though through fire*[^137] (for what he had not without enticing love he does not lose without burning grief).

See, as it seems to me, the fire has been found that damns none of them, but enriches the one, brings loss to the other, and tests them both.

**26. 3.** But if we wished to take that fire in this passage to be the fire of which the Lord will say to those on his left, *depart from me, you cursed, into the eternal fire*[^138]—so that among them are believed to be also those who build upon the foundation wood, hay, stubble, and the merit of the good foundation frees them from that fire after a time apportioned for their evil deserts—

whom shall we suppose those on the right to be, to whom it will be said, *come, you blessed of my Father, possess the kingdom prepared for you*[^139], if not those who built upon the foundation gold, silver, precious stones?

But into that fire of which it was said, *but only as though through fire*[^140], if it is to be understood in this way, both groups must be sent—those on the right, that is, and those on the left. For by that fire both are to be tested, the fire of which it was said: *for the day will declare it, since it will be revealed in fire, and the fire will test what sort of work each one's is*[^141].

If, then, the fire will test both, so that if anyone's work remains—that is, has not been consumed by the fire—he may receive a reward for what he built up, but if anyone's work burns up he may suffer loss: then surely that fire is not the eternal one. For into that one only those on the left will be sent, in their final and perpetual damnation, whereas this fire tests those on the right. But it tests some of them in such a way that it does not burn up and consume the building it finds to have been constructed by them upon Christ the foundation; and others otherwise, that is, so that what they built up burns and they suffer loss thereby, but are saved, because they held Christ, firmly set in the foundation, with surpassing love.

But if they are to be saved, surely they will both stand on the right and hear with the rest, *come, you blessed of my Father, possess the kingdom prepared for you*, and not on the left, where those will be who will not be saved and who will therefore hear, *depart from me, you cursed, into the eternal fire*.

For no one will be saved from that fire, since all those will go into eternal punishment, where their worm does not die and their fire is not quenched, by which they will be tormented day and night forever and ever.

**26. 4.** To be sure, after the death of this body, until one comes to that last day of damnation and reward which is to be after the resurrection of bodies, if in this interval of time the spirits of the dead are said to undergo a fire of this kind—a fire that those do not feel who, in the life of this body, did not have such ways of life and loves that their wood and hay and stubble should be consumed, but that others do feel who carried with them buildings of this kind, whether there only, or both here and there, or here precisely so that they may not there find the transitory tribulation's fire consuming worldly things which, though pardonable, escape damnation—I do not contradict it, because perhaps it is true.

For even the very death of the flesh, which was conceived by the perpetration of the first sin, can belong to this tribulation, so that the time which follows it is felt by each person according to his own building. Persecutions too, by which the martyrs were crowned and which any Christians suffer, test both kinds of building like fire, and consume some buildings together with the builders themselves, if they do not find Christ in them as foundation; others without the builders, if they do find him, because, though with loss, they themselves will be saved; but others they do not consume, because they find such things as remain forever.

There will also be, at the end of the age, a tribulation in the time of Antichrist, such as never was before[^142]. How many buildings there will be then, whether of gold or of hay, upon that best foundation, which is Christ Jesus, so that that fire may test both and bring joy from some and loss from others, yet destroy neither group in whom it finds these things, on account of the stable foundation!

But whoever sets before Christ—I do not say a wife, in whose very bodily union he uses her for carnal pleasure, but the very names of devotion that are foreign to delights of this kind—loving them carnally in human fashion, does not have Christ in the foundation and therefore will not be saved through fire, but will not be saved at all, because he cannot be with the Savior, who, speaking most plainly about this matter, said: *he who loves father or mother more than me is not worthy of me; and he who loves son or daughter above me is not worthy of me*[^143]. But he who loves these kindred so carnally that he yet does not set them before Christ the Lord, and would rather lack them than Christ, if he should be brought to this point of trial, will be saved through fire, because from their loss grief must necessarily burn him to the degree that his love had clung to them. Further, he who has loved father, mother, sons, daughters according to Christ, so as to take counsel for them toward obtaining his kingdom and cleaving to him, or loves this in them, that they are members of Christ—far be it that such love should be found among the wood, hay, and stubble to be consumed; rather it will be reckoned to the building of gold, silver, and gems. And how can he love more than Christ those whom he loves precisely for the sake of Christ?

## The difficulty concerning almsgiving

**27. 1.** It remains to answer those who say that only those will burn in eternal fire who neglect to give worthy alms for their sins, on account of what the apostle James says: *for judgment is without mercy to one who has shown no mercy*[^144]. He, then, who has shown mercy, they say, even though he has not corrected his ruinous ways but has lived wickedly and basely amid his very almsgiving, will have judgment with mercy, so that either he is not damned at all or, after some time, he is freed from the final damnation. And for no other reason do they suppose that Christ will make the distinction between those on the right and those on the left solely on the score of the choosing or the neglecting of almsgiving, sending some into the kingdom and others into eternal punishment.

And in order to imagine that their daily sins, which they never cease at all to commit, of whatever kind and however many, can be forgiven through alms, they try to take the prayer that the Lord himself taught both as their advocate and as their witness. For just as there is no day, they say, on which this prayer is not said by Christians, so there is no daily sin of whatever kind which is not forgiven through it, when we say, *forgive us our debts*, if we take care to do what follows: *as we also forgive our debtors*[^145]. For the Lord did not say, they say, "If you forgive men their sins, your Father will forgive you your small daily sins," but he said, *he will forgive you your sins*[^146]. Of whatever kind, therefore, or however many they may be, even if they are committed daily and life does not depart from them changed for the better, they presume that these can be remitted to them through the alms of a pardon that is not denied.

**27. 2.** But it is well that these people remind us that worthy alms are to be given for sins; for if they were to say that alms of any sort could obtain the divine mercy for sins both daily and great and committed in however great a habit of crimes, so that daily remission would follow, they would see that they were saying something absurd and ridiculous. For they would thus be forced to admit that it could happen that a most wealthy man, by spending ten little coins a day on alms, might redeem murders and adulteries and every wicked deed.

But if it is most absurd and most insane to say this, then surely, if it be asked what alms are worthy for sins—the alms of which that forerunner of Christ also spoke: *bring forth therefore fruits worthy of repentance*[^147]—it will beyond doubt be found that those do not give them who pierce their own life right up to death by the perpetration of daily crimes. First, because in taking away other people's goods they plunder far more, out of which, by giving the merest trifle to the poor, they suppose they are feeding Christ to this end: believing that they have bought from him a license for evildoing, or rather are daily buying it, they commit such damnable acts in security. And if for a single crime they were to distribute all their goods to the needy members of Christ, unless they ceased from such deeds by having the love that does not behave perversely, it could be of no profit to them[^148].

He, then, who gives worthy alms for his sins should begin to give them first to himself. For it is unworthy that one should fail to do for himself what he does for his neighbor, when he hears God saying, *you shall love your neighbor as yourself*[^149]; and likewise hears, *have mercy on your own soul, pleasing God*[^150]. This alms—that is, that he please God—if he does not give it to his own soul, how is he to be said to give worthy alms for his sins? To this end too it is written: *he who is evil to himself, to whom will he be good?*[^151]. Alms indeed help our prayers; and assuredly we must look to what we read: *my son, you have sinned; do not do so again, but pray about your past sins that they may be forgiven you*[^152]. For this reason, then, alms are to be given, so that, when we pray about past sins, we may be heard—not so that, persevering in them, we may believe we are purchasing for ourselves through alms a license for doing evil.

**27. 3.** Now the Lord foretold that he would reckon to those on the right the alms they had given, and to those on the left those they had not given, in order to show by this how much alms avail for blotting out former sins—not for committing everlasting ones with impunity. But those who refuse to change their life from the habit of crimes for the better are not to be said to give such alms.

For in this too, when he says, *as you did it not to one of the least of these, you did it not to me*[^153], he shows that they fail to do it even when they suppose they are doing it. For if they were to give bread to a hungry Christian because he is a Christian, they would surely not deny to themselves the bread of righteousness, which is Christ himself; since God attends not to the person to whom a thing is given, but to the spirit in which it is given.

He, then, who loves Christ in a Christian offers him alms in the spirit in which one approaches Christ, not in the spirit in which one wishes to depart unpunished from Christ. For each one deserts Christ all the more, the more he loves what Christ disapproves. For what does it profit anyone to be baptized if he is not justified? Did not he who said, *unless one is born again of water and the Spirit, he will not enter the kingdom of God*[^154], also say, *unless your righteousness exceeds that of the scribes and Pharisees, you will not enter the kingdom of heaven*[^155]? Why is it that, fearing the former, many hurry to be baptized, while, not fearing the latter, few care to be justified?

Therefore, just as the one who says to his brother, *you fool*, does not say it to his brother, when in saying it he is set not against his brotherhood but against his sin (otherwise he will be liable to the fire of hell[^156]); so, on the contrary, the one who offers alms to a Christian does not offer them to a Christian, if he does not love Christ in him; and he does not love Christ who refuses to be justified in Christ. And just as, if anyone is overtaken by this fault, so that he says to his brother, *you fool*—that is, reviling him unjustly, not wishing to take away his sin—it is too little for him, to redeem this, to give alms, unless he also adds the remedy of reconciliation that follows there (for there it follows: *if therefore you are offering your gift at the altar and there remember that your brother has something against you, leave your gift there before the altar and go first, be reconciled to your brother, and then come and offer your gift*[^157]); so it is too little to give alms of whatever size for any crime and to remain in the habit of crimes.

**27. 4.** But the daily prayer that Jesus himself taught, from which it is also called the Lord's Prayer, does indeed blot out daily sins, when daily it is said, *forgive us our debts*, and what follows is not only said but also done: *as we also forgive our debtors*[^158]; but it is said because sins are committed, not so that they may be committed because it is said. For through this prayer the Savior wished to show us that, however righteously we may live in the darkness and weakness of this life, we are not without sins, for the forgiveness of which we ought to pray, and to forgive those who sin against us, so that we too may be pardoned.

And so the Lord did not say, *if you forgive men their sins, your Father also will forgive you your sins*[^159], in order that, relying on this prayer, we should commit daily crimes in security—whether through a power by which we would not fear human laws, or through a cunning by which we would deceive men themselves; but in order that through it we might learn not to think ourselves without sins, even if we were free of grave offenses; just as God admonished the priests of the old Law of this very thing concerning the sacrifices which he commanded them to offer first for their own sins, then for the people's[^160].

For the very words of so great a Master and Lord of ours must be watchfully considered. For he did not say, "If you forgive men their sins, your Father also will forgive you sins of whatever kind," but he said, *your sins*. He was teaching a daily prayer and was of course speaking to disciples who had been justified. What, then, is *your sins*, if not "the sins without which not even you will be, who have been justified and sanctified"? Where, then, those who through this prayer seek an occasion for daily perpetrating crimes say that the Lord meant great sins also, since he did not say "he will forgive you small sins," but *your sins*: there we, considering to what sort of people he was speaking and hearing the phrase *your sins*, ought to think of nothing other than small sins, since the sins of such people were no longer great.

Yet even those great sins themselves, from which one must wholly withdraw with ways of life changed for the better, are not forgiven to those who pray unless what is said there is done: *as we also forgive our debtors*. For if the least sins, without which not even the life of the righteous exists, are not otherwise remitted, how much more do those entangled in many and great crimes, even if they now cease to perpetrate them, obtain no pardon, if they are inexorable in forgiving others whatever each may have sinned against them, since the Lord says, *but if you do not forgive men, neither will your Father forgive you*[^161].

For to this same end avails what the apostle James also says, that there will be *judgment without mercy to one who has shown no mercy*[^162]. For there ought to come to mind also that servant whose lord released him from a debt of ten thousand talents[^163], which he afterward ordered him to repay, because he himself had no mercy on his fellow servant, who owed him a hundred denarii. In those, then, who are children of the promise and vessels of mercy[^164], what the same Apostle adds in consequence avails: *but mercy triumphs over judgment*[^165]; since even those righteous ones who lived in such holiness that they may also receive others into eternal dwellings, having made them friends from the mammon of iniquity[^166], were themselves, that they might be such, freed by mercy by him who justifies the ungodly, reckoning the reward according to grace, not according to debt[^167]. For among their number is the Apostle, who says: *I have received mercy, so that I might be faithful*[^168].

**27. 5.** But of those who are received by such people into eternal dwellings, it must be admitted that they are not endowed with such ways of life that their own life could suffice to free them without the support of the saints, and for this reason mercy triumphs over judgment in them far more abundantly. Yet no one is on that account to be supposed, however wicked, in no way changed to a good or even more tolerable life, to be received into eternal dwellings because he obliged the saints out of the mammon of iniquity—that is, out of money or riches that had been ill acquired, or even if well acquired, yet not true riches, but those which iniquity supposes to be riches, since it does not know what the true riches are in which those abounded who also receive others into eternal dwellings.

There is, then, a certain manner of life neither so bad that to those who live it the bounty of almsgiving—by which the need of the righteous too is sustained, and friends are made who may receive them into eternal dwellings—is of no profit toward attaining the kingdom of heaven, nor so good that it suffices of itself for them to attain so great a blessedness, unless through the merits of those whom they have made friends they obtain mercy.

(I am, moreover, accustomed to marvel that this saying of the Lord is found even in Virgil, where the Lord says: *make for yourselves friends from the mammon of iniquity, that they too may receive you into eternal dwellings*[^169]; to which this saying too is most similar: *he who receives a prophet in the name of a prophet will receive a prophet's reward; and he who receives a righteous man in the name of a righteous man will receive a righteous man's reward*[^170]. For when that poet was describing the Elysian fields, where they think the souls of the blessed dwell, he placed there not only those who could reach those seats by their own merits, but added and said: *and those who by deserving well made others mindful of them*[^171]—that is, those who deserved well of others and, by deserving well, made them mindful of themselves; exactly as if they were saying to them what is often on a Christian's lips when, in humility, one commends himself to each of the saints and says, "Be mindful of me," and by deserving well brings it about that this can be so.)

But what this manner of life may be, and what those sins are which so hinder the attainment of the kingdom of God that they nevertheless obtain pardon through the merits of holy friends, is most difficult to discover, most dangerous to define. I, at any rate, up to this time, although I have busied myself with it, have not been able to arrive at their investigation.

And perhaps they remain hidden for this reason: that the zeal to make progress in guarding against all sins may not grow lazy. For if it were known what or what sort of offenses they are, for which—even when they persist and are not removed by progress in a better life—the intercession of the righteous is to be sought and hoped for, human sloth would wrap itself up in them in security, and would take no care to be unwound from such entanglements by the exertion of any virtue, but would seek only to be freed by the merits of others, whom it had made friends for itself from the mammon of iniquity by the bounty of almsgiving.

But now, while the measure of pardonable iniquity remains unknown, even though it persist, surely both the zeal to make progress toward better things is applied more watchfully by praying and pressing on, and the care to make the saints friends from the mammon of iniquity is not despised.

**27. 6.** But this deliverance, which comes about either through each one's own prayers or through the intercession of the saints, brings it about that one is not sent into eternal fire, not that, once he has been sent there, he is plucked out after some length of time. For even those who think that what is written—that the good earth bears abundant fruit, some thirtyfold, some sixtyfold, some a hundredfold[^172]—is to be understood in this way: that the saints, according to the diversity of their merits, free, some of them thirty people, others sixty, others a hundred—even these are accustomed to suppose this will happen on the day of judgment, not after the judgment.

When a certain man saw, on the basis of this opinion, people most perversely promising themselves impunity—since in this way all might seem able to share in deliverance—he is reported to have answered most elegantly that it is better, rather, to live well, so that each one may be found among those who are to intercede for the deliverance of others,

lest they be so few that, once each of them quickly reaches his own number—of thirty, or sixty, or a hundred—many should remain who could not then be plucked out of their punishments by the intercession of those people, and among them be found whoever, with the most empty rashness, promised himself the hope of another's fruit.

Let it suffice that I have answered in this way those who do not despise the authority of the sacred Scriptures, which we hold in common, but who, by understanding them badly, suppose that what is to come is not what those Scriptures say, but rather what they themselves want. And so, this answer having been given, we bring the book to an end, as we promised.
