# Book XII: God created the good angels, and man once in time

**1. 1.** Before I speak of the institution of man, where the origin of the two cities, as far as concerns the race of rational mortals, will appear, just as it already seems to have appeared in the angels in the preceding book[^1], I see that certain things must first be said by me concerning the angels themselves, by which it may be shown, as far as we can, how it is not unfitting or incongruous to say that there is a society of men and angels, so that there are not four cities (two, namely, of angels and as many of men), but rather two cities—that is, societies—deservedly to be said to exist, one constituted in the good, the other in the evil, not only of angels but also of men.

## [The good and the evil angels differ not by nature but by vice]

**1. 2.** It is not lawful to doubt that the mutually contrary desires of the good and the evil angels arose not from diverse natures and principles—since God, the good author and founder of all substances, created both—but from wills and cravings: while some persist constantly in the good common to all, which is God himself to them, and in his eternity, truth, and love; others, delighting rather in their own power, as though they were their own good to themselves, flowed down from the higher good, common to all and beatifying, to their own things, and, having the pride of elation in place of the most exalted eternity, the cunning of vanity in place of most certain truth, the pursuits of faction in place of undivided love, were made proud, deceitful, and envious.

The cause, therefore, of the blessedness of the former is to cleave to God; wherefore the cause of the misery of the latter is to be understood from the contrary, which is not to cleave to God. For which reason, if, when it is asked why the former are blessed, it is rightly answered, Because they cleave to God; and when it is asked why the latter are wretched, it is rightly answered, Because they do not cleave to God: there is no good of the rational or intellectual creature, by which it is blessed, except God.

Thus, although not every creature can be blessed (for wild beasts, wood, stones, and anything of this kind do not attain or receive this gift), that creature which can be so cannot be so from itself, because it was created from nothing, but from him by whom it was created. For by attaining him it is blessed, by losing him it is wretched. But he who is blessed not by another good but by himself, therefore cannot be wretched, because he cannot lose himself.

**1. 3.** We say, therefore, that there is no unchangeable good except the one true and blessed God; but that the things which he made are indeed good, because they are from him, yet changeable, because they were made not of him, but of nothing. Although, therefore, they are not the highest goods, since God is a greater good than they, yet those changeable goods are great which can cleave, that they may be blessed, to the unchangeable good, which is so far their good that without it they must of necessity be wretched.

Nor are the other things in this universe of creation better on this account, that they cannot be wretched; for it is not to be said that the other members of our body are better than the eyes because they cannot be blind. But just as a sentient nature, even when it is in pain, is better than a stone which can in no way be in pain, so the rational nature is more excellent even when wretched than that which is destitute of reason or sense, and therefore into which misery does not fall.

Since this is so, for this nature, which was created in such excellence that, although it is itself changeable, it nevertheless attains blessedness by cleaving to the unchangeable good, that is, the most high God, and does not satisfy its own need unless it is indeed blessed, and nothing but God suffices to satisfy it—surely not to cleave to him is a vice. But every vice harms a nature, and through this is contrary to nature. From that nature, therefore, which cleaves to God, this one differs not by nature, but by vice; by which vice, nevertheless, the nature itself is shown to be very great and much to be praised. For of whatever the vice is rightly blamed, without doubt the nature is praised. For the right blaming of a vice is this, that by it a praiseworthy nature is dishonored.

As, therefore, when blindness is called a vice of the eyes, it is shown that sight pertains to the nature of the eyes; and when deafness is called a vice of the ears, it is demonstrated that hearing pertains to their nature: so, when that is called a vice of the angelic creature by which it does not cleave to God, hence it is most plainly declared that it befits its nature to cleave to God. But how great a praise it is to cleave to God, so as to live for him, to be wise from him, to rejoice in him, and to enjoy so great a good without death, without error, without trouble, who can worthily ponder or express? Wherefore, even by the vice of the evil angels, by which they do not cleave to God, since every vice harms a nature, it is sufficiently made manifest that God created their nature so good that it is harmful to it not to be with God.

**2.** Let these things be said, lest anyone, when we speak of the apostate angels, suppose that they could have had another nature, as it were from another principle, and that God is not the author of their nature. From the impiety of this error each one will be free the more readily and easily, the more keenly he is able to understand what God said through an angel, when he was sending Moses to the children of Israel: *I am who I am*[^2].

For since God is the highest essence—that is, supremely is, and therefore is unchangeable—he gave being to the things which he created from nothing, but not supreme being, such as he himself is; and to some he gave to be more, to others less, and thus ordered the natures of essences by grades (for just as from "to be wise" comes "wisdom," so from "to be" comes "essence," by a new word indeed, which the ancient authors of Latin speech did not use, but which is now *customary* in our times, lest there should be lacking even to our language what the Greeks call οὐσία; for this word was expressed word for word, so that it might be called essence); and through this, to that nature which supremely is, by whose making whatever things are exist, there is no contrary nature except that which is not. For to that which is, not to be is contrary. And therefore to God, that is, to the highest essence and the author of all essences of whatever kind, no essence is contrary.

**3.** But those are called in the Scriptures enemies of God[^3] who oppose his rule not by nature but by vices, able to harm him in nothing, but only themselves. For they are enemies by the will of resisting, not by the power of harming. For God is unchangeable and in every way incorruptible. Therefore the vice by which those resist God who are called his enemies is an evil not to God, but to themselves, and this for no other reason than that it corrupts in them the good of nature. Nature, therefore, is not contrary to God, but vice, because it is evil, is contrary to good. But who would deny that God is supremely good? Vice, then, is contrary to God, as evil to good.

Moreover, the nature which it vitiates is also good; whence it is surely contrary to this good too; but to God only as evil to good, while to the nature which it vitiates it is not only evil but also harmful. For no evils are harmful to God, but only to changeable and corruptible natures—natures good, however, even by the testimony of the vices themselves.

For if they were not good, vices could not harm them. For what do they do by harming them, except take away integrity, beauty, health, virtue, and whatever good of a nature is wont to be removed or diminished by vice? But if this be wholly absent, by taking away no good it does no harm, and through this is not a vice. For it cannot be that a thing should be a vice and yet do no harm.

Whence it is gathered that, although a vice cannot harm the unchangeable good, it nevertheless cannot harm anything but a good, because it is present only where it does harm. This can also be said in this way: that a vice can be neither in the highest good nor anywhere but in some good.

Goods alone, therefore, can exist somewhere, evils alone nowhere; since even those natures which are vitiated from the beginning of an evil will are, in so far as they are vitiated, evil, but in so far as they are natures, good. And when a vitiated nature is in punishments, besides the fact that it is a nature, this too is there a good, that it is not unpunished. For this is just, and everything just is without doubt good. For no one pays penalties for natural, but for voluntary, vices. For even the vice which, strengthened by habit or by excessive advance, has, as it were, grown in naturally, took its beginning from the will. For we are now speaking of the vices of that nature in which there is a mind capable of the intelligible light, by which the just is distinguished from the unjust.

**4.** But to think damnable the vices of cattle and trees and other changeable and mortal things wholly lacking intellect or sense or life, by which their dissoluble nature is corrupted, is ridiculous, since these creatures received their measure at the nod of the Creator in such a way that, by giving way and following[^4], they carry out the lowest beauty of times, fitting in their own kind to the parts of this world. For earthly things were not to be made equal to heavenly ones, nor ought these to be lacking to the universe on the ground that those are better.

When, therefore, in these places where it was fitting that such things should be, some arise as others fail, and the lesser succumb to the greater and, overcome, are turned into the qualities of those that overcome them, it is the order of things passing away. The comeliness of this order does not delight us, for the reason that, woven into a part of it according to the condition of our mortality, we cannot perceive the whole, to which the little parts that offend us aptly and becomingly fit. Whence to us, who are less able to contemplate it, it is most rightly enjoined that the providence of the Founder be believed, lest, by the vanity of human rashness, we dare to find fault in any respect with the work of so great an artificer.

And yet the vices of earthly things too, neither voluntary nor penal, commend, by the same reasoning, the natures themselves—of none of which at all is God not the author and founder—if we attend prudently, because in them too it displeases us that this should be taken away by vice which in the nature pleases;

unless it be that to men the natures themselves very often displease, when they become harmful to them, since they consider not the natures but their own utility, as those animals whose abundance scourged the pride of the Egyptians[^5].

But in this way they could find fault even with the sun, since certain offenders or those who do not pay their debts are ordered by judges to be placed in the sun. Not, therefore, from our convenience or inconvenience, but considered in itself, a nature gives glory to its artificer.

So too the nature of eternal fire is without any doubt praiseworthy, although it will be penal to the damned impious. For what is more beautiful than fire, flaming, vigorous, shining? What more useful than fire, warming, healing, cooking? Although nothing is more troublesome than it, burning.

The very same thing, therefore, applied in one way is pernicious, which, fittingly used, is found most convenient. For who is sufficient to explain in words its uses in the whole world?

Nor are those to be heard who praise in fire the light, but find fault with the heat—evidently not from the force of nature, but from their own convenience or inconvenience. For they wish to see, but are unwilling to burn. But they little attend that this very light, which certainly pleases even them, harms weak eyes through unfittingness, and that in that heat, which displeases them, certain animals live healthily through fittingness.

**5.** All natures, therefore, since they are, and on that account have their own measure, their own form, and a certain peace of their own with themselves, are surely good; and when they are where they ought to be by the order of nature, they guard their being as much as they have received; and those which have not received to be always are changed for better or worse, according to the use and movement of things to which they are subject by the law of the Creator, tending by divine providence toward that outcome which the plan of governing the universe includes;

so that not even so great a corruption, as that which leads changeable and mortal natures all the way to destruction, so makes that not to be which was, that there should not thence follow consequently what was to be. Since this is so, God, who supremely is, and therefore by whom was made all essence which does not supremely exist (since neither ought that to be equal to him which was made of nothing, nor could it in any way exist if it had not been made by him), is neither to be blamed for the offense of any vices, nor to be praised by the consideration of all natures.

## [The cause of the first evil will]

**6.** Accordingly, the cause of the blessedness of the good angels is found to be most truly this, that they cleave to him who supremely is. But when the cause of the misery of the evil angels is sought, that deservedly occurs, that, turned away from him who supremely is, they were turned toward themselves, who do not supremely exist; and what else should this vice be called but pride? For *pride is the beginning of all sin*[^6]. They were unwilling, therefore, to keep their strength for him[^7], and, who would be more if they cleaved to him who supremely is, by preferring themselves to him preferred that which is less.

This was the first defect and the first want and the first vice of that nature which was so created that it neither supremely was, and yet could, for having blessedness, enjoy him who supremely is; from whom, turned away, it would be not indeed nothing, but nevertheless less, and on that account would become wretched.

Moreover, if the efficient cause of this evil will be sought, nothing is found. For what is it that makes the will evil, when the will itself makes the evil work? And through this the evil will is the efficient cause of the evil work, but of the evil will there is no efficient cause. For if some thing is the cause, either it has or does not have some will; if it has, surely it has either a good or an evil one; if a good one, who would be so foolish as to say that a good will makes an evil will? For there will be, if it is so, a good will the cause of sin, than which nothing more absurd can be thought. But if this thing which is supposed to make the evil will itself also has an evil will, then I ask, consequently, what made it, too; and so, that there may be some limit of inquiring, I inquire after the cause of the first evil will.

For that is not the first evil will which an evil will made; but that is the first which none made. For if something preceded by which it was made, that is prior which made the other. But if it be answered that no thing made it, and therefore that it always was: I ask whether it was in some nature. For if it was in none, it was not at all; but if in some, it vitiated and corrupted it, and was harmful to it, and through this deprived it of good.

And therefore an evil will could not be in an evil nature, but in a good one, yet changeable, to which this vice could do harm. For if it did no harm, it surely was not a vice, and through this neither is it to be said to have been an evil will. But if it did harm, it surely did harm by taking away or diminishing a good. There could not, therefore, be an everlasting evil will in that thing in which a natural good preceded, which the evil will, by harming, could take away.

If, then, it was not everlasting, I ask who made it. It remains to be said that some thing in which there was no will made the evil will. I inquire whether this is higher, or lower, or equal. But the higher is surely better; how, then, is it the cause of no will, and not rather of a good will? The same, surely, holds of the equal. For two things, as long as they are equally of a good will, do not make in each other an evil will. It remains that a lower thing, in which there is no will, made the evil will of the angelic nature which first sinned. But even that thing itself, whatever it be that is lower, down to the lowest earth, since it is a nature and an essence, is without doubt good, having its own measure and form in its own kind and order. How, then, is a good thing the efficient cause of an evil will? How, I say, is a good the cause of an evil? For when the will, leaving the higher, turns itself to lower things, it is made evil—not because that is evil to which it turns itself, but because the turning itself is perverse. Therefore it was not the lower thing that made the will evil, but the will itself that desired the lower thing perversely and inordinately, because it is made.

For if two men, equally affected in soul and body, see the beauty of one body, and, this being seen, one of them is moved to enjoy it unlawfully, while the other perseveres stable in a chaste will, what do we think is the cause that an evil will arises in the one but not in the other? What thing made it in him in whom it arose? For not that beauty of the body; for it did not make it in both, since it presented itself not differently to the gaze of both. Or is the flesh of the one who beholds the cause? Why not also of the other? Or rather the soul? Why not of both? For we have said before that both were equally affected in soul and body. Or is it to be said that one of them was tempted by the hidden suggestion of a malignant spirit—as though he did not consent by his own will to that same suggestion and persuasion, whatever it was?

This consent, then, this evil will which he applied to the one persuading him to evil, what thing made it in him, we ask. For, that this impediment too may be removed from this question, if both be tempted by the same temptation, and one yield and consent to it, the other persevere the same as he had been: what else appears, except that one was unwilling, the other willing, to fall away from chastity? Whence, except by his own will, where the same affection of body and soul had been in both? The same beauty was seen equally by the eyes of both, the same hidden temptation pressed equally upon both; to those, therefore, who wish to know what thing made the evil will the property of one of them, if they look well, nothing occurs.

For if we say that he himself made it, what was he himself before the evil will except a good nature, of which God is the author, who is the unchangeable good?

He, then, who says that the one who consented to the tempter and persuader—to whom the other did not consent—in order to use unlawfully the beautiful body which was equally present to both to be seen, when before that seeing and temptation both were alike in soul and body, made for himself the evil will, being surely good before the evil will: let him ask why he made it, whether because it is a nature, or because it was made from nothing; and he will find that the evil will begins to be not from the fact that it is a nature, but from the fact that the nature was made from nothing.

For if a nature is the cause of an evil will, what else are we forced to say, except that evil comes from good and that good is the cause of evil? Since indeed from a good nature an evil will comes. But whence can it come that a good nature, although changeable, before it has an evil will, should make something evil, that is, the evil will itself?

**7.** Let no one, therefore, seek the efficient cause of the evil will; for it is not efficient, but deficient, because the evil will itself is not an effecting but a defecting. For to defect from that which supremely is, to that which is less, this is to begin to have an evil will. But to wish to find the causes of these defections, since they are not efficient, as I have said, but deficient, is like wishing to see darkness or to hear silence—both of which, nevertheless, are known to us, the former only through the eyes, the latter only through the ears, yet not in form, but in the privation of form.

Let no one, then, seek to know from me what I know that I do not know, unless perhaps in order to learn not to know what must be known to be incapable of being known.

For those things which are known not in form, but in the privation of form, are, if it can be said or understood, in a manner known by not knowing, so that by knowing they are not known. For when the keenness even of the bodily eye runs through corporeal forms, it nowhere sees darkness except where it has begun not to see. So too it pertains not to any other sense, but to the ears alone, to perceive silence, which nevertheless is in no way perceived except by not hearing. So our mind indeed "beholds" intelligible forms by understanding; but where they are lacking, it learns by not knowing. For *who understands his transgressions?*[^8]

**8.** This I know, that the nature of God can never, nowhere, in no part be deficient, and that those things can be deficient which were made from nothing. Which things, nevertheless, the more they are and do good things (for then they do something), have efficient causes; but in so far as they are deficient and from this do evils (for what do they then do but vain things?), have deficient causes.

And likewise I know that, in whomever an evil will arises, it arises in him in such a way that, if he were unwilling, it would not arise; and therefore not necessary, but voluntary defects a just penalty follows. For one defects not to evil things, but evilly—that is, not to evil natures, but evilly for this reason, that, contrary to the order of natures, it is from that which supremely is to that which is less.

For the falling away is not toward evil things, but is itself evil: not toward evil natures, but evil because, contrary to the order of natures, it turns from that which exists supremely to that which exists in a lesser degree.

For avarice is not a vice of gold, but of the man perversely loving gold, with justice forsaken, which ought incomparably to have been preferred to gold; nor is luxury a vice of beautiful and sweet bodies, but of the soul perversely loving bodily pleasures, with temperance neglected, by which we are fitted to things spiritually more beautiful and incorruptibly sweeter;

nor is boastfulness a vice of human praise, but of the soul perversely loving to be praised by men, with the testimony of conscience despised; nor is pride a vice of the one giving power, or even of power itself, but of the soul perversely loving its own power, with the more just power of a mightier one contemned. And through this, whoever perversely loves the good of any nature, even if he attains it, himself becomes evil in the good and wretched, deprived of a better good.

**9. 1.** Since, therefore, there is no natural or, if it can be so said, essential efficient cause of an evil will (for from the will itself begins the evil of changeable spirits, by which the good of nature is diminished and depraved, and nothing makes such a will except the defection by which God is forsaken, of which defection too the cause is surely deficient):

## [The cause of the good will of the good angels]

if we say that there is no efficient cause of the good will either, we must beware lest the good will of the good angels be believed to be not made, but coeternal with God. Since, then, they themselves were made, how shall it be said that the good will was not made? Moreover, because it was made, was it made together with them, or did they exist before it? But if together with them, there is no doubt that it was made by him by whom they too were; and as soon as they were made, they cleaved, by the love with which they were made, to him by whom they were made;

and the latter are separated from the society of the former by this, that the former remained in that same good will, while the latter were changed by defecting from it—by an evil will, namely, in this very thing, that they defected from the good; from which they would not have defected, had they but been unwilling.

But if the good angels first existed without a good will, and made it in themselves without God working it: were they, then, made better by themselves than by him? Far from it. For what were they without a good will but evil?

Or if they were not evil on that account, because neither was an evil will present in them (for they had not defected from a will which they had not yet begun to have), certainly they were not yet such, not yet so good as they began to be when they had a good will. But if they could not make themselves better than he had made them, than whom no one makes anything better: surely they could not have a good will, by which they would be better, except by the working of the help of the Creator.

And when their good will brought it about that they were turned not to themselves, who were less, but to him who supremely is, and, cleaving to him, were more, and by participation in him lived wisely and blessedly: what else is shown except that a will, however good, would have been needy, remaining in desire only, unless he who had made the good nature from nothing capable of himself were to make it, by filling it from himself, better—first making it, by rousing it, more eager?

**9. 2.** For this too must be examined: if the good angels made the good will in themselves, whether they made it by some will or by none. If by none, then surely they did not make it. If by some, whether by an evil or a good one? If by an evil one, how could an evil will be the maker of a good will? If by a good one, then they already had it. And who had made this, except him who created them with a good will, that is, with the chaste love by which they would cleave to him, at once both founding their nature and bestowing grace on them? Whence it is to be believed that the holy angels were never without a good will, that is, the love of God.

But those who, though created good, are nevertheless evil (evil by their own will, which the good nature did not make, except when it defected of its own accord from the good, so that the cause of evil is not the good, but the defection from the good), either received a lesser grace of divine love than those who persisted in it, or, if both were created equally good, while these fell by an evil will, those were more helped and came to that fullness of blessedness whereby they became most certain that they would never fall; as we have already treated in the book which this one follows[^9].

It must therefore be confessed, with due praise of the Creator, that it pertains not only to holy men, but can be said also of the holy angels, that the love of God has been poured out in them through the Holy Spirit, who has been given to them[^10]; and that not only of men, but first and chiefly of the angels, is that good which is written: *but for me it is good to cleave to God*[^11]. To those to whom this good is common, they have, both with him to whom they cleave and among themselves, a holy society, and are one city of God, and likewise his living sacrifice and his living temple.

A part of which—that part which, to be joined to the immortal angels, is gathered out of mortal men, and now changeably goes into exile on the earth, or, in those who have undergone death, rests in the hidden receptacles and seats of souls—how it arose, the same God creating, as has been said of the angels, I now see must be said. For from one man, whom God first founded, the human race took its beginning, according to the faith of the holy Scripture, which not undeservedly has a marvelous authority in the world and among all the nations, which, among the other true things it spoke, it foretold by true divinity would believe in it[^12].

## [The institution of the human race]

**10. 1.** Let us, therefore, pass over the conjectures of men who do not know what they are saying about the nature or institution of the human race. For some, just as they have believed concerning the world itself, suppose that men have always existed. Whence Apuleius too says, in describing this race of living beings: *singly mortal, yet all, in the universal race, perpetual*[^13]. And when it has been said to them, if the human race has always existed, in what way their history speaks the truth in narrating who were the inventors of various things, who first established the liberal disciplines and the other arts, or by whom this or that region and part of the lands, this or that island, first began to be inhabited, they answer that by floods and conflagrations, at certain intervals of time, not indeed all, but most of the lands are so laid waste[^14] that men are reduced to a small fewness, from whose progeny again the former multitude is restored; and thus, again and again, things are found and established as though for the first time, when rather those things are being restored which had been interrupted and extinguished by those great devastations; but that man can in no way exist except from man. They say, however, what they think, not what they know.

**11. (10. 2.)** They are deceived too by certain most mendacious writings, which they claim contain, in the history of the ages, many thousands of years, whereas from the sacred Letters we reckon that not yet six thousand years have been completed since the institution of man. Whence, that I may not dispute at length how the vanity of those writings, in which far more thousands of years are reported, is refuted, and no adequate authority for this matter is found in them: that letter of Alexander the Great to his mother Olympias, which he wrote conveying the narration of a certain Egyptian priest, which he brought forth from writings held sacred among them, contains also kingdoms which Greek history too knows;

in which the kingdom of the Assyrians, in that same letter of Alexander, exceeds five thousand years; but in Greek history it has about one thousand three hundred from the very principate of Belus, whom both that Egyptian and the Greeks place at the beginning of that same kingdom[^15]; but the empire of the Persians and Macedonians, down to Alexander himself, to whom he was speaking, he sets at more than eight thousand years, whereas among the Greeks, the years of the Macedonians down to the death of Alexander are found to be four hundred and eighty-five, but those of the Persians, until they were ended by the victory of Alexander himself, are reckoned at two hundred and thirty-three.

These numbers of years, therefore, are far smaller than those Egyptian ones, nor, even if they were reckoned three times as great, would they equal them. For the Egyptians are reported once to have had such short years that they were ended in four months each; whence a fuller and truer year, such as is now both ours and theirs, comprised three of their ancient years. But not even so, as I have said, does Greek history agree with the Egyptian in the number of times. And therefore the Greek is rather to be trusted, because it does not exceed the truth of the years contained in our Letters, which are truly sacred.

Moreover, if this letter of Alexander, which became most widely known, departs greatly in its spans of time from the probable trustworthiness of things: how much less are those writings to be believed which men have wished to bring forth, full of fabulous, as it were, antiquities, against the authority of the most well known and divine books, which foretold that the whole world would believe in it, and in which the whole world, as was foretold by it[^16], has believed; which has shown that it narrated true things past, from the fact that the things it foretold as future are fulfilled with such great truth.

**12. (11.)** But others, who do not consider this world to be everlasting—whether they suppose that there are not it alone, but innumerable worlds, or that it alone indeed exists, but at certain intervals of ages innumerably arises and perishes—must of necessity confess that the race of men first existed without begetting men.

For just as, by floods and burnings of the lands, which they do not think happen over the whole world at once, and therefore contend that a few men always remain, from whom the former multitude is restored—so these men cannot think that anything of mankind is left in the world when the world perishes; but, just as they suppose the world itself to be reborn from its own matter, so in it the human race springs up from its elements, and then the offspring of mortals from parents, as of other animals[^17].

## [The year of the first founding of man]

**13. (12.)** But what we answered, when the question was being turned over concerning the origin of the world, to those who are unwilling to believe that it did not always exist, but began to be, as Plato too most plainly confesses (although by some he is believed to have thought otherwise than he says): this same thing I would answer concerning the first founding of man, on account of those who are similarly moved as to why man was not created through the innumerable and infinite times back behind, and was created so late that, since he began to be, fewer than six thousand years are found in the sacred Letters.

For if the brevity of time offends them, because the years seem to them so few since man, as is read in our authorities, was instituted: let them consider that nothing is long-lasting in which there is some last point, and that all the spans of the ages, defined, if compared to interminable eternity, are to be considered not small, but none at all. And through this, if not five or six, but even sixty thousand, or six hundred thousand, or sixty times or six hundred times, or six hundred thousand times that number of years were named, or if this sum were likewise multiplied as many times by as many, until we had no name for the number, since God made man: it could similarly be asked why he did not make him before.

For God's cessation from the creation of man, eternal backward without beginning, is so great that, if there be compared to it any multitude of times, however great and ineffable—which, nevertheless, enclosed by the end of a certain span, is bounded—it ought not to seem so much even as if we should compare the briefest drop of water to the whole sea, even as far as the ocean flows around; since of these two the one indeed is very tiny, the other incomparably great, but both are finite; whereas that span of time which proceeds from some beginning and is confined by some bound, by however great a magnitude it be extended, compared to that which has no beginning, I know not whether it is to be reckoned as least or rather as nothing. For if from its end the briefest moments be taken away one by one, the number decreasing, though so vast that it finds no name, returning backward (as if you should take away the days of a man from that on which he now lives, all the way to that on which he was born), that taking-away will at some point be brought to the beginning. But if there be taken away backward, in a span which began from no beginning, not, I say, single tiny moments, nor the quantities of hours or days or months or even years, but spans as great as that sum of years comprehends—which now cannot be uttered by any reckoners, but which nevertheless is consumed by the bit-by-bit taking-away of moments—and if these so great spans be taken away not once and again and more often, but always: what happens, what is brought about, when one never arrives at the beginning, which is altogether none?

Wherefore what we now ask after five thousand years and the years that run beyond, posterity too could ask, with the same curiosity, even after six hundred thousand years, if this mortality of men, by arising and dying, and this unskilled weakness, should so long endure. Those too who were before us could, in the very recent times of created man, raise this question. The first man himself, finally, either on the next day or on the same day after he was made, could ask why he was not made before; and whenever he had been made before, this controversy concerning the beginning of temporal things would find then no other resources, and no other now or even later.

**14. (13. 1.)** But the philosophers of this world thought that they could not, or ought not, otherwise resolve this except by introducing cycles of times, by which they asserted that the same things have always been renewed and repeated in the nature of things, and that thus, henceforth, the rolling-rounds of coming and passing ages would be without cessation; whether these cycles took place in a permanent world, or whether a world arising and perishing at certain intervals always exhibited the same things, as though new, which have passed and which are to come. From which mockery they cannot at all free the immortal soul, even when it has received wisdom, going without cessation to a false blessedness and returning without cessation to true misery.

For how is it a true blessedness, of whose eternity there is never any confidence, while the soul either most unskillfully in truth is ignorant of coming misery, or most unhappily in blessedness dreads it?

But if, never thereafter to return to miseries, it proceeds from these to blessedness: then something new comes about in time, which has no end of time. Why, then, not the world too? Why not man too, made in the world? So that those false cycles, whatever they be, discovered by false and deceitful wise men, may, in sound doctrine, be avoided on the path of the right road.

**13. 2.** For certain men wish even that which is read in the book of Solomon called *Ecclesiastes*—*what is it that has been? The very thing that will be. And what is it that has been done? The very thing that will be done; and there is nothing at all new under the sun. Who will speak and say: behold, this is new: it has already been in the ages which were before us*[^18]—to be understood as said on account of these cycles returning into the same things and recalling all things into the same; whereas he said this either of those things of which he was speaking above, that is, of generations, some going, others coming, of the windings of the sun, of the courses of torrents; or certainly of the kinds of all things which arise and perish.

For there were men before us, there are men with us, there will be men after us; so any living beings or shrubs. The monstrous births too, which are born unusual, although they are diverse among themselves, and some of them are reported to have happened once, yet, according to that which they are generally—miracles and monstrous births—surely both have been and will be; nor is it recent and new that a monstrous birth should be born under the sun.

Although some have so understood these words as if that wise man had wished it to be understood that all things had already been done in the predestination of God, and therefore that nothing is new under the sun. But far be it from right faith that we should believe those cycles to have been signified by these words of Solomon, by which they think that the same rollings-round of times and of temporal things are so repeated that, for example, just as in this age the philosopher Plato taught his disciples in the city of Athens and in that school which is called the Academy, so, through innumerable ages back—at very long intervals indeed, but nevertheless fixed ones—both the same Plato and the same city and the same school and the same disciples have been repeated, and through innumerable ages thereafter are to be repeated.

Far be it, I say, that we should believe these things. For Christ died once for our sins[^19]; but *rising from the dead, he dies no more, and death shall no longer have dominion over him*[^20]; and we, after the resurrection, *shall always be with the Lord*[^21], to whom we now say what the sacred Psalm admonishes: *you, O Lord, will keep us and guard us from this generation and forever*. But what follows seems to me to suit these men well enough: *the impious walk round about*[^22]; not because their life is to recur through the circles which they suppose, but because such is now the way of their error, that is, their false doctrine.

## *Against the cycles, the unity and original goodness of man are set forth*

**15. (14.)** But what wonder is it, if, wandering in these cycles, they find neither entrance nor exit? Because they do not know by what beginning the human race and this our mortality began, nor by what end it is closed; since indeed they cannot penetrate the depth of God, whereby he, although he himself is eternal and without beginning, nevertheless began times from some beginning, and made man, whom he had never made before, in time—yet not by a new and sudden, but by an unchangeable and eternal counsel.

Who is able to trace this unsearchable depth and to scrutinize the inscrutable, according to which God founded temporal man—before whom no man ever was—in time, not by a changeable will, and multiplied the human race from one? Since indeed the Psalm itself, when it had set forth and said, *you, O Lord, will keep us and guard us from this generation and forever*, and then had struck back at those in whose foolish and impious doctrine no eternity of the soul's liberation and blessedness is preserved, immediately subjoining, *the impious walk round about*: as though it were said to it, "What, then, do you believe, perceive, understand? Is it to be supposed that it suddenly pleased God to make man, whom he had never made before through the infinite eternity back behind—to whom nothing new can happen, in whom there is nothing changeable?" immediately answered, speaking to God himself: *according to your depth you have multiplied the sons of men*[^23].

Let men, he says, perceive what they think, and suppose and dispute what pleases them: *according to your depth*, which no man can know, *you have multiplied the sons of men*. For it is very deep both that he always was, and that he willed to make man, whom he had never made, for the first time from some point of time, and yet did not change his counsel and will.

**16. (15. 1.)** I indeed, just as I do not dare to say that the Lord God was at some time not lord, so I ought not to doubt that man never existed before and that the first man was created from a certain point of time. But when I consider of what thing he was always lord, if there was not always a creature, I am afraid to affirm anything, because I look upon myself too and recall that it is written: *what man can know the counsel of God, or who will be able to think what the Lord wills? For the thoughts of mortals are timid, and our devisings uncertain. For the corruptible body weighs down the soul, and the earthly habitation presses down the mind that ponders many things*[^24].

Of these things, therefore, which in this earthly habitation I ponder as many (and many indeed, because the one thing among them, or beyond them, which I perhaps do not ponder, that is true, I cannot find), if I should say that there was always a creature whose lord he was, who is always lord and was never not lord—but now this one, now another, through diverse spans of times, lest we say that any is coeternal with the Creator, which faith and sound reason condemn:

care must be taken lest it be absurd and alien to the light of truth that a mortal creature has always existed through the changes of times, one departing, another succeeding, but that an immortal creature did not begin to be except when our age was reached, when the angels too were created—if that light first made rightly signifies them, or rather that heaven of which it is said, *in the beginning God made heaven and earth*[^25]—since nevertheless they did not exist before they were made, lest the immortals, if they are said to have always existed, be believed to be coeternal with God.

But if I should say that the angels were not created in time, but that they too existed before all times, whose lord God was, who was never anything but lord: it will be asked of me also, if they were made before all times, whether they who were made could always have existed.

Here it might perhaps seem the answer should be: How not always, since that which is at every time may not unfittingly be said always to be? But these existed at every time to such a degree that they were even made before all times; if, however, times began from heaven, and they already were before heaven.

But if time was not from heaven, but existed even before heaven—not indeed in hours and days and months and years (for those measurements of temporal spans, which are usually and properly called times, manifestly began from the motion of the stars; whence God too, when he instituted these, said, *and let them be for signs and for times and for days and for years*[^26])—but in some changeable motion, of which one part passes earlier, another later, in that they cannot be together;

if, then, before heaven there was some such thing in the angelic motions, and therefore time already existed, and the angels, from the time they were made, were moved temporally: even thus they existed at every time, since indeed times were made with them. And who would say: That did not always exist which existed at every time?

**15. 2.** But if I should answer this, it will be said to me: How, then, are they not coeternal with the Creator, if he always existed, and they always existed? How also are they to be called created, if they are understood always to have existed? What shall be answered to this? Is it to be said that they both always existed, since they existed at every time, who were made with time, or with whom times were made, and yet were created? For neither shall we deny that the times themselves were created, although no one doubts that time existed at every time.

For if time did not exist at every time, then there was a time when there was no time. Who, however foolish, would say this? For we can rightly say: there was a time when Rome did not exist; there was a time when Jerusalem did not exist; there was a time when Abraham did not exist; there was a time when man did not exist, and anything of this kind; finally, if the world was made not with the beginning of time, but after some time, we can say: there was a time when the world did not exist; but, there was a time when there was no time, we say as unfittingly as if anyone should say: there was a man when there was no man, or: this world existed when this world did not exist. For if it be understood of one thing and another, it can be said in some way, that is: there was another man when this man did not exist; so, then: there was another time when this time did not exist, we can rightly say; but, there was a time when there was no time, who, even most foolish, would say?

As, therefore, we say that time was created, although it is said always to have existed because at every time there was time: so it does not follow that, if the angels always existed, they are therefore not created, so that they should be said always to have existed because they existed at every time, and to have existed at every time because in no way could the times themselves exist without them. For where there is no creature by whose changeable movements times are carried out, times can in no way exist; and through this, even if they always existed, they were created, nor, if they always existed, are they therefore coeternal with the Creator. For he always existed by an unchangeable eternity; but they were made; yet they are said always to have existed, because they existed at every time, without which times could in no way exist;

but time, since it runs by changeableness, cannot be coeternal with unchangeable eternity. And through this, even if the immortality of the angels does not pass away in time, and is neither past as though it now were not, nor future as though it were not yet: nevertheless their movements, by which times are carried out, pass from the future into the past, and therefore they cannot be coeternal with the Creator, in whose movement it is not to be said either that there was what now is not, or that there is to be what is not yet.

**15. 3.** Wherefore, if God was always Lord, he always had a creature serving his dominion; yet not begotten of himself, but made by him from nothing, and not coeternal with him; for he was before it, although at no time without it—preceding it not by a span of time running on, but by an abiding perpetuity.

But if I should answer this to those who ask how he was always Creator, always Lord, if a serving creature did not always exist; or how it was created and is not rather coeternal with the Creator, if it always existed: I fear I may more easily be judged to affirm what I do not know than to teach what I know.

I return, therefore, to that which our Creator willed us to know; but as for those things which he has permitted to be known either by the wiser in this life, or has reserved to be known only by the perfect in another life, I confess to be beyond my powers. But I have thought they should be treated without affirmation, so that those who read these things may see from what perils of questions they ought to refrain,

and may not think themselves fit for all things, but rather understand how the Apostle is to be obeyed, when he gives this wholesome precept, saying: *but I say, through the grace of God which has been given to me, to all who are among you, not to be wiser than it is proper to be wise, but to be wise unto temperance, to each one as God has apportioned the measure of faith*[^27]. For if an infant be nourished according to its strength, it will come about that, by growing, it grasps more; but if it exceed the strength of its capacity, it will fail before it grows.

**17. (16.)** What ages passed before the human race was instituted, I confess myself ignorant; yet I do not doubt that nothing at all of creation is coeternal with the Creator. The Apostle too speaks of eternal times—and these not future, but, what is more to be wondered at, past. For he thus says: *in hope of eternal life, which God, who does not lie, promised before the eternal times; but he has manifested in his own times his word*[^28]. Behold, he said that there were back behind eternal times, which nevertheless were not coeternal with God, since indeed he not only was *before the eternal times*, but also promised eternal life, which he manifested in his own, that is, fitting, times—what else than his Word? For this is eternal life. But how did he promise it, since he certainly promised it to men who did not yet exist *before the eternal times*, except because in his eternity and in his Word itself, coeternal with him, it was already fixed by predestination what was to be in its own time?

## [Time, infinity, and divine knowledge]

**18. (17. 1.)** This too I do not doubt, that before the first man was created, there was never any man—neither that very same man, revolved I know not how many times through I know not what cycles, nor any other like him in nature. Nor do the arguments of the philosophers deter me from this faith, of which that is reckoned the most acute, by which they say that no infinite things can be comprehended by any knowledge; and through this, they say, God has with him all the finite reasons of all the finite things which he makes; but his goodness, they say, is to be believed never to have been idle, lest his working be temporal, of which there was back behind an eternal cessation, as though he had repented of his former idleness without beginning, and on that account had taken up the beginning of his work;

and therefore, they say, it is necessary that the same things be always repeated, and that the same things, always to be repeated, run their course—either with the world remaining, changeably, which, though it never was not, was nevertheless made without beginning of time, or with its rising and setting too always repeated in those cycles and always to be repeated;

lest, that is, if the works of God are said to have begun at some first point, he be believed in a manner to have condemned his former idleness without beginning as inert and slothful and therefore displeasing to himself, and on that account to have changed it; but if he be reported to have always worked temporal things, yet some and others, and thus at some point to have come even to the making of man, whom he had never made before, he should seem to have made what he made not by knowledge, by which they think that whatever is infinite cannot be comprehended, but as if for the hour, as it came into his mind, by a certain fortuitous inconstancy.

Moreover, if those cycles be admitted, they say, by which, whether with the world remaining or with the world itself inserting its revolving risings and settings into the same cycles, the same temporal things are repeated, neither is sluggish idleness—especially of so long a duration without beginning—attributed to God, nor an improvident rashness of his works; since, if the same things be not repeated, infinite things, varied by an infinite diversity, cannot be comprehended by any knowledge or foreknowledge of his.

**17. 2.** These argumentations, by which the impious try to turn our simple godliness aside from the right way, that we may walk with them in a cycle[^29], if reason could not refute them, faith ought to deride. To this is added that, with the help of the Lord our God, manifest reason breaks in pieces these rolling circles which opinion feigns. For herein especially do these men err, that they would rather walk in a false cycle than by a true and right road—because they measure the divine mind, altogether unchangeable, capable of any infinity and numbering all innumerable things without alternation of thought, by their own human, changeable, and narrow mind;

and there happens to them what the Apostle says: *for, comparing themselves with themselves, they do not understand*[^30]. For because, whatever new thing comes into their mind to be done, they do by a new counsel (for they bear changeable minds): surely, thinking not of God, whom they cannot conceive, but of themselves in his place, they compare not him, but themselves, and not to him, but to themselves.

But it is not lawful for us to believe that God is affected in one way when he is idle and in another when he works; since he is not even to be said to be affected, as though something should come about in his nature which was not before. For he who is affected suffers something, and everything that suffers anything is changeable.

Let not, therefore, sluggishness, sloth, inertia be thought of in his idleness, just as neither labor, effort, industry in his work. He knows how to act while resting and to rest while acting. He can apply to a new work a counsel not new, but everlasting; nor by repenting, because he had before rested, did he begin to do what he had not done.

But even if he first rested and afterward worked (which I know not how it can be understood by man): this, without doubt, which is called "before" and "after," was in things not existing before and existing after;

but in him no following will changed or took away a preceding one, but by one and the same everlasting and unchangeable will he both brought it about that the things which he founded were not before, as long as they were not, and that they should be afterward, when they began to be—thereby, perhaps, marvelously showing to those who can see such things how he did not need them, but founded them by a gratuitous goodness,

since without them, from an eternity lacking beginning, he remained in no lesser blessedness.

**19. (18.)** But as for that other thing they say, that even by God's knowledge infinite things cannot be comprehended: it remains for them to dare to say, and to plunge themselves into this gulf of profound impiety, that God does not know all numbers. That these are infinite is most certain; since, in whatever number you suppose an end is to be made, that same number can, I do not say by adding one be increased, but, however great it be and however vast a multitude it contains, in the very reckoning and knowledge of numbers be not only doubled but even multiplied.

But each number is so bounded by its own properties that none of them can be equal to any other. Therefore they are both unlike and diverse among themselves, and the single ones are finite, and all are infinite. Does God, then, not know all numbers on account of their infinity, and does the knowledge of God reach up to a certain sum of numbers and is ignorant of the rest? Who, even most demented, would say this?

Nor will these men dare to despise numbers and say that they do not pertain to the knowledge of God, among whom Plato commends God with great authority as fashioning the world by numbers[^31]. And among us it is read that it was said to God: *you have ordered all things in measure and number and weight*[^32]; of whom the prophet too says: *who brings forth the world by number*[^33], and the Savior in the Gospel: *the hairs of your head*, he says, *are all numbered*[^34].

Far be it, therefore, that we should doubt that every number is known to him, *of whose understanding*, as it is sung in the Psalm, *there is no number*[^35]. The infinity of number, therefore, although of infinite numbers there is no number, is nevertheless not incomprehensible to him *of whose understanding there is no number*. Wherefore, if whatever is comprehended by knowledge is bounded by the comprehension of the one knowing: surely all infinity too is, in a certain ineffable way, made finite to God, because it is not incomprehensible to his knowledge.

Wherefore, if the infinity of numbers cannot be infinite to the knowledge of God by which it is comprehended: who, after all, are we little men, that we should presume to fix limits to his knowledge, saying that, unless the same temporal things be repeated by the same cycles of times, God cannot either foreknow all the things he makes so as to make them, or know them when he has made them? Whose wisdom, simply manifold and uniformly multiform, comprehends all incomprehensible things by so incomprehensible a comprehension that, whatever new and dissimilar things, following upon the preceding, he should wish always to make, he could not have them disordered and unforeseen, nor would he foresee them from the proximate time, but would contain them by an eternal foreknowledge.

**20. (19.)** Whether he does this so, and the things which are called *ages of ages*[^36] are linked by a continuous connection with one another, yet running on, some and others, by an ordered dissimilarity—at least with those who are freed from misery abiding without end in their blessed immortality; or whether they are called *ages of ages* in such a way that there are understood ages abiding in the wisdom of God with unshaken stability, as the producers, as it were, of these ages which pass away with time, I do not dare to define. For perhaps that which is "ages" might be called *age*, so that *age of an age* should be nothing other than "ages of ages"; just as *heaven of heaven*[^37] is nothing other than "heavens of heavens." For God called the firmament heaven, above which are the waters[^38]; and yet the Psalm says: *and the waters that are above the heavens, let them praise the name of the Lord*[^39].

Which of these two it is, or whether, besides these two, something else can be understood concerning the *ages of ages*, is a most profound question, and it does not impede what we are now treating, if it be for the time left undiscussed; whether we may be able to define something in it, or whether the more diligent treatment of it itself makes us more cautious, lest, in so great an obscurity of things, we dare rashly to affirm anything.

For now we are disputing against the opinion by which those cycles are asserted, by which the same things are supposed of necessity always to be repeated through intervals of times; but whichever of those opinions concerning the *ages of ages* be true, it pertains nothing to these cycles; since whether the *ages of ages* be not the same things repeated, but running on, one from another, by a most ordered connection, with the blessedness of the freed remaining most certain without any recurrence of miseries, or whether the *ages of ages* be eternal, ruling, as it were, over the temporal ones as their subjects, those cycles revolving the same things have no place, which the eternal life of the saints most of all refutes[^40].

## [Blessedness: a refutation of the theory of the cycles of times]

**21. (20. 1.)** For whose godly ears could bear that, after a life measured out among so many and so great calamities (if indeed it is to be called a life, which is rather a death, so grievous that death, which frees from it, is feared through love of this death), after evils so great and so many and so horrible, at length at some point expiated and ended through true religion and wisdom, one should so come to the sight of God and so be made blessed by the contemplation of the incorporeal light, through participation in the unchangeable immortality of him with whose attainment we burn with love, that it must at some time be forsaken, and that those who forsake it, cast down from that eternity, truth, felicity, should be entangled in tartarean mortality, in shameful folly, in execrable miseries, where God is lost, where truth is held in hatred, where blessedness is sought through unclean wickednesses—and this likewise and likewise without any end of prior and posterior things, at fixed intervals and measures of ages, done and to be done;

and this for the reason that God may be able to know his own works through defined cycles forever going and returning, through our false blessednesses and true miseries, alternately indeed, but everlasting by an unceasing revolution—since he can neither rest from making nor, by knowing, trace out the things which are infinite?

Who would hear these things? Who would believe them? Who would bear them? Which things, if they were true, not only would more prudently be kept silent, but also (that I may say what I wish in the way I can) would more learnedly be unknown. For if we shall not have these things in memory there, and on that account shall be blessed, why is our misery here weighed down the more by the knowledge of them?

But if there we are necessarily to know them, let us at least here not know them, so that here the expectation may be happier than there the attainment of the highest good; since here eternal life to be obtained is awaited, but there a blessed, but not eternal, life is known, at some point to be lost.

**20. 2.** But if they say that no one can come to that blessedness unless, in the schooling of this life, he has known these cycles, in which blessedness and misery alternate by turns: how, then, do they confess that the more anyone has loved God, the more easily he will come to blessedness, while they teach things by which love itself grows torpid? For who would not love more remissly and tepidly him whom he thinks he must of necessity forsake, and against whose truth and wisdom he must come to feel—and this when he shall have come, by the perfection of blessedness, to a full knowledge of him according to his capacity? Since one cannot even faithfully love a human friend, to whom he knows he will be an enemy.

But far be it that those things should be true which threaten us with a true misery never to be ended, but often and without end to be broken by the interpositions of a false blessedness.

For what is more false and more deceitful than that blessedness, where we either, in so great a light of truth, are ignorant that we shall be wretched, or, in the highest citadel of felicity, fear it?

For if we are to be ignorant of the coming calamity, our misery here is more skilled, where we know the coming blessedness; but if there the impending disaster will not be hidden from us, the wretched soul passes more blessedly the times after which it is to be lifted up to blessedness, than the blessed soul those after which it is to be revolved into misery.

And thus the hope of our unhappiness is happy, and that of our happiness unhappy. Whence it comes about that, because here we suffer present evils, there we dread impending ones, we can more truly always be wretched than at some time blessed.

**20. 3.** But since these things are false, with godliness crying out and truth convicting them (for that true felicity is truly promised us, of which there will be a certain security, always to be retained and to be broken by no unhappiness): let us, following the right way, which Christ is to us[^41], with him as our leader and savior, turn our journey of faith and our mind away from the vain and inept cycle of the impious.

For if, concerning these cycles and the goings and returnings of souls alternating without cessation, Porphyry the Platonist was unwilling to follow the opinion of his own school—whether moved by the vanity of the thing itself or now revering the Christian times—and, as I mentioned in the tenth book[^42], preferred to say that the soul was given to the world for the sake of knowing evils, so that, freed and purified from them, when it has returned to the Father, it should suffer nothing further of the kind: how much more ought we to detest and shun this falsehood, hostile to the Christian faith!

But these cycles being emptied out and brought to nothing, no necessity compels us to think, on that account, that the human race has no beginning of time since it began to be, because, through I know not what cycles, there is nothing new in things which has not been before at fixed intervals of times and will be afterward.

For if a soul is freed, never to return to miseries, as it was never before freed: something comes about in it which was never before done, and this indeed a very great thing, that is, eternal felicity which never ceases. But if in an immortal nature so great a newness comes about, repeated by no cycle, to be repeated by no cycle: why is it contended that it cannot come about in mortal things?

If they say that no newness of blessedness comes about in the soul, since it returns to that in which it always was, certainly the liberation itself comes about as new, when it is freed from a misery in which it never was, and the very newness of misery came about in it which never was.

But if this newness comes not into the order of things which are governed by divine providence, but rather happens by chance, where are those determined and measured cycles, in which no new things come about, but the same things which have been are repeated? But if this newness too is not excluded from the ordering of providence, whether the soul was given or fell: new things can come about which have neither been done before nor are alien from the order of things.

And if the soul could, through imprudence, make for itself a new misery, which was not unforeseen by divine providence, so that providence included this too in the order of things, and not improvidently freed it from this: by what rashness, after all, of human vanity do we dare to deny that the divinity can make things new, not to itself, but to the world, which it has neither made before nor ever had unforeseen?

But if they say that souls, once freed, will not indeed return to misery, but that when this happens in things nothing new comes about, since always some and others have been freed and are freed and will be freed: this at least they concede, if it is so, that new souls come into being, for which there is both a new misery and a new liberation.

For if they say that the souls are ancient and everlasting back behind, from which new men are daily made, from whose bodies, if they have lived wisely, they are so freed that they never are revolved back to miseries, they will consequently have to say that they are infinite.

For however great a finite number of souls there had been, it could not suffice for the infinite ages back behind, so that from it men should always be made, whose souls were always to be freed from this mortality, never thereafter to return to it. Nor will they in any way explain how, in things which they wish to be finite, that they may be able to be known to God, the number of souls is infinite.

**20. 4.** Wherefore, since those cycles are now exploded, by which the soul was thought of necessity to return to the same miseries: what remains more agreeable to godliness than to believe that it is not impossible for God both to make new things which he never made, and, by an ineffable foreknowledge, not to have a changeable will?

Moreover, whether the number of freed souls, never thereafter to return to miseries, can always be increased, let those see to it who dispute so subtly about the curbing of the infinity of things; but we bound our reasoning on either side.

For if it can, what reason is there that it should be denied that something could be created which had never been created before, if the number of freed souls, which never before existed, was not only made once, but will never cease to be made? But if it must be that there is a certain fixed number of freed souls, which never return to misery, and that this number is not thereafter increased: that very number too, without doubt, whatever it shall be, surely never existed before; which could surely not grow and come to the term of its quantity without some beginning; which beginning in that way never existed before. That this, then, might be, man was created, before whom there was none.

## [God's creation of the human race from one man]

**22. (21.)** This most difficult question, then, on account of the eternity of God creating new things without any newness of will, having been explained as far as we could, it is not hard to see that it was much better what was done, that God should multiply the human race from one man, whom he first founded, than if he had begun it from several. For although he established some living beings as solitary and, as it were, lone-wandering—that is, which seek solitude rather, such as are eagles, kites, lions, wolves, and whatever are so—and others as gregarious, which prefer to live gathered together and in flocks, as are doves, starlings, stags, does, and the rest of this kind: yet he propagated each kind not from single individuals, but commanded several to exist at once.

But man, whose nature he was founding as, in a manner, midway between the angels and the beasts, so that, if subject to his Creator as to his true lord, he should keep his precept by godly obedience, he would pass into angelic fellowship, attaining a blessed immortality without any term and without intervening death;

but if he should offend the Lord his God by using his free will proudly and disobediently, he would, given over to death, live bestially, a slave of lust and destined after death to eternal punishment—him he created one and single, not, surely, to be left alone without human society, but that in this way the unity of that very society and the bond of concord might be the more strongly commended to him, if men were knit together not only by the likeness of nature, but also by the affection of kinship; since it pleased him not even to create the woman herself, who was to be joined to the man, as he created the man, but from the man, that the human race might be diffused altogether from one man.

**23. (22.)** Nor was God ignorant that man would sin, and, already subject to death, would propagate men who were to die, and that mortals would advance to such an enormity of sinning that the beasts, destitute of rational will, whose beginning sprang up in greater numbers from the waters and the lands, would live among themselves more safely and peaceably, each of its own kind, than men, whose race was propagated from one for the commending of concord.

For never have lions among themselves, or dragons among themselves, waged such wars as men. But he also foresaw, by his grace, that a people of the godly was to be called into adoption, and, with their sins forgiven, justified by the Holy Spirit, was to be associated with the holy angels in eternal peace, the last enemy, death, being destroyed[^43]; to which people the consideration of this thing would be profitable, that God had instituted the human race from one man, to commend to men how pleasing to him is unity even in many.

**24. (23.)** God, therefore, made man in his own image[^44]. For he created for him such a soul that, by reason and intelligence, he should be more excellent than all the animals of the land and the waters and the air, which did not have a mind of this kind. And when he had formed the man from the dust of the earth, and had put into him, by breathing, a soul such as I have said—whether one which he had already made, or rather one which he made by breathing, and willed that very breath which he made by breathing (for what else is it to breathe than to make breath?) to be the soul of man[^45]—he made for him also a wife, as a help in begetting, from a bone drawn from his side[^46], as God.

For these things are not to be conceived after the manner of the flesh, as we are wont to see craftsmen fashioning, from any earthly matter, with bodily members, what the skill of their art has been able. The hand of God is the power of God, who works even visible things invisibly.

But those think these things to be fabulous rather than true who measure the power and wisdom of God—by which he knows how, and is able, even without seeds to make the very seeds—by these customary and daily works; but the things which were first instituted, since they do not know them, they ponder unbelievingly; as though these very things which they know concerning human conceptions and births, if they were narrated to those who had not experienced them, would not seem more incredible; although even these very things most people assign rather to the corporeal causes of nature than to the works of the divine mind.

## [God alone is creator]

**25. (24.)** But with those who do not believe that the divine mind makes or cares for these things, we have no business in these books. But those who believe their Plato that all mortal animals—among which man would hold the chief place, akin to the gods themselves—were made not by that most high God who fashioned the world, but by other lesser gods[^47], whom indeed he himself created, by his permission or command, will easily be free of the error of this opinion too, if they are free of the superstition by which they seek how they may seem justly to perform sacred rites and sacrifices, as it were, to their founders.

For it is not lawful to believe and say that the creator of any nature, however small and mortal, is any but God, even before this can be understood. But the angels, whom they more willingly call gods, even if they apply their working, whether commanded or permitted, to the things which are begotten in the world, we so do not call creators of animals as we do not call farmers the creators of crops and trees.

**26. (25.)** For one form is that which is applied "from without" to any corporeal matter, as when men work who are potters and smiths and craftsmen of that kind, who also paint and fashion forms like to the bodies of animals; but another is that which has its efficient causes "from within," from the secret and hidden choice of a living and understanding nature, which makes not only the natural forms of bodies, but the very souls of living beings, while it is not itself made: let the former form be attributed to any craftsmen whatever; but this other to none but the one artificer, the creator and founder God, who made the world itself and the angels without any world and without any angels.

For by that divine and, so to say, effective power—which does not know how to be made, but to make—the roundness of the heaven and the roundness of the sun received their form when the world was made:

by that same divine and effective power—which does not know how to be made, but to make—the roundness of the eye and the roundness of an apple, and the other natural figures which we see in any things being born, received their form, applied not from without, but by the inmost power of the Creator, who said, *I fill heaven and earth*[^48], and whose wisdom it is which *reaches mightily from end to end and orders all things sweetly*[^49].

Accordingly, what kind of ministry the angels, first made, offered to the Creator in making the rest, I do not know; nor do I dare to attribute to them what perhaps they cannot do, nor ought I to take away what they can. The creation and founding of all natures, however, by which it comes about that they are natures at all, I attribute—with their favor too—to that God, to whom they themselves also know, with thanksgiving, that they owe their being.

Not only, therefore, do we not call the farmers the creators of any crops, since we read: *neither he who plants is anything nor he who waters, but God who gives the increase*[^50]; but not even the earth itself, although it seems to be the fruitful mother of all, which advances the things bursting forth in shoots and holds those fixed by their roots, since we likewise read: *God gives it a body as he has willed, and to each of the seeds its own body*[^51]. So neither ought we to call the woman the creatress of her own offspring, but rather him who said to a certain servant of his: *before I formed you in the womb, I knew you*[^52]. And although the soul of a pregnant woman, affected in this way or that, may be able, as it were, to clothe the fetus with certain qualities—as Jacob did with the variegated rods, that the cattle might be born variegated in color[^53]—yet that nature which is begotten she no more made than she made herself.

Whatever corporeal or seminal causes, therefore, be applied to the begetting of things—whether by the workings of angels or of men or of any animals, or by the minglings of males and females; whatever desires too or movements of the mother's soul may be able to sprinkle some lineaments or colors upon the tender and soft conceptions: the natures themselves, which are affected in this way or that in their own kind, are made by none but the most high God, whose hidden power, penetrating all things by an uncontaminable presence, makes to be whatever in any way is, in whatever degree it is; because, unless he made it, it would not be such or such, but could not be at all.

Wherefore, if, in that form which craftsmen impose from without upon corporeal things, we say that the city of Rome and the city of Alexandria had as their founders not the smiths and architects, but the kings, by whose will, counsel, and command they were built—the one Romulus, the other Alexander: how much more ought we to call none but God the founder of natures, who neither makes anything from matter which he himself did not make, nor has workmen except those whom he himself created; and if he should withdraw his, so to say, fabricating power from things, they would not be, just as before they were made they were not. But "before" I say by eternity, not by time. For what other creator of times is there, except him who made the things by whose movements times might run?

**27. (26.)** Thus indeed Plato wished the lesser gods, made by the most high God, to be the makers of the other animals, in such a way that they took the immortal part from him, but themselves wove on the mortal part. Accordingly, he was unwilling that they be the creators of our souls, but of our bodies[^54]. Whence, since Porphyry says that, for the purification of the soul, every body is to be fled, and at the same time, with his Plato and other Platonists, holds that those who have lived immoderately and dishonorably return, for the paying of penalties, to mortal bodies—Plato indeed even to the bodies of beasts, but Porphyry only to those of men[^55]—it follows for them that they say that these gods, whom they wish to be worshiped by us as, it were, our parents and founders, are nothing other than the smiths of our fetters or prisons, and not our establishers, but our enclosers and binders in toilsome workhouses and most heavy chains.

Either, therefore, let the Platonists cease to threaten the souls with penalties from these bodies, or let them not proclaim to us as to be worshiped those gods whose working in us they exhort us to flee and escape as much as we can—though both are most false. For neither do the souls thus pay penalties, when they are revolved anew to this life, and of all living beings, whether in heaven or on earth, there is no founder except him by whom heaven and earth were made.

For if there is no cause of living in this body except for the paying of punishments: how does the same Plato say that the world could not otherwise be made most beautiful and best, unless it were filled with the kinds of all animals, that is, both immortal and mortal[^56]? But if our institution, by which we were founded even as mortals, is a divine gift: how is it a punishment to return to these bodies, that is, to divine benefits? And if God, as Plato constantly mentions[^57], contained in his eternal intelligence the forms both of the universal world and of all animals: how did he not himself found all things? Or would he be unwilling to be the artificer of some, the art of whose making his ineffable and ineffably praiseworthy mind possessed[^58]?

## [The social nature of humanity]

**28. (27. 1.)** Deservedly, therefore, true religion acknowledges and proclaims him to be the founder of the universal world to be also the founder of all animals, that is, both of souls and of bodies. Among which earthly beings, the chief, made by him to his own image, for that cause which I have said, and perhaps for some other greater one that lies hidden, was made one—but was not left alone.

For there is nothing so discordant by vice, so social by nature, as this race. Nor could human nature speak more fittingly against the vice of discord, whether to guard against its arising or to heal it when it had arisen, than by the remembrance of that parent whom God for this reason willed to create one, from whom a multitude should be propagated[^59], that by this admonition a concordant unity might be preserved even among many. But that the woman was made for him from his side, here too it was sufficiently signified how dear ought to be the joining of husband and wife.

These works of God are surely unusual for this reason, that they are the first. But those who do not believe these things ought to believe that no prodigies have been done; for these too, if they were begotten in the customary course of nature, would not be called prodigies. But what, under so great a governance of divine providence, although its cause lies hidden, is begotten in vain? A certain sacred Psalm says: *come and see the works of the Lord, what prodigies he has set upon the earth*[^60]. Why, then, the woman was made from the side of the man, and what this first, as it were, prodigy prefigured, will be said in another place, as far as God shall help me.

**27. 2.** Now, since this book must be closed, let us suppose that in this first man, who was first made, there arose in the human race two societies, as it were two cities—not yet indeed according to evidence, but already according to the foreknowledge of God. For from him there were to be men, some to be associated with the evil angels in punishment, others with the good in reward, although by a hidden judgment of God, yet a just one. For since it is written, *all the ways of the Lord are mercy and truth*[^61], neither can his grace be unjust nor his justice cruel.
