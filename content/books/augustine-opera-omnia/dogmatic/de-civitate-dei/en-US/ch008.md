# Book VI: The worshippers of the gods conduct themselves unworthily.

## Preface

In the five preceding books I seem to myself to have disputed sufficiently against those who think that the many and false gods—whom the Christian truth convicts of being either useless images, or unclean spirits and ruinous demons, or at any rate creatures, not the Creator—are to be venerated and worshipped, for the sake of the usefulness of this mortal life and of earthly things, with that rite and service which in Greek is called λατρεία and is owed to the one true God.

And who does not know that for excessive folly or obstinacy neither these five nor any other books, of however great a number, can be sufficient? Since that is thought the glory of vanity, to yield to no forces of truth—to the ruin, certainly, of him over whom so monstrous a vice rules.

For even against all the industry of one tending him, it is not the fault of the physician but the incurable disease of the sick man that is unconquered. But those who weigh the things they read, understood and considered either with no, or with no great and excessive, obstinacy of the old error, will more easily judge that we have, in this number of five volumes set as a limit, discussed more than the necessity of the question itself demanded, rather than less; and they will not be able to doubt that all the odium which the inexperienced try to fasten on the Christian religion, on account of the disasters of this life and the grinding down and changing of earthly things—the learned not only dissembling, but, against their own conscience, even favoring it, whom a raving impiety has possessed—is altogether empty of right thought and reason, and full of the most frivolous rashness and most ruinous animosity.

## The question of true felicity is to be treated with the philosophers.

**1.** 1. Now, then, since next, as the promised order requires, those too must be refuted and taught who contend that the gods of the gentiles, whom the Christian religion destroys, are to be worshipped not for the sake of this life, but for the sake of that which will be after death: it pleases me to take the beginning of my disputation from the truth-telling oracle of the holy psalm: *blessed is the man whose hope is the Lord God, and who has not looked upon vanities and lying follies*[^1].

Yet among all the vanities and lying follies, the philosophers are far more tolerably to be heard, to whom those opinions and errors of the peoples were displeasing—the peoples who set up images for the deities and either feigned, or believed feigned, many false and unworthy things about those whom they call the immortal gods, and mingled the things believed with their worship and the rites of their sacred ceremonies.

With these men, who, even if not by freely proclaiming, yet at least somehow by muttering in disputations, have testified that they disapprove such things, this question is not so inappropriately treated: whether, for the sake of the life which will be after death, not one God, who made every spiritual and corporeal creature, ought to be worshipped, but many gods, whom some of those same philosophers, more excellent and more noble than the rest, judged to have been made by that one God and sublimely placed[^2].

**1.** 2. But who could bear it to be said and contended that those gods, certain of whom I recounted in the fourth book[^3], to each of whom single offices over single tiny things are distributed, bestow eternal life on anyone?

Or will those most skilled and most acute men, who boast that they have taught, as a great benefit, things written down so that it might be known why each god ought to be supplicated, what ought to be sought from each—lest, by a most base absurdity, such as is wont to be done jokingly in a mime, water be sought from Liber, wine from the Lymphs[^4]—will they be authorities to any man supplicating the immortal gods, so that, when he has sought wine from the Lymphs and they have answered him, "We have water, seek this from Liber," he can rightly say, "If you do not have wine, at least give me eternal life"?

What is more monstrous than this absurdity? Will not those Lymphs, laughing aloud (for they are wont to be easily moved to laughter)[^5], if they do not aim to deceive as demons do,

answer the suppliant: "O man, do you think we have life in our power, who—as you hear—do not have even the vine (*vitem*)?"

It is, therefore, of the most shameless folly to seek or hope for eternal life from such gods, who are asserted to guard the single particles of this most toilsome and most brief life and of whatever pertains to supporting and propping it up,

so that, if what is under the guardianship and power of one is sought from another, it is so unfitting and absurd as to seem most like mimic buffoonery. When this is done by knowing mimes, they are deservedly laughed at in the theater; but when by ignorant fools, they are more deservedly laughed at in the world.

To what god or goddess, then, and for what, one should supplicate—as far as concerns those gods whom the cities instituted—was skillfully discovered by the learned and committed to memory: what from Liber, for example, what from the Lymphs, what from Vulcan, and so from the rest, some of whom I recounted in the fourth book, others I thought should be passed over. Further, if it is an error to seek wine from Ceres, bread from Liber, water from Vulcan, fire from the Lymphs: how much greater a madness ought it to be understood to be, if any of these be supplicated for eternal life!

**1.** 3. Wherefore, if, when we asked about the earthly kingdom—what gods or goddesses it was to be believed could confer it on men—it was shown, all things being discussed, far removed from the truth that any of these many and false deities should be thought to establish even earthly kingdoms[^6]:

is it not of the most insane impiety, if it be believed that eternal life—which is without any doubt or comparison to be preferred to all earthly kingdoms—can be given to anyone by any of these?

For such gods seemed unable to give even an earthly kingdom, not because they are great and lofty and this something small and abject, which they would not deign to care for in so great a sublimity;

but, however much anyone may rightly, in consideration of human frailty, despise the falling summits of an earthly kingdom, those gods appeared such that they seemed most unworthy that even these things should be committed to them, to be given and guarded. And through this, if (as the earlier matters treated in the last two books taught) no god out of that throng, whether of, as it were, plebeian or of, as it were, noble gods, is fit to give mortal kingdoms to mortals, how much less can he make the immortal out of the mortal!

**1.** 4. To this is added that, if we are now dealing with those who think the gods are to be worshipped not for the sake of this life, but for the sake of the life which will be after death, then they are not at all to be worshipped even for the sake of those things which are attributed as though parceled out

and proper to the power of such gods—not by the reason of truth, but by the opinion of vanity—as those believe who contend that their worship is necessary for the conveniences of this mortal life;

against whom I have already, in the five preceding volumes, disputed sufficiently, as far as I could.

Since these things are so, if the very age of those who worshipped the goddess Iuventas flourished more notably, but her despisers either fell within the years of youth or grew cold in it as though in senile torpor;

if bearded Fortune clothed the cheeks of her worshippers more handsomely and festively, but those by whom she was spurned we saw smooth or ill-bearded[^7]:

even so we would most rightly say that these goddesses can avail only thus far, limited in a manner to their own offices, and through this that eternal life ought not to be sought from Iuventas, who would not give a beard, nor any good after this life to be hoped for from bearded Fortune, whose power in this life was none, so that she should furnish at least that very age which is clothed with a beard.

But as it is, since their worship is not necessary even for those very things which they think subject to them—because many who worship the goddess Iuventas have by no means flourished in that age, and many who do not worship her rejoice in the strength of youth,

and likewise many suppliants of bearded Fortune could attain to no beard, or a deformed one, and if any venerate her to obtain a beard, they are mocked by her bearded despisers:

does the human heart so dote that, when it recognizes the worship of those gods to be empty and ludicrous for the sake of those very temporal and quickly passing gifts over which they are said, single gods over single things, to preside, it yet believes it fruitful for the sake of eternal life?

That these gods can give this, not even those have dared to say who, that they might be worshipped by foolish peoples, distributed to them these temporal works parceled out bit by bit—since they thought them too many—lest any of them should sit idle.

## By what method Varro discusses divine matters.

**2.** Who has sought these things more curiously than Marcus Varro? Who has discovered them more learnedly? Who has considered them more attentively? Who has distinguished them more acutely? Who has written them down more diligently and more fully?

Though he is less pleasing in his eloquence, he is nonetheless so packed with learning and with sound judgments that, in the whole range of erudition which we call secular and they call liberal, he instructs the student of things as much as Cicero delights the student of words.

Indeed, Cicero himself bears such witness to him that in his *Academica* he says that the discussion treated there was one he carried on with Marcus Varro, *a man*, he says, *easily the most acute of all and beyond any doubt the most learned*[^8]. He does not say "the most eloquent" or "the most fluent," since in that capacity Varro is in truth far inferior; but he says *easily the most acute of all*, and in those books, that is, the *Academica*, where he contends that everything must be doubted, he added *beyond any doubt the most learned*.

On this point he was so certain that he set aside the doubt he was accustomed to bring to everything, as though, when about to argue on this one matter even on behalf of the Academics' doubting, he had forgotten that he was an Academic.

And in the first book, when he was praising the literary works of this same Varro, he says: *While we were wandering and straying like strangers in our own city, your books brought us home, as it were, so that we could at last recognize who and where we were. You laid open the age of our homeland, the chronology of its history, the laws of its sacred rites and its priesthoods, its domestic and its public order, the situation of its regions and places, and the names, kinds, functions, and causes of all things divine and human*[^9].

This man, then, so notable and of such surpassing skill—and of whom Terentianus too says briefly in a most elegant little verse, *Varro, the most learned of men in every field*[^10]—

who read so much that we wonder he had any time left to write anything, and wrote so much that we can scarcely believe anyone could have read it all—this man, I say, so great in talent and so great in learning, if he had been an assailant and destroyer of the so-called divine matters about which he wrote, and had declared that they belong not to religion but to superstition, I do not know whether he would have set down in them so many things to be laughed at, despised, and detested.

But since he worshiped these same gods and judged that they should be worshiped,

so much so that in that very work of his he says he fears they may perish, not through any onslaught of enemies, but through the negligence of the citizens—

from which ruin, as it were, he says they are rescued by him and stored away and preserved in the memory of good men through books of this kind, by a more useful care than that by which Metellus is said to have rescued the sacred objects of Vesta from the fire,

and Aeneas the household gods from the destruction of Troy[^11];

and yet he hands down to the ages, for reading, things that are rightly judged by the wise and the foolish alike to deserve rejection and to be utterly hostile to the truth of religion—what are we to suppose except that a man of the keenest intelligence and the greatest learning, yet not made free by the Holy Spirit, was held down by the custom and laws of his city, and yet, under the appearance of commending religion, was unwilling to keep silent about the things that troubled him?

**3.** He wrote forty-one books of *Antiquities*; these he divided into "human matters" and "divine matters," assigning twenty-five to human matters and sixteen to divine.

In that partition he followed this plan: to give six books each to four divisions of human matters. For he had in view who act, where they act, when they act, and what they do.

And so in the first six he wrote about human beings, in the second six about places, in the third six about times, and in the fourth six—the last—he treated of things done.

Now four times six is twenty-four. But he set at the head a single separate book to speak in a general way about all of them beforehand. In divine matters the same form of division was kept by him, as far as concerns the things that are to be rendered to the gods. For sacred rites are rendered by human beings in places and at times.

These four headings I have named he covered in three books each: for the first three he wrote about human beings, the next about places, the third about times, the fourth about sacred rites,

here too commending with the subtlest distinction who perform them, where they perform them, when they perform them, and what they perform.

But because it was necessary to say—and this above all was awaited—to whom they perform them, he composed three final books about the gods themselves,

so that five times three made fifteen. But there are in all, as we said, sixteen, because at the beginning of these too he placed a single separate book to speak about all of them beforehand.

When this was finished, he then subdivided, out of that fivefold distribution, the three preceding books that pertain to human beings in this way: the first is about the pontiffs, the second about the augurs, the third about the fifteen men in charge of the sacred rites;

the second three, pertaining to places, in this way: in one of them he spoke about shrines, in another about sacred buildings, in the third about religious places; the next three that follow these and pertain to times, that is, to festal days, in this way: he made one of them about holy days, another about the games of the circus, the third about the stage games;

of the fourth three, pertaining to sacred rites, he gave one to consecrations, another to private rites, the last to public rites. After this procession, as it were, of ministrations, the gods themselves bring up the rear in the three books that remain, the gods on whom all this worship is bestowed:

in the first the "certain gods," in the second the "uncertain gods," in the third and very last of all the "principal and select gods."

**4. 1.** In this whole sequence of a most beautiful and subtle distribution and distinction, that eternal life is sought and hoped for, or even most shamelessly wished for, in vain, will most easily appear—from what we have already said and from what is to be said hereafter—to anyone who is not, with an obstinate heart, his own enemy.

For these institutions are either of human beings or of demons—not such as those people call good demons, but, to speak more plainly, of unclean spirits and, beyond dispute, of malign ones, who with wondrous spite secretly insert into the thoughts of the impious those harmful opinions by which the human soul grows more and more empty and cannot be fitted to and cleave to the unchangeable and eternal truth; and who sometimes openly thrust them upon the senses and confirm them, where they can, by deceitful attestation.

This very Varro testifies that he wrote about human matters first and about divine matters afterward for this reason: that cities existed first, and then these institutions were established by them. But the true religion was not established by any earthly city; rather, it itself established the heavenly city. It is the true God, the giver of eternal life, who inspires and teaches that city to his true worshipers.

**4. 2.** This, then, is the reason given by Varro when he admits that he wrote about human matters first and about divine matters afterward, because these divine matters were established by human beings: *Just as the painter is prior to the painted panel, and the builder prior to the building, so cities are prior to the things that are established by cities*[^12].

But he says that he would have written about the gods first and about human beings afterward, if he were writing about the whole nature of the gods—

as if here he were writing about some part of it and not about the whole, or as if even some part, though not the whole, of the nature of the gods ought not to be prior to that of human beings.

And what of the fact that, in those three last books, carefully setting out the certain and the uncertain and the select gods, he seems to pass over no nature of the gods?

What, then, does he mean when he says: *If we were writing about the whole nature of gods and human beings, we would have finished the divine matters before we touched the human*?

For he is writing either about the whole nature of the gods, or about some part of it, or about none of it at all.

If about the whole, then it is certainly to be set before human matters; if about some part, then why should not that very part also precede human matters?

Or is some part of the gods unworthy to be set even before the whole nature of human beings? And if it is too much that some divine part be set before all human matters, it is at least worthy to be set before the Roman ones.

For he wrote the books of human matters not as they pertain to the whole world, but only as they pertain to Rome alone, and yet he said that he had rightly preferred them, in the order of writing, to the books of divine matters—just as a painter is preferred to the painted panel and a builder to the building—most openly admitting that these divine matters too, like the painting and the structure, were established by human beings.

It remains that he should be understood to have written about no nature of the gods at all, and to have been unwilling to say this openly, but to have left it to those who understand.

For when "not all" is said, it is indeed usually understood as "some"; but it can also be understood as "none," since what is "none" is neither "all" nor "some."

For, as he himself says, if the nature of the gods about which he was writing were the whole, it would have to be set, in the order of writing, before human matters;

but, as the truth cries out even while he keeps silent, it would certainly have to be set before Roman matters, even if it were not the whole but at least some part. Rightly, however, it is set after them; therefore it is none.

And so he did not wish to set human matters before divine matters, but he was unwilling to set false things before true ones.

For in what he wrote about human matters, he followed the history of things actually done; but what he wrote about the things he calls divine—what was it but the opinions of empty things?

This, no doubt, is what he wished to show by a subtle hint, not only in writing about the latter after the former, but also in giving the reason why he had done so. For had he kept silent about it, others might perhaps have defended this act of his in some other way.

But in the very reason he gave, he left nothing for others to surmise at their own pleasure, and he sufficiently proved that he had set human beings before the institutions of human beings, not the nature of human beings before the nature of the gods.

Thus he confessed that he had written his books of divine matters not about the truth that pertains to nature, but about the falsehood that pertains to error.

This he set down more openly elsewhere, as I recalled in the fourth book: that he would have written according to the pattern of nature, if he were himself founding a new city; but since he had found an old one already in being, he could do nothing but follow its custom.

## That the theology of the fabulous kind, or that of the poets . . .

**5. 1.** Then, what is to be made of the fact that he says there are three kinds of theology, that is, of the account given of the gods—one called mythical, another physical, and the third civil? If usage allowed it in Latin, we would call the kind he put first "fabulary"; but let us call it "fabulous," for from fables it is called *mythical*, since in Greek μῦθος means "fable." That the second be called "natural," the habit of speech already allows. The third he himself rendered in Latin too, which is called "civil."

Then he says: *The kind they call 'mythical' is the one the poets chiefly use; the 'physical,' the one the philosophers use; the 'civil,' the one the peoples use. The first kind that I named,* he says, *contains many things invented contrary to the dignity and nature of the immortals.*

*For in it we have one god born from a head, another from a thigh, another from drops of blood; in it the gods have committed thefts, have committed adultery, have served a man; in short, in it everything is attributed to the gods that can befall not only a man, but even the most contemptible man*[^13].

Here surely, where he could, where he dared, where he thought it would go unpunished, he set forth without any cloud of ambiguity how great an injury was being done to the nature of the gods by most lying fables. For he was speaking not about natural theology, nor about civil, but about the fabulous, which he thought he was free to blame.

**5. 2.** Let us see what he says about the second kind. *The second kind is the one I have set forth,* he says, *about which the philosophers have left many books; in them is treated who the gods are, where they are, of what kind, of what character: whether the gods have existed from some point of time or from everlasting;*

*whether they are from fire, as Heraclitus believes, or from numbers, as Pythagoras, or from atoms, as Epicurus says.*

*And so with other matters, which ears can bear more easily within the walls of a school than outside in the forum*[^14].

He found no fault with this kind, which they call physical and which pertains to the philosophers, except that he recalled their disputes among themselves, through which there arose a multitude of disagreeing sects.

Yet he removed this kind from the forum, that is, from the peoples, and shut it up in the schools and within walls. But that first kind, most lying and most shameful, he did not remove from the cities.

O religious ears of the peoples, and among them even the Roman ones!

What the philosophers dispute about the immortal gods, they cannot bear; but what the poets sing and the actors perform—things invented against the dignity and nature of the immortals, since they can befall not only a man but even the most contemptible man—these they not only bear but even gladly hear.

And not only that, but they also decree that these things please the gods themselves, and that the gods are to be placated by them.

**5. 3.** Someone may say: "Let us distinguish these two kinds, the mythical and the physical—that is, the fabulous and the natural—from this civil kind now under discussion, the kind from which he himself also distinguished them, and let us now see how he sets forth the civil kind itself."

I see indeed why the fabulous ought to be set apart: because it is false, because it is shameful, because it is unworthy. But to wish to set the natural apart from the civil—what else is this than to confess that the civil itself is faulty?

For if that kind is natural, what has it deserving of blame, that it should be shut out? And if this kind called civil is not natural, what has it deserving of merit, that it should be admitted?

This, indeed, is the reason why he wrote about human matters first and about divine matters afterward: because in divine matters he followed not nature, but the institutions of human beings.

Let us look, then, at the civil theology as well. *The third kind is the one that citizens in the cities, and especially the priests, ought to know and administer. In it is treated which gods are to be worshiped publicly, and what sacred rites and sacrifices it is fitting for each one to perform.*

Let us attend further to what follows. *The first theology,* he says, *is best suited to the theater, the second to the world, the third to the city.* Who does not see to which he gave the palm? Certainly to the second, which he said above belongs to the philosophers.

For he testifies that this one pertains to the world, than which those men think nothing more excellent exists among things.

But did he distinguish or join those two theologies, the first and the third, namely that of the theater and that of the city?

For we see that what belongs to the city cannot at once also pertain to the world, although we see that cities exist in the world; for it can happen that in a city, according to false opinions, things are worshiped and believed whose nature is nowhere either in the world or outside the world. But where is the theater except in the city?

Who established the theater except the city? For what did it establish it except for the stage games? And where are the stage games except among the divine matters about which these books are composed with such great skill?

**6. 1.** O Marcus Varro, since you are the most acute of all men and beyond any doubt the most learned, yet still a man, not God, and not lifted up by the Spirit of God into truth and freedom to see and announce divine things, you do indeed discern how greatly divine matters are to be set apart from human trifles and lies;

but you are afraid to give offense to the most corrupt opinions and customs of the peoples in their public superstitions—opinions which you yourself perceive to be abhorrent to the nature of the gods, or even of such beings as the weakness of the human mind suspects to be in the elements of this world, when you consider them on every side, and which the whole of your literature resounds with.

What can the human mind do here, however excellent it may be? What does your human learning, however manifold and vast, avail you in these straits?

You desire to worship the natural gods; you are compelled to worship the civil ones.

You have found others, the fabulous gods, against whom you may more freely vomit out what you feel, and with which feeling you also drench, willy-nilly, those civil gods too.

For you say that the fabulous gods are suited to the theater, the natural to the world, the civil to the city,

whereas the world is a divine work, but cities and theaters are works of men; and the gods who are laughed at in the theaters are no other than those who are adored in the temples, nor do you put on games for any but those to whom you immolate victims.

How much more freely and subtly you would have made these divisions, by saying that some gods are natural, others established by human beings,

but that, concerning the gods established by human beings, the writings of the poets say one thing and those of the priests another,

and that both, nonetheless, are so friendly with each other in their fellowship of falsehood that both are pleasing to the demons, to whom the teaching of the truth is hostile!

## Both the fabulous and the civil theology are shameful . . .

**6. 2.** Setting aside for a little while, then, the theology they call natural, about which we must treat later, is it after all to be approved that eternal life should be sought or hoped for from the gods of the poets, of the theater, of the games, of the stage? God forbid;

rather, may the true God turn away so monstrous and sacrilegious a madness. What? Is eternal life to be demanded from gods who are pleased by these things and are placated by them, when their own crimes are enacted there?

No one, I think, raves to so great a precipice of the most frenzied impiety. Therefore by neither the fabulous nor the civil theology does anyone attain everlasting life.

For the one sows shameful things about the gods by inventing them, the other reaps them by favoring them;

the one scatters lies, the other gathers them;

the one assails divine matters with false charges, the other embraces, among divine matters, the games made of those charges;

the one makes the wicked fictions of men about the gods resound in songs, the other consecrates them in the festivals of the gods themselves;

the misdeeds and disgraces of the divinities the one sings, the other loves;

the one betrays or invents them, while the other either attests to the true ones or takes delight in the false ones as well.

Both are shameful, and both are damnable; but the one that belongs to the theater professes its shamefulness openly, while the one that belongs to the city is adorned with the other's shamefulness.

Is eternal life to be hoped for from that source by which this brief and temporal life is defiled?

Or does the fellowship of wicked men defile our life, if they insinuate themselves into our affections and assents, while the company of demons, who are worshiped by means of their own crimes, does not defile it?

If the crimes are true, how wicked the demons are! If false, how wickedly they are worshiped!

**6. 3.** When we say these things, it may perhaps seem to someone too ignorant of these matters that the only things celebrated about such gods which are unworthy of the divine majesty and ridiculously detestable are those that are sung in the songs of the poets and acted out in the stage games;

but that those sacred rites which not actors but priests perform are cleansed of and free from all dishonor.

If this were so, no one would ever have judged that the shameful things of the theater should be celebrated in their honor, nor would the gods themselves ever have commanded that they be performed for them.

But the reason there is no shame in doing such things in the theaters as an obedience to the gods is that similar things are done in the temples.

In short, when the aforementioned author tried to distinguish civil theology from the fabulous and the natural as a kind of third sort of its own, he wished it to be understood as blended from both rather than separated from both.

For he says that the things the poets write are too little for the peoples to follow, but the things the philosophers write are too much for it to profit the common crowd to examine.

*They are so abhorrent,* he says, *that nevertheless not a few things from both kinds have been taken up into the civil accounts.*

*Therefore the things that will be common with what is proper to us we shall write together with the civil matters; in regard to which our fellowship ought to be greater with the philosophers than with the poets*[^15].

So there is, then, no slight fellowship with the poets.

And yet in another place[^16] he says that, regarding the generations of the gods, the peoples were more inclined toward the poets than toward the natural philosophers.

For here he said what ought to be done, there what is done. He said that the natural philosophers wrote for the sake of usefulness, the poets for the sake of delight.

And on this account, the things written by the poets which the peoples ought not to follow are crimes of the gods—which nonetheless delight both the peoples and the gods.

For the poets write, as he says, for the sake of delight, not of usefulness; yet they write the very things that the gods seek and the peoples render.

**7. 1.** The fabulous theology of the theater and the stage, then, full of unworthiness and shamefulness, is brought back to the civil theology, and this whole theology, which is rightly judged to deserve blame and rejection, is a part of the one that is held to deserve worship and observance;

not, indeed, an incongruous part, as I have undertaken to show—one foreign to the whole body and unsuitably attached to it and hung upon it—but altogether consonant and most fittingly joined as a member of the same body.

For what else do those images, forms, ages, sexes, and attire of the gods show? Do the poets have a bearded Jupiter and a beardless Mercury, while the pontiffs do not?

Did the mimes give Priapus monstrous private parts, and not the priests as well? Or does he stand to be adored in the sacred places in a different shape from the one in which he comes forward to be laughed at in the theaters?

Are the old man Saturn and the youthful Apollo masks of the actors in such a way that they are not also statues in the shrines? Why are Forculus, who presides over doors, and Limentinus, who presides over the threshold, male gods, while among them Cardea, who guards the hinge, is female?

Are not these things found in the books of divine matters which serious poets judged unworthy of their songs? Does the theatrical Diana carry weapons, while the civil one is simply a maiden? Is the Apollo of the stage a lyre player, while the Delphic one is free from this art?

But these things are more decent in comparison with viler ones. What did they think about Jupiter himself, who placed his nurse on the Capitol?

Did they not bear witness to Euhemerus, who wrote, not with fabulous chatter but with historical diligence, that all such gods had been men and mortal?

And those who set "feasting gods," parasites of Jupiter, at his table—what else did they wish but that the sacred rites be like those of the mimes?

For if a mime had said that parasites of Jupiter were brought to his banquet, he would certainly have seemed to be seeking a laugh.

It was Varro who said it! And he said it not when mocking the gods, but when commending them;

the books of divine, not of human, matters testify that he wrote this, and not where he was setting forth the stage games, but where he was laying open the laws of the Capitol.

In short, by such things he is overcome and confesses that, just as they made the gods in human form, so they believed them to be delighted by human pleasures[^17].

**7. 2.** For the malign spirits, too, did not fail in their business of confirming these harmful opinions by the mockery of human minds. From this comes also that story of how the keeper of the temple of Hercules, being idle and at leisure, played at dice by himself, alternating with both hands, setting Hercules on one and himself on the other, on this condition: that if he himself won, he would prepare himself a dinner from the temple offerings and hire a girlfriend; but if the victory fell to Hercules, he would furnish this same thing, from his own money, for the pleasure of Hercules;

then, when he had been beaten by himself, as it were by Hercules, he gave to the god Hercules the dinner that was owed and the most renowned harlot Larentina. But she, when she had slept in the temple, saw in her dreams that Hercules had lain with her and had told her that, on departing from there, in the first young man she met she would find the reward which she ought to believe had been paid her by Hercules. And so, as she was leaving, when the very wealthy young man Tarutius first met her and, falling in love with her, kept her with him for a long while, she became his heir at his death. Having acquired a most ample fortune, and lest she seem ungrateful for the divine reward, she too, thinking it most acceptable to the divinities, wrote the Roman people her heir; and when she did not appear, her will was found. By these merits they say that she even earned divine honors.

**7. 3.** If the poets had invented these things, if the mimes had acted them out, they would without doubt be said to belong to the fabulous theology and would be judged to be separated from the dignity of the civil theology.

But since these disgraces are betrayed by so great a teacher as belonging not to the poets but to the peoples, not to the mimes but to the sacred rites, not to the theaters but to the temples—

that is, not to the fabulous but to the civil theology—

it is not in vain that the actors, by their playful arts, represent so great a shamefulness of the gods, but it is plainly in vain that the priests, by what they call sacred rites, try to represent a decency of the gods which is none at all.

There are sacred rites of Juno, and these were celebrated on her beloved island of Samos, where she was given in marriage to Jupiter;

there are sacred rites of Ceres, in which Proserpina, carried off by Pluto, is sought; there are sacred rites of Venus, in which her beloved Adonis, a most handsome youth slain by the tusk of a boar, is mourned; there are sacred rites of the Mother of the gods, in which Attis, a beautiful young man loved by her and, through womanly jealousy, castrated, is lamented even by the misfortune of the castrated men whom they call Galli.

Since these things are more misshapen than all the foulness of the stage, why is it that they strive to set apart, as it were, the fabulous fictions of the poets about the gods—belonging, of course, to the theater—from the civil theology, which they wish to belong to the city, as though setting unworthy and shameful things apart from honorable and worthy ones? And so it is rather a matter for which thanks should be owed to the actors, who spared the eyes of men and did not lay bare in their spectacles everything that is hidden within the walls of the sacred buildings.

What good is to be thought about their sacred rites that are covered in darkness, when the things brought forth into the light are so detestable? And certainly, what they do in secret by means of the castrated and the effeminate, let them see to it themselves; yet they were utterly unable to conceal those same men, wretchedly and shamefully unmanned and corrupted.

Let them persuade whom they can that they perform anything holy by means of such men, whom they cannot deny to be numbered and to move about among their holy things.

We do not know what they do, but we do know by what sort of men they do it.

But we do know what is done on the stage, where a castrated or an effeminate man has never entered, even into a chorus of harlots; and yet even these things are performed by the base and the infamous—for indeed they ought not to have been performed by honorable persons.

What, then, are those sacred rites for whose performance holiness chose such men as not even the obscenity of the stage admitted into itself?

## What the physical account says about the Mother of the gods and Saturn.

**8. 1.** But, they say, these things have certain physiological interpretations, as they call them, that is, interpretations of natural accounts. As though in this discussion we were seeking physiology and not theology, that is, an account not of nature but of God!

For although he who is the true God is God not by opinion but by nature, nevertheless not every nature is god; for the nature of a man and of a beast, of a tree and of a stone, is certainly a nature, and none of these is God. But if the chief point of this interpretation, when the rites of the Mother of the gods are in question, is certainly that the Mother of the gods is the earth, what need is there to seek further, what need to examine the rest[^18]?

What more evidently supports those who say that all these gods were men? For thus they are earth-born, thus the earth is their mother. But in the true theology the earth is the work of God, not his mother.

Yet in whatever way they interpret her sacred rites and refer them to the nature of things, for men to suffer what is womanly is not according to nature, but against nature.

This disease, this crime, this disgrace has among those sacred rites an open profession, which among the vicious morals of men scarcely wins confession even under torture. Then, if these sacred rites, which are convicted of being fouler than the shameful things of the stage, are excused and cleansed on the ground that they have their own interpretations by which they are shown to signify the nature of things, why are not the poetic ones likewise excused and cleansed?

For many have interpreted these too in the same manner, to such a degree that what is said by them most monstrously and most unspeakably—that Saturn devoured his own sons—some interpret thus: that the length of time[^19], which is signified by the name of Saturn, consumes whatever it itself begets; or, as the same Varro thinks, that Saturn pertains to the seeds, which fall back again into the earth from which they spring[^20]. And likewise others in other ways, and the rest similarly.

## The theatrical and the civil theology are of the same shamefulness.

**8. 2.** And yet it is called the fabulous theology, and, together with all its interpretations of this kind, it is rebuked, cast off, and condemned—and not only by the natural theology, which is that of the philosophers, but even by this civil theology we are discussing, which is asserted to belong to the cities and peoples; on the ground that it has falsely fabricated unworthy things about the gods, it is rightly marked off as deserving rejection, and that, no doubt, with this design:

that, since the most acute and most learned men by whom these things were written understood that both ought to be condemned—both that fabulous one and this civil one—but dared to condemn the fabulous and did not dare to condemn the civil;

they set forth the fabulous as deserving blame, and they exhibited the civil for comparison as being like it—

not so that the civil should be chosen to be held in preference to the fabulous,

but so that it should be understood to deserve rejection along with the fabulous, and thus, without danger to those

who were afraid to rebuke the civil theology, both being held in contempt, the one they call natural might find a place among the better minds.

For both the civil and the fabulous are fabulous, and both are civil; both will be found fabulous by anyone who prudently inspects the vanities and obscenities of both, and both civil by anyone who notices the stage games belonging to the fabulous theology in the festivals of the civil gods and in the divine matters of the cities.

How, then, is the power of giving eternal life attributed to any of these gods, whom their own images and sacred rites convict of being most like the fabulous gods—who have been most openly disapproved—in their forms, ages, sex, attire, marriages, generations, and rites? In all of which they are understood either to have been men, and to have had sacred and solemn rites established for them according to the life or death of each, the demons insinuating and confirming this error; or at any rate that, on some occasion or other, the most unclean spirits crept in to deceive human minds.

**9. 1.** What of this? The very functions of the divinities, so meanly and minutely cut up—on the ground of which they say one must make supplication to them according to the proper office of each, of which we have said, not indeed all[^21],

but we have already said much—do they not accord with the buffoonery of the mimes rather than with the divine dignity? If anyone were to engage two nurses for an infant, of whom one gave nothing but food and the other nothing but drink, just as these men engaged two goddesses for this purpose, Educa and Potina, he would surely seem to be a fool and to be doing in his own house something like a mime.

They hold that Liber is named from "liberation," because by his benefit males in coupling are freed by the emission of their seed; and that Libera does this same thing in females—she whom they also think to be Venus—because they hold that she too emits seed; and that for this reason the male part of the body is placed in the temple for Liber, the female for Libera.

To these things they add women assigned to Liber, and wine for stirring up lust. Thus the Bacchanalia were celebrated in the highest madness[^22]; where Varro himself confesses that such things could not have been done by the Bacchantes except with a mind disturbed.

These things, however, afterward displeased a saner senate, and it ordered them to be done away with[^23]. Here at last, perhaps, they finally perceived what unclean spirits, while they are taken for gods, can do in the minds of men. These things certainly would not be done in the theaters; for there they play, they do not rage—although to have gods who are delighted even by such games is like raving.

**9. 2.** But what is to be made of the fact that, while he distinguishes the religious man from the superstitious by this difference—that by the superstitious man the gods are feared, but by the religious man only reverenced as parents, not feared as enemies—and says they are all so good that it is easier for them to spare the guilty than to harm any innocent person[^24],

he nevertheless recalls that three guardian gods are engaged for a woman in childbed after the birth, lest the god Silvanus enter by night and harass her; and that, to signify these guardians, three men go around the threshold of the house by night, and first strike the threshold with an axe, then with a pestle, and third sweep it with brooms,

so that by these given signs of cultivation the god Silvanus may be prevented from entering, because trees are neither cut down nor pruned without iron, nor is grain prepared without a pestle, nor are crops heaped up without brooms;

and that from these three things three gods are named: Intercidona from the cutting of the axe, Pilumnus from the pestle, Deverra from the brooms; by which guardian gods the woman in childbed is preserved against the force of the god Silvanus[^25]?

Thus the guardianship of good gods would not avail against the savagery of a harmful god, unless there were several against one, and unless they fought against him—harsh, dreadful, uncouth, woodland as he is—with the signs of cultivation, as though with their opposites.

Is this, then, the innocence of the gods, this their concord? Are these the wholesome divinities of the cities, more to be laughed at than the mockeries of the theaters?

**9. 3.** When male and female are joined, the god Iugatinus is engaged; let this be borne. But the bride must be led home; the god Domiducus too is engaged; that she may be in the house, the god Domitius is engaged;

that she may remain with her husband, the goddess Manturna is added. What more is sought? Let human modesty be spared;

let the concupiscence of flesh and blood carry out the rest, the secret of modesty being seen to. Why is the bedchamber filled with a crowd of divinities, when even the groomsmen depart from it?

And it is filled for this purpose: not that the thought of their presence may make for greater care of chastity, but that, with these cooperating, the virginity of the woman—weak in her sex, frightened by the newness of it—may be taken away without any difficulty.

For the goddess Virginensis is present, and the father god Subigus, and the mother goddess Prema, and the goddess Pertunda, and Venus, and Priapus. What is this? If the man laboring in that work had to be helped at all by the gods, would not some one god or some one goddess suffice?

Would Venus alone be too little—she who is even said to be so named because without force a woman does not cease to be a virgin[^26]?

If there is any shame in human beings—which there is not in the divinities—when the married couple believe that so many gods of both sexes are present and intent on this work, are they not so affected with shame that he is moved less and she struggles more?

And surely, if the goddess Virginensis is present that the maiden's girdle may be loosed; if the god Subigus is present that she may be brought under the man; if the goddess Prema is present that, once brought under, she may be held down so as not to move—what is the goddess Pertunda doing there?

Let her blush, let her go outside; let the husband too do something. It is most dishonorable that anyone but he should perform what she is named for. But perhaps she is tolerated because she is said to be a goddess, not a god. For if a male were believed in, and were called Pertundus, the husband would ask greater help against him for his wife's chastity than the woman in childbed asks against Silvanus.

But why should I say this, when Priapus too is there, an excessive male, upon whose monstrous and most shameful member the new bride was bidden to sit, after the most honorable and most religious custom of matrons?

**9. 4.** Let them go on, then, and let them, with whatever subtlety they can, try to distinguish the civil theology from the fabulous theology, the cities from the theaters, the temples from the stages, the sacred rites of the pontiffs from the songs of the poets,

as though distinguishing honorable things from shameful, truthful from deceitful, weighty from trivial, serious from playful, things to be sought from things to be rejected.

We understand what they are doing: they know that the theatrical and fabulous theology hangs upon the civil one and is reflected back from it, in the songs of the poets, as from a mirror;

and therefore, having set forth the civil one, which they dare not condemn, they more freely accuse and rebuke that image of it, so that those who recognize what they mean may detest this very face of which the other is the image;

a face which, however, the gods themselves, beholding themselves as it were in the same mirror, so love that who and what they are may be better seen in both.

Hence they have even compelled their worshipers, by terrible commands, to dedicate to themselves the uncleanness of the fabulous theology, to set it in their solemnities, to hold it among their divine matters,

and thus they have both more manifestly taught that they are themselves most unclean spirits, and have made that theatrical theology—abject and reprobate—a member and part of this civil one, as though it were chosen and approved;

so that, since the whole is shameful and deceitful and contains within itself fictitious gods, one part of it is in the writings of the priests, the other in the songs of the poets.

Whether it has also other parts is another question; for now, on account of Varro's division, I have, I think, sufficiently shown that both the civil and the theatrical theology belong to the one civil theology. Hence, since both are of like shamefulness, absurdity, unworthiness, and falsehood, far be it from true religious souls that eternal life should be hoped for from either the one or the other.

**9. 5.** In short, Varro himself began to recall and enumerate the gods from the conception of a human being, the number of whom he began with Janus, and he carried that series all the way to the death of a decrepit man, and he closed the gods pertaining to the human being himself with the goddess Nenia, who is sung at the funerals of the old;

then he began to set forth other gods, who pertained not to the human being himself, but to the things that are a human being's, such as food and clothing and whatever else is necessary for this life[^27], showing in all of them what is the function of each and why supplication ought to be made to each; in the whole of which diligence he pointed out or named no gods from whom eternal life is to be sought—the one thing for the sake of which we are properly Christians.

Who, then, would be so dull as not to understand that this man, by so diligently setting forth and laying open the civil theology, and by showing it to be like that fabulous, unworthy, and shameful one, and by teaching clearly enough that the fabulous itself is a part of this civil one, is working to make a place in the minds of men only for that natural theology which he says belongs to the philosophers—

with such subtlety that he rebukes the fabulous, but indeed does not dare to rebuke the civil, yet by betraying it shows it to be reprehensible,

and thus, both being condemned in the judgment of those who rightly understand, the natural alone may remain to be chosen? About which, in its own place, with the help of the true God, we must discuss more diligently[^28].

## Seneca scorns the unmanning . . .

**10. 1.** The freedom, indeed, which this man lacked—so that he did not dare openly to rebuke this civil theology, so very like the theatrical, as he rebuked the latter—was not lacking to Annaeus Seneca, who by certain indications we find to have flourished in the times of our apostles; not lacking wholly, to be sure, but in some part. For it was present to him as he wrote; it was lacking to him as he lived.

For in that book which he composed against the superstitions, he himself rebukes this civil and city theology far more copiously and more vehemently than Varro rebuked the theatrical and fabulous[^29].

For when he was treating of the images, he says: *They dedicate, in the cheapest and most lifeless material, gods that are sacred, immortal, and inviolable; they clothe them in the shapes of men and beasts and fishes, and some indeed in bodies of mixed sex and of diverse kinds; they call them divinities, which, if they suddenly came to life and met us, would be reckoned monsters.*

Then, somewhat later, when, in commending the natural theology, he had set out the opinions of certain philosophers, he put a question to himself and said:

*At this point someone says: Am I to believe that heaven and earth are gods, and that there are some above the moon and some below it? Am I to bear with either Plato or the Peripatetic Strato, of whom the one made a god without a body, the other a god without a mind?*

And answering this, he says: *What then, after all, do the dreams of Titus Tatius, or of Romulus, or of Tullus Hostilius seem to you truer? Tatius dedicated the goddess Cluacina; Romulus, Picus and Tiberinus; Hostilius, Fear and Pallor, the most loathsome affections of men,*

*of which the one is a motion of a terrified mind, the other not even a disease but a mere color of the body. Will you rather believe these to be divinities and receive them into heaven?*

But how freely he wrote about the rites themselves, cruelly shameful as they are! *One man*, he says, *cuts off his own virile parts, another gashes his arms. Where do they fear the gods' wrath, who thus earn their favor?*

*But the gods ought to be worshiped by no such manner, even if they wish it. So great is the frenzy of a mind disturbed and driven from its seat, that the gods are placated in a way in which not even the most loathsome of men, those of a cruelty handed down into fable, rage.*

*Tyrants have mangled the limbs of some, but they ordered no one to mangle his own. For the pleasure of royal lust some have been castrated, but no one, at his master's bidding, laid hands on himself that he might cease to be a man.*

*They butcher themselves in the temples; they make supplication with their own wounds and blood.*

*If anyone has leisure to look at what they do and what they suffer, he will find things so unbecoming to the honorable, so unworthy of the free, so unlike the sane, that no one would have doubted that they were mad, if they raged in fewer numbers;*

*as it is, the multitude of the insane is the defense of their sanity.*

**10. 2.** Now those things which he recalls as wont to be done in the Capitol itself, and which he convicts altogether fearlessly—who would believe they are done except by people mocking or raving?

For when he had derided that, in the Egyptian rites, Osiris is mourned as lost and presently celebrated with great joy as found—since both his loss and his finding are feigned, yet that grief and that gladness are truly expressed by those who have lost nothing and found nothing—he says: *This frenzy, however, has its fixed time. It is tolerable to be mad once a year.*

*Go into the Capitol; you will be ashamed of the madness made public, of the office which empty frenzy has assigned to itself.*

*One man whispers names to God, another announces the hours to Jupiter; one is a lictor, another an anointer who, with an empty motion of his arms, mimics one anointing.*

*There are women who dress the hair of Juno and Minerva* (standing far from the temple, not only from the image, they move their fingers in the manner of those adorning), *there are women who hold a mirror;*

*there are those who summon the gods as sureties for their bail bonds, there are those who present petitions and instruct them in their case.*

*A learned chief mime, an old man now decrepit, used to perform his mime daily in the Capitol, as though the gods gladly watched him whom men had ceased to watch. Every kind of craftsman sits idle there, employed in the service of the immortal gods.*

And a little later he says: *These, however, even if they promise the god a superfluous service, promise no shameful or infamous one. There sit certain women in the Capitol who think themselves loved by Jupiter; they are not frightened even by the regard of Juno, the most wrathful of goddesses, if you are willing to believe the poets.*

**10. 3.** This freedom Varro did not have; he dared only to rebuke the poetic theology, and did not dare to rebuke the civil, which this man cut to pieces. But if we attend to the truth, the temples where these things are done are worse than the theaters where they are feigned.

Hence, regarding these rites of the civil theology, Seneca rather chose these roles for the wise man: that he should not hold them in the religion of his soul, but should feign them in his acts. For he says: *All these things the wise man will observe as commanded by laws, not as pleasing to the gods.*

And a little later he says: *What of the fact that we even join the gods in marriages—and not even piously, of brothers and sisters! We mate Bellona with Mars, Venus with Vulcan, Salacia with Neptune. Yet some we leave unwed, as though a match had failed, especially since some goddesses are widows, such as Populonia, or Fulgora, and the goddess Rumina;*

*at whom I do not wonder that a suitor was wanting. All that ignoble crowd of gods which long superstition has heaped up over a long age we shall worship in such a way that we remember that its worship pertains more to custom than to reality.*

Neither those laws, then, nor custom established in the civil theology anything that was pleasing to the gods or pertained to reality. But this man, whom the philosophers had as it were made free, nevertheless—because he was a senator of the illustrious Roman people—worshiped what he rebuked, performed what he reproved, adored what he blamed; because, no doubt, philosophy had taught him something great, namely, that he should not be superstitious in the world, but that, on account of the laws of the citizens and the customs of men, he should not indeed perform the part of an actor feigning in the theater, but should imitate it in the temple;

all the more damnably because he so performed the things he did falsely that the people supposed he performed them truly; whereas the actor, by playing, would rather delight than deceive by deception.

## What Seneca thinks of the worship of the Jews.

**11.** Here, among other superstitions of the civil theology, he also rebukes the sacred observances of the Jews, and especially the Sabbaths, affirming that they act unprofitably, in that, by those single days interposed every seventh, they lose nearly a seventh part of their life in idleness, and are harmed by not doing many things that press for action in the meantime.

Yet the Christians, even then most hateful to the Jews, he did not dare to mention on either side, lest he either praise them against the old custom of his country, or rebuke them perhaps against his own will.

When he was speaking, indeed, of those Jews, he said: *Meanwhile the custom of that most wicked nation has so prevailed that it has now been received throughout all lands; the conquered have given laws to the conquerors.*

He marveled as he said these things, and, not knowing what was being divinely brought about, he plainly added a sentence by which he showed what he thought of the rationale of their observances. For he said: *They, however, know the causes of their rite; the greater part of the people does what it does not know why it does.*

But concerning the sacred observances of the Jews—why and how far they were established by divine authority, and afterward, at the time when it was fitting, taken away by the same authority from the people of God, to whom the mystery of eternal life was revealed—we have spoken elsewhere, especially when we were arguing against the Manicheans[^30], and in this work it must be said in a more suitable place[^31].

## The worship of many gods contributes nothing to eternal life.

**12.** Now, concerning the three theologies which the Greeks call mythical, physical, and political, and which in Latin can be called fabulous, natural, and civil—since eternal life is to be hoped for neither from the fabulous, which even the worshipers of the many false gods have most freely rebuked, nor from the civil, of which the fabulous is convicted of being a part, and to which the fabulous is found most like, or even worse—

if to anyone what has been said in this volume is not enough, let him add also those things which in the earlier books, and especially in the fourth, were argued at length about God the giver of happiness[^32].

For to whom but to happiness alone, for the sake of eternal life, would men have to be consecrated, if happiness were a goddess? But since it is not a goddess but a gift of God, to what God are we to be consecrated but to the giver of happiness, we who, with devout love, love the eternal life in which there is true and full happiness? But that none of these gods—who are worshiped with such great shamefulness, and, unless they are so worshiped, grow angry far more shamefully, and on this account confess themselves to be most unclean spirits—is the giver of happiness, no one, I think, ought to doubt, from the things that have been said. And further, he who does not give happiness, how could he give eternal life?

For we call that life eternal in which there is happiness without end. For if the soul lives in eternal punishments, by which the unclean spirits themselves will also be tormented, that is rather eternal death than life.

For there is no greater or worse death than where death does not die. But since the nature of the soul, in that it was created immortal, cannot be without some kind of life, its highest death is alienation from the life of God in the eternity of punishment.

Eternal life, then—that is, life happy without any end—he alone gives, who gives true happiness.

And since those whom this civil theology worships have been convicted of being unable to give it,

they are not to be worshiped—not only on account of those temporal and earthly things, as we showed in the preceding five books, but much more on account of the eternal life that is to come after death, which in this one book we have treated, with their own help as well.

But since the force of inveterate custom has its roots too deep, if to anyone I seem to have argued too little about this civil theology's deserving to be rejected and shunned, let him turn his mind to another volume, which, with God's help, is to be joined to this one.
