# Book III: The deeds of the Romans are surveyed with judgment

**1**. I think enough has now been said about the evils of morals and of souls, which are above all to be guarded against—that the false gods took no care to come to the aid of their worshiping people, that it might not be pressed down by the heap of those evils, but rather acted that it might be pressed down most of all.

Now I see that I must speak about those evils which alone these people refuse to suffer, such as are famine, disease, war, despoiling, captivity, slaughter, and any like things we have already recalled in the first book. For the wicked count as evils only those which do not make men wicked; nor do they blush, among the goods which they praise, to be themselves wicked who praise them, and they are more vexed if they have a bad farm than if they have a bad life, as if this were the greatest good of a man, to have all things good except himself.

But not even such evils, which alone these people dread, did their gods, when they were freely worshiped by them, stand in the way of, that they might not befall them. For when, at various times in various places before the coming of our Redeemer, the human race was worn down by innumerable and some even incredible disasters, what other gods than these did the world worship, except one Hebrew people and certain men outside that people, wherever they were worthy of divine grace by the most hidden and most just judgment of God? But, not to make it too long, I will be silent about the most grievous evils of other nations everywhere: as far as pertains to Rome and the Roman empire, I will speak only of this—that is, of the city itself properly, and whatever lands were joined to it either by alliance or subjected by condition—what they suffered before the coming of Christ, when they already pertained to its body, as it were, of the commonwealth.

## The many gods did not profit Troy.

**2**. First, Troy itself, or Ilium, whence is the origin of the Roman people (for it must not be passed over or dissembled, which I touched on in the first book also[^1]), having and worshiping these same gods, why was it conquered, captured, and destroyed by the Greeks?

*To Priam*, they say, *were repaid the paternal perjuries of Laomedon*[^2]. Is it then true that Apollo and Neptune served that same Laomedon in hired labors? For he is said to have promised them a wage and to have sworn falsely[^3]. I wonder that Apollo, named a diviner, labored in so great a work not knowing that Laomedon was going to deny what was promised. Although it did not befit even Neptune himself, his uncle, the brother of Jupiter, the king of the sea, to be ignorant of the future.

For Homer brings him in divining something great about the stock of Aeneas, from whose posterity Rome is—since the same poet is said to have lived before that city was founded[^4]—whom he also snatched away in a cloud, as he says, lest he be killed by Achilles, *though he desired to overturn from the bottom* (which in Virgil he confesses) *the walls of perjured Troy, built by his own hands*[^5]. Not knowing, then, that Laomedon was going to deny them the wage, so great gods, Neptune and Apollo, were builders of the Trojan walls without thanks and to the ungrateful.

Let them see whether it is not graver to believe such gods than to swear falsely by such gods. For Homer himself did not easily believe this, who makes Neptune indeed fight against the Trojans, but Apollo for the Trojans, although the fable narrates that both were offended by that perjury. If, then, they believe the fables, let them blush to worship such divinities; if they do not believe the fables, let them not put forward the Trojan perjuries, or let them wonder that the gods punished the Trojan perjuries and loved the Roman ones.

For whence did the conspiracy of Catiline, in so great and so corrupt a city, have even a great supply of those whom their hand and tongue fed by perjury or by civil blood[^6]? For what else did senators, so often corrupted in trials, so often the people in their votes or in whatever causes were conducted before them in assemblies, do, except sin even by swearing falsely? For with the most corrupt morals the ancient custom of swearing was preserved to this end, not that they might be kept from crimes by the fear of religion, but that perjuries too might be added to the rest of their crimes.

## The fables of the gods are repugnant to a rule for leading life.

**3**. There is therefore no reason why the gods, by whom, as they say, that empire had stood[^7], since they are proved to have been conquered by the prevailing Greeks, should be imagined to have been angry at the perjuring Trojans.

Nor were they incensed at the adultery of Paris, so as to desert Troy, as again they are defended by some. For they are wont to be authors and teachers of sins, not avengers. *The city of Rome*, says Sallust, *as I have received it, the Trojans founded and held at the beginning, who, fugitives under the leadership of Aeneas, wandered with uncertain dwellings*[^8]. If, then, the divinities judged that the adultery of Paris was to be avenged, it had to be punished either more in the Romans, or at any rate even in the Romans, because the mother of Aeneas did this. But how did they hate that shameful deed in him, who did not hate (to omit other things) in their own ally Venus what she had committed with Anchises, from whom she had borne Aeneas? Or was it because the one was done with Menelaus indignant, but the other with Vulcan consenting? For the gods, I suppose, are not jealous of their wives, so far that they even deign to have them in common with men.

Perhaps I am thought to mock the fables and not to treat seriously a cause of such weight. Let us not, then, believe, if you please, that Aeneas is the son of Venus: behold, I grant it, if not also that Romulus is the son of Mars. But if the latter, why not the former too?

Or is it lawful for gods to be mingled with human females, but wrong for male humans to be mingled with goddesses? A hard, or rather an unbelievable condition, that what was permitted to Mars in intercourse by the right of Venus should not be permitted to Venus herself in her own right.

But both are confirmed by Roman authority. For the more recent Caesar believed Venus his grandmother[^9] no less than the more ancient Romulus believed Mars his father.

**4**. Someone will say: "Do you then believe these things?" I indeed do not believe them.

For their most learned man Varro too almost confesses—though not boldly nor confidently—that these things are false. But he says that it is useful to cities that brave men should believe themselves, even if it be false, to have been begotten of the gods, so that in this way the human soul, bearing as it were the confidence of a divine stock, may more boldly presume to attempt great things, act more vehemently, and on this account, by this very security, accomplish them more happily[^10].

You see how broad a place this opinion of Varro, expressed as best I could in my own words, opens to falsehood, so that we understand from it that more sacred and as it were religious things could be fabricated, where even lies about the gods themselves were thought to profit the citizens.

**5**. But whether Venus could bear Aeneas from intercourse with Anchises, or Mars beget Romulus from intercourse with Numitor's daughter, let us leave in the middle. For almost such a question arises even from our Scriptures, in which it is asked whether the transgressing angels lay with the daughters of men, from which, when giants were born—that is, exceedingly great and strong men—the earth was then filled[^11].

Accordingly, let our discussion for now be referred to both. For if those things are true which are read among them about the mother of Aeneas and the father of Romulus, how can the adulteries of men displease the gods, which they bear concordantly in themselves? But if they are false, not even thus can those be angry at true human adulteries who are delighted even by their own false ones.

To this is added that, if the story about Mars is not believed, so that this about Venus too is not believed, then by no pretext of divine intercourse is the cause of Romulus's mother defended.

But she was a Vestal priestess, and therefore the gods ought rather to have avenged that sacrilegious shameful deed upon the Romans than the adultery of Paris upon the Trojans.

For the ancient Romans themselves even buried alive the priestesses of Vesta detected in unchastity, but adulterous women they punished, though with some condemnation, yet with no death: so much more gravely did they avenge what they thought the divine sanctuaries than human bedchambers.

## Equal desert, unequal lot of Rome and Troy.

**6**. I add another thing: that, if the sins of men displeased those divinities, so that, offended by the deed of Paris, they gave deserted Troy over to sword and fires, the brother of Romulus killed ought to move them more against the Romans than the Greek husband mocked against the Trojans; the parricide of the nascent city ought to provoke them more than the adultery of the reigning one. Nor does it matter to the cause we are now pleading whether Romulus ordered this to be done or Romulus did it, which many through shamelessness deny, many through shame doubt, many through grief dissemble.

Nor, then, let us delay in inquiring into that matter more diligently through the weighed testimonies of many writers: it is openly established that the brother of Romulus was killed, not by enemies, not by strangers. If Romulus either perpetrated or commanded this, he himself was more the head of the Romans than Paris was of the Trojans; why, then, did that ravisher of another's wife provoke the wrath of the gods against the Trojans, and this slayer of his own brother invite the protection of those same gods for the Romans?

But if that crime is foreign to the deed and command of Romulus: since it certainly ought to have been avenged, the whole city did this, in that the whole despised it, and killed not now a brother but a father, which is worse. For each was a founder, where one, removed by crime, was not permitted to be a ruler.

There is no reason, as I judge, why it should be said what evil Troy merited, that the gods should desert it so that it could be extinguished, and what good Rome merited, that the gods should inhabit it so that it could be increased; except that, conquered, they fled from there and betook themselves to these people, whom they might equally deceive;

indeed they both remained there to deceive, in their own manner, those who would again inhabit those same lands, and here, by exercising the same arts of their deceit even more, gloried in greater honors.

## Troy was destroyed undeservedly.

**7**. For certainly, when civil wars were already teeming, what wretched thing had Ilium committed, that it should be overthrown by Fimbria, a most wicked man of the Marian party, far more fiercely and cruelly than once by the Greeks? For then both many fled from there and many, taken captive, at least lived in slavery; but Fimbria first set forth an edict that no one should be spared, and burned the whole city and all the men in it with fire.

This Ilium merited, not from the Greeks whom it had provoked by its own iniquity, but from the Romans whom it had propagated by its own calamity—those gods held in common availing nothing to repel these things, or, what is true, having no power. Did *all the gods then too depart, the shrines and altars left behind*[^12], by whom that town had stood, restored after the ancient fires and ruins of the Greeks?

But if they had departed, I ask the cause, and the better I find that of the townsmen, the worse I find that of the gods. For they had closed their gates against Fimbria, to keep the city whole for Sulla; hence he, angry, burned them, or rather utterly extinguished them. But Sulla was still the leader of the better civil party, still striving by arms to recover the commonwealth: of these good beginnings he had not yet the evil outcomes. What better, then, could the citizens of that town do, what more honorable, what more faithful, what more worthy of their Roman kinship, than to keep the city for the better cause of the Romans and to close their gates against the parricide of the Roman commonwealth?

But to how great a destruction this turned for them, let the defenders of the gods attend. The gods deserted the adulterers and left Ilium to the flames of the Greeks, that from its ashes a chaster Rome might be born: why did they afterward also desert that same city, kinsman to the Romans, not rebelling against Rome its noble daughter, but keeping a most constant and most pious fidelity to its more just party, and leave it to be destroyed not by the brave men of the Greeks, but by the foulest man of the Romans?

Or if the cause of the Sullan party, for which the wretched men, keeping the city, had closed their gates, displeased the gods: why did they promise and foretell to that same Sulla such great goods? Or are they here too recognized as flatterers of the fortunate rather than defenders of the unfortunate?

Not, then, was Ilium even then overthrown when it was deserted by them. For the demons, always most vigilant for deceiving, did what they could. For when all the images had been overthrown and burned along with the town, the image of Minerva alone, under so great a ruin of that temple, as Livy writes[^13], is reported to have stood whole—not that it might be said, *O ancestral gods, under whose divinity Troy ever is*[^14], to their praise, but that it might not be said, *all the gods departed, the shrines and altars left behind*, to their defense. For they were permitted to be able to do that, not so that they might be proved powerful, but so that they might be convicted of being present.

**8**. To the gods of Ilium, then, after the lesson of Troy itself, by what prudence, after all, was Rome committed to be guarded? Someone might say that they were already accustomed to dwell at Rome, when Ilium fell as Fimbria stormed it. Whence, then, did the image of Minerva stand? Then, if they were at Rome when Fimbria destroyed Ilium, perhaps they were at Ilium when Rome itself was taken and burned by the Gauls; but, as they are most acute of hearing and most swift of movement, at the voice of the goose they quickly returned, that they might at least guard the Capitoline hill, which had remained; but to defend the other things they were warned to return too late.

## What rites and peace there were under King Numa.

**9**. These gods too are believed to have aided Numa Pompilius, the successor of Romulus, so that throughout the whole time of his reign he had peace and closed the gates of Janus, which are wont to stand open in wars—by this merit, namely, that he established many rites for the Romans[^15]. But that man was to be congratulated for so great a leisure, only if he had known to spend it on wholesome matters and, neglecting a most pernicious curiosity, to seek out the true God with true piety.

But now the gods did not confer that leisure on him; but they would perhaps have deceived him less, if they had not found him at leisure. For the less occupied they found him, the more they themselves occupied him. For what he contrived, and by what arts he could ally such gods to himself or to that city, Varro discloses[^16]; which, if it please the Lord, will be discussed more diligently in its place[^17]. But now, because the question is about their benefits:

peace is a great benefit, but it is the benefit of the true God, for the most part bestowed even, like the sun, like the rain, and the other supports of life, upon the ungrateful and worthless. But if those gods conferred this so great good on Rome or on Pompilius, why did they never afterward grant it to the Roman empire through its very praiseworthy times? Or were the rites more useful when they were being instituted than when, instituted, they were being celebrated?

And yet then they did not yet exist, but were being added that they might exist; but afterward they already existed, and were being preserved that they might profit. What is it, then, that those forty-three—or, as others will have it, thirty-nine—years were passed in so long a peace with Numa reigning[^18], and afterward, with the rites instituted and the gods themselves, who had been invited by those same rites, now as protectors and guardians, scarcely, after so many years from the founding of the city down to Augustus, is one year recalled, after the first Punic War, as a great miracle, in which the Romans could close the gates of war?

**10**. Or do they answer that, except by continual wars succeeding one another without break, the Roman empire could not be so far and wide increased and made famous with such great glory? A fit cause indeed! that, in order for the empire to be great, it should have to be restless? Is it not, in the bodies of men, more satisfactory to have a modest stature with health than to arrive at some giant bulk by perpetual afflictions, nor, when you have arrived, to rest, but to be agitated by evils the greater the greater the limbs? But what evil would there be—or rather, not very much good—if those times endured which Sallust touched on, where he says: *therefore at the beginning kings (for that was the first name of rule on earth) were divided: some exercised their mind, others their body; even then the life of men was conducted without desire, his own things were sufficiently pleasing to each*[^19]? Or, that the empire might be so greatly increased, ought that to have happened which Virgil detests, saying: *until gradually a worse and discolored age and the madness of war and the love of having succeeded*[^20]?

But plainly, for so many wars undertaken and waged, there is a just defense of the Romans: that not greed for attaining human praise, but the necessity of guarding their safety and liberty, compelled them to resist enemies rushing upon them inopportunely. Be it so, plainly. For *after their affairs*, as Sallust himself writes, *increased in laws, morals, lands, seemed prosperous enough and powerful enough, as most things of mortals are held, envy arose from opulence. Therefore neighboring kings and peoples tried them by war; a few of their friends were a help, for the rest, struck with fear, kept away from dangers. But the Romans, intent at home and in the field, hastened, prepared, exhorted one another, went to meet the enemy, covered with arms their liberty, fatherland, and parents. Afterward, when by valor they had repelled the dangers, they bore aid to their allies and friends, and procured friendships more by giving than by receiving benefits*[^21]. Fittingly Rome grew by these arts.

But with Numa reigning, that there might be so long a peace, did the wicked rush in and try them by war, or did none of these things happen, so that that peace could persist? For if even then Rome was assailed by wars, and arms were not borne to meet arms: by whatever means it was brought about that the enemies, overcome by no battle, terrified by no martial onset, were quieted, by these means it would always be brought about, and Rome, with the gates of Janus closed, would always reign in peace.

But if this was not in their power, then Rome had peace not as long as their gods, but as long as its neighboring men round about willed, who provoked it by no war; unless perhaps such gods will dare to sell even to a man what another man either willed or did not will.

It matters indeed—now by their own fault—how far evil minds these demons are permitted either to terrify or to incite; but if they could always do this, and nothing else were often done otherwise against their effort by a more secret and higher power, they would always have in their power peaces and warlike victories, which almost always happen through the motions of human souls; which, however, are for the most part brought about against their will, as not only the fables that lie much and scarcely indicate or signify anything of the truth confess, but even Roman history itself.

**11**. For not from any other source was that Apollo of Cumae, when war was being waged against the Achaeans and King Aristonicus, announced to have wept for four days[^22]; by which prodigy the soothsayers, terrified, when they thought that the image ought to be thrown into the sea, the elders of Cumae interposed and related that such a prodigy had appeared in the same figure in the war both of Antiochus and of Perseus, and, because it had turned out happily for the Romans, they testified that by decree of the senate gifts had been sent to that same Apollo of theirs. Then the soothsayers, summoned as more skilled, answered that the weeping of the image of Apollo was therefore prosperous for the Romans, since the Cumaean colony was Greek, and Apollo, weeping, had signified grief and disaster to his own lands, from which he had been summoned—that is, to Greece itself. Then soon it was announced that King Aristonicus was conquered and captured, whom Apollo certainly did not wish to be conquered, and grieved, and indicated this even by the tears of his stone.

Then the soothsayers, summoned as men supposedly more expert, answered that the weeping of Apollo’s statue was favorable to the Romans, because Cumae was a Greek colony: by weeping, Apollo had signified mourning and disaster for his own country, from which he had been summoned, that is, for Greece itself. Soon afterward it was reported that King Aristonicus had been defeated and captured. Apollo, of course, had not wished him to be defeated and grieved over it, indicating this even by the tears of his stone image.

Whence, not altogether incongruously, the morals of the demons are described in the songs of the poets—fabulous, indeed, yet similar to the truth. For Diana grieved for Camilla in Virgil, and Hercules wept for Pallas who was about to die[^23].

Hence perhaps Numa Pompilius too, abounding in peace, but not knowing nor inquiring by whose giving, when at leisure he was considering to what gods he might commit the safety and kingdom of Rome to be guarded, and did not think that that true and almighty supreme God cared about these earthly things, and recalled that the Trojan gods, whom Aeneas had brought, had been able to preserve long neither the Trojan nor the Lavinian kingdom founded by Aeneas himself: thought that others should be provided, whom he might apply to those former gods—who had either already passed over to Rome with Romulus, or were at some time to pass over when Alba was overthrown—either as guards to fugitives, as it were, or as helpers to the weak.

he thought that other gods should be provided, to serve those earlier ones—who had either already migrated to Rome with Romulus or would migrate there when Alba was destroyed—as guards for fugitives, so to speak, or as helpers for the powerless.

## Foreign gods do not soften evils.

**12**. Nor yet did Rome deign to be content with these rites, which Pompilius had there established so many. For it did not yet have the supreme temple of Jupiter himself; for King Tarquin built the Capitol there[^24]; but Aesculapius journeyed about from Epidaurus to Rome[^25], that as a most skilled physician he might more gloriously exercise his art in the most noble city; the mother of the gods too, I know not whence, from Pessinus[^26]; for it was unworthy that, while her son already presided over the Capitoline hill, she herself should still lie hidden in an ignoble place.

But if she is the mother of all the gods, she not only followed certain of her sons to Rome, but even preceded others who were also to follow. I wonder indeed whether she bore Cynocephalus, who came long afterward from Egypt. Whether the goddess Fever too was born of her, let Aesculapius, her great-grandson, see; but from wherever she was born, the foreign gods will not, I suppose, dare to call her, a Roman citizen goddess, ignoble.

Under the protection of so many gods (who can number them, native and foreign, of heaven and earth, of the underworld and the sea, of springs and rivers, and, as Varro says, *certain and uncertain*, and in all the kinds of gods, as in animals, males and females[^27]?)—under the protection, then, of so many gods established, Rome ought not to have been agitated and afflicted by such great and horrible disasters, of which I will recall a few out of many. For it had gathered too many gods by its great smoke, as if by a given signal, for its protection; and by instituting and providing for them temples, altars, sacrifices, priests, it offended the supreme true God, to whom alone these things rightly done are owed.

And indeed it lived happier with fewer; but the greater it became, like a ship its sailors, the more it thought ought to be applied; believing, I suppose, that those fewer gods, under whom it had lived better in comparison with a worse life, did not suffice to assist its greatness.

For first, under the kings themselves, except Numa Pompilius, of whom I have already spoken above, how great an evil of discordant strife it was that compelled the brother of Romulus to be killed!

## The rape of the Sabine maidens was a monstrous crime.

**13**. How could neither Juno, who with her Jupiter already *cherished the Romans, lords of the world and the toga-clad nation*[^28], nor Venus herself help her descendants of Aeneas, so that they might obtain marriages by a good and equitable custom, and so great a disaster of this lack rushed upon them, that they seized the women by guile and soon were compelled to fight with their fathers-in-law, so that the wretched women, not yet reconciled to their husbands after the wrong, were already dowered with the blood of their parents[^29]? But, it is said, in this conflict the Romans conquered their neighbors. With how many and how great wounds and deaths on both sides, of kinsmen so near and borderers, did these victories stand! On account of one Caesar, a father-in-law, and one son-in-law of his, Pompey, when Caesar's daughter, Pompey's wife, was already dead, with how great and how just an instinct of grief does Lucan cry out: *wars more than civil over the Emathian plains, and right given to crime, we sing*[^30].

The Romans conquered, then, that with bloody hands they might wrest from the daughters of their fathers-in-law, amid the slaughter of those fathers, their wretched embraces; nor did those women dare to weep for their slain fathers, lest they offend their victorious husbands, for whom—while these were still fighting—they did not know on whose behalf to make their vows. To such marriages not Venus but Bellona made the Roman people a gift; or perhaps that infernal fury Alecto, with Juno now favoring them, had more license against them than when she had been stirred up at her prayers against Aeneas[^31].

Andromache was more happily taken captive than those Roman marriages were wed. Slavish though they were, yet after her embraces Pyrrhus killed none of the Trojans; but the Romans killed their fathers-in-law in battles, whose daughters they were already embracing in their bridal chambers. She, subjected to the victor, could only grieve, not fear, the death of her own[^32]; these, allied to combatants, feared the deaths of their parents as their husbands went forth, grieved them as they returned, and had neither their fear free nor their grief.

For at the destruction of their kindred—brothers, parents—they were either piously tormented or cruelly gladdened at the victories of their husbands. To this was added that, as the chances of wars are alternate, some lost their husbands by the sword of their parents, some by the sword of both their parents and their husbands.

For not even among the Romans were those crises small, since indeed it came even to the siege of the city, and with the gates closed they defended themselves; and when these had been opened by guile and the enemies admitted within the walls, in the very forum a wicked and too atrocious battle was joined between sons-in-law and fathers-in-law, and those ravishers too were being overcome, and, often fleeing among their own houses, more grievously befouled their former victories, though these too were shameful and to be lamented.

Here, however, Romulus, now despairing of the valor of his men, prayed Jupiter that they might stand, and from this occasion Jupiter found the name "Stayer"[^33]; nor would there have been an end of so great an evil, had not those ravished women darted forth with torn hair, and, prostrating themselves before their parents, soothed their most just wrath, not by victorious arms, but by suppliant piety[^34].

Then Romulus was compelled to bear Titus Tatius, king of the Sabines, as a partner in the kingdom, he who was impatient of a sharer who was his own twin: but when would he long tolerate this man too, who did not endure his brother and twin? Hence, this man too being killed, that he might be the greater god, he obtained the kingdom alone.

What are these rights of marriage, these incitements of wars, these covenants of brotherhood, of kinship, of society, of divinity? What, finally, is the life of the city under so many guardian gods? You see how much could be said here, and how many things, did not the attention to what remains of our work claim us, and our discourse hasten to other matters.

## The shameful deeds of the Alban war are surveyed.

**14**. What then after Numa, under the other kings? With how great an evil—not only their own, but also the Romans'—were the Albans provoked into war, because, namely, the so long peace of Numa had grown cheap! how frequent were the slaughters of the Roman and Alban armies, and the diminishing of both cities! For that Alba, which Ascanius, the son of Aeneas, founded—a mother of Rome nearer than Troy itself—provoked by King Tullus Hostilius, clashed, and in clashing was both afflicted and afflicted, until there was weariness, on both sides equally exhausted, of the contests.

Then it pleased them to try the outcome of the war by triplet brothers on each side: from the Romans the three Horatii came forth, from the Albans the three Curiatii; by the three Curiatii two of the Horatii, but by the one Horatius the three Curiatii were overcome and slain. So Rome stood forth victorious by that slaughter, even in the final contest, so that of six one returned home.

To whom was the loss on both sides, to whom the grief, except to the stock of Aeneas, to the posterity of Ascanius, to the offspring of Venus, to the grandsons of Jupiter? For this too was a war more than civil, when a daughter city fought with its mother city.

Another atrocious and horrible evil was added to this last battle of the triplets. For as the two peoples had previously been friends (neighbors indeed and kindred), the sister of the Horatii had been betrothed to one of the Curiatii; she, after she beheld the spoils of her betrothed on her victorious brother, was killed by that same brother because she wept. The feeling of this one woman seems to me to have been more human than that of the whole Roman people. For she whom she still held by a plighted faith as her future husband, or perhaps even grieving for her brother himself who had killed the man to whom he had promised his sister[^35], did not, I think, weep blamably.

For whence in Virgil does "pious" Aeneas grieve praiseworthily for an enemy slain even by his own hand[^36]? Whence did Marcellus, recalling the city of Syracuse, considering the common condition—that its height and glory had a little before suddenly fallen beneath his hands—pity it by weeping[^37]? I ask that we obtain from human feeling that a woman wept without blame for her betrothed slain by her brother, if men wept even with praise for enemies conquered by themselves. Therefore, when that woman wept for the death brought upon her betrothed by her brother, then Rome rejoiced that it had warred against its mother city with such great slaughter and conquered with such great shedding of kindred blood on both sides.

Why is the name of "praise" and the name of "victory" set before me? Let the obstacles of insane opinion be removed, let the deeds be seen naked, weighed naked, judged naked. Let the cause of Alba be stated, as the adultery of Troy was stated. None such, none similar is found; only that *Tullus might rouse to arms idle men and columns now unused to triumphs*. By that vice, then, was perpetrated so great a crime of social and kindred war, which vice Sallust touches on in passing as great.

For when, praising, he had briefly recalled the more ancient times, when the life of men was conducted without desire and his own things were sufficiently pleasing to each: *but afterward*, he says, *when in Asia Cyrus, in Greece the Lacedaemonians and Athenians began to subdue cities and nations, to hold the lust of dominating as a cause of war, to think the greatest glory in the greatest empire*, and the rest which he had set out to say. For me let it suffice up to this point to have set down his words. That *lust of dominating* agitates and wears down the human race with great evils. By this lust Rome, then conquered, triumphed that it had conquered Alba, and named the praise of its own crime "glory": *since the sinner is praised*, says our Scripture, *in the desires of his soul, and he who does iniquitous things is blessed*[^38].

Let the deceitful coverings, then, and the deceptive whitewashings be taken away from things, that they may be inspected by a sincere examination. Let no one say to me: that man and that man were great, because they fought with this man and that man and conquered. Gladiators too fight, and they themselves conquer, and that cruelty too has the rewards of praise; but I think it more satisfactory to pay the penalties of any sloth than to seek the glory of those arms. And yet if gladiators should come forth into the arena to fight among themselves, of whom one were a son, the other a father, who would bear such a spectacle? Who would not take it away? How, then, could the contest of arms between a city that was the mother of one and the daughter of the other be glorious? Or was it different on this account, that there was no arena, and broader fields were filled not with two gladiators but with the many deaths of two peoples, nor were those contests girt by an amphitheater, but an impious spectacle was offered to the whole world, and then to the living and to posterity, as far as that fame extends?

Yet those gods, the protectors of the Roman empire, suffered violence to their zeal and were as it were theatrical spectators of such contests, until the sister of the Horatii, on account of the three slain Curiatii, herself too as a third, from the other side, was added by her brother's sword to the two brothers, lest Rome, which had conquered, have fewer deaths even than the other. Then for the fruit of the victory Alba was overthrown, where, after Ilium, which the Greeks overthrew, and after Lavinium, where Aeneas had set up a foreign and fugitive kingdom, in the third place those Trojan divinities had dwelt. But in their manner they had perhaps already migrated even from there, and therefore it was destroyed. They had departed, you see—*all the gods, the shrines and altars left behind, by whom that empire had stood*.

They had departed, certainly—behold, now for the third time—so that a fourth, Rome, might most providently be entrusted to them. For Alba too had displeased them, where Amulius reigned with his brother expelled, and Rome had pleased them, where Romulus reigned with his brother killed.

But before Alba was destroyed, its people, they say, was poured over into Rome, that from each one city might be made. Be it so, let it have been done thus; yet that city, the kingdom of Ascanius and the third dwelling of the Trojan gods, was overthrown by its daughter city, the mother by the daughter; but that the remnants of war from two peoples might make one, much blood of both, poured out beforehand, was the wretched coagulation.

Why should I now say one by one, under the other kings, how often the same wars were renewed, which seemed ended by victories, and again and again completed with such great slaughters, again and again, after a treaty and peace, repeated between fathers-in-law and sons-in-law and their stock and posterity? No small indication of this calamity was that none of them closed the gates of war. None of them, then, under so many guardian gods, reigned in peace.

## History and fable extol Romulus undeservedly.

**15**. But what were the ends of the kings themselves? About Romulus let the fabulous flattery see, by which he is said to have been received into heaven; let certain of their writers see, who said that he was torn apart by the senate on account of his ferocity, and that one Julius Proculus or other was suborned, who would say that he had appeared to him and had through him commanded the Roman people that he be worshiped among the divinities[^39], and that in this way the people, which had begun to swell against the senate, was repressed and quieted. For an eclipse of the sun too had happened, which the unskilled multitude, in its ignorance, attributed to the merits of Romulus, though it was brought about by the fixed reckoning of its own course.

As though, indeed, if that had been a mourning of the sun, he ought not rather on that account to have been believed killed, and the very crime indicated also by the turning away of the daily light; as in fact happened when the Lord was crucified by the cruelty and impiety of the Jews[^40]. That this obscuration of the sun did not happen from the canonical course of the stars is sufficiently shown by the fact that it was then the Passover of the Jews; for that is solemnly held at the full moon, but a regular eclipse of the sun happens only at the end of the moon.

Cicero too sufficiently signifies that that reception of Romulus among the gods was thought rather than done, when, even praising him in the books *On the Republic* and in the speech of Scipio, he says: *he attained so much that, when, the sun being suddenly obscured, he did not appear, he was thought to have been placed in the number of the gods—an opinion which no mortal could ever attain without an exceptional glory of virtue*[^41]. (But by his saying that he *suddenly did not appear*, surely there is understood either the violence of a storm or the concealment of a slaying and a crime; for other writers of theirs add to the eclipse of the sun also a sudden storm, which surely either offered occasion for the crime or itself consumed Romulus[^42]).

For about Tullus Hostilius too, who was the third king from Romulus, who himself also was consumed by a thunderbolt, the same Cicero says in those same books[^43] that this man too was not believed received among the gods by such a death, because perhaps the Romans were unwilling to make common—that is, to make cheap—what had been proved (that is, persuaded) in the case of Romulus, if this were easily attributed to another also.

He says also openly in his invectives: *him who founded this city, Romulus, we have raised by goodwill and report to the immortal gods*[^44], so that he might show it was not truly done, but, on account of the merits of his virtue, benevolently bruited about and spread abroad. But in the dialogue *Hortensius*, when he was speaking about the canonical eclipses of the sun: *so as to bring about*, he says, *the same darkness which it brought about at the death of Romulus, which happened at the obscuration of the sun*[^45]. Here certainly he did not at all fear to say the death of a man, because he was a disputant rather than a praiser.

But the other kings of the Roman people, except Numa Pompilius and Ancus Marcius, who died by disease, what horrible ends they had! Tullus Hostilius, as I said, the conqueror and overthrower of Alba, was burned up with his whole house by a thunderbolt. Priscus Tarquinius was slain by the sons of his predecessor. Servius Tullius was killed by the wicked crime of his son-in-law Tarquinius Superbus, who succeeded him in the kingdom. Nor did *the gods depart, the shrines and altars left behind*, when so great a parricide was perpetrated against the best king of that people—gods who, they say, were moved by the adultery of Paris to do this to wretched Troy and to leave it to the Greeks to be destroyed and burned; but, on top of that, Tarquinius himself succeeded, having killed his father-in-law.

This wicked parricide, reigning by the killing of his father-in-law, and moreover glorying in many wars and victories and building the Capitol from the spoils, those gods saw not departing, but present and remaining, and they suffered their king Jupiter to preside and reign for them in that loftiest temple—that is, in the work of a parricide. For he did not build the Capitol while still innocent and afterward, for his evil deserts, was driven from the city, but he arrived at the very kingdom in which he might build the Capitol by the perpetration of a most monstrous crime.

But that the Romans afterward drove him from the kingdom and shut him out from the walls of the city was not his own sin about the rape of Lucretia, but his son's, committed not only without his knowledge, but even in his absence. He was then besieging the city of Ardea, waging war for the Roman people; we do not know what he would have done, if his son's shameful deed had been brought to his knowledge; and yet, his judgment unexplored and untried, the people took the command from him, and, the army having been received—by which he had been ordered to be deserted—then, with the gates closed, did not allow him to enter as he returned.

But he, after most grievous wars, by which he wore down those same Romans with the neighbors stirred up, after, deserted by those on whose help he relied, he was unable to recover the kingdom, in the town of Tusculum, near Rome, for fourteen years, as it is said, held a private life at rest and grew old with his wife, with an end perhaps more to be wished for than his father-in-law, who was killed by the crime of his son-in-law, with his daughter, as it is reported, not unaware of it.

And yet the Romans did not call this Tarquinius cruel or wicked, but proud—perhaps not bearing his royal arrogance out of another pride.

For the crime of the best king, his father-in-law, killed by him, they so far despised that they made him their king; where I wonder if they did not, by a graver crime, repay so great a reward to so great a crime. Nor did *the gods depart, the shrines and altars left behind*. Unless perhaps someone so defends these gods as to say that they remained at Rome for this reason, that they might rather punish the Romans with punishments than help them with benefits, seducing them with empty victories and wearing them down with most grievous wars.

This was the life of the Romans under the kings, in the praiseworthy time of that commonwealth, down to the expulsion of Tarquinius Superbus, for about two hundred and forty-three years, when all those victories, bought with so much blood and such great calamities, scarcely extended that empire within twenty miles from the City; a space so far from being now compared even to the territory of some Gaetulian city.

## The iniquity of Brutus was not glory.

**16**. To this time let us add also that time up to which Sallust says it *was conducted by fair and moderate right, while the fear of Tarquin and the grave war with Etruria lasted*[^46]. For as long as the Etruscans aided Tarquin, trying to return to the kingdom, Rome was shaken by a grave war. Therefore he says the commonwealth was conducted by fair and moderate right with fear pressing, not justice persuading.

In which most brief time how deadly was that year in which the first consuls were created, the royal power having been expelled! For they did not complete their year. For Junius Brutus dishonored and cast out from the City his colleague Lucius Tarquinius Collatinus[^47]; then soon he himself fell in war, with mutual wounds with the enemy, having first killed his own sons and his wife's brothers, because he had learned that they had conspired for the restoring of Tarquin[^48]. After Virgil had praiseworthily recalled this deed, he immediately shuddered at it with clemency. For when he had said: *and the father will call his sons, stirring up new wars, to punishment for the sake of fair liberty*, soon thereafter he exclaimed and said: *unhappy, however posterity shall bear those deeds*. However, he says, posterity may bear those deeds—that is, prefer and extol them—he who killed his sons is unhappy. And as if to console the unhappy man he subjoined: *the love of the fatherland conquers, and the immense desire of praises*[^49].

For after saying, *and the father will summon to punishment, for the sake of glorious liberty, his sons who stir up new wars*, he immediately exclaimed: *unhappy man, however posterity may regard those deeds*.

However posterity may regard those deeds, he says—that is, however it may approve and exalt them—the man who killed his sons is unhappy. And as though to console the unhappy man, he added: *love of country prevails, and an immeasurable desire for praise*[^50].

Is not, in this Brutus—who both killed his sons and, struck by the son of Tarquin whom he himself had struck, could not survive him, but rather Tarquin himself survived him—the innocence of his colleague Collatinus seen to be avenged, who, a good citizen, suffered, when this Tarquin was expelled, what the tyrant Tarquin himself suffered? For that same Brutus too is reported to have been a kinsman of Tarquin[^51]; but Collatinus, you see, the likeness of name pressed down, because he too was called Tarquinius. He would, then, be compelled to change his name, not his fatherland; finally, in his name this word would be less, he would be called only L. Collatinus. But he did not lose what he could have lost without any loss, so that he was ordered to lack both honor, as first consul, and citizenship, as a good citizen.

Is even this glory—the detestable iniquity of Junius Brutus, and in no way useful to the commonwealth? Did even to perpetrate this *the love of the fatherland conquer, and the immense desire of praises*? With the tyrant Tarquin now certainly expelled, Lucretia's husband L. Tarquinius Collatinus was created consul with Brutus. How justly the people attended to morals in a citizen, not to a name! How impiously Brutus deprived of both fatherland and honor a colleague in that first and new power, whom, if he was offended by this, he could have deprived only of his name! These are the evils done, these the adversities that befell, when in that commonwealth *it was conducted by fair and moderate right*.

How justly the people considered a citizen’s character rather than his name! How impiously Brutus deprived his colleague in that first, newly established magistracy of both his country and his office, when he could have deprived him merely of his name, if that offended him! These evils were committed, these misfortunes occurred, when that republic *was governed with fair and moderate justice*.

Lucretius too, who had been substituted in Brutus's place, was consumed by disease before that same year ended. So Publius Valerius, who had succeeded Collatinus, and Marcus Horatius, who had been substituted for the deceased Lucretius, completed that funereal and tartarean year, which had five consuls[^52]; in which year the Roman commonwealth inaugurated the new honor and power of the consulship itself.

## There were many evils of the commonwealth before the Punic wars, at home and in the field.

**17**. Then, with fear now a little diminished—not because the wars had quieted, but because they did not press with so grave a weight, the time being ended, namely, in which *it was conducted by fair and moderate right*—there followed the things which the same Sallust briefly sets forth: *then the fathers exercised the plebs with servile command, decided about their life and back in the manner of kings, drove them from their land, and, with the rest dispossessed, held power over the soil alone. Oppressed by these cruelties and most of all by usury, the plebs, while in continual wars it endured tribute and military service at once, took up arms and occupied the Sacred Mount and the Aventine, and then procured for itself tribunes of the plebs and other rights. The end of discords and of the struggle on both sides was the second Punic War*[^53].

Why, then, should I, in writing, endure such great delays, or bring them to those who will read? How wretched that commonwealth was, throughout so long an age, through so many years, down to the second Punic War, with wars from without not ceasing to disturb it and with discords and civil seditions within, has been briefly intimated by Sallust. Accordingly those victories were not the solid joys of the blessed, but the empty consolations of the wretched, and enticing incitements, to men by no means at rest, for undergoing other and other barren evils.

Why, then, should I endure so much delay in writing, or impose it on my readers? Sallust briefly indicated how wretched that republic was over so long a period, through so many years down to the Second Punic War, with wars abroad unceasingly disturbing it and discord and civil uprisings within. Thus those victories were not the solid joys of the happy but the empty consolations of the wretched, and seductive incitements for men who were never at peace to undertake one barren evil after another.

And let not the good and prudent Romans be angry with us because we say this: although on this matter they are neither to be entreated nor admonished, since it is most certain that they will not at all be angry. For we say nothing graver, or graver things, than their own authors, to whom we are far inferior both in style and in leisure; which authors, however, they themselves both labored to learn by heart and compel their sons to labor at.

But those who are angry—how would they bear me, if I said what Sallust says? *Very many disturbances, seditions, and at last civil wars arose, while a few powerful men, into whose favor most had yielded, under the honorable name of fathers or of plebs, aimed at dominations; and citizens were called good and bad, not for their merits toward the commonwealth, all being equally corrupted, but as each was wealthiest and stronger in doing wrong, because he defended present things, he was reckoned good*[^54].

Further, if those writers of history judged it to pertain to honorable liberty not to be silent about the evils of their own city, which in many places they were compelled to praise with great heralding, since they had no other, truer one, in which the citizens are to be chosen as eternal: what does it befit us to do, whose hope, the better and more certain it is in God, the greater ought our liberty to be, when they impute present evils to our Christ, that the weaker and more unskilled minds may be alienated from that city in which alone one must live perpetually and happily?

Nor do we say more horrible things against their gods than their own authors, again and again, whom they read and proclaim, since indeed both we received from them the things we would say, and we are in no way able to say either such things or all of them.

Where, then, were those gods who are thought to be worshiped for the scant and deceitful happiness of this world, when the Romans, to whom by a most lying cunning they peddled themselves to be worshiped, were vexed with such great calamities? Where were they, when the consul Valerius was killed defending the Capitol, set on fire by exiles and slaves, and he himself could more easily help the temple of Jupiter than that crowd of so many divinities, with their greatest and best king, whose temple he had freed, could come to his aid?

Where were they, when the city, wearied by the densest evils of seditions, while it awaited a little while at rest the envoys sent to Athens to borrow laws, was laid waste by a grave famine and pestilence? Where were they, when again the people, when it labored under famine, first created a prefect of the grain supply, and, that famine growing strong, Spurius Maelius, because he gave grain freely to the hungry multitude, incurred the charge of aiming at kingship, and, at the insistence of that same prefect, was killed by the dictator L. Quintius, decrepit in age, through Quintus Servilius the master of horse, with the greatest and most dangerous tumult of the city? Where were they, when a very great pestilence having arisen, the people, long and much wearied with useless gods, thought that new lectisternia, which it had never done before, should be exhibited? But couches were spread in honor of the gods, whence this rite, or rather sacrilege, received its name.

Where were they, when, through ten continuous years, by fighting badly, the Roman army had received frequent and great disasters at Veii, until at last it was aided through Furius Camillus, whom the ungrateful city afterward condemned? Where were they, when the Gauls took Rome, despoiled it, burned it, and filled it with slaughter?

Where were they when that notable plague brought on so vast a slaughter—the plague in which the famous Furius Camillus perished, who had earlier defended the ungrateful republic against the Veientes and afterward avenged it upon the Gauls? In this plague the theatrical games introduced another new pestilence, not into the bodies of the Romans but, what is far more ruinous, into their morals.

Where were they when another grievous plague was thought to have arisen from the poisons of the matrons, whose morals—those of many noble women, beyond belief—were found to be more grievous than any plague?

Or when both consuls, besieged with their army by the Samnites at the Caudine Forks, were forced to make a shameful treaty with them, so that, six hundred Roman knights being given as hostages, the rest, having lost their arms and being stripped and deprived of their other coverings, were sent under the enemy's yoke clothed in a single garment apiece?

Or when, amid a grievous plague that afflicted the rest, many in the army as well perished struck by lightning?

Or when, by yet another intolerable plague, Rome was driven to summon Aesculapius from Epidaurus and to call him in as a god of medicine—since Jove, the king of all, who had long been seated on the Capitol, had not had the leisure, on account of the many seductions in which he had indulged as a young man, to learn medicine?

Or when, with the enemy conspiring all at one time—Lucanians, Bruttians, Samnites, Etruscans, and Senonian Gauls—first the Roman envoys were slain by them, and then an army was overwhelmed together with its praetor, seven tribunes perishing along with him and thirteen thousand of the soldiers?

Or when, after long and grievous seditions at Rome—in which at last the common people had withdrawn to the Janiculum in a hostile secession—the calamity of this evil was so dreadful that, for this cause, as was customary in the gravest perils, Hortensius was created dictator, who, after recalling the people, expired while still in that same office—a thing that had befallen no dictator before, and which, now that Aesculapius was present, was a graver charge against those gods?

Then indeed so many wars broke out everywhere and so frequently that, owing to a shortage of soldiers, those proletarians—who had received this name because, being unfit for military service through poverty, they were left free to beget offspring (*proles*)—were enrolled for war. Pyrrhus too, king of Greece, then celebrated for his immense glory, was summoned by the Tarentines and made an enemy of the Romans. To him indeed, when he consulted it about the future outcome of events, Apollo gave so ambiguous an oracle, quite cleverly, that whichever of the two things came to pass, he himself would be held divine. For he said: *I say that you, Pyrrhus, can conquer the Romans.* And so, whether Pyrrhus were conquered by the Romans or the Romans by Pyrrhus, the soothsayer could await either outcome with security. What dreadful and horrible slaughter there was then on both sides! Yet in it Pyrrhus came off the better, so that he might already have been able to proclaim Apollo divine according to his own understanding, had not the Romans soon come off the better in another battle.

And amid so great a slaughter of wars there arose also a grievous plague among the women. For before they could bring their pregnancies to term, the pregnant women were dying. Here, I suppose, Aesculapius excused himself, on the ground that he professed himself a chief physician, not a midwife. The cattle too were likewise perishing, so that it was believed the whole race of animals would fail.

What of that memorable winter, raging with such incredible savagery that, with the snows lying to a fearful depth even in the forum for forty days, the Tiber too was frozen hard with ice? Had it happened in our times, what things, and how great, these men would have said!

What of that equally vast plague—how long it raged, how many it carried off! And when it stretched into another year much more grievously, with Aesculapius present in vain, recourse was had to the Sibylline books. In this class of oracles, as Cicero recounts in his books *On Divination,* it is customary to put more trust in the interpreters as they guess at doubtful matters as they can or as they will. Then, therefore, it was declared that this was the cause of the plague: that many men were keeping a great many sacred buildings in private possession. Thus for the time being Aesculapius was acquitted of a grave charge of incompetence or negligence.

But why had those buildings been occupied by many, with no one forbidding it, except that for so long that great throng of deities had been supplicated in vain, and so little by little the places were deserted by their worshippers, so that, as if empty, they could without offense to anyone be claimed at least for human uses? For if those buildings were then carefully sought out again and restored as if to allay the plague, but afterward, neglected and usurped in the same way, lay hidden, then surely it would not be credited to Varro's great learning that, in writing of the sacred buildings, he records so many that were unknown. But for the time being an elegant excusing of the gods was provided—not an averting of the plague.

**18**. But now, in the Punic wars, when victory hung long doubtful and uncertain between the two empires, and two most powerful peoples were driving the most valiant and wealthy assaults against each other, how many smaller kingdoms were crushed! What ample and noble cities were destroyed, how many states afflicted, how many ruined! How far and wide were so many regions and lands laid waste! How often were the conquerors on this side or that conquered! What a multitude of men was consumed, whether of fighting soldiers or of peoples free from arms! What a great force of ships was overwhelmed in naval battles as well, and sunk by the variety of diverse storms! Were we to attempt to relate or recount it, we should be nothing other than writers of history ourselves[^55].

Then the Roman state, thrown into great panic, ran to vain and laughable remedies. By the authority of the Sibylline books the Secular Games were restored, the celebration of which had been instituted at intervals of a hundred years but, in happier times, had been lost through forgetfulness. The pontiffs also renewed the sacred games for the gods of the underworld, games that had themselves been abolished in the better years gone by. No wonder—for when they were renewed, the gods of the underworld, enriched by so great an abundance of the dying, were pleased even to take their sport, while the wretched men were waging those rabid wars themselves, with their bloody animosities and deadly victories on this side and that, as great games for the demons and a rich banquet for the powers below.

Nothing more pitiable, to be sure, befell in the First Punic War than that the Romans were so defeated that even the famous Regulus was captured, of whom we made mention in the first and second books—plainly a great man, who had earlier conquered and subdued the Carthaginians, and who would even have brought that First Punic War itself to an end, had he not, through an excessive greed for praise and glory, imposed on the exhausted Carthaginians harsher terms than they could bear. If that man's most unexpected captivity and most unworthy slavery, his most faithful keeping of his oath and his most cruel death do not force those gods to blush, then it is true that they are made of air and have no blood.

Nor were there lacking, at that time, most grievous evils within the walls. For when the river Tiber overflowed beyond its wont, nearly all the low-lying parts of the city were laid waste, some places driven down by the force of what was like a torrent, others soaked and undermined as if by a long-standing pool. Then a still more ruinous fire followed upon this calamity, which, having caught hold of all the loftier buildings around the forum, did not spare even the temple of Vesta, its closest familiar, where the virgins, not so much honored as condemned, had been wont to grant it, as it were, perpetual life by most diligently replacing the wood. There, indeed, the fire not only lived but even raged. Terrified by its onslaught, the virgins, since they could not free those fateful sacred objects—which had already burdened three cities in which they had been kept—from that conflagration, Metellus the pontiff, forgetting in a manner his own safety, rushed in and, half-burned, snatched them away.

For the fire did not recognize even him, nor in truth was there any divinity there which, had it been there, would not also have fled. A man, then, was able to be of more use to Vesta's sacred things than they to a man. But if they could not repel the fire from themselves, what could they avail against those waters and flames to help the city, whose safety they were thought to guard? As indeed the event itself made plain, they could avail nothing at all.

These things we would by no means cast in their teeth, if they said that those sacred rites were instituted not to guard these temporal goods but to signify eternal ones, and that therefore, when it happened that they perished—since they were corporeal and visible—nothing was thereby *diminished* in the things for whose sake they had been instituted, and that they could be restored anew for the same uses.

But as it is, with marvelous blindness they suppose that, by sacred rites which could perish, it could come to pass that the earthly safety and temporal felicity of the city could not perish. Accordingly, when it is shown that, even while those sacred rites remained, either the destruction of their safety or unhappiness rushed upon them, they blush to change the opinion which they cannot defend.

## The most grievous disasters of the Second Punic War, among them the destruction of the Saguntines.

**19**. In the Second Punic War, moreover, it would be too long to recount the disasters of two peoples fighting between themselves so far and wide, so that—by the admission even of those who set out not so much to narrate the Roman wars as to praise the Roman empire—the one who conquered was more like a conquered man[^56]. For when Hannibal arose from Spain, crossed the Pyrenees mountains, ran through Gaul, broke through the Alps, and—after so long a circuit, his forces increased—rushed like a torrent upon the throat of Italy, laying all waste or subduing it: what bloody battles were fought, how often were the Romans defeated! How many towns went over to the enemy, how many were captured and crushed! What dreadful battles, and how often glorious to Hannibal through Roman disaster!

But of the marvelously horrible evil at Cannae, what shall I say—where Hannibal, cruel as he was, is yet reported to have ordered that, sated with so great a slaughter of his most savage foes, his men should be spared? From there he sent to Carthage three pecks of gold rings, that they might understand how great a portion of the Roman nobility had fallen in that battle, so that it could more easily be reckoned by measure than by number[^57]; and hence the slaughter of the rest of the throng—so much more numerous as it was lowlier, lying without rings—was thought to be left to conjecture rather than reported.

Finally, so great a shortage of soldiers followed that the Romans gathered men guilty of crimes, with impunity offered, and bestowed liberty on slaves, and with these their army was not so much filled up as newly formed, to their shame. Arms, then, were lacking for these slaves—or rather, that we may do them no injustice, freedmen now—who were to fight for the Roman republic. They were stripped from the temples, as though the Romans were saying to their gods: "Lay down what you have so long held to no purpose, lest perhaps our chattels may be able to make something useful of it, since our divinities could not."

Then too, when the treasury had failed for paying the soldiers, private wealth came into public use, each man contributing what he had, so far that, except for their individual rings and individual bullas—pitiable insignia of rank—the senate itself, and how much more the other orders and tribes, left themselves no gold[^58]. Who would endure these men if in our times they were driven to such poverty—when even now we scarcely endure them, since for superfluous pleasure more is bestowed on actors than was then contributed to the legions for the last hope of safety?

**20**. But among all these evils of the Second Punic War, nothing was more pitiable, or more worthy of pitiable complaint, than the destruction of the Saguntines. For this city of Spain, most friendly to the Roman people, was overthrown while it was keeping faith with that same people. For it was here that Hannibal, having broken the treaty, sought occasions by which to provoke the Romans to war. He was therefore fiercely besieging Saguntum; and when this was heard at Rome, envoys were sent to Hannibal to make him withdraw from his siege. Slighted, they proceeded to Carthage and lodged their complaint of the broken treaty, and, the business accomplished nothing, returned to Rome. While these delays were going on, that wretched and most wealthy city, most dear to its own republic and to Rome, was destroyed by the Carthaginians in the eighth or ninth month. To read of its ruin, much more to write of it, is a thing of horror. Yet I will recount it briefly, for it bears much on the matter at hand.

First it wasted away with famine; for it is even reported to have been fed by some on the corpses of their own. Then, weary of all things, lest it should at least come captive into the hands of Hannibal, it publicly built a huge pyre, into which, as it blazed, they cast both themselves and their own, even slaughtering them with the sword. Here let those gods—those gluttons and good-for-nothings, gaping after the fat of sacrifices and deceiving men with the fog of lying divinations—do something;

here let them do something, let them come to the aid of a city most friendly to the Roman people, let them not allow it, perishing through the keeping of faith, to perish.

They themselves, surely, had presided as mediators when it was bound to the Roman republic by the treaty interposed. Therefore, while faithfully guarding what it had joined by the pact agreed under those very protectors, had bound by faith, had made fast by oath, it was besieged, overwhelmed, and consumed by a treacherous foe.

If those same gods afterward terrified Hannibal with storm and lightning when he was close to the Roman walls, and drove him far off, then let them have done some such thing first. For I dare to say that they could more honorably have raged with a storm on behalf of friends of the Romans who were thus endangered precisely so as not to break faith with the Romans, and who then had no help, than on behalf of the Romans themselves, who were fighting for themselves and were wealthy in resources against Hannibal.

If, then, they were the guardians of Roman felicity and glory, they would have averted from it so grave a charge as the disaster of Saguntum. But as it is, how foolishly it is believed that Rome did not perish, with Hannibal victorious, through the protection of those gods—gods who could not come to the aid of the city of Saguntum, that it might not perish for the sake of friendship with Rome!

If the people of Saguntum had been Christian and had suffered something of this kind for the faith of the Gospel—though it would not have destroyed itself with sword or fire, yet if it had suffered destruction for the faith of the Gospel—it would have suffered in that hope by which it had believed in Christ, not for a reward of the briefest time, but of an endless eternity.

But on behalf of these gods, who are said to be worshipped, and are sought out to be worshipped, precisely so that the felicity of these falling and passing things may be secure—what will their defenders and excusers answer us about the perishing Saguntines, except what they answered about the slain Regulus? The difference is only this: that he was one man, this a whole city; yet the cause of the destruction of each was the keeping of faith. For it was on this account that the one was willing to return to the enemy, and the other unwilling to go over to it.

Does faith kept, then, provoke the wrath of the gods? Or can not only individual men, but even whole cities perish, with the gods favorable? Let them choose which they will. For if those gods are angered by faith kept, let them look for the treacherous to worship them; but if even with them favorable men and cities can be afflicted with many grievous torments and perish, then they are worshipped with no fruit of this felicity.

Let them cease, then, to be angry—those who think that, because the sacred rites of their gods have been lost, they have been made unhappy. For they could, with those rites not only remaining but even favoring them, not merely murmur about misery as they do now, but, like Regulus and the Saguntines then, be horribly tortured and even utterly perish.

## The crimes of an ungrateful and wanton spirit are enumerated.

**21**. Furthermore, between the second and the last Carthaginian war—when Sallust said that the Romans conducted themselves with the best morals and the greatest concord[^59] (for I pass over much, mindful of the limits of the work undertaken)—

at that very same time, then, of the best morals and the greatest concord, that famous Scipio, liberator of Rome and Italy and the renowned and admirable finisher of that Second Punic War—so horrible, so destructive, so perilous—the conqueror of Hannibal and subduer of Carthage,

whose life from youth is described[^60] as devoted to the gods and nurtured in the temples, yielded to the accusations of his enemies, and, lacking the homeland which he had by his own valor rendered safe and free, spent and completed the rest of his life in the town of Liternum—captured, after his notable triumph, by no longing for that city, so that he is reported to have ordered that not even his funeral be held in his ungrateful homeland when he was dead[^61].

Then, for the first time, through the proconsul Gnaeus Manlius, who triumphed over the Galatians of Asia, Asiatic luxury crept into Rome, worse than any enemy. For then for the first time, it is reported, bronze couches and costly coverlets were seen[^62]; then lyre-girls were brought into banquets, and other licentious depravity.

But now I have set out to speak of those evils which men suffer intolerably, not of those which they do willingly. For which reason that point I noted about Scipio—that, yielding to his enemies, he died outside the homeland which he had freed—pertains more to the present discussion: that the Roman divinities, from whose temples he turned away Hannibal, did not repay him in kind, gods who are worshipped only for the sake of that felicity. But because Sallust said that at that time the morals there were the best, I thought I should recount this about Asian luxury, so that it may be understood that even that statement was made by Sallust by comparison with other times—times in which, in their gravest discords, the morals were certainly worse.

For then—that is, between the second and the last Carthaginian war—that Voconian law too was passed, that no one should make a woman his heir, not even an only daughter[^63]. What could be said or thought more unjust than this law, I do not know.

Yet throughout that whole interval between the two Punic wars the unhappiness was more bearable. The army was worn down only by wars abroad, but was consoled by victories; while at home no discords, as at other times, were raging. But in the last Punic war, by a single assault of the other Scipio—who for this very reason himself acquired the surname Africanus—the rival of the Roman empire was destroyed root and branch; and thereafter the Roman republic was overwhelmed by such heaps of evils that, by the prosperity and security of affairs from which those evils were heaped up upon morals too greatly corrupted, Carthage is shown to have done more harm by being so quickly overthrown than it had earlier done by being so long hostile.

Throughout all this whole time, down to Caesar Augustus—who seems to have wrested from the Romans, by every means, a liberty no longer glorious even in their own opinion, but contentious and destructive and now plainly enervated and languid, and to have recalled all things to his royal authority, and to have restored and, as it were, renewed the republic, which had collapsed as if from a diseased old age—

throughout all this time, then, I pass over the disasters of war, arising again and again from one cause or another, and the Numantine treaty, stained with a horrible disgrace; for the chickens had flown from their cage and, as they say, had made a bad augury for the consul Mancinus[^64]—as though, through so many years in which that tiny city, having beset a Roman army, had afflicted it and had already begun to be a terror to the Roman republic itself, others had advanced against it under a different augury.

**22**. But these things, I say, I pass over, although I would by no means have kept silent about this: that Mithridates, king of Asia, ordered the Roman citizens traveling everywhere in Asia, and intent on their business in numberless multitude, to be killed in a single day; and it was done[^65].

How pitiable was the face of things—that each man, wherever he was found, in the field, on the road, in a town, in a house, in a village, in the forum, in a temple, in bed, at a banquet, was unexpectedly and impiously butchered! What groans of the dying, what tears of the onlookers—perhaps even of those who struck the blows! How harsh the necessity laid on hosts not only of witnessing those abominable slaughters in their own homes, but even of perpetrating them, their faces suddenly changed from that gracious courtesy of hospitality to the carrying out of a hostile deed in time of peace—wounds, I should say, altogether mutual, since the one struck was wounded in his body and the one who struck in his soul!

Had all these men despised the auguries? Had they no gods, household and public, to consult when they set out from their own homes for that journey of no return? If this is so, these men have no reason to complain in this matter of our times; long ago the Romans despised these vain things. But if they did consult them, let it be answered what these gods availed, when, by human laws at least, with no one forbidding, such things were permitted.

## What the evils of the civil wars were.

**23**. But let us now briefly recount, as far as we can, those evils which, the more inward they were, the more wretched they proved: the civil—or rather uncivil—discords, no longer mere seditions, but now actual wars within the city, where so much blood was poured out, where the partisanship of factions raged no longer in the disputes of assemblies and varied outcries against one another, but now plainly with sword and arms; the wars of the allies, the wars of the slaves, the civil wars—how much Roman blood they shed, what devastation and desolation of Italy they wrought!

For before allied Latium roused itself against Rome, all the animals subjected to human use—dogs, horses, asses, oxen, and whatever other beasts had been under the dominion of men—suddenly grew wild and, forgetful of their domestic gentleness, abandoned their stalls and wandered free, and shunned the approach not only of others but even of their own masters, not without death or peril to anyone who ventured, if he pressed upon them from close at hand[^66]. Of how great an evil was it a sign, if it was a sign—seeing how great an evil it was, even if it was not a sign! Had this happened in our times, we should have to endure these men as more rabid than their animals were.

**24**. Now the beginning of the civil evils was the seditions of the Gracchi, stirred up by the agrarian laws. For they wished to divide among the people the lands which the nobility wrongly possessed. But to dare to uproot an iniquity now grown old was most perilous—indeed, as the event itself taught, most ruinous. What deaths were wrought when the elder Gracchus was slain! And what again, when his other brother was slain not long after! For it was not by laws and the order of the magistracies, but by mobs and the clashes of arms, that noble and ignoble alike were killed.

After the killing of the other Gracchus, the consul Lucius Opimius, who had taken up arms against him within the City and, after he and his allies were crushed and destroyed, had made an immense slaughter of citizens, is reported—when he held an inquiry, pursuing the rest now by judicial investigation—to have put three thousand men to death[^67]. From this one can understand how great a multitude of deaths the turbid clash of arms could have produced, when so great a number resulted from the inquiry of the courts, as it were duly weighed.

The man who struck down Gracchus himself sold his head to the consul for as great a weight of gold as it weighed; for this bargain had preceded the slaughter. In it Marcus Fulvius, a man of consular rank, was also killed together with his children.

**25**. By an elegant decree of the senate, indeed, on the very spot where that deadly tumult was joined, where so many citizens of every order fell, a temple of Concord was built, so that a witness to the punishment of the Gracchi might strike the eyes of those who harangued the assembly and prick their memory. But what else was this than a mockery of the gods—to build a temple to that goddess who, if she were in the city, would not have collapsed, torn apart by such great dissensions?

Unless perhaps Concord, guilty of this crime because she had deserted the minds of the citizens, deserved to be shut up in that temple as in a prison. For why, if they wished to suit the deed done, did they not rather build there a temple of Discord? Or is any reason given why Concord should be a goddess and Discord not a goddess—so that, according to Labeo's distinction, the one is good and the other bad? Nor does he himself seem to have followed anything else than that he noticed that at Rome a temple was set up to Fever as well as to Health. By that reasoning, then, a temple ought to have been set up not only to Concord but also to Discord.

Perilously, then, did the Romans wish to live with so evil a goddess angry, nor did they recall that the destruction of Troy took its origin from her offense. For she, because she had not been invited among the gods, contrived the quarrel of three goddesses by the placing of the golden apple; whence the brawl of the deities, and Venus the victor, and Helen carried off, and Troy destroyed.

Wherefore, if perchance, indignant that she had not deserved to have any temple in the City among the gods, she was for that reason now disturbing the state with such great tumults, how much more atrociously could she have been provoked, when in the place of that slaughter—that is, in the place of her own handiwork—she saw a temple set up to her adversary!

At these vain things we laugh, while those learned and wise men grow angry; and yet, worshippers of good gods and bad, they do not escape from this question of Concord and Discord—whether they have passed over the worship of these goddesses and preferred to them Fever and Bellona, to whom they made temples of old, or whether they have worshipped these as well, since Concord thus departing, raging Discord led them on even to the civil wars.

**26**. A splendid obstacle to sedition, indeed, they thought to set before those who harangued the assembly: the temple of Concord, witness to the slaughter and punishment of the Gracchi. How much they profited from this, the worse things that followed indicate.

For thereafter the haranguers labored not to avoid the example of the Gracchi, but to surpass their purpose—Lucius Saturninus, tribune of the people, and Gaius Servilius the praetor, and much later Marcus Drusus—by whose seditions slaughters, already most grievous then, first blazed up, and then the wars of the allies, by which Italy was vehemently afflicted and brought to a marvelous devastation and desolation. Then the slave war followed, and the civil wars.

What battles were joined, what blood poured out, so that almost all the peoples of Italy, over whom the Roman empire chiefly prevailed, were subdued as though they were a savage barbarian people! From the very fewest gladiators—that is, fewer than seventy—in what way the slave war was set in motion, to how great a number and how keen and fierce a force it reached, what commanders of the Roman people that number overcame, what cities and regions, and in what manner, it laid waste—scarcely could those who wrote the history sufficiently set forth[^68]. Nor was this the only slave war: the slaves had earlier ravaged the province of Macedonia, and then Sicily and the seacoast. And how great and how horrible were the things they committed—first robberies, then powerful wars of the pirates—who could tell in proportion to the magnitude of the matters?

But when Marius, already bloodied with civil blood, after many of the party opposed to him had been slain, had been defeated and had fled the City, and the state was scarcely catching its breath for a little while—to use Cicero's words: *afterward Cinna together with Marius prevailed. Then indeed, the most illustrious men being slain, the lights of the state were extinguished. Sulla afterward avenged the cruelty of this victory—with what diminution of citizens and what calamity to the republic there is no need even to say*[^69]. For of this vengeance, which was more ruinous than if the crimes that were being punished had been left unpunished, Lucan too says: *the cure exceeded measure and followed too far where the diseases had led its hand. The guilty perished; but by then only the guilty could survive*[^70].

In that Marian and Sullan war—apart from those who fell abroad in the line of battle—in the City itself too the streets, squares, forums, theaters, and temples were filled with corpses, so that it was hard to judge when the victors had wrought more deaths: whether before, in order that they might conquer, or afterward, because they had conquered;

when first, in the Marian victory—when Marius restored himself from exile—apart from slaughters committed indiscriminately on every side, the head of the consul Octavius was set on the rostra, the Caesars were butchered by Fimbria in their own homes, the two Crassi, father and son, were slaughtered in each other's sight, Baebius and Numitorius perished dragged by the hook, their entrails strewn about, Catulus withdrew himself from the hands of his enemies by drinking poison, and Merula, the priest of Jupiter, with his veins cut open, made offering to his own Jupiter even with his own blood. But before the very eyes of Marius himself, those were struck down at once to whom, when they saluted him, he had refused to extend his right hand.

**28**. But the Sullan victory that followed—the avenger, that is, of this cruelty—after so much blood of citizens by whose shedding it had been won, the war now ended and enmities living on, stalked more cruelly in peace. For now, after the former and most recent slaughters of the elder Marius, others more grievous had been added by the younger Marius and Carbo, of those same Marian factions, who, with Sulla pressing upon them and despairing not only of victory but even of their own safety, filled all things with further slaughters of their own.

For besides the slaughter spread far and wide through diverse places, the senate too was besieged, and men were led forth from the senate house itself, as if from a prison, to the sword. Mucius Scaevola the pontiff—since among the Romans nothing was held more sacred than the temple of Vesta—was killed while embracing the altar itself, and almost extinguished with his own blood that fire which, by the perpetual care of the virgins, always burned.

Then Sulla entered the City as victor, who in the Public Villa—no longer with war, but with peace itself raging—had laid low seven thousand men who had surrendered (and hence were certainly unarmed) not by fighting but by command.

But throughout the whole City each Sullan partisan struck down whomever he pleased, so that the deaths could not be counted at all, until it was suggested to Sulla that some ought to be allowed to live, so that there might be men over whom those who had conquered could rule. Then at last, the license of slaughter that was being carried furiously here and there everywhere being checked, that list was posted, with great rejoicing, which contained two thousand men of either splendid order—the equestrian, that is, and the senatorial—to be killed and proscribed. The number saddened, but the limit consoled; nor was there as much grief because so many were falling as there was gladness because the rest had nothing to fear.

But in the case of certain of those who had been ordered to die, the very security of the rest—cruel though it was—groaned at the refined kinds of death. For one man the hands of those who mangled him tore apart without any iron, men rending a living man more savagely than beasts are wont to tear a cast-off corpse. Another, with his eyes gouged out and his limbs cut off piece by piece, was forced to live long—or rather to die long—amid such great torments. Certain noble cities too were put up for auction, like farms; and one, just as a single defendant might be ordered to be led off, was thus ordered, whole, to be butchered.

These things were done in peace after war—not that victory might be hastened in the gaining, but that, once gained, it might not be despised. Peace contended with war in cruelty, and won. For war laid low the armed, but peace the unarmed. War was such that he who was struck might, if he could, strike back; but peace was such, not that he who had escaped might live, but that the dying man might not resist.

**29**. What rage of foreign nations, what savagery of barbarians can be compared to this victory of citizens over citizens? What more deadly, more foul, more bitter thing did Rome see—the incursion of the Gauls long ago and of the Goths a little while back, or the ferocity of Marius and Sulla and the other most illustrious men of their parties, like her own lights turned against her own members?

The Gauls, indeed, butchered the senate—whatever of it they could find in the whole City except the Capitoline citadel, which alone was somehow defended; but to those established on that hill they at least sold their lives for gold, lives which, though they could not seize them by the sword, they might yet consume by siege. The Goths, however, spared so many senators that it is more to be wondered at that they killed any.

But Sulla, while Marius was still alive, as victor occupied the Capitol itself—which had been safe from the Gauls—to decree slaughters; and when Marius had slipped away in flight, only to return more ferocious and more bloody, Sulla on the Capitol, by a decree of the senate as well, deprived many of life and property. As for the Marian party, with Sulla absent, what was sacred that they would spare, when they did not spare the wretched Mucius—a citizen, a senator, a pontiff—as he clung in embrace to the very altar where, as they say, the destinies of Rome resided? And that final Sullan list, to pass over countless other deaths, slaughtered more senators than the Goths were even able to plunder.

**30**. With what face, then, with what heart, with what shamelessness, with what folly—or rather madness—do they not charge those evils to their own gods, and charge these to our Christ? The cruel civil wars, more bitter than all wars against foreign enemies—as even their own authors admit—by which that republic was judged not merely afflicted but utterly ruined, arose long before the coming of Christ, and by a chain of wicked causes from the Marian and Sullan war to the wars of Sertorius and Catiline (of whom the one had been proscribed by Sulla, the other nurtured), thence to the war of Lepidus and Catulus (of whom the one desired to rescind, the other to defend, the Sullan deeds), thence to that of Pompey and Caesar (of whom Pompey had been a follower of Sulla and had either equaled or even already surpassed his power; while Caesar would not endure Pompey's power, but because he did not have it—which, however, once Pompey was conquered and slain, he transcended), and from here they reached the other Caesar, who was afterward called Augustus, in whose reign Christ was born.

For Augustus himself too waged civil wars with many, and in them too many most illustrious men perished, among them Cicero, that eloquent master of governing the republic. For Pompey's conqueror Gaius Caesar, who exercised his civil victory with clemency and granted life and dignity to his adversaries, a conspiracy of certain noble senators butchered in the senate house itself, as though for the liberty of the republic, on the charge that he aspired to kingship. His power Antony then seemed to be reaching after—a man very different in morals and stained and corrupted with every vice—against whom Cicero vehemently resisted, on behalf of that same liberty, as it were, of the homeland.

There had then emerged that other Caesar, a young man of marvelous disposition, the adopted son of that Gaius Caesar, who, as I said, was afterward called Augustus. To this young Caesar, that his power against Antony might be fostered, Cicero showed favor, hoping that, once Antony's domination was repelled and crushed, he would restore the liberty of the republic—so blind and unforeseeing of the future that the very young man whose dignity and power he was nurturing both allowed Cicero himself to be killed by Antony, by a kind of pact of concord, and subjected to his own sway that very liberty of the republic for which Cicero had cried out so much.

**31**. Let those who are ungrateful to our Christ for such great goods accuse their own gods for such great evils. Certainly, when those evils were taking place, *the altars of the deities were warm, fragrant with Sabaean incense and fresh garlands*[^71], the priesthoods were resplendent, the shrines gleamed, sacrifice and games and frenzy were carried on in the temples, while everywhere so much citizens' blood was being shed by citizens, not only in other places but even among the very altars of the gods.

Cicero did not choose a temple to which to flee, because Mucius had chosen one in vain. But these men, who much more unworthily insult the Christian times, either fled to places most dedicated to Christ, or the barbarians themselves led them there that they might live.

This I know, and anyone who judges me without partisan zeal most readily acknowledges it (to pass over the other things, the many I have recounted and the many more besides which I thought it long to recount): if the human race had received the Christian teaching before the Punic wars, and there had followed so great a devastation of affairs as ground down Europe and Africa in those wars, none of such men as we now suffer would have ascribed those evils to anything but the Christian religion. And much less, so far as the Romans are concerned, would their voices be endured, if upon the reception and spread of the Christian religion there had followed either that incursion of the Gauls, or that ravaging of the river Tiber and of the fires, or—what precedes all evils—those civil wars.

There were also other evils that befell so incredibly that they were counted among portents—if they had befallen in Christian times, to whom would they have cast them up as crimes, except to Christian men? For I pass over those things that were more marvelous than harmful: oxen that spoke, infants not yet born that cried out certain words from their mothers' wombs, serpents that flew, women and hens and men that were changed into the male sex, and other things of this kind, which in their books—not fabulous but historical[^72], whether they are true or false, bring upon men not ruin but astonishment.

But when it rained earth, when it rained chalk, when it rained stones (not in the sense in which hail is usually called by this name, but stones outright)[^73], these things assuredly could even do grievous harm. We read in them that, when the fires of Etna ran down from the very summit of the mountain to the nearest shore, the sea so boiled that the rocks were burned, that the pitch of the ships was melted[^74]. This was certainly no slight harm, however incredibly marvelous. By the same heat of fires again, they wrote that Sicily was filled with so great a force of ash that it overwhelmed and crushed and brought down the roofs of the city of Catina; moved by which calamity, the Romans mercifully remitted to it that year's tribute[^75]. They also recorded in their writings that there was in Africa a multitude of locusts like a portent, when it was already a province of the Roman people; for, the crops and the leaves of the trees being consumed, they say it was cast into the sea in an immense and incalculable cloud; and when this had died and been returned to the shores, and the air thereby corrupted, so great a plague arose that in the kingdom of Masinissa alone eight hundred thousand men are reported to have perished, and many more in the lands nearest the shores[^76]. Then at Utica, they affirm, out of thirty thousand of the younger men who were there, ten thousand remained.

Such vanity, then, as we endure and are compelled to answer—which of these things would it not ascribe to the Christian religion, if it saw them in Christian times? And yet they do not ascribe these things to their gods, whose worship they seek out for this very reason, that they may not suffer such things, or even lesser ones—when they suffered greater ones from those by whom they were formerly worshipped.
