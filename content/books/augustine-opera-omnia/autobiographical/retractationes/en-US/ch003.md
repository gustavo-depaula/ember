# Book II

## Chapter I (XXVIII): To Simplician, Two Books

**1**. Of the books I worked out as a bishop, the first two are to Simplician, the prelate of the Church of Milan, who succeeded the most blessed Ambrose, on various questions, two of which, from the *Letter* of Paul the Apostle *to the Romans,* I gathered into the first book.

The first of these is: *on that which is written: 'what then shall we say? Is the law sin? Far from it!'*[^1] down to that place where he says: *who will free me from the body of this death? The grace of God through Jesus Christ our Lord*[^2]. In which[^3] those words of the Apostle, *the law is spiritual, but I am carnal*[^4], and the rest, by which the flesh is shown to contend against the spirit, I so expounded as though a man were being described who is still established *under the law,* not yet *under grace*[^5]. For long afterward I came to know that these words can be even those of the spiritual man—and this more probably[^6].

The second question in this book is from that place where he says: *and not only so, but Rebecca also, having conceived at one intercourse by Isaac our father*[^7], down to that place where he says: *unless the Lord of Sabaoth had left us a seed, we would have become like Sodom and would have been made similar to Gomorrah*[^8]*.*

In the solution of this question[^9] labor was indeed expended in favor of the free choice of the human will, but the grace of God prevailed; nor could one arrive except at this: that the Apostle is understood to have said, with the clearest truth: *for who distinguishes you? And what do you have that you have not received? But if you have received, why do you boast as though you had not received?*[^10] Wishing to show this too, the martyr Cyprian comprised the whole in the very title, saying: *that one must boast in nothing, since nothing is ours*[^11].

**2**. In the second book the remaining questions are treated, and solved according to our small ability, which are all from the Scripture which is called *Kingdoms.*

The first of these is: *on that which is written: 'and the spirit of the Lord leapt upon Saul'*[^12]*,* while elsewhere it is said: *'and the evil spirit of the Lord upon Saul'*[^13]*.* Expounding which, I said: *although it is in the power of each what he wills, yet it is not in anyone's power what he can do*[^14]*.* This was said for the reason that we do not say a thing is in our power except what comes about when we will; where, first and chiefly, is the willing itself. For without any interval of time the will itself is at hand when we will. But this power too, for living well, we receive from above, when *the will is prepared by the Lord*[^15].

The second question is: *how it was said: 'it repents me that I made Saul king'*[^16].

The third: *whether the unclean spirit that was in the witch could bring it about that Samuel was seen by Saul and spoke with him*[^17]*.*

The fourth: *on that which is written: 'King David went in and sat before the Lord'*[^18]*.*

The fifth: *on that which Elijah said: 'O Lord, witness of this widow with whom I dwell at her house, you have done evil to kill her son'*[^19]*.*

This work begins thus: *Most pleasing indeed and most sweet.*

## Chapter II (XXIX): Answer to the Letter of Mani Which They Call The Foundation, One Book

The book *Answer to the Letter of Mani Which They Call The Foundation* refutes only its opening parts; but in its other portions notes were affixed where it seemed fitting, by which the whole is overturned, and by which I might be reminded, if ever I should have leisure to write against the whole.

This book begins thus: *The one true almighty God.*

## Chapter III (XXX): The Christian Combat, One Book

The book *The Christian Combat* was written for the brothers unlearned in Latin eloquence, in a humble style, containing the rule of faith and precepts of living.

In it, that which was set down: *nor let us listen to those who deny that the resurrection of the flesh is to come and mention what the apostle Paul says: 'flesh and blood shall not possess the kingdom of God'*[^20]*, not understanding what the Apostle himself says: 'this corruptible must put on incorruption, and this mortal must put on immortality'*[^21]*; for when this has been done, it will no longer be flesh and blood but a heavenly body*[^22]*,* is not to be understood as though the substance of flesh will not exist, but the Apostle is to be understood to have called by the name of *flesh* and *blood* the very corruption of flesh and blood[^23], which certainly will not exist in that kingdom, where the flesh will be incorruptible; although it can also be understood otherwise, so that we take the Apostle to have called by flesh and blood the works of *flesh* and *blood,* and to mean that those who have perseveringly loved these things will not possess *the kingdom of God.*

This book begins thus: *The crown of victory.*

## Chapter IV (XXXI): On Christian Doctrine, Four Books

**1**. The books *On Christian Doctrine,* since I found them unfinished, I preferred to finish rather than, leaving them thus, to pass on to reviewing other things. I therefore completed the third, which had been written up to that place[^24] where the testimony from the Gospel is mentioned *about the woman who hid leaven in three measures of meal, until the whole was leavened*[^25]*.* I added also a last book, and filled out that work with four books, the first three of which help that the Scriptures may be understood, but the fourth, how the things we understand are to be set forth.

**2**. In the second book, indeed[^26], about the author of the book which many call *The Wisdom of Solomon*—that this too, like *Ecclesiasticus,* was written by Jesus Sirach—I afterward learned that it is not so established as was said by me, and I found it altogether more probable that he is not the author of this book.

But where I said: *with these forty-four books the authority of the Old Testament is bounded*[^27]*,* I called it the Old Testament from the custom by which the Church now speaks. But the Apostle does not seem to call anything the Old Testament except that which was given on Mount Sinai[^28].

And in that which I said: *that holy Ambrose solved a question of the history of the times,* as though Plato and Jeremiah had been contemporaries, my memory deceived me. For what that bishop said on this matter is read in the book which he wrote *On the Sacraments, or On Philosophy*[^29].

This work begins thus: *There are certain precepts.*

## Chapter V (XXXII): Against the Party of Donatus, Two Books

There are two books of mine whose title is *Against the Party of Donatus*[^30]. In the first of these I said *that it did not please me that schismatics be violently constrained to communion by the force of any secular power*[^31]*.* And truly it did not then please me, since I had not yet experienced either how much evil their impunity would dare, or how much the diligence of discipline could contribute toward changing them for the better.

This work begins thus: *Since the Donatists to us.*

## Chapter VI (XXXIII): The Confessions, Thirteen Books

**1**. The thirteen books of my *Confessions* praise the just and good God, both for my evils and for my goods, and rouse the human intellect and affection toward him. As far as concerns me, they did this in me when they were written, and do it when they are read. What others think of them, they themselves shall see; but I know that they have pleased and do please many brothers greatly. From the first to the tenth they are written about me; in the other three about the holy Scriptures, from that which is written, *in the beginning God made heaven and earth*[^32], down *to the rest of the sabbath*[^33].

**2**. In the fourth book, when I was confessing the misery of my mind over the death of a friend, saying that our soul had in a manner been made one out of two: *and therefore,* I said, *perhaps I feared to die, lest he whom I had greatly loved should wholly die*[^34]. This seems to me like a light declamation rather than a grave confession, although this ineptitude is somewhat tempered by what was added: *perhaps.*

And in the thirteenth book, what I said—*that a firmament was made between the spiritual upper waters and the corporeal lower ones*[^35]*,* was said without sufficient consideration; but the matter is very deeply hidden.

This work begins thus: *Great are you, O Lord.*

## Chapter VII (XXXIV): Answer to Faustus the Manichee, Thirty-three Books

**1**. *Answer to Faustus the Manichee,* who blasphemed the Law and the Prophets and their God and the incarnation of Christ, and said that the Scriptures of the New Testament, by which he is convicted, had been falsified—I wrote a great work, setting forth his words and giving my responses. There are thirty-three disputations; why should I not call them books? For although some among them are very short, they are nevertheless books. But one of them, where the life of the Patriarchs is defended by us against his accusations, is of as great length as almost none of my books[^36].

**2**. In the third book, then, when I was solving the question how Joseph could have two fathers[^37], I said indeed *that, born of one, he was adopted by another*[^38]*;* but I ought also to have stated the kind of adoption. For what I said sounds as though another father had adopted him while living. But the Law adopted sons even for the dead, commanding that the brother of a brother who had died without sons should take his wife and raise up seed from her for his deceased brother. This indeed affords there a readier account concerning the two fathers of one man. Now they were brothers of the same mother in whom this happened, so that one took the wife of the other who had died, namely Jacob, by whom Matthew relates that Joseph was begotten[^39]. But he begot him for his maternal brother, whose son Luke says Joseph was[^40]—certainly not begotten, but adopted by the law.

This is found in the writings of those who wrote on this matter in recent memory after the Lord's ascension. For Africanus did not pass over in silence even the name of the same woman who bore Jacob, the father of Joseph, by her former husband Matthan—who was the father of Jacob, the grandfather of Joseph according to Matthew—and who by her later husband Melchi bore Heli, whose adoptive son Joseph was[^41]. This indeed, when I answered Faustus, I had not yet read; but nevertheless I could not doubt that, through adoption, it could come about that one man had two fathers.

**3**. In the twelfth and in the thirteenth[^42], concerning the second son of Noah, who was called Ham, it was so argued as though he had been cursed by his father not in his son Canaan, as Scripture shows, but in himself[^43]*.*

In the fourteenth, such things were said of the sun and moon as though they perceive and therefore endure their vain worshipers[^44], although the words there can be taken as transferred from the animate to the inanimate, by the manner of speaking which is called in Greek *metaphor*; as it is written of the sea: *that it roars in its mother's womb, wishing to go forth*[^45], since it certainly has no will.

In the twenty-ninth: *far be it,* I said, *that there be any shamefulness in the members of the saints, even the genital ones. They are indeed called dishonorable, because they do not have that form of comeliness which the members placed in the open have*[^46]*.* But a more probable account was given afterward in other writings of ours[^47] of why the Apostle too called them *dishonorable*—namely, on account of the *law in the members warring against the law of the mind*[^48], which came about from sin, not from the first establishment of our nature.

This work begins thus: *There was a certain Faustus.*

## Chapter VIII (XXXV): Answer to Felix the Manichee, Two Books

Against a certain Manichee named Felix, in the presence of the people, I disputed in the church for two days. For he had come to Hippo, intending to sow the same error; for he was one of their teachers, although unlearned in the liberal letters, but nevertheless more cunning than Fortunatus[^49]. The proceedings are ecclesiastical, but are counted among my books. There are, then, two books, in the second of which there was disputation about the free choice of the will, whether for working evil or good. But to dispute more diligently about the grace by which men become truly free—of whom it is written, *if the Son shall free you, then you will be truly free*[^50]—we were compelled by no necessity, since such was the man with whom we were dealing.

This work begins thus: *When Honorius Augustus was consul for the sixth time, on the seventh day before the Ides of December.*

## Chapter IX (XXXVI): The Nature of the Good, One Book

The book *The Nature of the Good* is against the Manichees, where it is shown that the unchangeable nature is God and the highest good, and that from him are the other natures, whether spiritual or corporeal, and that all of them, in so far as they are natures, are good; and what evil is, or whence it is, and how many evils the Manichees place in the nature of the good, and how many goods in the nature of the evil—which natures their error invented.

This book begins thus: *The highest good, than which there is no higher, is God.*

## Chapter X (XXXVII): Answer to Secundinus the Manichee, One Book

A certain Secundinus, not from those whom the Manichees call "the elect," but from those whom they call "hearers"[^51], whom I did not even know by face, wrote to me as a friend, honorifically reproaching me for assailing that heresy in writing, and admonishing me not to do so, and rather exhorting me to follow it, with a defense of it and a reproach of the Catholic faith.

To him I replied; but because at the head of the same little work I did not set down who was writing to whom, it is held not among my letters but among my books. There, from the beginning, his letter too is written out. But the title of this volume of mine is *Answer to Secundinus the Manichee,* which in my opinion I easily prefer to all that I have been able to write against that plague.

This book begins thus: *Your goodwill toward me, which appears in your letter.*

## Chapter XI (XXXVIII): Against Hilarus, One Book

Meanwhile a certain Hilarus, a man of tribune's rank, a Catholic layman, irritated—I do not know from what cause, as usually happens—against the ministers of God, kept tearing apart with slanderous reproach, wherever he could, the custom which had then begun to be at Carthage[^52], that hymns be said at the altar from the book of *Psalms,* whether before the oblation or while what had been offered was being distributed to the people, asserting that it ought not to be done[^53]. To him I replied, at the bidding of the brothers, and the book itself is called *Against Hilarus.*

This book begins thus: *Those who say that the mention of the Old Testament.*

## Chapter XII (XXXIX): Questions on the Gospels, Two Books

There are certain expositions of certain passages from the Gospel according to Matthew, and others likewise according to Luke; the former were gathered into one book, the latter into the other. The title of this work is *Questions on the Gospels.* But why only those passages of the above-mentioned gospel books were expounded which are contained in these books of mine, and what they are, my prologue sufficiently indicates, with the same questions appended and numbered, so that anyone may find what he wishes to read by following the numbers.

In the first book, then, in that which was set down—*that the Lord recounted his passion apart to two disciples*[^54]—the faultiness of the copy deceived us; for it was written *twelve,* not *two*[^55].

In the second book[^56], wishing to expound how Joseph, whose spouse the Virgin Mary was said to be, could have had two fathers, *that which is reported—that a brother took the wife of his deceased brother, to raise up seed for him according to the law*[^57]—I said *was weak, because the one who would be born, the law commanded to receive the name of the deceased.* This is not true; for the law commanded that the name of the deceased, as it was said, avail for this: that the son be called his son, not that the son be called by his name.

This work begins thus: *This work was not written in such a way.*

## Chapter XIII (XL): Notes on Job, One Book

The book whose title is *Notes on Job*—whether it is to be held as mine, or rather as belonging to those who, as they could or wished, gathered into one body the notes written out from the margins of the copy—I could not easily say. For they are pleasing to very few who understand, who nevertheless must necessarily be offended at not understanding many things, because not even the very words that are expounded are written out in many places in such a way that it is clear what is being expounded.

Then such obscurity has followed the brevity of the sentences that the reader can scarcely bear it, who must necessarily pass over very many things not understood. Finally, I found the work itself so faulty in our copies that I could not correct it, nor would I wish it to be said to have been published by me, except that I know the brothers have it, to whose zeal it could not be denied.

This book begins thus: *And great works were his upon the earth.*

## Chapter XIV (XLI): The Catechizing of the Uninstructed, One Book

There is also our book *The Catechizing of the Uninstructed,* headed with this very title. In this book, where I said: *nor did the angel who, with the other spirits, his attendants, by being proud forsook obedience to God and became the devil, harm God at all, but himself; for God knows how to order the souls that forsake him*[^58], it would be more fitting to say *the spirits that forsake him,* since the discussion was about angels.

This book begins thus: *You have asked of me, brother Deogratias.*

## Chapter XV (XLII): On the Trinity, Fifteen Books

**1**. I wrote fifteen books *On the Trinity,* which is God, over a number of years. But when I had not yet finished the twelfth of them, and was holding them longer than those could bear who vehemently desired to have them, they were taken from me less corrected than they ought and could have been, when I should have wished to publish them.

After I learned this—because other copies of them too had remained with us—I had decided no longer to publish them myself, but to keep them so, that in some other little work of mine I might say what had befallen me concerning them.

Nevertheless, at the urging of the brothers, whom I had not the strength to resist, I corrected them as much as I thought they should be corrected, and completed and published them, attaching to them at the head the letter which I wrote to the venerable Aurelius, bishop of the Church of Carthage; in which, as in a prologue, I set forth both what had happened, and what I had wished by my own thinking to do, and what I had done at the compulsion of the brothers' love.

**2**. In the eleventh book of these, when I was treating of the visible body, I said: *wherefore to love that is to be estranged*[^59]. This was said according to that love by which something is so loved that he who loves it thinks himself blessed in enjoying it. For to love corporeal form to the praise of the Creator, so that anyone, enjoying the Creator himself, may be truly blessed, is not to be estranged.

Likewise in the same book, where I said: *nor do I remember a four-footed bird, because I have not seen one, but I behold such a fancy most easily, while to some winged form, such as I have seen, I add two other feet, such as I have likewise seen*[^60]*,* in saying these things I could not recall the four-footed winged creatures which the law mentions[^61]. For it does not count among the feet the two hinder legs by which locusts leap, which it calls clean and therefore distinguishes from those unclean winged creatures which do not leap with those legs, such as beetles. For all winged creatures of this kind are called four-footed in the law.

**3**. In the twelfth[^62], the so-called *exposition of the Apostle's words, where he says: 'every sin whatever a man commits is outside the body'*[^63]*,* does not satisfy me; nor do I think that what was said—*but he who fornicates sins against his own body*—is to be understood as though he does this who, for the sake of attaining the things that are sensed through the body, so as to place in them the end of his good, does something. For this comprises far more sins than that fornication which is perpetrated by unlawful intercourse[^64], of which it appears the Apostle was speaking when he said this.

This work, except for the letter which was afterward attached at its head, begins thus: *The reader of these things which we discuss concerning the Trinity.*

## Chapter XVI (XLIII): Agreement among the Evangelists, Four Books

During the same years in which I was dictating, little by little, the books *On the Trinity,* I wrote others too, with continuous labor, interposing them in the times of those, among which are the four books *Agreement among the Evangelists,* on account of those who slander them as though they disagreed. The first book of these was written against those who, as though they honor Christ as a most wise man, or pretend to honor him, and therefore refuse to believe the Gospel, because those things were written not by him but by his disciples, whom they suppose to have attributed to him by error the divinity by which he was to be believed God.

In this book, what I said—*that the nation of the Hebrews began from Abraham*[^65]—this too is indeed credible, that the Hebrews seem to be called as it were Abrahaeans. But they are more truly understood to be named from that one who was called Heber, as it were Heberaeans[^66], on which matter I discussed sufficiently in the sixteenth book *The City of God*[^67].

In the second, *when I was treating of the two fathers of Joseph, I said he was begotten by one and adopted by the other.* But it should have been said, "adopted for the other"[^68]; for he had been adopted, according to the law[^69], for the deceased[^70]—which is rather to be believed—since the one who begot him had taken his mother, the wife of his deceased brother.

Likewise, where I said: *but Luke ascends to David himself through Nathan, the prophet through whom God expiated his sin*[^71]*,* I ought to have said, "through the prophet of that name," lest it be thought to have been the same man, when it was another, although he too was called by this name[^72].

This work begins thus: *Among all the divine authorities.*

## Chapter XVII (XLIV): Answer to the Letter of Parmenian, Three Books

In the three books *Answer to the Letter of Parmenian,* the Donatist bishop of Carthage and successor of Donatus, a great question is treated and solved: whether, in the unity and communion of the same sacraments, the evil contaminate the good; and it is argued how they do not contaminate, on account of the Church spread throughout the whole world, against which, by slander, they made a schism.

In the third book of that work[^73], where I discussed how we are to understand the Apostle's words *drive out the wicked person from among yourselves*[^74]*,* I said that what I had written—*that each should drive out the evil from himself*—is not to be understood in that way, but rather that an evil person is to be removed from among good people, which happens through the discipline of the Church. The Greek makes this sufficiently clear, for there it is written without ambiguity so that one understands *this wicked man*, not "this evil thing"—although it was according to this latter sense too that I had replied to Parmenian.

This work begins thus: *Many things, indeed, elsewhere against the Donatists.*

## Chapter 18 (45): Baptism, seven books

Against the Donatists, who endeavor to defend themselves on the authority of the most blessed bishop and martyr Cyprian, I wrote seven books, *On Baptism*. In them I taught that nothing is so effective for refuting the Donatists and for shutting their mouths altogether, so that they may not defend their schism against the Catholic Church, as the letters and the conduct of Cyprian himself.

Wherever in these books I mentioned[^75] *the Church that has no spot or wrinkle*[^76]*,* it is not to be taken as though she were already such, but as one that is being prepared to be such, when she will also appear in glory. For now, on account of certain ignorances and weaknesses of her members, she has reason to say each day, as a whole: *forgive us our debts*[^77].

In the fourth book, when I said *that suffering can take the place of baptism*[^78]*,* I gave a not altogether suitable example, that of the thief on the cross, since it is uncertain whether he had not in fact been baptized[^79].

In the seventh book, concerning the vessels of gold and silver set in a great house, I followed the view of Cyprian, who took these to refer to good people, and the vessels of wood and earthenware to evil people, applying to the former what is said, *some indeed for honor*, and to the latter what is said, *but others for dishonor*[^80]*.* But I more approve what I afterward found, or noticed, in Tyconius: that in both kinds some are to be understood as being *for honor*—not the gold and silver alone—and again in both kinds some *for dishonor*—not the wood and earthenware alone[^81].

This work begins thus: *In those books which I wrote against the letter of Parmenian.*

## Chapter 19 (46): Against What Centurius Brought from the Donatists, one book

While we were carrying on many matters *against the party of Donatus* in frequent disputations, a certain layman of theirs at that time brought to the Church some things dictated or written against us, in the form of a few supposed proofs which they think support their cause; to these I replied very briefly.

The title of this little book is *Against What Centurius Brought from the Donatists*[^82]. And it begins thus: *You say that, because it is written by Solomon, 'keep yourself from a strange woman.'*[^83]

## Chapter 20 (47): To the Inquiries of Januarius, two books

The two books whose title is *To the Inquiries of Januarius* contain many discussions of the sacraments, both those the Church observes universally and those she observes only in part—that is, not uniformly in all places; yet it was not possible to mention them all, but enough was said in answer to what had been asked. The first of these books is a letter, for it has at its head a statement of who is writing to whom. But this work is reckoned among the books because the one that follows, which does not bear our names, is much longer, and in it many more matters are treated.

In the first book, then, where I said of the manna *that it tasted in each one's mouth according to his own will*[^84]*,* I cannot recall any source from which this could be proved except the book of *Wisdom*[^85], which the Jews do not receive into the canonical authority. This, however, could have happened to the faithful, not to those murmurers against God, who would surely not have longed for other food if the manna had tasted to them of whatever they wished.

This work begins thus: *To the things about which you have questioned me.*

## Chapter 21 (48): The Work of Monks, one book

What compelled me to write the book *The Work of Monks* was this necessity: when monasteries had begun to be established at Carthage, some monks supported themselves by their own hands, obeying the Apostle[^86], while others wished to live off the offerings of the devout in such a way that, doing no work by which to obtain or supply what they needed, they reckoned and boasted that they were rather fulfilling the gospel precept where the Lord says: *look at the birds of the air and the lilies of the field*[^87].

From this there had even begun to arise, among laypeople of a lower way of life but nonetheless fervent in zeal, tumultuous quarrels by which the Church was disturbed, some defending one position and some another. To this was added that some of those who said one ought not to work wore long hair. Hence the contentions grew, with some reproaching them and others, as it were, defending them, according to the partisan zeal of each side.

On account of these things the venerable elder Aurelius, bishop of the Church of that city, ordered me to write something on the matter, and I did so.

This book begins thus: *In response to your command, holy brother Aurelius.*

## Chapter 22 (49): The Excellence of Marriage, one book

**1**. The heresy of Jovinian, by equating the merit of consecrated virgins with conjugal chastity, had such force in the city of Rome that some nuns, about whose chastity no suspicion had previously arisen, were said to have been brought down into marriage, especially since he pressed them with this argument, saying: *Are you, then, better than Sarah, better than Susanna or Anna?* And by recalling other women highly commended by the testimony of holy Scripture, he urged that those nuns could not think themselves better than these, or even equal to them. In this way too he broke down the holy celibacy of holy men by recalling the married patriarchs and comparing them.

The holy Church that is there resisted this monstrous teaching most faithfully and most firmly. But these arguments of his had lingered in the little speeches and whispers of certain people, which no one dared to urge openly. Yet, since the poisons were creeping about even in secret, they had to be met, by the means the Lord granted; and especially because it was being boasted that Jovinian could not be answered with praise of marriage, but only with disparagement of it.

For this reason I published a book whose title is *The Excellence of Marriage.* There, a great question was deferred—concerning the propagation of children before human beings, by sinning, deserved death, since the intercourse of mortal bodies seems to be a bodily matter; but it is explained afterward in other writings of ours, sufficiently, I think[^88].

**2**. I also said in one place: *What food is to the health of the individual, intercourse is to the health of the race; and neither is without carnal pleasure, which nonetheless, when it is moderated and brought back to its natural use by the restraint of temperance, cannot be lust*[^89]*.* This was said because lust is not a good and right use of sexual desire. For just as it is evil to use good things badly, so it is good to use evil things well. On this matter I have argued more carefully elsewhere, especially against the new heretics, the Pelagians[^90].

What I said about Abraham—that *by this obedience that father Abraham, who was not without a wife, was ready to be without his only son, and slain by his own hand*[^91]*,* I do not altogether approve. For he ought rather to be believed to have believed that his son, if he were slain, would at once be restored to him by resurrection[^92], as one reads in the *Letter to the Hebrews*[^93].

This book begins thus: *Since each individual human being is a part of the human race.*

## Chapter 23 (50): Holy Virginity, one book

After I had written *The Excellence of Marriage*, it was expected that I would write *Holy Virginity*; nor did I delay, and in a single volume, as best I could, I showed how great a gift of God it is and with how great a humility it must be guarded.

This book begins thus: *We recently published the book on the excellence of marriage.*

## Chapter 24 (51): The Literal Meaning of Genesis, twelve books

**1**. Around the same time I wrote twelve books *On Genesis*, from its beginning down to where Adam was sent out of paradise and *a flaming sword was set to guard the way to the tree of life*[^94]. But when eleven books had been completed up to this point, I added a twelfth, in which there is a more careful discussion of paradise.

The title of these books is inscribed *The Literal Meaning of Genesis,* that is, not according to allegorical significations[^95], but according to the proper character of the events that took place. In this work more things were sought than found, and of those that were found fewer were established, while the rest were set down as though they had still to be inquired into.

These books I indeed began later, but finished before *On the Trinity.* Therefore I have now reviewed them in the order in which I began them.

**2**. In the fifth book, and wherever in these books I set down *concerning the offspring to which the promise was made, that it was ordained 'through angels by the hand of a Mediator'*[^96]*,* the Apostle does not have it so, as I afterward saw in the more accurate manuscripts, especially the Greek ones. For it was said of the law, which many Latin manuscripts, through a translator's error, have as though it were said of the *offspring*.

In the sixth book, what I said—*that Adam lost by sin the image of God according to which he was made*[^97]*,* is not to be taken as though none of it remained in him, but that it was so disfigured as to need reforming.

In the twelfth book, concerning the regions below, I think I ought rather to have taught that they are beneath the earth than to have given a reason why they are believed or said to be beneath the earth*,* as if it were not so[^98].

This work begins thus: *All divine Scripture is twofold.*

## Chapter 25 (52): Answer to the Writings of Petilian, three books

Before I finished the books *On the Trinity* and the books *The Literal Meaning of Genesis*, there arose the occasion of replying to the writings of Petilian the Donatist, which he had written against the Catholic Church; and this I could not put off.

And I wrote three volumes on this matter. In the first I replied, with what speed and truthfulness I could, to the first part of his letter which he had written to his own people—since it had not all come into our hands, but only its earlier, small part. This too is itself a letter to our people, but it is reckoned among the books because the other two on the same matter are books.

For afterward we found the whole of it, and I replied to it with as much diligence as I had to Faustus the Manichee—that is, first setting down his words under his own name, piece by piece, and under my own name my reply to each. But what I had written before, prior to finding the whole, reached Petilian, and in anger he tried to reply, saying against me rather whatever he pleased, but altogether failing in the matter at issue. Although this could very easily have been observed by comparing the writings of us both, I nonetheless took care, for the sake of slower readers, to demonstrate it by my reply. Thus a third book was added to this same work of ours.

This work begins, in the first book, thus: *You know that we have often wished;*

and in the second thus: *In the first parts of Petilian's letter;*

and in the third thus: *I have read your letter, Petilian.*

## Chapter 26 (53): To Cresconius, a Grammarian — of the Party of Donatus, four books

A certain Donatist grammarian named Cresconius, too, when he had come upon my letter in which I refuted the first parts of Petilian's letter that had then come into our hands[^99], thought he ought to answer me, and wrote to me to that effect. To this work of his I replied in four books, in such a way, however, that I completed in three what the whole reply demanded.

But when I saw that everything he had written could be answered from the case of the Maximianists alone—whom they had condemned as their own schismatics, yet had afterward received some of them back into their own offices, and had not repeated the baptism given by them outside their communion—I added a fourth book as well, in which I showed this very point as carefully and clearly as I could. When I wrote these four books, the emperor Honorius had already issued laws against the Donatists[^100].

This work begins thus: *Not knowing when my writings could reach you, Cresconius.*

## Chapter 27 (54): Proofs and Testimonies against the Donatists, one book

After this, in order that the documents necessary against their error and on behalf of Catholic truth might reach the Donatists, I took care to gather them—whether from ecclesiastical records, or from public records, or from the canonical Scriptures. And first I sent these promised materials to them, so that they themselves might, if it could be done, demand them.

When these had come into the hands of some of them, someone—I do not know who—came forward to write against them without giving his name, confessing himself a Donatist in such a way as if that were his name. Replying to him, I wrote another book. But those documents which I had promised I joined to the very little book in which I had promised them, and I wished the two to be one; and I published it in such a way that it was first posted up to be read on the walls of the basilica which had formerly belonged to the Donatists. Its title is *Proofs and Testimonies against the Donatists.*

In this book we set down the acquittal of Felix of Aptungi[^101], the ordainer of Caecilian, not in that order which afterward became clear to us when the consuls had been carefully examined, but as though he had been acquitted after Caecilian*,* whereas it had taken place before.

There is also this: after I had cited the testimony of the apostle Jude, where he says, *these are the ones who set themselves apart, worldly people, not having the Spirit*[^102]*,* I added, saying*: of whom the Apostle Paul also says, 'the unspiritual man does not receive the things that are of the Spirit of God'*[^103]*,* these latter are not to be put on a level with those whom schism has cut off altogether from the Church.

For those latter the same apostle Paul says are *babes in Christ*, whom, not yet able to take solid food, he nonetheless nourishes with milk; but the others are to be counted not among little children but among the dead and the lost, so that, if any of them, being corrected, should be joined to the Church, it may rightly be said of him: *he was dead and has come to life again, he was lost and is found*[^104].

This book begins thus: *You who fear to consent to the Catholic Church.*

## Chapter 28 (55): Against an Unnamed Donatist, one book

I wished the title of the other book, which I mentioned above, to be *Against an Unnamed Donatist.*

Here likewise, concerning the acquittal of Caecilian's ordainer, the true order of time is not given.

There is also this, that I said: *to the multitude of the weeds*[^105]*, where all heresies are understood,* lacks one necessary conjunction; for it ought to have been said: *where* both *all heresies are understood,* or: *where* even *all heresies are understood.* As it stands, it was said as though the weeds were only outside the Church and not also within the Church—whereas she herself is the kingdom of Christ, from which his angels will gather all causes of stumbling at the time of harvest[^106].

Hence Cyprian the martyr also says: *Although weeds appear to be in the Church, our faith or our love ought not on that account to be hindered, so that, because we perceive that there are weeds in the Church, we ourselves should withdraw from the Church*[^107]. This view we too have defended elsewhere[^108], and especially against these same Donatists present at the conference.

This book begins thus: *We promised proofs of the necessary matters, gathered in a brief summary.*

## Chapter 29 (56): A Warning to the Donatists about the Maximianists, — one book

When I saw that many were hindered from learning, by the labor of reading, how utterly devoid of reason and truth the party of Donatus is, I made a very short little book, in which I thought they should be warned by the case of the Maximianists alone, so that, being easy to copy, it might come into the hands of more people, and by its very brevity be more easily committed to memory. I gave it the title *A Warning to the Donatists about the Maximianists.*

This book begins thus: *Whoever you are who are moved by the slanders and accusations of men.*

## Chapter 30 (57): On the Divination of Demons, one book

Around the same time it fell to me, out of a certain discussion, that I had to write the little book *On the Divination of Demons*, whose title is just this.

But in a certain place in it, where I said: *that demons sometimes learn with complete ease even the dispositions of human beings, not only those uttered by the voice but even those conceived in thought, when certain signs are expressed from the mind upon the body*[^109]*,* I stated a most hidden matter with a bolder assertion than I ought. For it has been ascertained, even by some experiences, that these things come to the knowledge of demons. But whether certain signs are given from the body of those who are thinking—signs perceptible to them but hidden from us—or whether they know these things by some other power, and that a spiritual one, can be discovered by human beings only with the greatest difficulty, or not at all.

This book begins thus: *On a certain day, in the holy days of the octave.*

## Chapter 31 (58): Questions Expounded against the Pagans, six in number

Meanwhile there were sent to me from Carthage six questions, which a certain friend, whom I longed to see become a Christian, had put forward, so that they might be answered against the pagans—especially because he said that some of them had been raised by the philosopher Porphyry. But I do not think he is that famous Porphyry the Sicilian. I gathered the discussions of these questions into one book, not a long one, whose title is *Six Questions Expounded against the Pagans*[^110]*.*

The first of them is: *On the Resurrection;*

the second: *On the Time of the Christian Religion;*

the third: *On the Distinction of Sacrifices;*

the fourth: *On what is written, 'with the measure you give it will be measured to you'*[^111]*;*

the fifth: *On the Son of God according to Solomon;*

the sixth: *On the Prophet Jonah.*

In the second of these, what I said: *the salvation of this religion, through which alone true salvation is truly and truthfully promised, was never lacking to anyone who was worthy of it; and to whomever it was lacking, he was not worthy*[^112]*,* I did not say as though anyone were worthy by his own merits, but in the way the Apostle says that it was *not by works but by him who calls* that it was said: *the elder shall serve the younger*[^113],

a calling which he asserts to belong to the purpose of God. Hence he says: *not according to our works, but according to his own purpose and grace*[^114]; and hence he likewise says: *we know that for those who love God all things work together for good, for those who are called according to his purpose*[^115]. Of this calling he says: *that he may make you worthy of his holy calling*[^116].

This book, after the letter which was afterward added at its head[^117], begins thus: *It troubles certain people, and they ask.*

## Chapter 32 (59): Exposition of the Epistle of James to the Twelve Tribes, one book

Among my little works I found an *Exposition of the Epistle of James*, which, on reviewing it, I noticed was rather a set of notes on certain passages of it, reduced to a book by the diligence of the brethren, who did not wish them to remain in the margins of the manuscript. They are therefore of some help, except that the epistle itself, which we were reading when I dictated these things, we did not have carefully translated from the Greek.

This book begins thus: *To the twelve tribes that are in the dispersion, greeting.*

## Chapter 33 (60): The Punishment and Forgiveness of Sins, and the Baptism of Infants, to Marcellinus, three books

There also came a necessity compelling me to write against the new Pelagian heresy, against which previously, when there was need, we had acted not by writings but by sermons and conversations[^118], each of us as he could or ought. So when there were sent to me from Carthage their questions, which I was to resolve by writing back, I first wrote three books, whose title is *The Punishment and Forgiveness of Sins.* There the chief discussion is of the baptism of infants on account of original sin, and of the grace of God by which we are justified, that is, made righteous—although in this life no one so *keeps the commandments*[^119] of righteousness as not to need, in praying for his sins, to say: *forgive us our debts*[^120]. Holding views contrary to all this, they founded the new heresy.

In these books I thought their names should still be passed over in silence[^121], hoping that in this way they could more easily be corrected; indeed, even in the third book—which is a letter, but reckoned among the books because of the two to which I thought it should be joined[^122]—I set down the name of Pelagius himself not without some praise, since his life was being commended by many; and I refuted those views which in his writings he had set down not in his own person, but expounding what was being said by others—views which afterward, when now a heretic, he defended with most obstinate animosity. Celestius, however, his disciple, had already on account of such assertions deserved excommunication at Carthage in an episcopal court at which I was not present[^123].

In the second book, in a certain place, I said: *this he will grant to some at the end, that they may not feel death by reason of a sudden change*[^124]*,* reserving the matter for a more careful inquiry. For either they will not die, or, passing from this life into death and from death into eternal life by a most rapid change, as *in the twinkling of an eye*[^125], they will not feel death.

This work begins thus: *Although in the midst of great and surging cares.*

## Chapter 34 (61): On the One Baptism, against Petilian, to Constantine, one book

At the same time a certain friend of mine received from some Donatist presbyter a book *On the One Baptism*, with the information that Petilian, their bishop, had written it for Constantine. He brought it to me and urgently begged me to reply to it; and so it was done. I wished my own book, in which I replied, to have the same title, that is, *On the One Baptism.*

In this book, what I said—*that the emperor Constantine, when the Donatists were bringing charges against Felix of Aptungi, the ordainer of Caecilian, did not deny him the opportunity of an accusation, although he had found them slanderous in the false charges against Caecilian*[^126]*,* was, when the order of times was afterward considered, found to be otherwise[^127]. For the emperor first had the case of Felix heard by the proconsul, where he is recorded as acquitted; and afterward he himself, having had Caecilian heard together with his accusers, found him innocent, and there found them slanderous in their charges against him. This order of times, made clear by the consuls, far more forcefully convicts and utterly overturns the slanders of the Donatists in that case, as we have shown elsewhere[^128].

This book begins thus: *To answer those who hold differing views.*

## Chapter 35 (62): On the Maximianists, against the Donatists, one book

Among other works against the Donatists I also wrote a book, not very short as before, but large, and much more carefully done. In it appears how the case of the Maximianists alone utterly overturns their impious and most arrogant error against the Catholic Church—a schism that arose from the very party of Donatus.

This book begins thus: *We have already said many things, we have already written many things.*

## Chapter 36 (63): On the Grace of the New Testament, to Honoratus, — one book

At that very time, when we were being hard pressed against the Donatists and had already begun to be pressed against the Pelagians, a certain friend sent me five questions from Carthage and asked that I expound them for him in writing. They are these:

*what is the meaning of that cry of the Lord: 'My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?'*[^129];

And: *what is the meaning of what the Apostle says,* '*that, being rooted and grounded in love, you may have power to comprehend with all the saints what is the breadth and length and height and depth'*[^130].

And: *which are the five foolish virgins and which the wise*[^131]*.*

And: *what is the outer darkness*[^132].

And: *how is it to be understood, 'the Word was made flesh'*[^133].

But I, looking to the above-mentioned new heresy hostile to the grace of God, set myself a sixth question, *On the Grace of the New Testament*. Discussing this, and inserting an exposition of the twenty-first Psalm, at the head of which is written what the Lord cried out on the cross—which that friend had put to me first of all to expound—I resolved all those five questions, not in the order in which they had been put, but as they could fittingly come up, as it were in their own places, while I was discussing *the grace of the New Testament*[^134].

This book begins thus: *You have put to me five questions to be treated.*

## Chapter 37 (64): The Spirit and the Letter, to Marcellinus, one book

To him I had written three books, whose title is *The Punishment and Forgiveness of Sins,* where there is also a careful discussion of the baptism of infants[^135]. He wrote back to me that he had been disturbed by my saying that it could happen that a person be without sin, if his will be not lacking, with the help of divine aid, although no one of such perfect righteousness either has been, or is, or will be in this life. For he asked how I could say that a thing could happen of which there is no example. On account of this inquiry of his I wrote a book whose title is *The Spirit and the Letter,* treating the apostolic sentence where he says: *the letter kills, but the Spirit gives life*[^136]. In this book, as far as God helped me, I argued keenly against the enemies of the grace of God, by which the ungodly is justified.

But when I was treating of the observances of the Jews, who abstain from certain foods according to the old law, I said: *the ceremonies of certain foods*[^137]*,* a word which is not in use in the Holy Writings. It nonetheless seemed fitting to me, because I remembered that *ceremonies*[^138] were so named from "lacking" (carere), as it were *carimoniae*, in that those who observe them lack the things from which they abstain. But if there is another origin of this word, one that is repugnant to true religion, it was not according to that that I spoke, but according to the one I mentioned above.

This book begins thus: *Having read the little works which I recently composed for you, dearest son Marcellinus.*

## Chapter 38 (65): Faith and Works, one book

Meanwhile there were sent to me, by certain brethren who were laymen indeed but devoted to the divine words, some writings which so distinguished Christian faith from good works as to urge that without this faith one cannot, but without those works one can, attain to eternal life. Replying to them, I wrote a book whose name is *Faith and Works.* In it I discussed not only how those who are reborn by the grace of Christ ought to live, but also what sort of people are to be admitted *to the washing of regeneration*[^139].

This book begins thus: *It seems to certain people.*

## Chapter 39 (66): A Summary of the Conference with the Donatists, three books

After our conference with the Donatists had taken place, I briefly recorded what had been done, and set it down in writing according to the three days on which we conferred with them. I judged this work to be useful, so that anyone, being thereby informed, might either know without labor what was done, or, by consulting the numbers which I noted against each item, might read in the records themselves any passage he wished—since those records weary the reader by their excessive length. The title of this work is *A Summary of the Conference.*

This work begins thus: *When the Catholic bishops and those of the party of Donatus.*

## Chapter 40 (67): After the Conference, against the Donatists, one book

I also wrote a book, large enough as I judge, carefully addressed to the Donatists themselves after the conference we had with their bishops, lest they be led astray by them any further. There I replied also to certain empty claims of theirs, such as could come to us, which, when defeated, they kept boasting where and as they could—besides what I said about the records of the conference, from which what was done might be briefly known. But I treated this much more briefly in a certain letter addressed again to the same people; yet because in the council of Numidia it was resolved by all of us who were there that this should be done, it is not among my letters. For it begins thus: *Silvanus the elder, Valentinus, Innocentius, Maximinus, Optatus, Augustine, Donatus, and the other bishops of the council of Zerta, to the Donatists*[^140].

This book begins thus: *Why, Donatists, are you still being led astray?*

## Chapter 41 (68): On Seeing God, one book

I wrote a book *On Seeing God*[^141], where I deferred a more careful inquiry into the spiritual body which will exist in the resurrection of the saints—whether, or how, *God, who is spirit*[^142], will be seen even through such a body. But afterward I explained that surely most difficult question sufficiently, as I think, in the last, that is, the twenty-second book, of *The City of God*[^143]. I also found, in a certain manuscript of mine in which this book too is contained, a kind of memorandum made by me on this matter to Fortunatianus, bishop of Sicca, which is noted in the list of my little works neither among the books nor among the letters.

This book begins thus: *Mindful of my debt;*

and that one: *as I asked when present, so now I remind you*.

## Chapter 42 (69): Nature and Grace, one book

There also came into my hands at that time a certain book of Pelagius, in which he defended human nature, with all the argumentation he could, against the grace of God by which the ungodly is justified and by which we are Christians. So the book in which I replied to it, defending grace not against nature but as that by which nature is set free and governed, I named *Nature and Grace*.

In it I defended certain words, which Pelagius set down as though they were those of Xystus, the Roman bishop and martyr, as if they were truly the words of that same Xystus[^144]; for so I had thought. But afterward I read that they were of Sextus the philosopher, not of Xystus the Christian.

This book begins thus: *The book which you sent.*

## Chapter 43 (70): The City of God, twenty-two books

**1**. Meanwhile Rome was overthrown by the inrush of the Goths acting under their king Alaric, and by the onslaught of a great disaster. The worshipers of the many false gods, whom by the usual name we call pagans, trying to lay this overthrow to the charge of the Christian religion, began to blaspheme the true God more sharply and bitterly than usual. Wherefore I, burning with zeal for the house of God[^145], set out to write the books *The City of God* against their blasphemies and errors. This work occupied me for several years, because many other things kept intervening which ought not to be deferred and which claimed me first for their resolution. But this great work *The City of God* was at last completed in twenty-two books.

The first five of these refute those who wish human affairs to prosper in such a way that they reckon the worship of the many gods, which the pagans were accustomed to offer, to be necessary for this, and who contend that, because it is forbidden, these evils arise and abound.

The next five speak against those who acknowledge that these evils have never been lacking, nor will be lacking, to mortals, and that they vary—now great, now small—according to places, times, and persons, but who argue that the worship of the many gods, in which sacrifice is offered to them, is useful on account of the life that is to be after death.

In these ten books, then, those two empty opinions, hostile to the Christian religion, are refuted.

**2**. But lest anyone reproach us with having only refuted the views of others and not asserted our own, the other part of this work, which is contained in twelve books, does that—although, where there is need, we also assert what is ours in the first ten, and refute what is opposed in the last twelve.

Of the twelve following books, then, the first four contain the rise of the two cities, of which one is God's and the other this world's; the second four, their course, or running on; the third, which are also the last, their due ends.

Thus all twenty-two books, though they are written about both cities, nonetheless took their title from the better, so that it was called rather *The City of God.*

In the tenth of these books it ought not to have been put down as a miracle that in Abraham's sacrifice a flame made from heaven ran among the divided victims[^146], since this was shown to him in a vision.

In the seventeenth, what was said of Samuel—*he was not of the sons of Aaron*[^147]*,* ought rather to have been said: "he was not the son of a priest." For it was more the lawful custom that the sons of priests should succeed the priests when they died. For Samuel's father is found among the sons of Aaron; but he was not a priest, nor among the sons in the sense that Aaron himself begot him, but in the sense that all of that people are called sons of Israel.

This work begins thus: *The most glorious city of God.*

## Chapter 44 (71): To Orosius the Presbyter, against the Priscillianists and Origenists, one book

Meanwhile I replied, with as much brevity and clarity as I could, to the inquiry of a certain Spanish presbyter, Orosius, concerning the Priscillianists and certain views of Origen which the Catholic faith disapproves. The title of this little work is *To Orosius, against the Priscillianists and Origenists.* For the inquiry itself is joined to my reply at its head.

This book begins thus: *To answer you in your questioning, most beloved son Orosius.*

## Chapter 45 (72): To Jerome the Presbyter, two books: one on the origin of the soul and the other on the saying of James

I also wrote two books to the presbyter Jerome[^148], who was dwelling in Bethlehem: one *On the Origin of the Soul* of man, the other *On the Saying of the Apostle James,* where he says: *whoever keeps the whole law but fails in one point has become guilty of all of it*[^149], consulting him on both. But in the former I did not myself resolve the question I had put forward, while in the latter I did not keep silent about what seemed to me the way to resolve it, but consulted him on whether he too approved this. He wrote back praising this consultation of mine, but answered that he had no leisure to reply. I, however, did not wish to publish these books while he was still in the body, lest perhaps he should reply at some time, and they might be published together with his very reply.

But after his death I published the former for this purpose: that whoever reads it may be admonished either not to inquire at all how the soul is given to those being born, or at least, on a most obscure matter, to admit such a solution of this question[^150] as is not contrary to those most evident things which the Catholic faith knows about original sin in infants—who, unless they are reborn in Christ, are without doubt to be condemned; and the latter for this purpose: that the solution of the question there treated, the very one that seemed right to us, may also be known.

This work begins thus: *Our God, who has called us.*

## Chapter 46 (73): To Emeritus, Bishop of the Donatists, after the Conference, one book

To Emeritus, bishop of the Donatists, who in our conference with them seemed to be the chief advocate of their cause, I wrote, some while after that same conference, a book useful enough, since it embraces with convenient brevity the matters by which they are overcome, or shown to have been overcome.

This book begins thus: *If even now, brother Emeritus.*

## Chapter 47 (74): The Deeds of Pelagius, one book

Around the same time, in the East—that is, in Syria Palestine—Pelagius was brought before an episcopal court by certain Catholic brethren, and, in the absence of those who had presented the document against him, since they could not arrive by the day of the synod, he was heard by fourteen bishops[^151], who, when he condemned those very doctrines that were read out against him from the document as hostile to the grace of Christ, pronounced him Catholic.

But when those same proceedings had come into our hands, I wrote a book about them, lest, on the supposition that he had been acquitted, the judges should be thought to have approved also those same doctrines—which, unless he had condemned them, he would in no way have departed from them except condemned.

This book begins thus: *After there came into our hands.*

## Chapter 48 (75): On the Correction of the Donatists, one book

At the same time I also wrote a book *On the Correction of the Donatists* on account of those who did not wish them to be corrected by the imperial laws[^152].

This book begins thus: *I praise and congratulate and admire.*

## Chapter 49 (76): On the Presence of God, to Dardanus, one book

I wrote a book *On the Presence of God*, in which our purpose is especially watchful against the Pelagian heresy, though it is not named expressly. But in it there is also a laborious and subtle discussion concerning the presence of that nature which we call the supreme and true God, and concerning his temple[^153].

This book begins thus: *I confess, most beloved brother Dardanus.*

## Chapter 50 (77): Against Pelagius and Celestius, on the Grace of Christ and on Original Sin, to Albina, Pinianus, and Melania, two books

After the Pelagian heresy, together with its authors, had been convicted and condemned by the bishops of the Roman Church—first Innocent, then Zosimus[^154]—with the cooperation of the letters of the African councils, I wrote two books against them: one, *On the Grace of Christ,* the other, *On Original Sin.*

This work begins thus: *How greatly we rejoice over your welfare, both bodily and especially spiritual.*

## Chapter 51 (78): The Proceedings with Emeritus, Bishop of the Donatists, — one book

Some while after the conference we had with the Donatist heretics, a necessity arose for us of going to Mauretania Caesariensis. There, at Caesarea itself, we saw Emeritus, bishop of the Donatists—one, that is, of the seven whom they had chosen for the defense of their cause, and who had labored most in that cause. What we transacted with him, in the presence of the bishops of that province and the people of the Church of Caesarea—the city of which he was both a citizen and the bishop of the said heretics—the ecclesiastical records testify, which are contained among my little works. There, finding nothing to answer, he heard, as though mute, the whole of my address, which I delivered concerning the Maximianists alone in his hearing and that of all who were present.

This book, or these proceedings, begin thus: *In the consulship of the most glorious emperors Honorius, for the twelfth time, and Theodosius, for the eighth, on the twelfth day before the Kalends of October, at Caesarea, in the greater church.*

## Chapter 52 (79): Answer to an Arian Sermon, one book

Meanwhile there came into my hands a certain sermon of the Arians, without the name of its author. To this, at the request and insistence of the one who had sent it to me, I replied with all the brevity and speed I could; and I joined the same sermon to the head of my reply, and applied numbers to each section, so that by examining them one might easily observe what I had answered to each passage.

This book, after their sermon, which is joined at the head, begins thus: *To their preceding argument I reply with this argument.*

## Chapter 53 (80): Marriage and Desire, to Valerius, two books

I wrote two books to the illustrious count Valerius, when I had heard that the Pelagians had written something to him about us—namely, that we were condemning marriage by asserting original sin. The title of these books is *Marriage and Desire.* For we defended the goodness of marriage, lest it be thought that the defect of marriage is *the desire of the flesh and the law in the members warring against the law of the mind*[^155]—an evil of lust which conjugal chastity uses well for the procreation of children.

As to how there came to be two books: the first came into the hands of Julian the Pelagian, and he wrote four books against it[^156], from which someone excerpted certain passages and sent them to count Valerius, and he in turn to us. When I had received these, I replied to them in another book[^157].

The first book of this work begins thus: *The new heretics, most beloved son Valerius;*

and the second thus: *Amid the cares of your military service.*

## Chapter 54 (81): Expressions, seven books

I made seven books on the seven books of the divine Scriptures—that is, the five of Moses, and one of Joshua son of Nun, and another of Judges—noting the expressions of each that are less usual in our language, expressions which those who read the divine words, paying too little attention to the sense, treat as questions, when it is really a turn of phrase; and sometimes they dig out something that does not, indeed, conflict with the truth, but which the author by whom it was written is not found to have meant—rather, it appears more credible that he said it as a turn of phrase.

Now many obscure things in the holy Scriptures become clear once the turn of phrase is recognized. For this reason these turns of phrase are to be learned where the meaning is plain, so that even where it is hidden this very knowledge may come to one's aid and open them up to the attention of the reader.

The title of this work is *Expressions on Genesis,* and so on for each book.

But what I set down in the first book[^158]—that it is written: *and Noah did all the words that the Lord commanded him, so he did*[^159], and which expression I said was similar to the one in the founding of creation where, after it is said *and so it was done,* there is added *and God made*[^160]*,*—does not seem to me to be similar in every way to that. For in the latter the meaning too lies hidden, whereas in the former it is only a turn of phrase.

This work begins thus: *The expressions of the Scriptures.*

## Chapter 55 (82): Questions, seven books

**1**. At the same time I also wrote seven books of *Questions* on those same divine books, which I wished to name thus because the things discussed there I put forward as matters rather to be inquired into than as questions resolved—although many more of them seem to me to be treated in such a way that they could not undeservedly be judged also resolved and expounded.

We had also begun to consider the *books of Kingdoms* in the same way; but, not having gone far, we turned our mind to other matters that pressed more urgently.

But in the first book, where the matter discussed is the spotted rods that Jacob set in the water, so that the ewes, set there at conception, might see them when they drank and bring forth spotted young[^161], the reason was not well explained by us why he did not set them out for those conceiving again—that is, when they were conceiving other young—but only at the first conception[^162]. For the explanation of the other question[^163], where it is asked why Jacob said to his father-in-law, *and you cheated me of my wages by ten lambs*[^164], when unraveled truly enough, shows that this question, as it ought to have been resolved, was not resolved.

**2**. In the third book also, where the matter discussed is the high priest[^165]—how he begot sons, since he had the necessity of entering twice a day into the Holy of Holies, where the altar of incense was, to offer incense morning and evening[^166], into which, as the law says, no unclean person could enter, and since the same law says that a man is made unclean even by conjugal intercourse[^167], whom indeed it commands to wash with water, yet says that even after washing he is *unclean until evening*—whence I said that *it followed that either he had to be continent, or on some days the incense had to be interrupted,* I did not see that this did not follow. For what is written, *he shall be unclean until evening*, can be so understood that by evening itself he was no longer unclean, but only up to it, so that at the evening hour he might offer incense clean, having joined with his wife after the morning incense for the begetting of sons.

Likewise, where it was asked how the high priest was forbidden to go in over the dead body of his father[^168], since he ought not to be made priest, when there was only one, except after the death of his priest father, I said: *that for this reason it was necessary, while the father was not yet buried, immediately after his death, to appoint his son to succeed the father, also on account of the continuation of the incense, which had to be offered twice a day*[^169]*,* a priest who is forbidden to go in over the death of his not-yet-buried father.

But I attended too little to the fact that this could be commanded rather on account of those who were to be high priests, not succeeding fathers who were high priests, but nonetheless from among the sons—that is, from the posterity of Aaron—if perchance a high priest either had no sons, or had such reprobate ones that none of them ought to succeed his father; just as Samuel succeeded the high priest Eli[^170], though he was not himself a priest's son, yet was from among the sons, that is, from the posterity of Aaron.

**3**. Concerning the thief too, to whom it was said, *today you will be with me in paradise*[^171], I put down as though certain that he had not been visibly baptized, whereas it is uncertain, and it is rather to be believed that he had been baptized, as I too afterward argued elsewhere[^172].

Likewise, what I said in the fifth book, where the mothers are mentioned in the gospel genealogies—*that they are set down only together with the fathers*[^173]—is indeed true, but does not pertain to the matter under discussion. For the matter under discussion was those who married the wives of brothers or kinsmen who had died without children, on account of the two fathers of Joseph, one of whom Matthew mentions and the other Luke. On this question I discussed carefully in this work, when we were reviewing our work *Answer to Faustus the Manichee*[^174].

This work begins thus: *When the holy Scriptures, which are called canonical.*

## Chapter 56 (83): The Nature and Origin of the Soul, four books

At the same time a certain Vincentius Victor, in Mauretania Caesariensis, found in the possession of a certain Spanish presbyter, Peter, a little work of mine, where in a certain place I confessed that I did not know about the origin of the soul of each individual human being—whether they are propagated from that one soul of the first man and then from the parents, or whether, like that one, they are given individually to individuals without any propagation[^175]—but that I nonetheless knew the soul to be not a body but a spirit; and against these statements of mine he wrote two books to that same Peter, which the monk Renatus sent me from Caesarea.

Having read these, I gave my reply in four: one to the monk Renatus, another to the presbyter Peter, and two to that same Victor. But the one to Peter, although it has the length of a book, is nonetheless a letter, which I did not wish to separate from the other three. In all of these, in which many necessary matters are discussed, I defended my hesitation concerning the origin of the souls that are given to individual human beings, and I pointed out many errors and the perversities of his presumption. Yet I treated him, as a young man not to be hastily detested but still to be taught, with all the gentleness I could; and I received from him a written reply of his correction.

The book of this work addressed to Renatus begins thus: *Your sincerity toward us;*

and that to Peter thus: *To the lord, most beloved brother and fellow presbyter Peter;*

and the first of the two last, to Vincentius Victor, begins thus: *What I thought I should write to you.*

## Chapter 57 (84): To Pollentius, on Adulterous Marriages, two books

I wrote two books *Adulterous Marriages*, as far as I could according to the Scriptures, desiring to resolve a most difficult question. Whether I have done this most clearly I do not know; rather, I am aware that I have not arrived at the perfection of this matter, although I have opened up many of its recesses—as anyone who reads with understanding will be able to judge.

The first book of this work begins thus: *The first question is, most beloved brother Pollentius.*

And the second thus: *To the things you had written to me.*

## Chapter 58 (85): Answer to an Enemy of the Law and the Prophets, two books

Meanwhile a certain book of some heretic—whether a Marcionist or one of those whose error holds that this world was not made by God, and that the God of the Law given through Moses and of the Prophets pertaining to that same Law is not the true God but a most wicked demon—was being read at Carthage, with many gathering and listening most attentively, in the seaside square. Some most zealously Christian brethren came upon it and sent it to me to be refuted without any delay, earnestly asking that I not put off replying. I refuted it in two books, which I therefore entitled *Answer to an Enemy of the Law and the Prophets*, because the very manuscript that was sent did not have the name of its author.

This work begins thus: *The book which you sent, most beloved brethren.*

## Chapter 59 (86): Answer to Gaudentius, Bishop of the Donatists, two books

Around the same time Dulcitius, a tribune and notary, was here in Africa as the executor of the imperial orders issued against the Donatists. When he had sent a letter to Gaudentius of Thamugadi, bishop of the Donatists—one of those seven whom they had chosen in our conference as the authors of their defense—exhorting him to Catholic unity, and dissuading him from the burning with which he was threatening to consume himself and his people, together with the very Church in which he was, adding also that, if they thought themselves righteous, they should rather flee, according to the precept of the Lord Christ[^176], than burn themselves up in unholy fires,

he wrote back two letters: one short, because, as he asserted, the bearer was in haste, the other long, replying as though more fully and carefully. These the above-mentioned tribune thought ought to be sent to me, so that I might rather refute them myself; and both of these I refuted in one book.

When this had come into the hands of that same Gaudentius, he wrote back to me what seemed good to him, answering with no reasoning, but rather declaring that he could neither answer nor keep silent. Although this could appear plainly enough to those who read with understanding and compared our words and his, I nonetheless did not wish to leave whatever it was without a written reply. Hence it came about that these books of ours against him were two.

This work begins thus: *Gaudentius of Thamugadi, bishop of the Donatists.*

## Chapter 60 (87): Against Lying, one book

Then I also wrote a book *Against Lying*, the occasion of which work was this: that, in order to track down the Priscillianist heretics, who think their heresy should be concealed not only by denying and lying[^177] but even by perjury, it seemed good to certain Catholics that they ought to feign themselves Priscillianists[^178], so as to penetrate their hiding places. Forbidding this to be done, I composed this book.

This book begins thus: *You sent me many things to read.*

## Chapter 61 (88): Against Two Letters of the Pelagians, — four books

There follow four books, which I wrote *Against Two Letters of the Pelagians* to Boniface, bishop of the Roman Church, because, when they had come into his hands, he himself had sent them to me, finding in them my name slanderously inserted.

This work begins thus: *I had indeed known you, by the report of your most celebrated fame.*

## Chapter 62 (89): Answer to Julian, six books

Meanwhile the four books of Julian the Pelagian[^179], which I mentioned above, also came into our hands. In them I found that the things which the one who had sent them to count Valerius had excerpted from them were not all written to that same count in the way Julian had stated them, but that some of them had been somewhat altered. So I wrote six books against those four[^180]; but the first two of mine refute, with the testimonies of the saints who defended the Catholic faith after the Apostles, the impudence of Julian, who thought a Manichaean doctrine should be charged against us because we say that original sin is drawn from Adam—which sin is loosed *through the washing of regeneration*[^181], not only in adults but also in infants. But how greatly Julian himself, by certain of his statements, aids the Manichees, I showed in the latter part of my first book. The other four of mine are given, one to each of theirs.

But in the fifth volume of this so great and so elaborate a work, where I mentioned that a deformed husband, lest his wife bear deformed children, was accustomed to set before her in intercourse a beautiful picture[^182], I put down as though certain the name of the man who was accustomed to do this, when it is uncertain, because my memory deceived me. For Soranus, an authority on medicine, wrote that a king of Cyprus was accustomed to do this, but did not give his proper name.

This work begins thus: *Your insults and abusive words, Julian.*

## Chapter 63 (90): To Laurentius, on Faith, Hope, and Charity, one book

I also wrote a book *On Faith, Hope, and Charity*, when the one to whom it was written had asked of me that he might have some little work of mine that would never leave his hands—the kind the Greeks call an *enchiridion*. There[^183] I seem to myself to have set out carefully enough how God is to be worshiped, which divine Scripture defines to be the true wisdom of man[^184].

This book begins thus: *It cannot be said, most beloved son Laurentius, how greatly I delight in your learning.*

## Chapter 64 (91): The Care to Be Taken for the Dead, to Bishop Paulinus, one book

I wrote the book *The Care to Be Taken for the Dead* when I had been asked by letter whether it profits anyone after death that his body is buried near the memorial shrine of some saint.

This book begins thus: *For a long time, to your Holiness, venerable fellow bishop Paulinus.*

## Chapter 65 (92): The Eight Questions of Dulcitius, one book

The book which I entitled *The Eight Questions of Dulcitius* would not be mentioned in this work among my books, since it is composed of things written by me before in other works, were it not that some discussion is also found inserted in it by us, and that to one of those questions I gave a reply not from any other little work of mine, but such as could occur to me at the time.

This book begins thus: *As far as it seems to me, most beloved son Dulcitius.*

## Chapter 66 (93): To Valentinus and the Monks with him, on Grace and Free Choice, one book

On account of those who, when the grace of God is defended[^185], think that free choice is being denied, and so themselves defend free choice in such a way as to deny the grace of God[^186], asserting that it is given according to our merits, I wrote a book whose title is *Grace and Free Choice.* I wrote it to the monks of Hadrumetum, in whose monastery a contention about this matter had begun, so that some of them were compelled to consult me.

This book begins thus: *On account of those who [defend] the free choice of man.*

## Chapter 67 (94): To the Same Persons as Above, on Rebuke and Grace, one book

Again, to the same persons I wrote another book, which I entitled *Rebuke and Grace*, when it had been reported to me that someone there had said that no one should be rebuked if he does not do the commandments of God, but that one should only pray for him, that he may do them.

This book begins thus: *Having read your letter, most beloved brother Valentinus.*

## Epilogue

I have recalled that I dictated these ninety-three works in two hundred and thirty-two books, when I reviewed them, not knowing whether I was still going to dictate any more; and the very review of them I published in two books, at the urging of the brethren, before I had begun to review the letters and the sermons to the people—some of them dictated, others delivered by me extempore.
