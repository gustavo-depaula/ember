# Book I

## Chapter I: Answer to the Skeptics, Three Books

**1**. When, then, I had given up either what I had attained in the desires of this world or what I wished to attain, and had betaken myself to the leisure of the Christian life, I first wrote, not yet baptized, *Against the Academics* or *On the Academics*, in order to remove from my mind—as far as I could, by all the reasonings I was able to muster—their arguments, which instill in many a despair of finding the truth and forbid one to assent to anything at all and altogether forbid the wise man to approve anything as though it were manifest and certain, since to them all things seem obscure and uncertain; and these arguments moved me too. This was accomplished by the Lord's mercy and help.

**2**. But in those same three books of mine it does not please me that *I so often invoked fortune*[^1]*,* although by this name I did not wish any goddess to be understood, but the chance outcome of things, whether in the goods or evils of our body or in external ones. Hence too come those words that no religion forbids us to say: "perhaps" (*forte*), "maybe" (*forsan*), "perchance" (*forsitan*), "possibly" (*fortasse*), "by chance" (*fortuito*)—all of which, nevertheless, must be referred back to divine providence.

This too I did not pass over in silence there, saying: *for perhaps what is commonly called fortune is governed by some hidden order, and we call nothing in things chance except that whose reason and cause is hidden*[^2]. I did indeed say this; yet I regret having named fortune in that way there, since I see that men have it as a very bad habit, where it should be said "this God willed," to say "this fortune willed."

But as for what I said in a certain place: *it has been so arranged, whether for our merits or by the necessity of nature, that the harbor of philosophy in no way receives the divine mind while it clings to mortal things*, and the rest[^3]—either none of these two things should have been said, since even so the sense could have been complete, or it was enough to say *for our merits,* as it is true that misery is drawn from Adam[^4], and not to add *or by the necessity of nature,* seeing that the hard necessity of our nature arose deservedly from preceding iniquity.

Likewise, what I said there: *that nothing at all is to be worshiped, and that whatever is discerned by mortal eyes, whatever any sense attains, is to be cast aside entirely*[^5]*,* words should have been added so that it would be said: *whatever* any sense of the mortal body *attains;* for there is also a sense of the mind. But I was then speaking in the manner of those who call nothing "sense" except that of the body, and nothing "sensible" except corporeal things. And so, wherever I spoke in this way, the ambiguity is scarcely avoided, except among those who are accustomed to this manner of speaking.

Likewise I said: *what do you suppose it is to live happily, if not to live according to that which is best in man?*[^6] And, explaining a little later what I had said *was best in man*: *who,* I said, *would doubt that the best of man is nothing other than that part of the mind to whose rule the other things that are in man ought rightly to submit? And this—lest you ask for another definition—can be called mind or reason*[^7]*.* This indeed is true; for as far as man's nature is concerned, there is nothing in him better than mind and reason. But he who wishes to live happily ought not to live according to it; otherwise he lives according to man, whereas *one must live according to God*[^8], so that he may be able to attain to blessedness; and in order to attain it our mind ought not to be content with itself, but must be made subject to God.

Likewise, answering him with whom I was disputing: *here clearly,* I said, *you are not mistaken—which I would gladly wish to be an omen to you for the rest*[^9]*.* Although this was said not in earnest but in jest, I would nevertheless not wish to use that word. For I do not recall having read *omen* either in our sacred Scriptures[^10] or in the discourse of any ecclesiastical disputant, although from it is derived "abomination," which is found constantly in the divine books.

**3**. In the second book, that fable, as it were, about "philocalia" and "philosophy"—that *they are sisters and begotten of the same parent*[^11]—is utterly inept and insipid. For either "philocalia," as it is called, consists in nothing but trifles, and for this reason is by no reasoning a sister to "philosophy"; or, if this name is to be honored for the reason that, translated into Latin, it means love of beauty, and the beauty of wisdom is the true and highest beauty, then in incorporeal and highest things "philocalia" is the very same as "philosophy," and they are in no way like two sisters.

In another place, when I was treating of the soul, I said: *destined to return more secure into heaven*[^12]*.* I would more safely have said "destined to go" rather than *to return,* on account of those who think that human souls, for the merits of their sins, were cast down or thrust from heaven into these bodies[^13].

But I did not hesitate to say this for the reason that I said *into heaven* as though I were saying "to God," who is its author and founder, just as the blessed Cyprian did not hesitate to say: *for since we possess a body from the earth and a spirit from heaven, we ourselves are earth and heaven*[^14], and in the book of *Ecclesiastes* it is written: *let the spirit return to God who gave it*[^15]; which is certainly to be understood in such a way that no resistance is offered to the Apostle when he says that those *not yet born had done* nothing *good or evil*[^16].

Without controversy, then, the original region of the soul's blessedness is God himself, who begot it not indeed from himself, but founded it from no other thing, just as he founded the body from earth. For as to its origin, by which it comes to be in the body—whether it is from that one soul which was first created, when man was made into a living soul[^17], or whether in like manner individual souls are made for individual persons—I neither knew then nor do I yet know.

**4**. In the third book: *if you ask what seems to me,* I said, *I judge that the highest good of man is in the mind*[^18]*.* I would more truly have said "in God"; for the mind enjoys him, that it may be blessed, as its highest good.

Nor does that please me which I said: *it is clear that one may swear by everything divine*[^19]*.*

Likewise what I said about the Academics, *that they knew the truth, the likeness of which they called the verisimilar, and that this very verisimilar I called the false thing which they approved*[^20]*,* was said wrongly for two reasons: either because what was in some way like some truth would be false—since in its own kind this too is true—or because they approved those false things which they called verisimilar, whereas they approved nothing and affirmed that the wise man approves nothing. But because they also called this very verisimilar the "probable," it came about that I said this of them.

The very praise, too, with which I exalted Plato or the Platonist or Academic philosophers[^21] as much as it was not fitting to exalt impious men, not undeservedly displeased me, especially since against their errors Christian teaching must be greatly defended.

That also, in the comparison with the arguments of Cicero which he used in his books of the *Academics*, I called my own efforts mere trifles[^22]*,* by which I refuted those arguments with the most certain reasoning—although it was said in jest and seems rather to be irony—ought nevertheless not to have been said.

This work begins thus: *O Romanianus, would that a man fit for himself.*

## Chapter II: On the Happy Life, One Book

It happened that I wrote the book *On the Happy Life* not after the books *On the Academics,* but in their midst. It arose on the occasion of my birthday and was completed in a disputation of three days, as the work itself sufficiently indicates. In this book it was agreed among us, who were inquiring together, that there is no happy life except the perfect knowledge of God.

Now it displeases me there that I attributed more than I ought to Manlius Theodorus*,* to whom I wrote the book itself, although he was a learned and Christian man[^23]*;* and that there too I often named fortune[^24]*;* and that I said the happy life dwells, during the time of this life, in the mind of the wise man alone, however his body may be[^25]*,* whereas the Apostle hopes for the perfect knowledge of God—that is, the knowledge than which none greater is possible for man—in the future life[^26], which alone is to be called the happy life, where also the incorruptible and immortal body[^27] will be made subject to its spirit without any trouble or resistance.

Indeed I found this book in our copy interrupted, and lacking not a little; and it was thus copied out by certain brothers, nor had I yet found it complete with anyone, from which I might correct it, when I revised these works.

This book begins thus: *If to the harbor of philosophy.*

## Chapter III: On Order, Two Books

**1**. During the same period, among those that were written *On the Academics,* I also wrote two books *On Order,* in which a great question is treated: whether the order of divine providence contains all good and evil things. But since I saw the matter to be difficult to understand, and that those with whom I was dealing could only with great difficulty be brought by disputation to grasp it, I preferred to speak of the order of study, by which one can advance from corporeal to incorporeal things.

**2**. But in these books too it displeases me that the word *fortune* is often inserted[^28]*;* and that I did not add "of the body" when I named the sense of the body[^29]. And that I attributed much to the liberal disciplines[^30]*,* which many holy men greatly do not know; some, too, who do know them are not holy[^31]; and that I mentioned the Muses as though they were certain goddesses, though in jest[^32]*;* and that I called wonder a vice[^33]*;* and that I said philosophers not endowed with true piety shone with the light of virtue[^34]*;* and that I commended two worlds, one sensible and the other intelligible[^35]*,* not in the person of Plato or of the Platonists, but in my own, as though the Lord too had wished to signify this[^36]*,* because he did not say "my kingdom is not of the world," but *my kingdom is not of this world*[^37]*,* although it can be found to have been said by some manner of speaking; and if another world was signified by the Lord Christ, that one could more fittingly be understood in which there will be *a new heaven and a new earth*[^38], when there shall be fulfilled what we pray when we say: *your kingdom come*[^39].

Nor indeed did Plato err in this, in that he said there is an intelligible world, if we are willing to attend not to the word, which is unusual in ecclesiastical usage for that thing, but to the thing itself. For he called the intelligible world that eternal and unchangeable reason by which God made the world. He who denies that this exists must consequently say either that God made what he made irrationally, or that, when he was making it, or before he made it, he did not know what he was making, if there was no reason for making it with him. But if there was—as there was—Plato seems to have called this very thing the intelligible world. We would not, however, use this name, if we were already sufficiently versed in ecclesiastical letters.

**3**. Nor does that please me, that when I had said *the greatest effort must be given to the best morals,* I at once added: *for our God will not be able to hear us otherwise, but those who live well he will hear most easily*[^40]*.* For it was said as though God does not hear sinners[^41]—which someone said in the Gospel, but one who had not yet come to know Christ, by whom he had already been enlightened in body.

Nor does that please me, that I gave so much praise to the philosopher Pythagoras[^42]*,* that whoever hears or reads this might think I had believed there were no errors in the Pythagorean teaching, whereas there are many, and those capital ones.

This work begins thus: *Concerning the order of things, Zenobius.*

## Chapter IV: The Soliloquies, Two Books

**1**. Among these I also wrote two volumes, in accordance with my zeal and love for searching out the truth by reason concerning those matters I most desired to know, questioning myself and answering myself, as though we were two, reason and I, when I was alone; whence I named this work the *Soliloquies.*

But it remained unfinished, yet in such a way that in the first book it was asked, and somehow made clear, what sort of person he must be who wishes to perceive wisdom—which is certainly perceived not by the sense of the body but by the mind—and by a certain reasoning it is concluded at the end of the book that those things which truly are, are immortal.

In the second book, however, the matter of the immortality of the soul is long debated and not brought to completion.

**2**. In these books, to be sure, I do not approve what I said in prayer: *God, who willed that none but the pure should know the truth*[^43]*.* For it can be answered that many who are not pure know many true things; nor was it defined here what the truth is which none but the pure can know, and what it is to know;

and that which is placed there: *God, whose kingdom is the whole world, which sense does not know*[^44]. If God is to be understood, words should have been added so that it would be said "*which* the sense of the mortal body *does not know.*" But if the world was meant, which sense does not know, that one is rightly understood which is to come with *a new heaven and a new earth*[^45]; but here too those words should have been added so that it would be said: "the sense of the mortal body."

But I was still speaking in that manner in which sense is properly called that of the body; nor need that be repeated constantly which I have already said above on this point[^46], but this should be recalled wherever this manner of speaking is found in my writings.

**3**. And where I said of the Father and the Son: *he who begets and he whom he begets is one*[^47]*,* it should have been said "are one," just as the Truth herself openly speaks, saying: *I and the Father are one*[^48].

Nor does that please me, that I said *that in this life, once God is understood, the soul is already blessed*[^49]—unless perhaps in hope.

Likewise what I said: *that one comes to union with wisdom not by one way alone*[^50]*,* does not sound well, as though there were another way besides Christ, who said: *I am the way*[^51]. This offense to religious ears was therefore to be avoided, although there is that one universal way, and there are other ways of which we also sing in the Psalm: *make your ways known to me, O Lord, and teach me your paths*[^52].

And in that which was said there: *that these sensible things are to be fled altogether*[^53]*,* care had to be taken lest we be thought to hold that view of Porphyry the false philosopher, by which he said that every body is to be fled[^54]. But I did not say "all" sensible things, but *these,* that is, corruptible ones. Yet this should rather have been said; for such sensible things will not exist in the *new heaven and new earth* of the age to come.

**4**. Likewise in a certain place I said that *those instructed in the liberal disciplines, without doubt, dig out by learning those disciplines buried in them by forgetfulness, and in a manner unearth them again*[^55]*.* But this too I disapprove. For it is more credible that those who are even unskilled in certain disciplines answer truly about them, when they are questioned well, because the light of eternal reason is present to them, as far as they are able to grasp it, in which they behold these unchangeable truths—not because they once knew them and have forgotten them, as it seemed to Plato or such men[^56]. Against their opinion, so far as the occasion afforded by the work undertaken allowed, I discoursed in the twelfth book of *On the Trinity*[^57].

This work begins thus: *As I was turning over within me many and various things.*

## Chapter V: On the Immortality of the Soul, One Book

**1**. After the books of the *Soliloquies,* having now returned from the countryside to Milan, I wrote the book *On the Immortality of the Soul,* which I had wished to be for me a kind of reminder for finishing the *Soliloquies,* which had remained incomplete. But somehow, against my will, it came into the hands of men, and it is named among my little works. It is so obscure, by the contortion and brevity of its reasonings, that it wearies even my own attention when it is read, and is scarcely understood by myself.

**2**. Then, thinking of nothing other than the minds of men, I said in a certain argument of that same book: *nor can there be knowledge in that which learns nothing*[^58]. Likewise in another place I said: *nor does knowledge embrace any thing except what pertains to some discipline*[^59]*;* nor did it come to mind that God does not learn a discipline, but possesses the knowledge of all things, in which there is also foreknowledge of things to come. Of the same kind is that which was said there: *that life with reason belongs to nothing except the soul*[^60]*;* for God's life is not without reason, since with him there is both the highest life and the highest reason;

and that which I said somewhat earlier: *that what is understood is always of such a kind*[^61]*,* whereas the mind too is understood, which is certainly not always of such a kind.

But what I said: *that the mind cannot for this reason be separated from the eternal reason, because it is not joined to it by place*[^62]*,* I would surely not have said, if I had then been so instructed in the sacred Scriptures as to recall what is written: *your sins separate between you and God*[^63]. Whence we are given to understand that separation can be spoken of even of those things which had been joined not by places but incorporeally.

**3**. What I meant, too, when I said: *if the soul lacks a body, it is not in this world*[^64]*,* I have not been able to recall. For surely the souls of the dead either do not lack a body or are not in this world—as though indeed the lower regions were not in this world? But because I set "to lack a body" as a good, perhaps by the word *body* I meant corporeal plagues. And if that is so, I used the word too unusually.

This too was rashly said: *that by the supreme essence form is bestowed on the body through the soul, by which it is, in whatever degree it is. Through the soul, therefore, the body subsists, and exists by that very thing by which it is animated, whether universally, as the world, or particularly, as each individual animal within the world*[^65]*.* All this was said utterly rashly.

This book begins thus: *If anywhere there is a discipline.*

## Chapter VI: On the Books of the Disciplines, One Book

During the same period, when I was at Milan about to receive baptism, I also attempted to write *Books on the Disciplines,* questioning those who were with me and who did not shrink from studies of this kind; desiring, through corporeal things, either to arrive at or to lead others to incorporeal things by certain steps, as it were. But of these I was able to complete only the book *On Grammar,* which I afterward lost from our cupboard, and six volumes *On Music,* as far as concerns that part which is called *Rhythm.* But these same six books I wrote when I was already baptized and had already returned from Italy to Africa, for I had only begun this discipline at Milan. Of the other five disciplines likewise begun there—of dialectic, of rhetoric, of geometry, of arithmetic, and of philosophy—only the beginnings remained, which we have also lost; but I think they are held by some.

## Chapter VII (VI): On the Ways of Life of the Catholic Church and the Ways of Life of the Manichees, Two Books

**1**. Now when, already baptized, I was at Rome, and could not bear in silence the boasting of the Manichees about their false and deceitful continence or abstinence, by which, to deceive the unskilled, they even set themselves above true Christians, with whom they are not to be compared, I wrote two books, one *On the Ways of Life of the Catholic Church,* and the other *On the Ways of Life of the Manichees.*

**2**. In the one, then, which is *On the Ways of Life of the Catholic Church,* where I set down the testimony in which is read: *for your sake we are afflicted all the day long; we are counted as sheep for the slaughter*[^66]*,* the faultiness of our copy deceived me, who was then less mindful of the Scriptures, in which I was not yet practiced. For other copies of the same translation do not have *for your sake we are afflicted,* but *for your sake we are put to death,* which others expressed in one word: *we are slain.*

That this is truer the Greek books indicate, from which language the translation of the ancient divine Scriptures was made into Latin according to the *Septuagint* translators. And yet according to these words, that is, *for your sake we are afflicted,* I said much in my discussion which, in the matters themselves, I do not disapprove as false. Nevertheless, the agreement of the old and new Scriptures, which I desired to demonstrate, I certainly did not demonstrate from these words. I have said whence the error crept upon me; but from other testimonies I sufficiently showed the same agreement.

**3**. Similarly, a little later too, I set down a testimony from the book of *Wisdom* according to our copy, in which was written: *for wisdom teaches sobriety, and justice, and virtue*[^67]*.* And according to these words I discussed things that are indeed true, but found on the occasion of a faulty text. For what is truer than that wisdom teaches the truth of contemplation, which I thought was signified by the word "sobriety," and uprightness of action, which I wished to be understood by the other two, justice and virtue, whereas truer copies of the same translation have: *for it teaches sobriety, and wisdom, and justice, and virtue*? For by these names the Latin translator named those four virtues which are most commonly on the lips of the philosophers, calling temperance "sobriety," giving prudence the name "wisdom," expressing fortitude by the word "virtue," and rendering justice alone by its own name. But these four virtues, named by their own names in the same book of *Wisdom,* as they are called by the Greeks, we found long afterward in the Greek copies.

Likewise what I set down from the book of *Solomon*: *vanity of those who act vainly, said Ecclesiastes,* I did indeed read in many copies; but the Greek does not have this, but rather *vanity of vanities*[^68], which I afterward saw, and found that those Latin copies are truer which have *of vanities,* not *of those who act vainly.* Yet on the occasion of this faulty text, whatever I discussed appears to be true in the things themselves.

**4**. But as for what I said: *that we should first love with full love the very one whom we wish to know,* that is, God[^69]*,* it would be better said "sincere" than *full,* lest perhaps it be thought that the love of God will not be greater when we shall see *face to face*[^70]. So, then, let this be understood as though *full* were said of that love than which none can be greater so long as we walk by faith; for it will be fuller, indeed fullest, but through sight.

Likewise what I said of those who help the needy, that they *are called merciful, even if they are so wise that they are no longer disturbed by any pain of mind*[^71]*,* is not to be understood as though I had defined that there are such wise men in this life; for I did not say "since they are," but I said *even if they are.*

**5**. In another place, where I said: *but now, when this human love has nourished and strengthened the mind that clings to your breasts, making it fit for following God, then, when his majesty has begun to disclose itself in as great a measure as suffices for man while he is an inhabitant of this earth, so great an ardor of love is born, and so great a fire of divine love rises up, that, with all vices burned away and the man sanctified and purified, it sufficiently appears how divinely it was said, 'I am a consuming fire'*[^72]*,* the Pelagians can think that I said this perfection can come to pass in this mortal life.

But let them not think this; for the ardor of love, made fit for following God and so great that it consumes all vices, can be born and grow in this life, but to accomplish that for which it is born—that no vice be present in a man—cannot here follow as a consequence, although so great a thing is accomplished by that same ardor of love where it can be accomplished and when it can be accomplished; so that, just as the washing of regeneration purifies from the guilt of all sins, which human birth has drawn down and iniquity has contracted, so that perfection may purify from the stain of all vices, without which human weakness cannot exist in this world;

as is to be understood also what the Apostle says: *Christ loved the Church and gave himself up for her, cleansing her by the washing of water in the word, that he might present to himself a glorious Church, having no spot or wrinkle or any such thing*[^73]. For this is the *washing of water in the word* by which the Church is cleansed. But since the whole Church, so long as it is here, says: *forgive us our debts*[^74], it is certainly not here *without spot or wrinkle or any such thing*; yet from what it here receives, it is brought to that glory, which is not here, and to perfection.

**6**. In the other book, whose title is *On the Ways of Life of the Manichees,* that which I said: *the goodness of God so orders all defecting things that they are there where they can most fittingly be, until by ordered movements they return to that from which they fell away*[^75], is not to be understood as though all things *return to that from which they fell away,* as it seemed to Origen[^76], but those that do return. For those who will be punished with everlasting fire do not return to God from whom they fell away, although all defecting things are so ordered that they are there where they can most fittingly be, since those too who do not return are most fittingly in punishment.

In another place: *scarcely anyone,* I said, *doubts about beetles, that they come to be from dung rolled into a ball by them and buried*[^77]*,* whereas many doubt whether this is true, and many have not even heard of it.

This work begins thus: *In other books I think we have dealt sufficiently.*

## Chapter VIII (VII): On the Size of the Soul, One Book

**1**. In the same city I wrote a dialogue, in which many things are asked and discussed about the soul; that is, whence it comes, what it is like, how great it is, why it was given to the body, what it becomes when it comes to the body, and what it is like when it has departed[^78]*.* But since how great it is was discussed most carefully and subtly—so that we might show, if we could, that it is not of corporeal magnitude and yet is something great—from this one inquiry the whole book took its name, so that it was called *On the Size of the Soul.*

**2**. In this book, that which I said: *that it seemed to me the soul brought all the arts with it, and that what is called learning is nothing other than remembering and recollecting*[^79]*,* is not to be understood as though from this it were approved that the soul once lived, either here in another body or elsewhere, whether in a body or outside a body, and that the things it answers when questioned, though it did not learn them here, it learned beforehand in another life[^80].

For it can be the case, as we have already said above in this work[^81], that this is possible for the reason that the nature of the soul is intelligible and is connected not only with intelligible but also with unchangeable things, made in such an order that, when it moves itself to those things to which it is connected or to itself, in so far as it sees them, in so far does it answer truly about them.

Nor indeed did it bring all the arts with it in that way and hold them with it; for of the arts that pertain to the senses of the body—as many parts of medicine, as all of astrology—it can say nothing except what it has learned here.

But those things which the understanding alone grasps, for the reason I have said, it answers when it has been well questioned, either by itself or by another, and has recollected them.

**3**. In another place: *I would wish,* I said, *to say more on this point and to bind myself, while I as it were instruct you, to do nothing other than to be restored to myself, to whom I most owe myself*[^82]*,* where I seem rather to have had to say *be restored to God, to whom I most owe myself.* But because a man must first be restored to himself, so that there, as if by a step made, he may rise from there and be lifted up to God—just as that younger son first returned to himself and then said: *I will arise and go to my father*[^83]—therefore I spoke thus. Finally I soon added: *and so to become a friendly servant to the Lord*[^84]*.* So what I said, *to whom I most owe myself,* I referred to men; for I owe myself to myself more than to other men, although to God more than to myself.

This book begins thus: *Since I see that you abound in leisure.*

## Chapter IX (VIII): On Free Will, Three Books

**1**. While we were still staying at Rome, we wished to inquire by disputation whence evil comes. And we disputed in such a way that, if we could, the reason, when considered and treated, might bring what we believed about this matter—subject to divine authority—even to our understanding, as far as we could manage by discussion with God's help. And since it was agreed among us, after the matter had been carefully examined by reason, that evil arose from nothing but the free choice of the will, the three books to which that same disputation gave birth were called *On Free Will.* The second and third of these I finished in Africa, having already been ordained a priest at Hippo Regius, as best I then could.

**2**. In these books many things were discussed in such a way that certain incidental questions, which either I could not unravel or which required a long discourse at the time, were so deferred that, on either side, or on all the sides of those same questions—in which it was not clear which view better agreed with the truth—our reasoning was nevertheless brought to this conclusion: that, whichever of them might be true, God was to be believed worthy of praise, or even shown to be such[^85].

For that disputation was undertaken on account of those who deny that the origin of evil is derived from the free choice of the will, and who contend that God, if it is so, the Creator of all natures, is to be blamed; wishing in this way, according to the error of their impiety (for they are Manichees), to introduce a certain nature of evil, unchangeable and coeternal with God. But of the grace of God, by which he so predestined his elect that he himself also prepared the wills of those who now use free choice in them, nothing was discussed in these books, on account of the question proposed. Where, however, occasion arose for grace to be mentioned, it was mentioned in passing, not defended with laborious reasoning, as though that were the subject. For it is one thing to ask whence evil comes, and another to ask whence one returns to the former good, or attains to a greater one.

**3**. Therefore let the new Pelagian heretics, who so assert the free choice of the will that they leave no place for the grace of God—since they assert that it is given according to our merits—not exalt themselves, as though I had pleaded their cause, because I said many things in these books in favor of free choice, which the purpose of that disputation required.

For I said in the first book: *that evil deeds are punished by the justice of God,* and I added: *for they would not justly be punished, unless they were done by the will*[^86].

Likewise, when I was showing that the good will itself is so great a good that it is deservedly to be set before all corporeal and external goods, I said: *you see, therefore, now, as I think, that it is placed in our will whether we enjoy or lack so great and so true a good. For what is so placed in the will as the will itself?*[^87]

And in another place: *what reason, then,* I said, *is there why we should think it doubtful that, even if we were never wise before, we nevertheless by the will deserve and lead a praiseworthy and happy life, and by the will a base and wretched one?*[^88]

Likewise in another place: *from which it is concluded,* I said, *that whoever wishes to live rightly and honorably, if he wishes to will this in preference to fleeting things, attains so great a thing with such ease that for him to have what he willed is nothing other than to will it*[^89].

Likewise elsewhere I said: *for that eternal law, to the consideration of which it is now time to return, has established with unchangeable stability that merit lies in the will, but reward and punishment in blessedness and misery*[^90].

And in another place: *what each one chooses to pursue and embrace,* I said, *was settled to be placed in the will*[^91]*.*

And in the second book: *for man himself,* I said, *in so far as he is man, is some good, because when he wills he can live rightly*[^92]*.*

And in another place I said: *that one cannot act rightly except by that same free choice of the will*[^93]*.*

And in the third book: *what need is there to ask,* I said, *whence this movement arises by which the will is turned away from the unchangeable good to a changeable good, since we confess that it belongs to the soul alone and is voluntary and for this reason culpable, and all teaching on this matter is useful for this: that, with that movement disapproved and restrained, we may turn our will away from the fall into temporal things toward enjoying the eternal good?*[^94]

And in another place: *most excellently,* I said, *does truth cry out about you. For you could not perceive that anything else is in our power except what we do when we will. Therefore nothing is so much in our power as the will itself. For it is at hand at once, with no interval whatever, as soon as we will*[^95]*.*

Likewise in another place: *for if you are praised,* I said, *for seeing what you ought to do, since you do not see it except in him who is the unchangeable truth, how much more he who both commanded the willing and supplied the power and did not permit unwillingness to go unpunished?*[^96]

Then I added, saying: *for if each one owes what he has received, and man was so made that he necessarily sins, then this he owes: that he sin. When, therefore, he sins, he does what he owes. But if it is wicked to say this, no one is forced by his own nature to sin*[^97]*.*

And again: *what, at last, could be,* I said, *the cause of the will before the will? For either it too is a will, and one does not depart from this root of the will, or it is not a will, and it has no sin. Either, therefore, the will is the first cause of sinning, or no sin is the first cause of sinning, and there is no one to whom sin is rightly imputed except the one sinning. There is, therefore, no one to whom it is rightly imputed except the one willing*[^98].

And a little later: *who,* I said, *sins in that which can in no way be guarded against? But sin is committed; therefore it can be guarded against*[^99]*.*

Pelagius used this testimony of mine in a certain book of his. When I had answered that book, I wished the title of my book to be *Nature and Grace.*

**4**. In these and similar words of mine, because the grace of God is not mentioned—of which there was then no question—the Pelagians think, or can think, that I held their view. But they think this in vain. For it is the will by which one both sins and lives rightly; and this we treated of in these words. The will itself, therefore, unless it is freed by the grace of God from the servitude by which it was made *a slave to sin*[^100], and is helped to overcome the vices, cannot be lived rightly and piously by mortals. And this divine benefit by which it is freed, unless it preceded the will, would be given to its merits and would not be grace, which is certainly given freely. This we have treated of sufficiently in other little works of ours[^101], refuting those new heretics who are enemies of this grace;

although even in these books *On Free Will,* which were written not at all against them—since they did not yet exist—but against the Manichees, we were not altogether silent about this grace of God, which they try by abominable impiety to take away. For we said in the second book: *that not only great but also the least goods cannot exist except from him from whom are all goods, that is, God*[^102]*;*

and a little later: *the virtues,* I said, *by which one lives rightly are great goods; but the forms of any bodies whatever, without which one can live rightly, are the least goods; while the powers of the soul, without which one cannot live rightly, are intermediate goods. No one makes bad use of the virtues; but of the other goods, that is, the intermediate and the least, anyone can make use not only well but also badly. And therefore no one makes bad use of virtue, because the work of virtue is the good use of those things which we can also use not well, but no one who uses well uses badly. Wherefore the abundance and greatness of God's goodness has bestowed being not only on great but also on intermediate and least goods. His goodness is more to be praised in great than in intermediate, and more in intermediate than in least goods, but more in all of them than if he had not bestowed all*[^103]*.*

And in another place: *only hold fast,* I said, *an unshaken piety, so that no good may occur to you, whether sensing or understanding or thinking in any way whatever, which is not from God*[^104].

Likewise in another place I said: *but since man cannot of his own accord rise again as of his own accord he fell, let us hold with firm faith the right hand of God stretched out to us from above, that is, our Lord Jesus Christ*[^105]*.*

**5**. And in the third book, when I had said that which I mentioned Pelagius too had used from my little works[^106]: *for who,* I said, *sins in that which can in no way be guarded against? But sin is committed; therefore it can be guarded against*[^107]*,* I immediately went on to add: *and yet certain things done even through ignorance are disapproved and judged to be in need of correction, as we read in the divine authorities. For the Apostle says: 'I obtained mercy because I acted ignorantly'*[^108]*; and the Prophet says: 'remember not the sins of my youth and my ignorances'*[^109]*.*

*There are also things done by necessity that are to be disapproved, where a man wishes to act rightly and cannot. For whence come those words: 'for the good that I will I do not, but the evil that I hate, this I do'*[^110]*; and that: 'to will is present with me, but to accomplish the good is not'*[^111]*, and that: 'the flesh lusts against the spirit, and the spirit against the flesh; for these are opposed to one another, so that you do not do the things that you would'*[^112]*.*

*But all these things belong to men coming from that condemnation of death. For if this is not the punishment of man but his nature, these are not sins. For if there is no departing from the manner in which he was naturally made, so that he cannot be better, then he does what he ought when he does these things. But if he were a good man, he would be otherwise. Now, however, because it is so, he is not good, nor has he it in his power to be good, whether by not seeing what sort he ought to be, or by seeing and not being able to be such as he sees that he ought to be.*

*Who would doubt that this is a punishment? But every punishment, if it is just, is the punishment of sin and is called a penalty. But if it is an unjust punishment—since no one doubts that it is a punishment—it has been imposed on man by some unjust ruler.*

*Now, since to doubt about the omnipotence and justice of God is the mark of a madman, this punishment is just and is paid for some sin. For no unjust ruler could either filch man away as though from a God who was ignorant, or wrest him away from one unwilling, as though from one weaker, whether by terrifying or by contending*, *so as to torment man with an unjust punishment. It remains, therefore, that this just punishment comes from the condemnation of man*[^113].

And in another place: *to approve,* I said, *false things for true, so that one errs unwillingly, and, with the pain of the carnal bond resisting and tormenting, to be unable to refrain from lustful works, is not the nature of man as he was established, but the punishment of one condemned. But when we speak of the free will of acting rightly, we are speaking of course of that will in which man was made*[^114].

**6**. Behold, so long before the Pelagian heresy had arisen, we disputed thus, as though we were already disputing against them. For since all goods were said to be from God—that is, both great and intermediate and least—free choice of the will is indeed found among the intermediate goods, because we can also use it badly, but it is nevertheless such that without it we cannot live rightly. But the good use of it is already a virtue, which is found among the great goods, which no one can use badly. And because all goods, as has been said—both great and intermediate and least—are from God, it follows that the good use of free will, which is a virtue and is counted among the great goods, is also from God[^115]*.*

Then it was said: *from which misery, most justly inflicted on sinners, the grace of God frees us, because man of his own accord, that is, by free choice, was able to fall but not also to rise. To this misery of just condemnation belong the ignorance and difficulty which every man suffers from the beginning of his birth, nor is anyone freed from this evil except by the grace of God*[^116]*.*

This misery the Pelagians refuse to derive from just condemnation, denying original sin. And yet, even if ignorance and difficulty were the natural beginnings of man, even so God would be not to be blamed but to be praised, as we argued in that same third book[^117].

This disputation is to be held against the Manichees, who do not accept the holy Scriptures of the Old Testament, in which original sin is recounted, and who contend that whatever is read on this point in the *Apostolic Writings* was inserted with detestable shamelessness by corruptors of the Scriptures, as though it had not been said by the Apostles. But against the Pelagians this must be defended which both Scriptures, which they profess to accept, commend.

This work begins thus: *Tell me, I beg you, whether God is not the author of evil.*

## Chapter X (IX): On Genesis Against the Manichees, Two Books

**1**. Now, already settled in Africa, I wrote two books *On Genesis Against the Manichees.* For although in the earlier books, in whatever I discussed in order to show that God is the supremely good and unchangeable Creator of all changeable natures, and that no nature or substance is evil in so far as it is a nature and a substance, our attention was on guard against the Manichees, yet these two books were published most openly against them, in defense of the Old Law, which they attack with the vehement zeal of a mad error: in the first, from that which is written, *in the beginning God made heaven and earth*[^118]*,* until the seven days are completed, where it is read that God rested on the seventh day. In the second, from that which is written, *this is the book of the creation of heaven and earth*[^119]*,* until Adam and his wife were sent out of paradise, and a guard was set over the tree of life. Then at the end of the book I set the faith of catholic truth against the error of the Manichees, briefly and openly comprising what they say and what we say.

**2**. But as for what I said: *that light, however, does not feed the eyes of irrational birds, but the pure hearts of those who believe God and turn themselves away from the love of visible and temporal things to the fulfilling of his precepts, which all men can do if they will*[^120]*,* let not the new Pelagian heretics suppose it was said in accordance with their view. For it is entirely true that all men can do this if they will; but *the will is prepared by the Lord,* and is increased by the gift of love so much that they are able—which here was not said for the reason that it was not necessary to the present question.

But as for what is there read, that the blessing of God, by which it was said, *increase and multiply*[^121], is to be believed to have been changed into carnal fecundity after sin[^122]—if it cannot seem to be said in any other way than that those people would not have had human children unless they had sinned, I do not approve it at all.

Neither does it follow that, for this reason, what is said in the book of *Genesis*—that green herbs and fruit-bearing trees are given as food to every kind of beast and to all birds and to all serpents—is to be understood as taken only in allegory, because there are both quadrupeds and birds which seem to live on flesh alone[^123]*.* For it could be that they were nourished by men even from the fruits of the earth, if, on account of the obedience by which those men served God without any iniquity, they deserved to have all the beasts and birds serving them in every way.

Likewise it may give pause how I said of the people of Israel: *still, with bodily circumcision and sacrifices, that people served the law as though in the sea of the nations*[^124]—seeing that among the nations they could not sacrifice, just as we see them now too remaining without sacrifices[^125], unless perhaps the fact that they immolate a sheep for the Passover be reckoned as a sacrifice.

**3**. In the second book, too, that which I set down—that by the name of *fodder* life can be signified[^126]*,* whereas copies of a better translation do not have *fodder* but *hay*—does not seem aptly said. For the word "hay" does not suit the signification of "life" as *fodder* does.

Likewise I do not seem[^127] to have rightly called prophetic the words in which it is written: *why is earth and ashes proud?*[^128]*,* because it is not read in the book of one whom we are certain is to be called a prophet.

Nor did I understand, as he wished, that saying of the Apostle where he adduces a testimony from *Genesis,* saying: *the first man Adam was made a living soul*[^129], when I was expounding what is written: *God breathed into his face the breath of life, and man was made into a living soul,* or *a living soul*[^130]*.* For the Apostle adduced that testimony in order to prove that there is an animal body; but I thought it was to be shown from this that man was first made an animal being, not the body of man alone[^131]*.*

But as for what I said: *that sins harm no nature except their own*[^132]*,* I said it for this reason, that whoever harms a just man does not truly harm him, since indeed he even increases his *reward in heaven*[^133], but truly harms himself by sinning, because for that very will to harm he will receive what he has done. The Pelagians can indeed drag this saying to their dogma, and so say that the sins of others did not harm little children, because I said: *sins harm no nature except their own,* not considering that little children—who certainly belong to human nature—draw original sin for this reason, that in the first human beings human nature sinned, and through this no sins harmed human nature except its own. For *through one man*, in whom all sinned, *sin entered into the world*[^134]; for I said that sins harm not *any man,* but *any nature,* except their own.

Likewise, in that which I said a little later, *that there is no natural evil*[^135], they can seek a similar hiding place, unless this saying is referred to such a nature as was first established without fault; for that is truly and properly called the nature of man. But we use the word in a transferred sense, so as to call "nature" even man as he is born, according to which manner of speaking the Apostle said: *for we too were once by nature children of wrath, like the rest*[^136].

This work begins thus: *If the Manichees were choosing whom they might deceive.*

## Chapter XI (X): On Music, Six Books

**1**. Then, as I mentioned above[^137], I wrote six books *On Music,* of which the sixth itself became most widely known, since in it is treated a matter worthy of knowledge: how from corporeal and spiritual but changeable numbers one arrives at the unchangeable numbers, which are already in the unchangeable truth itself, and so *the invisible things of God are clearly seen, being understood through the things that are made*[^138]. Those who cannot do this and yet *live by the faith of Christ*[^139], come to behold those things more surely and happily after this life. But those who can, if the faith of Christ—who *is the one Mediator between God and men*[^140]—is lacking to them, perish with all their wisdom.

**2**. In this book, that which I said: *for bodies are the better the more they abound in numbers of this kind, but the soul becomes better by lacking these numbers which it receives through the body, when it turns itself away from the carnal senses and is reformed by the divine numbers of Wisdom*[^141]*,* is not to be understood as though there will be no corporeal numbers in the incorruptible and spiritual bodies, since they will be far more beautiful and seemly, or as though the soul will not sense them when it is in its best state, in the way that here it becomes better by lacking them.

For here it has need to turn itself away from the carnal senses in order to grasp intelligible things, because it is weak and less able to apply its attention to both at once; and among these corporeal things an enticement must now be guarded against, so long as the soul can be enticed to base delight. But then it will be so firm and perfect that it will not be turned away by corporeal numbers from the contemplation of Wisdom, and will so sense them that it is not enticed by them, nor become better by lacking them; but it will be so good and upright that they can neither escape its notice nor occupy it.

**3**. Likewise what I said: *but this health will then be most firm and most certain, when this body has been restored to its former stability at its own certain time and order*[^142]*,* is not to be thought said as though there will not be bodies after the resurrection better than those of the first human beings in paradise—since those bodies will no longer have to be nourished by the corporeal foods by which these were nourished; but the former stability is to be understood in this sense, that those bodies will suffer no sickness, just as these could not suffer it before sin.

**4**. In another place: *more laborious,* I said, *is the love of this world. For what the soul seeks in it—namely, constancy and eternity—it does not find, since the lowest beauty is completed by the passing away of things, and what in it imitates constancy is conveyed through the soul from the supreme God, since the form that is changeable only in time is prior to that which is changeable both in time and in place*[^143]*.*

These words, if they can be understood in such a way that the lowest beauty is understood only in the bodies of men and of all animals which live with bodily sense, manifest reason defends. For this is what in that beauty imitates constancy: that the same bodies remain in their structure, in so far as they remain, and this is conveyed into them from the supreme God through the soul[^144]. For the soul holds the structure itself together, that it may not dissolve and flow apart; which we see happens in the bodies of animals when the soul departs. But if the lowest beauty is understood in all bodies, this saying forces one to believe that the world itself is an animal, so that into it too, what in it imitates constancy is conveyed from the supreme God through the soul. But that this world is an animal, as Plato thought, and very many other philosophers[^145], I have neither been able to investigate by certain reason, nor have I learned that it can be persuaded by the authority of the divine Scriptures. Hence I noted that something of this sort, said by me in a way that could be so taken, was rashly said even in the book *On the Immortality of the Soul*[^146]—not because I affirm this to be false, but because I do not grasp it to be true, that the world is an animal. This indeed I do not doubt is to be held unshaken: that this world is not God to us, whether it has any soul or none; for if it has any, he who made it *is our God,* and if it has none, this world can be the God of nothing, much less ours. Yet that there is a spiritual and life-giving power—even if the world is not an animal—which power in the holy angels serves God for adorning and administering the world, and by whom it is not understood, is most rightly believed. But by the name "holy angels" I would now call every holy spiritual creature established in God's secret and hidden ministry; yet holy Scripture is not accustomed to signify angelic spirits by the name "souls."

Accordingly, in that which I said near the end of this book: *the rational and intellectual numbers of the blessed and holy souls receive the very law of God—without which a leaf does not fall from the tree, and by which the hairs of our head are numbered—with no nature interposed, and transmit it down to earthly and infernal jurisdictions*[^147]*,* I do not see how the word "souls," set down here according to the holy Scriptures, can be shown to be correct, since here I wished only the holy angels to be understood, whom I do not recall having read anywhere in the divine canonical writings to have souls.

This book begins thus: *For quite long enough, almost.*

## Chapter XII (XI): On the Teacher, One Book

During the same period I wrote a book whose title is *On the Teacher,* in which it is disputed and inquired and found that there is no teacher who teaches a man knowledge except God, according to that also which is written in the Gospel: *one is your Teacher, Christ*[^148].

This book begins thus: *What do we seem to you to be wishing to accomplish when we speak?*

## Chapter XIII (XII): On True Religion, One Book

**1**. Then too I wrote the book *On True Religion,* in which it is argued in manifold ways and most copiously that the one true God—that is, the Trinity, Father and Son and Holy Spirit—is to be worshiped with true religion; and with how great a mercy of his the Christian religion, which is the true religion, has been granted to men through the temporal dispensation; and how man is to be fitted to the same worship of God by a certain way of his life. Above all, however, this book speaks against the two natures of the Manichees.

**2**. In this book, in a certain place: *let it be,* I said, *manifest and perceived by you that no error could have existed in religion, if the soul did not worship in place of its God a soul or a body or its own phantasms*[^149]. Here I put *soul* for the whole incorporeal creation, not speaking in the manner of the Scriptures, which, when they do not use the word in a transferred sense, I do not know whether they wish anything to be understood by "soul" except that by which mortal animals live, among which are also men, so long as they are mortal. But a little later I comprised the same sense better and more briefly, where I said: *let us not, therefore, serve the creature rather than the Creator*[^150] *nor become vain in our thoughts*[^151]*, and religion is perfect*[^152]*.* For by the one word "creature" I signified both, that is, the spiritual and the corporeal. There remains what I said there: *or its own phantasms;* on account of which I said here: *nor become vain in our thoughts.*

**3**. Likewise what I said: *this is, in our times, the Christian religion, to know and to follow which is the most secure and most certain salvation*[^153]*,* was said according to this name, not according to the very thing of which this is the name. For the thing itself, which is now called the Christian religion, existed also among the ancients and was not lacking from the beginning of the human race, up until Christ himself came in the flesh, from which time the true religion, which already existed, began to be called Christian. For when, after his resurrection and ascension into heaven, the Apostles had begun to preach him, and very many believed, the disciples were first called "Christians" at Antioch, as it is written[^154]. Therefore I said, *this is, in our times, the Christian religion,* not because it did not exist in earlier times, but because it received this name in later times.

**4**. In another place: *attend, therefore,* I said, *to these things that follow, carefully and piously, as far as you can, for such persons God helps*[^155]*.* This is not to be understood as though he helps only such persons, since he also helps those who are not such, that they may be such—that is, that they may seek carefully and piously; but such persons he helps to find.

Likewise elsewhere: *then,* I said, *it will be a consequence that, after the bodily death which we owe to the first sin, at its own time and in its own order this body will be restored to its former stability*[^156]*.* This is to be understood thus, that even the former stability of the body, which we lost by sinning, had such great felicity that it did not incline toward the decline of old age. To this former stability, therefore, this body will be restored in the resurrection of the dead. But it will have more, so that it is not sustained by corporeal foods, but to its sufficiency *is given life by the spirit alone*[^157], *when it shall have risen into a life-giving spirit*[^158], for which reason it will also be spiritual. But that which existed first, although it would not have died unless man had sinned, was nevertheless made animal, that is, *into a living soul.*

**5**. And elsewhere: *so far is sin a voluntary evil,* I said, *that it is in no way sin if it is not voluntary*[^159]*.* This definition can seem false; but if it is carefully examined, it will be found most true. For one must think of that sin which is sin only, not that which is also the punishment of sin, as I showed above, when I mentioned certain things from the third book *On Free Will*[^160]. Although even those things which are not undeservedly called involuntary sins, because they are perpetrated either by those who do not know or by those who are forced, cannot in every way be committed without the will, since both he who sins in ignorance certainly sins by the will—in that he thinks that to be done which ought not to be done—and he who, *with the flesh lusting against the spirit*[^161], does not do the things he wills, lusts indeed unwillingly, and in that does not do what he wills, but if he is overcome, he consents to concupiscence willingly, and in that does only what he wills, being of course free from justice and a slave of sin[^162]. And that which in little children is called original sin, since they do not yet use the choice of the will, is not absurdly called "voluntary" as well, because, contracted from the first evil will of man, it has become in a manner hereditary. It is not, therefore, false that I said: *so far is sin a voluntary evil that it is in no way sin if it is not voluntary*[^163]*.* Therefore by the grace of God not only is the guilt of all past sins loosed in all who are baptized in Christ, which is done by the Spirit of regeneration, but in adults the will itself is healed and prepared by the Lord, which is done by the spirit of faith and love.

**6**. In another place, in that which I said of the Lord Jesus Christ: *he did nothing by force, but everything by persuading and admonishing*[^164]*,* it had not occurred to me that he cast the sellers and buyers out of the temple with a whip[^165]. But what is this, or how great is it? Although he also cast out the demons, unwilling, from men, not by the word of persuasion but by the force of power[^166]?

Likewise in another place: *first,* I said, *those are to be followed who say that the one supreme God, the only true God, is alone to be worshiped. If the truth does not shine forth among these, then at last one must move on*[^167]*.* This can seem to be said as though I had doubted about the truth of this religion. But I said it as was fitting for the one to whom I was writing. For I said, *if the truth does not shine forth among these,* in no way doubting that it would shine forth among them, just as the Apostle says: *if Christ has not risen*[^168]*,* certainly not doubting that he had risen.

**7**. Likewise what I said: *nor have those miracles been permitted to last into our times, lest the soul always seek visible things, and the human race grow cold by the habituation to those things by whose novelty it had been kindled*[^169]*,* is indeed true; for it is not the case now, when hands are laid on the baptized, that they so receive the Holy Spirit as to speak in the tongues of all nations[^170], nor now are the sick healed by the shadow of Christ's preachers passing by[^171], and any such things as were then done, which it is clear afterward ceased.

But what I said is not to be understood in such a way that no miracles are believed to be done now in the name of Christ. For I myself, when I wrote this very book, already knew that a blind man had been given sight at the bodies of the martyrs of Milan in that same city[^172], and several other things, of such a kind as so many are done even in these times, that we can neither know them all nor enumerate those that we know.

**8**. And in another place, that which I said: *as the Apostle says: 'all order is from God'*[^173]*,* the Apostle did not say in these words, although the sense seems to be the same. For he says: *but the things that exist are ordained by God*[^174].

And elsewhere: *altogether,* I said, *let no one deceive us: whatever is rightly blamed is rejected in comparison with something better*[^175]*.* This was said of substances and natures—for that was the subject under discussion—not of good actions and sins.

Likewise elsewhere: *but not even so,* I said, *is man to be loved by man as carnal brothers or sons or spouses or any kinsmen, or relatives by marriage, or fellow citizens are loved, for this love too is temporal. For we would have no such ties, which come about by being born and dying, if our nature, remaining in the precepts and image of God, were not relegated to this corruption*[^176]*.*

This sense I altogether disapprove, which I have already disapproved above too in the first book *On Genesis Against the Manichees*[^177]. For it leads to this: that those first spouses are believed not to have been going to beget human descendants unless they had sinned, as though it were necessary that those who were to die should be begotten, if they were begotten from the intercourse of male and female.

For I had not yet seen that it could have come about that those who were not to die should be born of those who were not to die, if human nature had not been changed for the worse by that great sin; and through this, if fecundity and felicity had remained both in the parents and in the children, up to the certain number of the saints which *God predestined*[^178], men would be born not to succeed parents who were dying, but to reign with parents who were living. There would, therefore, be these kinships and ties of marriage as well, if no one transgressed and no one died.

**9**. Likewise in another place: *tending toward the one God,* I said, *and binding back our souls to him alone, whence religion is believed to be so called, let us be free of all superstition*[^179]*.* In these words of mine the explanation that was given of why *religion* is so called pleased me more. For it does not escape me that the authors of the Latin language set forth another origin of this name: that *religion* is so called because it is re-chosen[^180]. This word is composed from "choosing" (*legendo*), that is, "electing" (*eligendo*), so that "religion" (*religo*) seems to be Latin in the same way as "I elect" (*eligo*).

This book begins thus: *Since the whole way of a good and happy life.*

## Chapter XIV (XIII): The Advantage of Believing, to Honoratus, One Book

**1**. Now, already a priest at Hippo Regius, I wrote the book *The Advantage of Believing* to my friend, whom I knew to have been deceived by the Manichees and to be still held in that error, and to mock the discipline of the catholic faith, because men were commanded to believe, but were not taught what was true by the most certain reasoning.

In this book I said: *yet in those precepts and commandments of the law which it is now not lawful for a Christian to use—such as the sabbath, or circumcision, or sacrifices, and anything of this kind—such great mysteries are contained that every pious person understands nothing to be more pernicious than that whatever is there be taken to the letter, that is, to the word, but nothing more wholesome than that it be revealed by the spirit. Hence it is: 'the letter kills, but the spirit gives life.'*[^181]

But I expounded these words of the apostle Paul otherwise, and, as it seems to me—or rather as appears from the things themselves—much more fittingly, in the book which is entitled *The Spirit and the Letter,* although this sense too is not to be rejected[^182].

**2**. Likewise I said: *for there are two kinds of persons praiseworthy in religion. One is of those who have already found, who must also be judged most blessed; the other of those who seek most diligently and most rightly. The first, then, are already in possession itself, the others on the way—by which, however, one arrives most certainly*[^183]*.* In these words of mine, if those who have already found—whom we said are already in possession itself—are so understood as most blessed, that they are most blessed not in this life but in that which we hope for and toward which we tend by the way of faith, this sense has no error.

For they are to be judged to have found what was to be sought, who are already there where we, by seeking and believing—that is, by holding the way of faith—desire to arrive. But if these are thought to be, or to have been, in this life, that does not seem to me to be true—not because in this life nothing true at all can be found which is discerned by the mind and not believed by faith, but because whatever it is, it is so little that it does not make men most blessed. For what the Apostle says, *we now see through a mirror in an enigma*[^184], and, *now I know in part,* is not discerned by the mind; it is plainly discerned, but does not yet make men most blessed.

For that makes men most blessed which he says: *but then face to face,* and, *then I shall know even as I am known*[^185]. Those who have found this are to be said to stand in the possession of blessedness, to which the way of faith leads which we hold, and to which by believing we desire to arrive. But who exactly are those most blessed who are already in that possession to which this way leads is a great question. And that the holy angels are there is no question.

But concerning holy men already deceased, whether they at least are to be said to stand already in that possession is rightly asked. For they have indeed already been stripped of the corruptible body by which the soul is weighed down; but they too still *await the redemption of their body*[^186], and their *flesh rests in hope*[^187], and does not yet shine forth in the incorruption to come. But whether, for contemplating the truth with the eyes of the heart, as it is said, *face to face,* they have any less on this account, this is not the place to inquire by disputation.

Likewise what I said: *for to know great and honorable, and even divine, things is most blessed*[^188]*,* we ought to refer to that same blessedness. For in this life, however much that is known, it is not yet most blessed, since incomparably far greater is what remains unknown of it.

**3**. And what I said: *that it makes much difference whether something is held by the certain reasoning of the mind, which we call to know, or is usefully commended to posterity to be believed on report or in writing;* and a little later: *what we know, therefore, we owe to reason; what we believe, to authority*[^189]*,* is not to be understood in such a way that, in more ordinary speech, we should fear to say we know what we believe on suitable witnesses.

For when we speak strictly, we say we know that alone which we comprehend by the firm reasoning of the mind. But when we speak in words more suited to common usage—as the divine Scripture too speaks—let us not hesitate to say that we know both what we perceive by the senses of our body and what we believe on trustworthy witnesses, provided, however, that we understand what difference lies between these and that.

**4**. Likewise what I said: *it is doubtful to no one that all men are either foolish or wise*[^190]*,* can seem to be contrary to that which is read in the third book *On Free Will*: *as though indeed human nature admitted no intermediate affection besides folly and wisdom*[^191]*.* But that was said there where it was asked, concerning the first man, whether he was made wise or foolish or neither—since in no way could we call foolish one who was made without fault, folly being a great fault; and how we should call wise one who could be seduced was not sufficiently clear. And therefore, for the sake of brevity, I wished to say: *as though indeed human nature admitted no intermediate affection besides folly and wisdom.* For I had in view also little children, who, although we confess that they draw original sin, can nevertheless properly be called neither wise nor foolish, since they do not yet use free choice either well or badly. But here I said that all men are either wise or foolish, wishing those to be understood who already use reason, by which they are distinguished from beasts so that they are men, just as we say that all men wish to be happy. For do we fear, in this saying so true and manifest, that little children—who cannot yet wish this—might be understood?

**5**. In another place, when I had mentioned the miracles which the Lord Jesus did when he was here in the flesh, I added, saying: *why, you say, are these not done now?*[^192] And I answered: *because they would not move us unless they were wonderful, but if they were customary, they would not be wonderful*[^193]*.* But I said this because such great miracles, and all of them, are not done now—not because none are done even now.

**6**. At the end of the book: *but since this discourse of ours,* I said, *has gone on much longer than I thought, let us here make an end of the book, in which I want you to remember that I have not yet begun to refute the Manichees, and have not yet attacked those trifles, nor disclosed anything great about the Catholic Church itself, but have wished only to root out for you, if I could, the false opinion about true Christians maliciously or ignorantly instilled in us, and to raise you up to learning certain great and divine things. Therefore let this volume be as it is; but when your mind has been made more peaceable, I shall perhaps be readier in the rest*[^194]*.*

I did not say these things as though I had written nothing against the Manichees up to then, or had committed nothing of catholic doctrine to writing, since so many volumes published above on both matters testify that I was not silent; but in this book written to him I had *not yet begun to refute the Manichees,* and had *not yet attacked those trifles,* nor disclosed *anything great about the Catholic Church itself,* since I hoped that, this beginning having been made, I would write to him the very things that here I had not yet written.

This book begins thus: *If to me, Honoratus, it seemed to be one and the same thing.*

## Chapter XV (XIV): On the Two Souls, Against the Manichees, One Book

**1**. After this book I wrote, still a priest, against the Manichees *On the Two Souls,* of which they say one is a part of God, the other from the race of darkness, which God did not create, and which is coeternal with God. And they rave that both these souls, one good and the other evil, are in one man; saying, of course, that this evil one belongs to the flesh, which flesh they also say belongs to the race of darkness; but that the good one is from an adventitious part of God, which clashed with the race of darkness and mingled the two. And all the good things of man they attribute to that good soul, but all the evil things to that evil soul.

In this book, that which I said: *that there is no life of any kind whatever which, by the very fact that it is life, and in so far as it is life at all, does not pertain to the supreme source and principle of life*[^195]*,* I said in such a way that it is understood to pertain to the Creator as a creature, but not to be thought to be of him as a part of him.

**2**. Likewise what I said: that *sin is nowhere except in the will*[^196]*,* the Pelagians can think was said in their favor on account of little children, whom for this reason they deny to have sin which is remitted to them in baptism, because they do not yet use the choice of the will. As though indeed the sin which we say they draw originally from Adam—that is, being implicated in his guilt, and on this account held liable to punishment—could be anywhere except in the will, by which will it was committed, when the transgression of the divine precept was made. This saying too can be thought false, by which we said, *sin is nowhere except in the will,* because the Apostle said: *but if I do that which I do not will, it is no longer I that work it, but the sin that dwells in me*[^197]. For this sin is so far from being in the will that he says: *that which I do not will, this I do.* How, then, is sin nowhere except in the will? But this sin, of which the Apostle spoke thus, is called sin for this reason, that it was made by sin and is the punishment of sin—since indeed this is said of the concupiscence of the flesh, which he discloses in what follows, saying: *I know that there does not dwell in me, that is, in my flesh, good: for to will is present with me, but to accomplish the good is not*[^198].

For the perfection of good is that not even concupiscence of sin be in a man, to which indeed, when one lives well, the will does not consent. Nevertheless it does not accomplish the good, because concupiscence is still present, against which the will struggles; the guilt of which concupiscence is loosed in baptism, but the weakness remains, against which, until it is healed, every faithful person who advances well struggles most diligently. But the sin which is *nowhere except in the will* is to be understood especially as that which just condemnation has followed; for this *entered into the world through one man*[^199]. Although this sin too, by which one consents to the concupiscence of sin, is committed only by the will. On account of this I said also in another place: *therefore one does not sin except by the will*[^200].

**3**. Likewise in another place I defined the will itself, saying: *the will is a movement of the mind, with nothing forcing it, either toward not losing or toward attaining something*[^201]*.* This was said in order that by this definition the willing might be distinguished from the unwilling, and so that the attention might be referred to those who were the first in paradise, the origin of evil for the human race, sinning with nothing forcing them—that is, sinning by free will, because they both knowingly acted against the precept, and that tempter persuaded that this be done, but did not force it. For he too who sinned in ignorance can not unfittingly be said to have sinned unwillingly, although he too did willingly what he did in ignorance; thus not even his sin could be without the will.

This *will,* of course, as it was defined, *was a movement of the mind, with nothing forcing it, either toward not losing or toward attaining something.* For, since if he had not willed he would not have done it, he was not forced to do it. Because he willed, therefore, he did it, even if he did not sin because he willed, not knowing that what he did was sin. Thus neither could such a sin be without the will, but it was by the will of the deed, not by the will of the sin, which nevertheless, once done, was sin; for that was done which ought not to have been done. But whoever sins knowingly, if he can without sin resist the one forcing him to sin and yet does not, certainly sins willingly, since he who can resist is not forced to yield. But he who cannot resist forcing desire with a good will, and therefore acts against the precepts of justice, this is now so far sin that it is also the punishment of sin. Wherefore it is most true that sin cannot exist without the will.

**4**. Likewise the definition of sin by which we said: *sin is the will to retain or to attain what justice forbids, and from which one is free to abstain*[^202]*,* is true for this reason, that what is defined is that which is sin only, not that which is also the punishment of sin. For when it is such that it is at once both sin and the punishment of sin, how much is it that the will avails under dominating desire, unless perhaps, if it is pious, to pray for help? For it is free in so far as it is freed, and in so far is it called will. Otherwise the whole of it is desire, which is properly to be called will, which is not, as the Manichees rave, an addition of an alien nature, but a fault of our own, from which we are not healed except by the grace of the Savior. But if anyone says that desire itself is nothing other than the will, but a faulty will and one in servitude to sin, he is not to be resisted, nor is a controversy to be made about words when the matter is agreed. For even so it is shown that there is no sin without the will, whether in act or in origin.

**5**. Again, in that which I said: *I could now ask whether that evil kind of souls, before it was mingled with the good, had had any will. For if it did not have one, it was without sin and innocent, and therefore in no way evil*[^203]. Why, then, they say, do you speak of the sin of little children, whose will you do not hold guilty? It is answered that they are held guilty not by the property of will, but by origin. For what is every earthly man by origin except Adam? But Adam certainly had a will, by which will, when he had sinned, *sin entered into the world* through him[^204].

**6**. Likewise, in that which I said: *souls can in no way be evil by nature*[^205], if it is asked how we are to understand what the Apostle says, *we too were by nature children of wrath, like the rest*[^206], we answer that by "nature" in these words of mine I wished to be understood that which is properly called nature, in which we were created without fault. For that is called "nature" on account of origin, which origin certainly has a fault, which is against nature.

And again, in that which was said: *that anyone be held guilty of sin because he did not do what he could not do is the mark of the utmost iniquity and madness*[^207]; why, then, they say, are little children held guilty? It is answered: because they are held guilty from the origin of him who did not do what he could do—namely, to keep the divine command.

But as for what I said: *whatever those souls do, if they do it by nature, not by will—that is, if they lack a movement of the mind free both to act and not to act—if, finally, no power is granted them of abstaining from their work, we cannot hold them guilty of sin*[^208], the question about little children does not disturb this for the reason that they are held guilty from the origin of him who sinned by will, when he did not lack a movement of the mind free both to act and not to act, and had the fullest power of abstaining from the evil work. This the Manichees do not say of the race of darkness, which they introduce in most fabulous fashion, and contend that this nature was always evil and never good.

**7**. But this can be asked: how did I say: *even if there are souls—which for now is uncertain—given over to corporeal functions not by sin but by nature, and which touch us, though they are inferior, by some inner nearness, they ought not on that account to be held evil, because we, when we follow them and love corporeal things, are evil*[^209]; seeing that I said this of those of whom I had begun to speak above, saying: *although, even if it be granted to them that we are enticed to base things by another, inferior kind of souls, they do not thence conclude either that those souls are evil by nature or that these are the supreme good*[^210]*.* For I carried the disputation about these down to this place, where I said: *even if there are souls—which for now is uncertain—given over to corporeal functions not by sin but by nature,* and the rest.

It can be asked, then, why I said *which for now is uncertain,* when I ought by no means to have doubted that there are no such souls. But I said this for the reason that I had met with men who said that the devil and his angels are good in their own kind and in that nature in which God created them, such as they are in their proper order, but that it is evil for us if we are enticed and seduced by them, while if we beware of them and overcome them, it is fitting and glorious. And those who say this seem to themselves, in order to prove it, to adduce suitable testimonies from the Scriptures: either that which is written in the book of Job, when the devil was being described: *this is the beginning of the Lord's creation, which he made to be mocked by his angels*[^211], or that in the hundred and fourth Psalm: *this dragon, which you formed to mock it*[^212].

This question, which was to be taken up and solved not against the Manichees, who do not hold this, but against others who do hold it, I did not then wish to treat and unravel, lest I make the book much longer than I wished, since I saw that, even if this were granted, the Manichees ought nevertheless—and now could—be refuted, who by a most insane error introduce a nature of evil coeternal with the eternal good. Therefore I said *which for now is uncertain,* not because I doubted about it, but because between me and those whom I had found to think thus, this question was not yet resolved; which, however, in other books of mine far later, *The Literal Meaning of Genesis,* I resolved according to the holy Scriptures with as much clarity as I could[^213].

**8**. In another place: *therefore,* I said, *by loving corporeal things we sin, because we are both commanded by justice to love spiritual things and able by nature to do so, and then we are in our own kind best and most blessed*[^214]*.* Here it can be asked why I said we are *able* by *nature* and not by grace. But against the Manichees the question concerned nature.

And it is certainly grace that brings it about that nature, once healed, may be able to do what, while corrupted, it cannot, through him who *came to seek and to save what was lost*[^215]. Yet, mindful even then of this grace, I prayed for my closest friends, who were still held by that deadly error, and I said: *great God, almighty God, God of supreme goodness, whom it is right to believe and understand to be inviolable and unchangeable, Triune Unity, whom the catholic Church worships, I pray as a suppliant, having experienced your mercy in myself, that you not permit the men with whom from boyhood I have had the fullest agreement in every association to dissent from me in your worship*[^216]*.*

Praying thus, I certainly held already by faith not only that those converted to God are helped by his grace to advance and be perfected—where it can still be said that this grace is given for the merit of their conversion—but also that they are converted to God belongs to the grace of God itself, since indeed I prayed for those who were very greatly turned away from him, and prayed that they might be converted to him.

This book begins thus: *With the help of God's mercy.*

## Chapter XVI (XV): The Proceedings Against Fortunatus the Manichee, One Book

**1**. In the same period of my priesthood I disputed against a certain Fortunatus, a priest of the Manichees, who had lived a very long time at Hippo and had seduced so many that on their account it pleased him to dwell there. This disputation, as we contended, was taken down by notaries, as though official acts were being compiled; for it has both a date and a consul. We took care to gather this into a book to be committed to memory. The question is treated there of whence evil comes, I asserting that the evil of man arose from the free choice of the will, while he labored to persuade us of a nature of evil coeternal with God. But on the following day he at last confessed that he found nothing to say against us. He did not indeed become a catholic, but nevertheless departed from Hippo.

**2**. In this book, that which I said: *I say that the soul was made by God, like all the other things that were made by God, and that among those which almighty God made, the chief place was given to the soul*[^217], I said in such a way that I wished it to be understood generally of the whole rational creation, although in the holy Scriptures that the angels are called "souls" either cannot be found at all or cannot easily be found, as we have already said above[^218].

Likewise in another place: *I say,* I said, *that there is no sin if one does not sin by one's own will*[^219]*.* Here I wished that sin to be understood which is not also the punishment of sin; for of such punishment I said elsewhere in the same disputation what was to be said[^220].

Likewise I said: *that afterward that same flesh, which tormented us with punishments while we remained in sins, may be made subject to us in the resurrection, and may shake us with no adversity so as to keep us from observing the law and the divine precepts*[^221]*.* This is not to be understood as though in that kingdom of God, where we shall have an incorruptible and immortal body, the law and precepts are to be taken from the divine Scriptures; but because there the eternal law will be most perfectly observed, and those two precepts about loving God and neighbor[^222] we shall hold not in reading, but in that perfect and everlasting love itself.

This work begins thus: *On the fifth day before the Kalends of September, when Arcadius Augustus for the second time and Rufinus, most illustrious men, were consuls.*

## Chapter XVII (XVI): Faith and the Creed, One Book

During the same period, before the bishops who commanded me this—who were holding a plenary council of all Africa at Hippo Regius—I, a priest, disputed *On Faith and the Creed.* This disputation, at the most diligent insistence of some of those who loved us more intimately, I gathered into a book; in which the matters themselves are discussed in such a way, however, that there is not made that weaving together of words which is handed down to the candidates to be held in memory.

In this book, when the resurrection of the flesh was being treated: *the body,* I said, *will rise according to the Christian faith, which cannot deceive. To whomever this seems incredible, he attends to what the flesh is now, but does not consider what it will be; because at that time of the angelic change it will no longer be flesh and blood but only body*[^223], and the rest, which I there discussed about the change of earthly bodies into heavenly bodies, since the Apostle said, when speaking of this: *flesh and blood shall not possess the kingdom of God*[^224]*.* But whoever so understands these things as to think that the earthly body, such as we now have, is so changed by the resurrection into a heavenly body that neither these members nor the substance of flesh will exist in the future, is without doubt to be corrected, being reminded of the body of the Lord, who after the resurrection appeared in those same members, not only to be beheld with the eyes but also to be handled with the hands, and confirmed by word also that he had flesh, saying: *handle and see, for a spirit does not have bones and flesh as you see me to have*[^225].

Whence it is clear that the Apostle did not deny that the substance of flesh will exist in the kingdom of God, but either called by the name of flesh and blood the men who live according to the flesh, or the very corruption of the flesh, which then certainly will be none. For when he had said, *flesh and blood shall not possess the kingdom of God*, it is rightly understood that, as if expounding what he had said, he immediately added: *nor shall corruption possess incorruption*[^226]. On this matter—difficult for persuading unbelievers—whoever reads the last book *The City of God* will find that I discussed it as diligently as I could[^227].

This book begins thus: *Since it is written.*

## Chapter XVIII (XVII): Unfinished Literal Commentary on Genesis, One Book

When I had composed two books *On Genesis Against the Manichees*[^228], since I had treated the words of Scripture according to their allegorical signification, not daring to expound to the letter such great secrets of natural things—that is, how the things said there could be taken according to historical literalness—I wished to try, in this most laborious and most difficult work too, what I could do; but in expounding the Scriptures my apprenticeship sank under so great a weight of the burden, and, with one book not yet finished, I rested from the labor which I could not sustain.

But in this work, when I was reviewing my little works, this very one, just as it was, unfinished, came into my hands—which I had neither published nor decided to destroy, since I afterward wrote twelve books whose title is *The Literal Meaning of Genesis*[^229]*.* In these, although many things seem rather to be sought than found, yet this one is in no way to be compared with them. But after I had reviewed this one too, I wished it to remain, that it might be an index, as I judge, not useless, of my first attempts at unfolding and scrutinizing the divine words; and I wished its title to be *Unfinished Literal Commentary on Genesis.*

For I found it dictated up to these words: *the Father is only Father, nor is the Son anything other than the Son; because even when he is called the likeness of the Father, although it shows that no unlikeness intervenes, yet the Father is not alone, if he has a likeness*[^230]*.* After these I took up again the words of Scripture, to be once more considered and treated: *and God said: 'let us make man to our image and likeness'*[^231]. Up to this point I had left the dictated book unfinished. But what follows there I thought should be added when I reviewed it; yet not even so did I finish it, but, with this too added, I left it unfinished. For if I had finished it, I would at least have discussed all the works and words of God which pertain to the sixth day.

In this book, to note the things that displease me, or to defend those which, not well understood, may displease others, has seemed to me superfluous. For I rather briefly advise that those twelve books be read, which I made long afterward as a bishop, and that this one be judged from them.

This one, then, begins thus: *Concerning the obscurities of natural things, which we perceive to have been made by almighty God the artificer, one must treat not by affirming but by inquiring.*

## Chapter XIX (XVIII): The Lord's Sermon on the Mount, Two Books

**1**. During the same period I wrote two volumes *On the Lord's Sermon on the Mount* according to Matthew. In the first of these, on account of that which is written, *blessed are the peacemakers, for they shall be called sons of God*[^232], I said: *Wisdom befits the peacemakers, in whom all things are already ordered, and no movement is in rebellion against reason, but all things obey the spirit of man, since he too obeys God*[^233]. This rightly gives pause, in the way I said it. For it cannot come about for anyone in this life that the law warring against *the law of the mind*[^234] be altogether not in the members, since indeed, even if the spirit of man so resisted it that he slipped into no assent to it, it would nevertheless not on that account fail to war against him. This, then, which was said—*that no movement is in rebellion against reason*—can rightly be understood of the peacemakers now doing this, by subduing the desires of the flesh, so that one may at some time come to that most full peace.

**2**. Accordingly, that which in another place, when, repeating the same gospel saying, I had said, *blessed are the peacemakers, for they shall be called sons of God,* I added, saying: *and these things can indeed be fulfilled in this life, as we believe they were fulfilled in the Apostles*[^235]*,* is to be understood not so that we suppose that in the Apostles living here no movement of the flesh warred against the spirit, but that these things can be fulfilled here only so far as we believe they were fulfilled in the Apostles—namely, by that measure of human perfection which perfection can have in this life.

For it was not said: these things can be fulfilled in this life, for we believe they were fulfilled in the Apostles; but it was said: *as we believe they were fulfilled in the Apostles,* so that they may be fulfilled as they were fulfilled in them—that is, by a certain perfection of which this life is capable—not as those things are to be fulfilled which we hope for in that most full peace, when it shall be said: *where, O death, is your contention?*[^236]

**3**. In another place, the testimony I inserted: *for God does not give the Spirit by measure*[^237]*,* I had not yet understood is more truly taken properly of Christ. For to other men, unless the Spirit were given by measure, Elisha would not have asked for double what was in Elijah[^238].

Likewise that which is written, *not one iota or one tittle shall pass from the law until all things are accomplished*[^239]*,* when I was expounding it, I said: *that nothing else can be understood except a vehement expression of perfection*[^240]*.* Here it is rightly asked whether this perfection can be so understood that it nevertheless remains true that no one who already uses the choice of the will lives here without sin.

For by whom can the law be fulfilled down to one tittle, except by him by whom all the divine commands are done? But among those same commands is also that which we are bidden to say: *forgive us our debts, as we also forgive our debtors*[^241], which prayer the whole Church says until the end of the age. All the commands, therefore, are reckoned to be done, when whatever is not done is forgiven.

**4**. As for what the Lord says: *whoever shall break one of these least commandments and teach men so*[^242], and the rest, down to that place where he says: *unless your justice shall abound more than that of the scribes and Pharisees, you shall not enter into the kingdom of heaven*[^243]—I expounded it much better and more fittingly in other later sermons of mine, which it would be long to weave again here. But the sense there is brought to this: that the justice of those who say and do is greater than that of the scribes and Pharisees. For of the scribes and Pharisees the Lord himself says in another place: *for they say and do not do*[^244].

We also understood better afterward[^245] that which is written: *whoever is angry with his brother*[^246]. For the Greek copies do not have *without cause,* as it is placed here, although the sense itself is the same. For we said that this is to be considered: what it is to be angry with one's brother, since he is not angry with his brother who is angry at his brother's sin. He, therefore, who is angry at his brother and not at his sin is angry without cause.

**5**. Likewise what I said: *this is to be understood also of father and mother and the other bonds of blood, so that in them we hate what the human race has been allotted by being born and dying*[^247]*,* sounds as though these ties would not exist if, with no preceding sin of human nature, no one died—a sense which I have already disapproved above[^248]. For there would surely be kinships and ties of marriage, even if, with no original sin existing, the human race grew and multiplied without death. And through this the question must be solved otherwise—why the Lord commanded that enemies be loved[^249], when in another place he commands that both parents and children be held in hatred[^250]—not as it is solved here, but as we have often solved it later[^251]: that is, that we love enemies in order to gain them for the kingdom of God, and hate kinsmen if they hinder us from the kingdom of God.

**6**. Likewise concerning the precept by which it is forbidden to dismiss a wife except for fornication[^252], I here disputed most scrupulously[^253]. But what fornication the Lord wishes to be understood, on account of which it is lawful to dismiss a wife—whether that which is condemned in unchastity, or that of which it is said, *you have destroyed everyone who fornicates away from you*[^254], in which this too is certainly included (for he does indeed fornicate away from the Lord who, *taking the members of Christ, makes them the members of a harlot*[^255])—must be thought over again and again, and sought out.

Nor do I wish, in a matter so great and so difficult to discern, that the reader think this disputation of ours ought to suffice for him; but let him read other things too, whether ours which were written afterward, or those of others better considered and treated[^256]; or, if he can, let him himself examine, with a more vigilant and intelligent mind, the things which here may rightly give pause. Not because every sin is fornication (for God does not destroy every sinner, who daily hears his saints saying, *forgive us our debts*[^257], while he destroys *everyone who fornicates away from him*), but how this fornication is to be understood and limited, and whether even on account of this it is lawful to dismiss a wife, is a most hidden question.

That it is, however, lawful on account of that which is committed in unchastity, there is no question. And where I said: *that this was permitted, not commanded,* I did not attend to another Scripture saying: *he who keeps an adulteress is foolish and impious*[^258]. Nor indeed would I say that that woman was to be reckoned an adulteress even after she heard from the Lord: *neither will I condemn you; go, and now sin no more*[^259], if she heard this obediently.

**7**. In another place, the sin of a brother unto death, of which the apostle John says, *I do not say that one should pray for it*[^260]*,* I so defined as to say: *I think the sin of a brother unto death is when, after the recognition of God through the grace of our Lord Jesus Christ, anyone attacks the brotherhood and is driven by the firebrands of envy against the very grace by which he was reconciled to God*[^261]*.* This indeed I did not affirm, since I said that I thought this; but nevertheless there should have been added: "if he ended this life in this so wicked perversity of mind," since one must certainly not despair of anyone, however wicked, established in this life, and one does not imprudently pray for him of whom one does not despair.

**8**. Likewise in the second book: *no one,* I said, *will be permitted to be ignorant of the kingdom of God, when his Only-Begotten shall come from heaven not only intelligibly but also visibly in the lordly man, 'to judge the living and the dead'*[^262]*.* But I do not see whether he is rightly called the lordly man who is *the Mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus*[^263], since he is certainly the Lord. But who in his holy household cannot be called a lordly man? And this, indeed, that I might say it, I read in certain catholic treaters of the divine words[^264]. But wherever I said this, I would wish I had not said it[^265]. For afterward I saw that it is not to be said, although it can be defended by some reasoning.

Likewise what I said: *for the conscience of almost no one can hate God*[^266]*,* I do not see should have been said; for there are many of whom it is written: *the pride of those who hate you*[^267].

**9**. In another place, in that which I said: *that the Lord said, 'sufficient for the day is its own evil,' for this reason, that the very necessity of taking food will press upon us, which I think was for this reason called evil, because it is penal for us, for it pertains to this frailty which we deserved by sinning*[^268]*,* I did not attend to the fact that bodily nourishment was given even to the first human beings in paradise, before they deserved this punishment of death by sinning. For they were so immortal in a body not yet spiritual but animal that nevertheless in immortality of this kind they made use of corporeal nourishments.

Likewise what I said: *which God chose for himself, 'a glorious Church having no spot or wrinkle'*[^269]*,* I did not say because it is now already such in every part, although it would not be doubted to have been chosen for this: that it may be such when *Christ, its life, shall appear*[^270]; for *then* it too will appear with him in glory, on account of which glory it was called *a glorious Church.*

Likewise, what the Lord says: *ask and you shall receive, seek and you shall find, knock and it shall be opened to you*[^271], I thought it laborious to expound how these three differ among themselves[^272]; but far better are they all referred to most insistent petition. For he shows this where he concluded them all with the one word, saying: *how much more will your Father who is in heaven give good things to those who ask him*[^273], for he did not say "to those who ask and seek and knock."

This work begins thus: *The sermon which the Lord spoke.*

## Chapter XX (XIX): Psalm against the Party of Donatus

Wishing also that the cause of the Donatists should come to the knowledge of the very humblest common folk, and of the wholly unskilled and untaught, and that it should cling to their memory as far as could be brought about through us, I made a Psalm to be sung to them, in Latin letters, but only up to the letter "V." Such pieces they call "abecedarians."

The last three letters I omitted; but in place of them I added at the end a kind of epilogue, as though Mother Church were addressing them. The hypopsalma too, which is the response, and the preface of the case, which was nonetheless to be sung, are not in the order of the letters; for their order begins after the preface. But I did not wish this to be done in any kind of meter, lest the necessity of meter compel me to certain words less commonly in popular use.

This Psalm begins thus: *All you who rejoice in peace, now judge truly,* which is its hypopsalma.

## Chapter XXI (XX): Against the Letter of Donatus the Heretic, One Book

**1**. I also wrote, in that same period of my priesthood, the book *Against the Letter of Donatus,* who was the second bishop of the party of Donatus after Majorinus at Carthage; in which letter he labors to have it believed that the baptism of Christ exists only in his communion—which we contradict in this book.

In it I said in a certain place of the apostle Peter, that *upon him as upon a rock the Church was founded*[^274]*,* which sense is also sung by the mouth of many in the verses of the most blessed Ambrose, where he says of the cock: *at his crowing the rock of the Church itself washed away its fault*[^275]. But I know that I afterward very often so expounded[^276] that which was said by the Lord: *you are Peter, and upon this rock I will build my Church*[^277], that it should be understood of him whom Peter confessed when he said: *you are the Christ, the Son of the living God*[^278], and thus Peter, named from this rock, should figure the person of the Church, which is built *upon this rock* and received *the keys of the kingdom of heaven*[^279]. For it was not said to him, "you are the rock," but, *you are Peter. But the rock was Christ*[^280]; whom Simon, having confessed—as the whole Church confesses him—was called *Peter.* But which of these two views is the more probable, let the reader choose.

**2**. In another place I said: *God seeks no one's death,* which is to be understood thus, that man acquired death for himself by forsaking God, and he acquires it who does not return to God, according to what is written: *God did not make death*[^281]. But that too is no less true: *life and death are from the Lord God*[^282]—life, namely, from the giver, death from the avenger.

**3**. Likewise what I said, that *Donatus,* whose letter I was refuting, *asked that the Emperor give bishops from overseas as judges between himself and Caecilian*[^283]*,* it is found more probable that not he but another Donatus, of the same schism, however, did this. But that one was not the Carthaginian bishop of the Donatists, but the one from Casae Nigrae, who nevertheless first committed that wicked schism at Carthage. Nor indeed did *Donatus of Carthage institute that Christians be rebaptized,* which I had believed him to have instituted when I was answering his letter. Nor did he himself remove from the middle of a sentence of the book *Ecclesiasticus* the words necessary to the matter, where, since it is written, *he who is baptized by a dead man, and again touches him, what does his washing profit?*[^284], this man set it down as though it were written: "he who is baptized by a dead man, what does his washing profit?" But that, even before there was a party of Donatus, very many copies—African ones, however—so had it that there was not in the middle *and again touches him,* we learned afterward. And if I had known this then, I would not have said so much against this man as against a thief of the divine word or a violator of it.

This book begins thus: *I had heard from you yourself when present.*

## Chapter XXII (XXI): Answer to Adimantus, a Disciple of Mani, One Book

**1**. In the same period there came into my hands certain *Disputations* of Adimantus, who had been a disciple of Mani, which he composed against the Law and the Prophets, attempting to show the gospel and apostolic writings as contrary to them. To him, then, I replied, setting down his words and giving to them my response. This work I concluded in one volume, and in it I answered certain questions not once but a second time, since what I had first answered had been lost and was found only after I had already answered a second time. Some of those same questions I solved in popular ecclesiastical sermons[^285]. Still others I have not yet answered; some remained which were passed over because other, more urgent matters pressed, the accumulation of forgetfulness being added as well.

**2**. In this, then, I said: *for by certain fixed shadows and figures of things before the Lord's coming, according to the wonderful and most orderly distribution of the times, that people was held which received the Old Testament; yet in it there is so great a preaching and foretelling of the New Testament that none, however lofty and divine, of the precepts and promises in the gospel and apostolic teaching are found which are lacking even to those old books*[^286]*.* But "almost" should have been added, and it should have been said: *so that* "almost" *no precepts and promises, however lofty and divine, are found in the gospel and apostolic teaching which are lacking even to those old books.*

For what is it that the Lord says in the gospel sermon on the mount: *you have heard that it was said to the ancients this, but I say to you this*[^287], if he himself commanded nothing more than was commanded in those old books? Then, that *the kingdom of heaven* was promised to that people, we do not read among the things which were promised by the Law given through Moses on Mount Sinai[^288], which is properly called the Old Testament; which the Apostle says was prefigured by the slave girl of Sarah and her son[^289]; but there the New too was figured by Sarah herself and her son.

Accordingly, if the figures are examined, all things are found prophesied there which have been presented or are awaited to be presented through Christ. Nevertheless, on account of certain precepts not figured but proper, which are found not in the Old Testament but in the New, it would be said more cautiously and moderately, "almost none," than that there are *none* here which are not there as well—although there are there those two precepts about the love of God and neighbor[^290], to which most rightly all things, both legal and prophetic and gospel and apostolic, are referred.

**3**. Likewise what I said: *that in three ways the name of "sons" is taken in the holy Scriptures*[^291]*,* was said less considerately. For we without doubt passed over certain other ways; as one is called *a son of hell*[^292], or *an adoptive son*[^293], which are certainly said neither according to nature nor according to teaching nor according to imitation. Of these three ways, as though they were the only ones, we gave examples: according to nature, as the Jews are *sons of Abraham*[^294]; according to teaching, as the Apostle calls his sons those whom the Gospel taught[^295]; according to imitation, as we are sons of Abraham, whose faith we imitate[^296].

But what I said: *when it shall have put on incorruption and immortality, it will no longer be flesh and blood*[^297]*,* was said of carnal corruption—that the flesh will not exist—not of the substance according to which the body of the Lord was called *flesh* even after the resurrection[^298].

**4**. In another place: *unless each one,* I said, *change his will, he cannot work good, which he teaches to be placed in our power in another place, where he says: 'either make the tree good and its fruit good, or make the tree bad and its fruit bad'*[^299]. This is not against the grace of God which we preach. For it is in the power of man to change his will for the better; but that power is none, unless it be given by God, of whom it was said: *he gave them power to become sons of God*[^300]. For since that is in our power which we do when we will, nothing is so much in our power as the will itself; but *the will is prepared by the Lord*[^301]. In this way, then, he gives the power. So too is to be understood what I said afterward: *that it is in our power that we deserve either to be grafted in by the goodness of God or to be cut off by his severity*[^302]*,* because nothing is in our power except what follows our will, which, when it is prepared strong and powerful by the Lord, the work of piety is easily done, even that which was difficult and impossible.

This book begins thus: *Concerning that which is written: 'in the beginning God made heaven and earth.'*

## Chapter XXIII (XXII): Exposition of Certain Propositions — from the Apostle's Letter to the Romans

**1**. While I was still a priest, it happened that at Carthage, among us who were together, the Apostle's *Letter to the Romans* was read, and I was asked certain questions by the brothers; and when I answered them as I could, they wished that what I said should rather be written down than poured forth without writing. When I complied with them, one book was added to my earlier little works.

In this book: *but what he says,* I said, '*we know that the law is spiritual, but I am carnal,' sufficiently shows that the law cannot be fulfilled except by spiritual persons, such as the grace of God makes them*[^303]. This I certainly wished to be taken not in the person of the Apostle, who was already spiritual, but of a man placed *under the law* and not yet *under grace*[^304]. For thus I first understood these words, which afterward, having read certain treaters of the divine words whose authority moved me[^305], I considered more diligently and saw that what he says—*we know that the law is spiritual, but I am carnal*—can be understood even of the Apostle himself. This I showed as diligently as I could in those books which I lately wrote against the Pelagians[^306].

In this book, then, both that which was said, *but I am carnal*, and then the rest down to that place where he says, *wretched man that I am, who will free me from the body of this death? The grace of God through Jesus Christ our Lord*[^307], I said describes a man still established under the law, not yet under grace, wishing to do good but, overcome by the concupiscence of the flesh, doing evil. From the domination of this concupiscence nothing frees but *the grace of God through Jesus Christ our Lord*[^308], by the gift of the Holy Spirit, through whom *love poured out in our hearts*[^309] overcomes the concupiscences of the flesh, so that we do not consent to them for doing evil, but rather do good.

Whence indeed the Pelagian heresy is already overthrown, which wishes that the love by which we live well and piously be not from God to us but from ourselves. But in those books[^310] which we published against them, we showed that these words are better understood even of the spiritual man, already established under grace, on account of the body of flesh, which is not yet spiritual but will be in the resurrection of the dead, and on account of the very concupiscence of the flesh, with which the saints so contend, not consenting to it for evil, that nevertheless they do not lack its movements—against which, as they war, they resist—in this life; but they will not have them in that life, where *death shall be swallowed up in victory*[^311].

On account of this concupiscence, therefore, and those very movements which are so resisted that they nevertheless are in us, any saint already placed *under grace* can say all these things which I here said are the words of a man not yet placed *under grace* but *under the law.* To show this here would be long, and where I have shown it has been said[^312].

**2**. Likewise, disputing what God chose in the one not yet born, to whom he said that the elder would serve, and what in the same elder, likewise not yet born, he rejected—concerning whom, on this account, is mentioned the prophetic testimony, though brought forth long afterward: *Jacob I loved, but Esau I hated*[^313]—I carried my reasoning to this point, that I said: *God, therefore, did not in his foreknowledge choose the works of anyone, which he himself was going to give, but chose faith in his foreknowledge, so that the very one whom he foreknew would believe in him, him he chose, to whom he would give the Holy Spirit, so that by working good he might also attain eternal life*[^314]*.*

I had not yet inquired more diligently, nor had I yet found, what is the election of grace, of which the same Apostle says: *a remnant has been saved by the election of grace*[^315]. Which is certainly not grace, if any merits precede it, lest then what is given be rendered not according to grace but according to debt, repaid to merits rather than bestowed.

Accordingly, what I immediately said: *for the same Apostle says: 'the same God who works all things in all'*[^316]*, but nowhere is it said: God believes all things in all,* and then I added: *that we believe, therefore, is ours; but that we do good is his who gives the Holy Spirit to those who believe*[^317]*,* I would surely not say, if I already knew that faith itself too is found among the gifts of God, which are given *in the same Spirit*[^318]. Both, therefore, are ours, on account of the choice of the will, and both are nevertheless given through the Spirit of faith and love. For it is not love alone, but, as it is written: *love with faith from God the Father and our Lord Jesus Christ*[^319].

**3**. And what I said a little later: *for it is ours to believe and to will, but his to give to those who believe and will the capacity to work well through the Holy Spirit, through whom 'love is poured out in our hearts'*[^320]*,* is indeed true; but by the same rule both are his, because he prepares the will, and both are ours, because it is not done except by our willing.

And through this, what I also said afterward: *that we can neither will unless we are called; and when, after the calling, we have willed, our will and our running do not suffice, unless God both supply strength to those who run and lead them where he calls*[^321]*,* and then I added: *it is clear, therefore, that 'it is not of him who wills nor of him who runs, but of God who shows mercy'*[^322] *that we work well,* is altogether most true.

But I discussed too little about the calling itself, which is made according to the purpose of God. For not of all who are called is it such, but only of the elect. And so what I said a little later: *for as in those whom God chooses it is not works but faith that begins the merit, so that through the gift of God they may work well, so in those whom he condemns, unbelief and impiety begin the merit of punishment, so that through that very punishment they also work evil*[^323], I said most truly. But that the merit of faith itself is also the gift of God, I neither thought ought to be inquired into nor said.

**4**. And in another place: *for on whom he has mercy,* I said, *he makes him work well, and whom he hardens, he leaves him to work evil. But that mercy is granted to a preceding merit of faith, and that hardening to a preceding impiety*[^324]*,* which is indeed true. But it had still to be asked whether the merit of faith too comes from the mercy of God—that is, whether this mercy is shown in a man only because he is faithful, or whether it was also shown that he might be faithful.

For we read the Apostle saying: *I obtained mercy that I might be faithful*[^325]; he did not say, "because I was faithful." To the faithful, therefore, it is indeed given, but it was given also that he might be faithful. And so most rightly I said in another place in the same book: *since, if not from works but from the mercy of God we are both called that we may believe, and to those who believe it is granted that we work well, this mercy is not to be envied to the gentiles*[^326]*,* although there I treated less diligently of that calling which is made through the purpose of God.

This book begins thus: *These are the meanings in the Letter to the Romans of Paul the Apostle.*

## Chapter XXIV (XXIII): Exposition of the Letter to the Galatians, One Book

**1**. After this book I expounded the same Apostle's *Letter to the Galatians,* not piecemeal, that is, passing over some things, but continuously and the whole. This exposition I comprised in one volume.

In it, that which was said: *the earlier Apostles, therefore, were truthful, who were sent not by men but by God through a man—namely, through Jesus Christ still mortal. Truthful too is the last Apostle, who was sent through Jesus Christ now wholly God after his resurrection*[^327]*,* was said *now wholly God* on account of the immortality which he began to have after the resurrection, not on account of the divinity always immortal, from which he never departed, in which he was wholly God even when he was still going to die. But the following words make this sense plain; for I added, saying: *the earlier ones are the other Apostles, through Christ still in part a man, that is, mortal; the last is the apostle Paul, through Christ now wholly God, that is, in every part immortal*[^328]*.* For this I said in expounding what the Apostle says: *not from men nor through a man, but through Jesus Christ and God the Father*[^329]*,* as though Jesus Christ were now not a man. For there follows: *who raised him from the dead,* so that from this it might appear why he said: *nor through a man*. Accordingly, on account of immortality, even now Christ God is not a man; but on account of the substance of human nature, in which *he ascended into heaven*, even now he is *the Mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus*[^330], since he will come in the same way as those who saw him going into heaven saw him[^331].

**2**. Likewise what I said: *the grace of God is that by which our sins are forgiven us so that we are reconciled to God, but peace is that by which we are reconciled to God*[^332]*,* is to be understood in such a way that we nevertheless know that both pertain to the general grace of God, just as in the people of God one thing is specially Israel, another Judah, and yet both generally Israel.

Likewise, when I was expounding: *what then? The law was set forth as the grace of transgression,* I thought it should be punctuated so that there is a question, *what then?* and then a response, *the law was set forth as the grace of transgression*[^333]*.* This indeed does not shrink from the truth; but a better punctuation seems to me this, that there is a question, *what then is the law?* And the response is brought in: *it was set forth for the sake of transgression*[^334].

But what I said: *most orderly, therefore, does he add: 'but if you are led by the Spirit, you are no longer under the law'*[^335]*, so that we understand those to be under the law whose spirit so lusts against the flesh that they do not do the things they will—that is, do not hold themselves unconquered in the love of justice, but are overcome by the flesh lusting against them*[^336]*,* arises from that understanding by which I thought that what was said—*the flesh lusts against the spirit, and the spirit against the flesh, for these are opposed to one another, so that you do not do the things that you would*[^337]—pertains to those who are *under the law,* not yet *under grace*.

For I had not yet understood that these words befit also those who are *under grace,* not *under the law,* for this reason: that they too, the concupiscences of the flesh against which they lust by the spirit, although they do not consent to them, would nevertheless wish not to have at all, if they could. And therefore they do not do whatever they will, because they will to lack them and cannot. For they will not have them then, when they will not have corruptible flesh either.

This book begins thus: *The reason for which the Apostle writes to the Galatians is this.*

## Chapter XXV (XXIV): Unfinished Exposition of the Letter to the Romans, One Book

I had undertaken an exposition of the Letter to the Romans too, as of the one to the Galatians. But if this work had been finished, there would have been many books. Of these I completed one in the disputation of the salutation alone, that is, from the beginning down to that place where he says: *grace to you and peace from God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ*[^338]. For it came about that we lingered, when we wished to solve a most difficult question that arose in our discourse, about the sin against the Holy Spirit, *which is not forgiven either in this age or in the age to come*[^339].

But then I ceased to add other volumes expounding the whole Letter, deterred by the magnitude and labor of the work itself, and I turned aside to other, easier things. So it came about that I left alone the book which I had made first, whose title I wished to be *Unfinished Exposition of the Letter to the Romans.*

There, what I said—that *grace* consists *in the remission of sins, but peace in reconciliation with God*[^340]*,* wherever I said this, is not to be understood as if peace itself and reconciliation do not pertain to general grace, but that by the name of grace he specially signified the remission of sins, just as we speak of the law both specially, according to what was said, *the Law and the Prophets*[^341], and generally, so that in it the Prophets too are included[^342].

This book begins thus: *In the Letter which Paul the Apostle wrote to the Romans.*

## Chapter XXVI (XXV): Miscellany of Eighty-three Questions, One Book

**1**. There is also among the things we have written a certain lengthy work, which is nevertheless reckoned as one book, whose title is *Miscellany of Eighty-three Questions.* But since they had been scattered over many sheets—because from the very first time of my conversion, after we came to Africa, they were dictated as I was questioned by the brothers, when they saw me at leisure, with no order kept by me—I, now a bishop, ordered them to be collected and one book made of them, with numbers added, so that whatever anyone wishes to read he may easily find.

**2**. The first of these questions is: *whether the soul is from itself.*

The second: *on free choice.*

The third: *whether man is made worse with God as the author.*

The fourth: *what the cause is that man is made worse.*

The fifth: *whether an irrational animal can be happy.*

The sixth: *on evil.*

The seventh: *what is properly called soul in a living being.*

The eighth: *whether the soul is moved by itself.*

The ninth: *whether truth can be perceived by the senses of the body.* In which, that which I said: *everything that the corporeal sense touches, which is also called sensible, is changed without any interval of time*[^343]*,* is without doubt not true in the incorruptible bodies of the resurrection; but now no sense of our body touches them, unless perhaps something of the kind is revealed by divine power.

The tenth: *whether the body is from God.*

The eleventh: *why Christ was born of a woman.*

The twelfth, in the place where the title is *the saying of a certain wise man,* is not mine. But because it became known through me to certain brothers, who were then collecting these things from me most diligently, and it pleased them, they wished to write it among ours. It is, however, by a certain Fonteius of Carthage, *On Cleansing the Mind for Seeing God*—which he wrote indeed as a pagan, but died a baptized Christian.

The thirteenth is: *by what evidence it is established that men excel beasts.*

The fourteenth: *that the body of our Lord Jesus Christ was not a phantasm.*

The fifteenth: *on the intellect.*

The sixteenth: *on the Son of God.*

The seventeenth: *on the knowledge of God.*

The eighteenth: *on the Trinity.*

The nineteenth: *on God and the creature.*

The twentieth: *on the place of God.*

The twenty-first: *whether God is not the author of evil.* Here it must be seen that what I said be not badly understood: *he is not the author of evil who is the Author of all things that exist, because in so far as they exist, in so far are they good*[^344]*,* and lest from this it be thought that the punishment of the evil is not from him, which is certainly evil to those who are punished. But I said this in the way in which it was said, *God did not make death*[^345], though elsewhere it is written, *death and life are from the Lord God*[^346]. The punishment of the evil, therefore, which is from God, is indeed evil to the evil, but it is among the good works of God, since it is just that the evil be punished, and everything that is just is certainly good.

The twenty-second is: *that God does not suffer necessity.*

The twenty-third is: *on the Father and the Son.* Here I said: *that he himself begot her by whom he is called wise, Wisdom*[^347]*;* but we treated this question better afterward in the book *On the Trinity*[^348].

The twenty-fourth is: *whether both sin and right action are in the free choice of the will.* That it is so is altogether most true; but that the will may be free for acting rightly, it is freed by the grace of God.

The twenty-fifth is: *on the cross of Christ.*

The twenty-sixth: *on the difference of sins.*

The twenty-seventh: *on providence.*

The twenty-eighth: *why God willed to make the world.*

The twenty-ninth: *whether there is anything up or down in the universe.*

The thirtieth: *whether all things were created for the use of man.*

The thirty-first is not mine either, but Cicero's[^349]. But because this too became known to the brothers through me, they wrote it among those things which they were collecting, wishing to know how the virtues of the mind were divided and defined by him.

The thirty-second is: *whether one person understands any thing more than another, and so the understanding of the same thing proceeds to infinity.*

The thirty-third: *on fear.*

The thirty-fourth: *whether nothing else is to be loved than to be free of fear.*

The thirty-fifth: *what is to be loved.* In which, that which I said: *that that is to be loved which is nothing other to have than to know*[^350]*,* I do not sufficiently approve. For they did not lack God to whom it was said: *do you not know that you are the temple of God, and the Spirit of God dwells in you?*[^351], and yet they did not know him, or did not know him as he is to be known. Likewise what I said: *no one, therefore, knows the happy life and is wretched*[^352]*; I said "knows"* in the way in which it is to be known. For who is wholly ignorant of it—at least among those who already use reason—since they know that they wish to be happy[^353]?

The thirty-sixth is: *on nourishing love.* Here I said: *God, therefore, and the mind, when they are loved, are properly called love most purified and consummated, if nothing else is loved*[^354]*.* But if this is true, how then does the Apostle say: *no one ever hated his own flesh*[^355], and from this admonishes that wives be loved? But it was said for this reason, *it is properly called love,* since the flesh is indeed loved, yet not properly, but on account of the soul to which it is subject for use. For even if it seems to be loved for its own sake, when we do not wish it to be deformed, its comeliness must be referred to something else—namely, to that from which all things are comely.

The thirty-seventh is: *on the ever-born.*

The thirty-eighth: *on the conformation of the soul.*

The thirty-ninth: *on foods.*

The fortieth: *since the nature of souls is one, whence come the diverse wills of men?*

The forty-first: *since God made all things, why did he not make them equal?*

The forty-second: *how the Wisdom of God, the Lord Jesus Christ, was both in his mother's womb and in heaven.*

The forty-third: *why the Son of God appeared in a man, and the Holy Spirit in a dove?*[^356]

The forty-fourth: *why the Lord Jesus Christ came so long afterward?* Here, when I was mentioning the ages of the human race as of one man, I said: *nor was it fitting that the Master come from God, by imitation of whom one might be formed unto the best morals, except at the time of youth*[^357]. And I added that to this purpose is what the Apostle says, that *under the law, as under a tutor, little ones were guarded*[^358]*.*

But it may give pause why elsewhere we said that Christ came in the sixth age of the human race, as in old age[^359]. This, then, which was said of youth, refers to the vigor and fervor of faith, *which works through love*[^360]; but that about old age refers to the number of the times. For both can be understood in the totality of men, which cannot be in the ages of individuals; just as in the body there cannot be at once both youth and old age, but in the mind there can—the one on account of alacrity, the other on account of gravity.

The forty-sixth: *on the ideas.*

The forty-seventh: *whether we shall ever be able to see our own thoughts.* Here, what I said: *that angelic bodies, such as we hope to have, are to be believed most luminous and ethereal*[^361]*,* if this is taken without the members which we now have and without the substance—though incorruptible—of flesh, is in error. But this question, about seeing our thoughts, was treated much better in the work *The City of God*[^362].

The forty-eighth is: *on things to be believed.*

The forty-ninth: *why the children of Israel sacrificed visibly the victims of cattle.*

The fiftieth: *on the equality of the Son.*

The fifty-first: *on man made to the image and likeness of God*[^363]. Here, what is it that I said: *man is not rightly called man without life*, when even the corpse of a man is called a man? Therefore I ought at least to have said, "is not properly called," where I said, *is not rightly called.* Likewise I said: *nor is it unskillfully distinguished that the image and likeness of God is one thing, and "to the image and likeness of God"—as we understand man to have been made—another*[^364]. This is not to be understood as though man is not called the image of God, since the Apostle says: *a man indeed ought not to veil his head, since he is the image and glory of God*[^365], but he is also said to be made "to the image" of God, which the Only-Begotten is not said to be, who is only the image, not "to the image."

The fifty-second: *on that which was said: 'it repents me that I made man'*[^366].

The fifty-third: *on the gold and silver which the Israelites received from the Egyptians*[^367]*.*

The fifty-fourth: *on that which is written: 'but for me it is good to cling to God'*[^368]*.* Here, what I said: *but that which is better than every soul, this we call God*[^369]*,* ought rather to have been said, "better than every created spirit."

The fifty-fifth: *on that which is written: 'there are sixty queens, eighty concubines, and young maidens of whom there is no number'*[^370].

The fifty-sixth: *on the forty-six years of the building of the Temple*[^371].

The fifty-seventh: *on the hundred and fifty-three fish*[^372].

The fifty-eighth: *on John the Baptist.*

The fifty-ninth: *on the ten virgins*[^373].

The sixtieth: *'but of the day and the hour no one knows, neither the angels of heaven nor the Son of man, but the Father alone'*[^374].

The sixty-first: *on that which is written in the Gospel, that the Lord fed the crowds on the mountain from five loaves*[^375]*.* Here, what I said: *that the two fish signify those two persons, namely the royal and the priestly, to whom also that most sacred anointing pertained*[^376], ought rather to have been said: "*pertained* especially," since we read that the Prophets too were sometimes anointed[^377]. Likewise what I said: *Luke, who presented Christ as a priest ascending after the abolition of sins, ascended through Nathan to David, because Nathan was the prophet sent, by whose rebuke David obtained the abolition of that very sin by repenting*[^378], is not to be understood as though that Nathan who was the son of David was himself the prophet[^379], because here too it was not said, "because he himself was the prophet sent," but it was said, *because Nathan was the prophet sent*[^380]*,* so that the mystery is understood not in the same man but in the same name.

The sixty-second is: *on that which is written in the Gospel: 'that Jesus baptized more than John, although he himself did not baptize, but his disciples'*[^381]*.* Here, what I said: *that robber to whom it was said, 'amen I say to you, today you shall be with me in paradise'*[^382]*, who had not received baptism itself*[^383]*,* this indeed we find that others before us, rulers of the holy Church, set down in their writings[^384]; but by what evidence it can be sufficiently shown that that robber was not baptized, I do not know. On this matter it is treated more diligently in certain of our later little works, especially in that which we wrote to Vincentius Victor, *On the Origin of the Soul*[^385].

The sixty-third: *on the Word.*

The sixty-fourth: *on the Samaritan woman*[^386].

The sixty-fifth: *on the resurrection of Lazarus*[^387].

The sixty-sixth: *on that which is written: 'or are you ignorant, brethren (for I speak to those who know the law), that the law has dominion over a man as long as he lives?'*[^388] down to that place where it is written: *he will give life also to your mortal bodies through his Spirit dwelling in you*[^389]. Here, wishing to expound that which the Apostle says: *we know that the law is spiritual, but I am carnal*[^390], I said: *that is, I consent to the flesh, not yet freed by spiritual grace*[^391]. This is not to be understood as though the spiritual man, already established under grace, cannot say this even of himself, and the rest down to that place where it was said: *wretched man that I am, who will free me from the body of this death?*[^392]—which I afterward learned, as I have already confessed before[^393]. Again, expounding that which the Apostle says, *the body indeed is dead because of sin*[^394], I said: *he calls the body dead so long as it is such that the want of temporal things troubles the soul*[^395]. But it seemed to me afterward much better[^396] that the body was called dead because it now has the necessity of dying, which it did not have before sin.

The sixty-seventh: *on that which is written: 'for I reckon that the sufferings of this time are not worthy of the future glory which shall be revealed in us'*[^397] *down to that which was said: 'for by hope we were saved'*[^398]*.* Here, when I was expounding that which is written, *and the creation itself shall be freed from the servitude of corruption*[^399]*,* I said: *'and the creation itself,' that is, man himself, when, the seal of the image having now been lost because of sin, he remained only a creature*[^400]*.* This is not to be understood as though man lost the whole of what he had of the image of God.

For if he had lost it altogether, there would be no reason why it should be said: *be reformed in the newness of your mind*[^401], and: *we are transformed into the same image*[^402]; but again, if he had lost the whole, nothing would remain from which it should be said: *although man walks in an image, yet he is troubled in vain*[^403].

Likewise what I said: *that the highest angels live spiritually, but the lowest live animally*[^404]*,* was said more boldly of the lowest than can be shown either by the holy Scriptures or by the things themselves, because even if perhaps it can, it can only with the greatest difficulty.

The sixty-eighth: *on that which is written: 'O man, who are you to answer back to God?'*[^405] Here I said: *that even if anyone, by lighter sins, or certainly by however graver and many sins, has nevertheless, by great groaning and grief of repenting, become worthy of the mercy of God, it is not of him—who, if he were left, would perish—but of God who shows mercy, who comes to the aid of his prayers and griefs. For it is too little to will, unless God shows mercy; but God, who calls to peace, does not show mercy unless the will has gone before unto peace*[^406]. This was said after repentance. For there is a mercy of God preceding even the will itself, which, if it were not, *the will would not be prepared by the Lord*[^407]. To that mercy belongs the calling itself, which precedes even faith[^408]. Treating of which a little later, I said[^409]: *but this calling, which works—whether in individual men or in peoples and in the human race itself—through the opportunities of the times, is of a high and deep ordering. To which pertains also that: 'in the womb I sanctified you'*[^410]*, and: 'when you were in the loins of your father I saw you'*[^411]*; and: 'Jacob I loved, but Esau I hated'*[^412]*,* and the rest; although that testimony, *when you were in the loins of your father I saw you,* whence it occurred to me as though it were Scripture, I do not know.

The sixty-ninth: *on that which is written: 'then the Son himself also will be subject to him who subjected all things to him'*[^413].

The seventieth: *on that which the Apostle says: 'death is swallowed up in victory. Where, O death, is your contention? Where, O death, is your sting? But the sting of death is sin, and the power of sin is the law'*[^414].

The seventy-first: *on that which is written: 'bear one another's burdens, and so you will fulfill the law of Christ'*[^415].

The seventy-fifth: *on the inheritance of God.*

The seventy-sixth: *on that which the apostle James says: 'but do you wish to know, O vain man, that faith without works is idle?'*[^418]

The seventy-seventh: *on fear, whether it is a sin.*

## Chapter XXVII (XXVI): Lying, One Book

Likewise I wrote the book *Lying,* which, even if it is understood with some labor, has nonetheless a not useless exercise of wit and mind, and is rather profitable for forming morals toward loving truthful speech. This one too I had decided to remove from my little works, because it seemed to me both obscure and convoluted and altogether troublesome, for which reason I had not even published it. Then, when I had afterward written another, whose title is *Against Lying,* I had decided, and ordered, much more that this one should not exist; but it was not done. And so, in this review of my little works, when I found it safe, I directed that this one too, once reviewed, should remain, especially because there are in it certain necessary things which are not in that other one. But the title of that one is *Against Lying;* of this one, *Lying,* because throughout that one there is an open assault on lying, but a great part of this one is engaged in the disputation of inquiry. Yet both are directed to the same end.
