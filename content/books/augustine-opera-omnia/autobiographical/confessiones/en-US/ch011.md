# Book 10: After God has been sought and known

## With what profit the author now confesses what he is

### Chapter 1: God is his hope

**1**. *Let me know* you, my knower; *let me know, even as I am known*[^1]. Strength of my soul, enter into it and fit it to yourself, that you may have and possess it *without spot or wrinkle*[^2]. This is my hope; therefore I speak, and in that hope I rejoice[^3], whenever I rejoice soundly.

But the other things of this life are the less to be wept over the more they are wept over, and the more to be wept over the less they are wept over amid them. For behold, *you have loved the truth*[^4], since *he who does it comes to the light*[^5]. I wish to do it in my heart before you, in confession, and with my pen before many witnesses.

### Chapter 2: He is already manifest to the Lord

**2**. And to you, indeed, Lord, to whose eyes the abyss of human conscience lies *bare*[^6], what could be hidden in me, even if I were unwilling to confess it to you[^7]? For I would be hiding you from myself, not myself from you. But now, since my groaning is witness that I am displeasing to myself, you shine forth and are pleasing and are loved and longed for, so that I blush over myself and cast myself off and choose you, and please neither you nor myself except in you.

To you, then, Lord, I am manifest, whatever I am. And with what profit I confess to you, I have said. Nor do I do it with words of the flesh and with sounds, but with the words of the soul and the cry of my thought, which your ear knows. For when I am wicked, to confess to you is nothing other than to be displeasing to myself; but when I am devout, to confess to you is nothing other than not to attribute this to myself, since *you, Lord, bless the righteous*[^8], but first you justify him while he is ungodly[^9].

And so my *confession*, my God, *in your sight*[^10], is made to you both silently and not silently. For it is silent in noise, but cries out in feeling. For I say nothing right to people that you have not first heard from me, nor do you hear anything of the kind from me that you have not first  
said to me.

### Chapter 3: He confesses to the Lord, that people may hear

**3**. What, then, have I to do with people, that they should hear my confessions, as if they themselves were going to heal all my *infirmities*[^11]? A race curious to know another's life, slothful to correct their own. Why do they seek to hear from me what I am, who do not wish to hear from you what they are? And how do they know, when they hear from me about myself, whether I am telling the truth, since *no one among men knows what goes on in a man except the spirit of the man, which is in him*[^12]?

But if they hear from you about themselves, they will not be able to say, "The Lord lies." For what is it to hear from you about oneself but to know oneself? And who knows himself and says, "It is false," unless he himself is lying?

But because *love believes all things*[^13]—at least among those whom, bound together, it makes one—I too, Lord, confess to you in such a way that people may hear, to whom I cannot prove whether I confess truly; but those whose ears love opens to me believe me.

#### He has confessed what he was; but now, what he is

**4**. But you, my inmost physician, make clear to me with what profit I do these things. For the confessions of my past evils, which you have forgiven and covered[^14], that you might make me blessed in you, changing my soul by faith and your sacrament—when they are read and heard, they rouse the heart, lest it sleep in despair and say, "I cannot," but rather awaken in the love of your mercy[^15] and the sweetness of your grace, by which everyone who is weak is made strong[^16], since through it he is made aware of his own weakness. And it delights the good to hear of the past evils of those who are now free of them; nor does it delight them because they are evils, but because they were and are not.

With what profit, then, my Lord—to whom my conscience daily confesses, more secure in the hope of your mercy than in its own innocence—with what profit, I ask, do I confess to people too, in your presence, by these writings, still what I am, not what I was? For that other profit I have seen and recalled.

But what I still am, behold, at this very time of my confessions—and many desire to know this, both those who have known me and those who have not known me, who have heard something from me or about me, but their ear is not at my heart, where I am whatever I am. They wish, then, to hear, as I confess, what I myself am within, where they can direct neither eye nor ear nor mind; yet they wish to believe—will they perhaps know? For the love by which they are good tells them that I do not lie in confessing about myself, and that very love in them believes me.

### Chapter 4: Let his readers breathe relief at his good, sigh over his evil

**5**. But with what profit do they wish it? Do they desire to congratulate me when they hear how near to you I come by your gift, and to pray for me when they hear how much I am held back by my own weight? To such I will disclose myself. For it is no small profit, Lord my God, *that thanks may be given to you by many on our behalf*[^17], and that you may be entreated by many for us.

Let a brotherly mind love in me what you teach is to be loved, and grieve in me over what you teach is to be grieved. Let a brotherly mind do this, not a stranger's, not that of *alien sons, whose mouth has spoken vanity, and whose right hand is a right hand of iniquity*[^18], but that brotherly mind which, when it approves me, rejoices over me, and when it disapproves me, is saddened for me, because whether it approves or disapproves me, it loves me.

To such I will disclose myself: let them breathe relief at my good, sigh over my evil. My good things are your institutions and your gifts; my evil things are my offenses and your judgments. Let them breathe relief at the former and sigh over the latter, and let a hymn and a weeping ascend into your sight from brotherly hearts, your censers[^19]. And you, Lord, delighted by the fragrance of your holy temple, *have mercy on me according to your great mercy*[^20], for your name's sake[^21]; and, in no way deserting what you have begun, complete what is imperfect in me[^22].

**6**. This is the fruit of my confessions—not what I was, but what I am—that I may confess this not only before you, with a secret exultation and trembling[^23] and a secret sorrow mixed with hope, but also in the ears of the believing sons of men[^24], the companions of my joy and the sharers of my mortality, my fellow citizens and fellow pilgrims, those who go before and those who follow and the comrades of my way. These are your servants, my brethren, whom you have willed to be your sons, my masters, whom you have commanded me to serve, if I wish to live with you, from you.

And this word of yours would have been too little for me, had it commanded by speaking only, and not gone before by doing[^25]. And I do this by deeds and by words; I do it under your wings[^26]—with exceedingly great peril, were it not that under your wings my soul is made subject to you[^27] and my weakness is known to you. I am a little one, but my Father lives forever, and my guardian is sufficient for me; for he is the same[^28] who begot me[^29] and watches over me; and you yourself are all my good things, you the Almighty, who are with me even before I am with you.

To such, then, as you command me to serve, I will disclose not what I was, but what I now am and what I still am; *yet I do not even judge myself*[^30]. And so let me be heard.

### Chapter 5: The Lord alone knows all things about a man

**7**. For you, Lord, judge me; because, although *no one among men knows the things of a man except the spirit of the man, which is in him*[^31], yet there is something of a man which not even the spirit of the man, which is in him, knows; but you, Lord, know[^32] all his things, you who made him. And I, although in your sight I despise myself and reckon myself *earth and ashes*[^33], yet know something of you that I do not know of myself. And surely *we now see through a mirror, in an enigma, not yet face to face*[^34]; and therefore, as long as I am a pilgrim away from you[^35], I am more present to myself than to you; and yet I know that you can in no way be violated; but I do not know which temptations I am able to resist and which I am not.

And there is hope, because *you are faithful, who do not let us be tempted beyond what we can bear, but with the temptation make also a way out, that we may be able to endure*[^36]. Let me confess, then, what I know of myself; let me confess too what I do not know of myself, since what I know of myself I know by your shining upon me, and what I do not know of myself I do not know until *my darkness becomes as noonday*[^37] in your countenance[^38].

## God is sought

### Chapter 6: What is loved, when God is loved?

**8**. With no doubtful, but with a certain awareness, Lord, I love you. You struck my heart with your word, and I loved you. But heaven also, and earth, and all that is in them—behold, on every side they tell me to love you, nor do they cease to tell it to all, *that they may be without excuse*[^39].

But more deeply, *you will have mercy on whom you will have mercy, and will show compassion to whom you will be compassionate*[^40]; otherwise heaven and earth speak your praises to the deaf[^41].

But what do I love when I love you? Not the beauty of a body, nor the comeliness of time; not the brightness of light, dear as it is to these eyes of mine; not the sweet melodies of songs of every kind; not the fragrance of flowers and ointments and spices; not manna and honey; not limbs welcome to the embraces of the flesh: it is not these I love when I love my God. And yet I do love a certain light and a certain voice and a certain fragrance and a certain food and a certain embrace when I love my God—the light, the voice, the fragrance, the food, the embrace of my inner man, where there shines for my soul what no place contains, and where there sounds what no time snatches away, and where there is a fragrance that no breeze scatters, and where there is a savor that no eating diminishes, and where there clings what no satiety tears away. This is what I love when I love my God.

#### God is to be sought above all bodies

> **9**. And what is this[^42]?  
> I asked the earth,  
> and it said, "I am not he";  
> and whatever is in it  
> confessed the same.  
> I asked the sea and the deeps[^43]  
> and the creeping things of living souls[^44],  
> and they answered:  
> "We are not your God; seek above us."  
> I asked the blowing breezes,  
> and the whole air with its inhabitants said:  
> "Anaximenes is mistaken; I am not God."  
> I asked the sky, the sun, the moon, the stars:  
> "Neither are we the God whom you seek," they said.  
> And I said to all the things  
> that stand about the doors of my flesh:  
> "Tell me of my God—  
> since you are not he—tell me something of him."  
> And they cried out with a great voice:  
> *He made us*[^45].  
> My questioning was my attention;  
> and their answer was their appearance.

And I turned myself to myself and said to myself: "Who are you?" And I answered: "A man." And behold, body and soul are at hand in me, the one outer and the other inner. Which of these is the one by which I ought to have sought my God, whom I had already sought through the body, from earth up to heaven, as far as I could send the rays of my eyes as messengers? But the inner is better. For to it all the bodily messengers reported back, as it presided and judged over the answers of heaven and earth and of all that is in them, saying: "We are not God," and: "He made us."

The inner man[^46] came to know these things through the ministry of the outer; I, the inner, came to know them—I, I the mind, through the senses of my body. I asked the mass of the world about my God, and it answered me: "I am not he, but he made me."

**10**. Does not this appearance show itself to all whose senses are whole? Why does it not speak the same to all? Animals small and great see it, but they cannot ask. For in them reason is not set as a judge over the senses that report. But human beings can ask, so that they may behold *the invisible things of God, understood through the things that have been made*[^47]; but by love they are made subject to them, and, being subject, they cannot judge.

And these things do not answer those who ask unless they also judge; nor do they change their voice—that is, their appearance—if one person merely sees while another, seeing, also asks, so that it should appear one way to the one and another way to the other; but, appearing in the same way to both, it is mute to the one and speaks to the other. Rather, it speaks to all, but those understand who compare its voice, received outwardly, with the truth within.

For the Truth says to me: "Your God is not earth and heaven, nor any body." Their nature says this. They see it: a mass is less in a part than in the whole. Now you are better, I say to you, my soul, since you enliven the mass of your body, giving it life, which no body can furnish to a body. But your God is, even for you, the life of life.

### Chapter 7: God is to be sought above the soul and the senses

**11**. What, then, do I love when I love my God? Who is he above the head of my soul? Through my soul itself I will ascend to him. I will pass beyond that power of mine by which I cling to the body and fill its frame with life. By that power I do not find my God; for the *horse and mule, which have no understanding*[^48], would find him too, and it is the same power by which their bodies also live.

There is another power, not only by which I give life, but also by which I give sense to my flesh, which the Lord fashioned for me, commanding the eye not to hear, and the ear not to see[^49], but the one to be that through which I see, the other that through which I hear; and to the other senses, each in its place and office, their own proper functions: these diverse things I, one mind, do through them. I will pass beyond this power of mine too; for the horse and mule have this also, since they too perceive through the body.

## The fields of memory

### Chapter 8: The treasuries of memory:

**12**. I will pass beyond this power of my nature too, ascending by degrees to him who made me; and I come into the fields and broad palaces of memory, where are the treasuries of innumerable images, brought in from things of every kind perceived by the senses.

There is stored away whatever we also think, whether by enlarging or diminishing or in any way varying the things the senses have touched, and whatever else has been entrusted and laid up that forgetfulness has not yet swallowed and buried.

There, when I am present, I demand that whatever I wish be brought forth, and some things come out at once, some are sought longer and are dug out, as it were, from certain more hidden recesses; some rush out in a throng, and, while something else is being sought and asked for, they leap into the midst as if to say: "Is it perhaps we?"

And I drive them away with the hand of my heart from the face of my remembrance, until what I wish is unclouded and comes forth into view from its hiding places.

Other things are supplied easily and in an undisturbed sequence, just as they are demanded, and the earlier give way to those that follow, and in giving way are stored up, to come forth again when I will. All this happens when I recount something from memory.

#### a) the images of things perceived by the senses

**13**. There all things are kept, distinct and by kind, which were brought in each by its own entrance: light and all colors and the forms of bodies through the eyes; through the ears, all kinds of sounds; all odors through the entrance of the nostrils; all flavors through the entrance of the mouth; and, by the sense of the whole body, what is hard, what is soft, what is hot or cold, smooth or rough, heavy or light, whether outside or inside the body. All these the great recess of memory receives, to be recalled and reconsidered when there is need, along with its secret and ineffable folds, I know not which; and all these enter into it, each by its own door, and are laid up in it.

And yet the things themselves do not enter; rather, the images of the things perceived are there at hand for the thought that remembers them. Who can say how these images were fashioned, though it is plain by which senses they were seized and stored away within?

For even while I dwell in darkness and silence, I bring forth colors in my memory, if I wish, and distinguish between white and black and between any others I wish; nor do sounds rush in and disturb what I am considering, drawn in through the eyes, although they too are there and lie hidden, as it were stored apart.

For these too I demand, if I please, and they are present at once; and with my tongue at rest and my throat silent I sing as much as I will, and those images of colors, which are there nonetheless, do not interpose themselves or interrupt, while another treasure is being handled, one that flowed in from the ears.

So too the other things, which were brought in and heaped up through the other senses, I recall as I please, and I distinguish the scent of lilies from that of violets while smelling nothing, and I prefer honey to boiled wine, the smooth to the rough, while then tasting and touching nothing, but only by remembering.

#### b) all things, whether experienced by himself or believed

**14**. These things I do within, in the vast hall of my memory. For there heaven and earth and sea are present to me, with all that I could perceive in them, except those things I have forgotten. There I meet even myself and recall myself—what, when, and where I did something, and in what way I was affected when I did it. There are all the things I remember, whether experienced by me or believed.

From the same abundance I myself also weave together, with things past, likenesses of things—either experienced, or believed on the basis of those I have experienced—now these, now those; and from these I likewise weave future actions and events and hopes, and all these I again contemplate as if present.

"I will do this and that," I say to myself in that very vast fold of my mind, full of the images of so many and so great things, and this or that follows. "Oh, if only this or that were so!" "God avert this or that!"—I say these things to myself, and, as I say them, the images of all that I say are at hand from the same treasury of memory, nor could I say any of them at all if they were absent.

#### The great power of memory

**15**. Great is this power of memory, exceedingly great, my God—a vast and boundless inner chamber. Who has reached its bottom? And this is a power of my mind, and belongs to my nature, yet I myself do not grasp the whole of what I am. Therefore the mind is too narrow to hold itself; so where is that part of itself which it does not hold? Is it outside it and not in it? How, then, does it not hold it?

Great wonder rises in me over this; amazement lays hold of me. And people go to marvel at the heights of mountains and the huge waves of the sea and the broad courses of rivers and the circuit of the Ocean and the revolutions of the stars—and they pass by

themselves, and do not marvel that, when I was speaking of all these things, I was not seeing them with my eyes; and yet I could not speak of them unless, within my memory, I were seeing the mountains and waves and rivers and stars, which I have seen, and the ocean, which I have believed in, over spaces just as vast as if I were seeing them outside.

And yet I did not swallow them up by seeing them, when I saw them with my eyes; nor are they themselves present with me, but their images; and I know by which sense of the body each was impressed upon me.

### Chapter 9: c) all that has been learned from the liberal disciplines

**16**. But this immense capacity of my memory does not carry those things alone. Here too are all those things that, learned from the liberal disciplines, have not yet slipped away, removed as it were to an inner place that is not a place; nor do I carry their images, but the things themselves.

For what literature is, what skill in disputation is, how many kinds of questions there are—whatever of these I know is in my memory in such a way that I have not retained an image while leaving the thing outside, nor that it sounded and passed away, like a voice impressed through the ears by a trace by which it could be recalled, as if it sounded when it no longer sounded;

nor as an odor, while it passes and vanishes into the winds, affects the sense of smell, from which it conveys into the memory an image of itself, which we recall by remembering; nor as food, which certainly no longer has taste in the belly and yet, as it were, has taste in the memory; nor as something perceived by touching with the body, which the memory imagines even when it is separated from us.

For these things are not admitted into it; rather, their images alone are seized with marvelous speed and laid up in marvelous cells, as it were, and brought forth marvelously by remembering.

### Chapter 10: From where, and by what way, do learning and knowledge enter the memory?

**17**. But when I hear that there are three kinds of questions—whether a thing is, what it is, of what kind it is—I do indeed hold the images of the sounds of which these words are made, and I know that they passed through the air with a noise and now are no more.

But the things themselves, which are signified by those sounds, I have touched with no bodily sense, nor seen anywhere except in my mind; and I have stored away in my memory not their images, but the things themselves. Let them say, if they can, from where they entered into me. For I run through all the doors of my flesh and do not find by which of them they came in.

For the eyes say: "If they are colored, we announced them"; the ears say: "If they sounded, they were reported by us"; the nostrils say: "If they had an odor, they passed through us"; the sense of taste also says: "If there is no flavor, ask me nothing"; touch says: "If it is not a body, I have not handled it; if I have not handled it, I have not reported it." From where, and by what way, did these things enter into my memory? I do not know how; for when I learned them, I did not believe them on another's heart, but recognized them in my own, and approved them as true, and entrusted them to it, as it were laying them up, to bring them forth when I wished. There, then, they were even before I learned them, but they were not in my memory.

Where, then, or why, when they were spoken, did I recognize them and say, "It is so, it is true," unless because they were already in my memory, but so far off and thrust back, as it were in more hidden caverns, that, unless they had been dug out at someone's prompting, I might perhaps not have been able to think them?

### Chapter 11: Why thought (*cogitatio*) is named from gathering (*cogere*)

**18**. Wherefore we find that to learn these things—whose images we do not draw in through the senses, but which we discern within, by themselves, without images, as they are—is nothing else than, by thinking, to gather together, as it were, and, by attending, to take care that the things which the memory contained scattered and unarranged should now, as if placed ready to hand in that same memory where before they lay hidden, scattered and neglected, easily present themselves to a now-familiar attention.

And how many things of this kind my memory carries, which have already been found and, as I said, placed as it were ready to hand—things we are said to have learned and to know. If I cease to recall them at modest intervals of time, they sink down again and slip away, as it were, into more remote inner chambers, so that they must be thought out anew, as if new, from that same place again (for there is no other region of them) and gathered together again, that they may be known—that is, collected, as it were, from a kind of dispersion; from which "to think" (*cogitare*) is named. For "I gather" (*cogo*) and "I think" (*cogito*) are related as "I do" (*ago*) and "I keep doing" (*agito*), "I make" (*facio*) and "I make repeatedly" (*factito*). Yet the mind has claimed this word as properly its own, so that not what is gathered elsewhere, but what is gathered—that is, collected—in the mind is now properly said to be thought.

### Chapter 12: d) the principles and laws of numbers and dimensions

**19**. Likewise the memory contains the innumerable principles and laws of numbers and dimensions, none of which a bodily sense has impressed, since they themselves are neither colored nor make a sound nor have an odor nor have been tasted or handled. I have heard the sounds of the words by which they are signified, when these things are discussed; but those sounds are one thing, and these are another. For the sounds are one thing in Greek, another in Latin, but these are neither Greek nor Latin nor any other kind of speech.

I have seen the lines drawn by craftsmen, even the finest, like a spider's thread; but those geometric lines are another thing—they are not the images of the ones which the eye of the flesh reported to me: anyone knows them who has recognized them within, without any thought of a body of any kind.

I have also perceived, with all the senses of the body, the numbers we count; but those by which we count are another thing, and they are not the images of these, and therefore they truly are.

Let him who does not see them laugh at me for saying these things, and let me grieve over him who laughs at me.

### Chapter 13: e) how all these things have been known

**20**. All these things I hold in memory, and how I learned them I hold in memory. Many things too that are most falsely argued against them I have heard and hold in memory; and although these are false, yet that I remember them is not false;

and that I distinguished between those truths and these falsehoods which are said against them—this too I remember; and I see that I distinguish these things now in one way, but I remember that I often distinguished them in another way, when I often thought about them.

Therefore I remember that I have more often understood these things; and what I now discern and understand, I store away in memory, that afterward I may remember that I understood it now.

Therefore I remember also that I remember; just as afterward, if I shall recall that I was able now to remember these things, I shall surely recall it through the power of memory.

### Chapter 14: f) the affections of the mind

**21**. The affections of my mind too the same memory contains—not in the way that the mind itself has them when it undergoes them, but in another, far different way, according to the manner of the power of memory. For I remember that I have been glad without being glad, and I recall my past sadness without being sad, and I call to mind that I once feared, without fear, and I am mindful of a former desire without desire.

Sometimes, on the contrary, I remember my past sadness with gladness, and my gladness with sadness. As to the body, this is not to be wondered at; for the mind is one thing, the body another. And so, if I joyfully remember a past pain of the body, that is not so wonderful.

But here, since the mind is also the memory itself (for when we charge someone to keep something in memory, we say, "See that you hold that in mind," and when we forget, we say, "It was not in mind" and "It slipped from mind," calling the memory itself the mind)—since, then, it is so, what is this[^50]: that, when I joyfully remember my past sadness, the mind has gladness and the memory sadness, and the mind is glad because gladness is in it, while the memory is not sad because of the sadness that is in it? Does the memory perhaps not belong to the mind? Who would say so?

Doubtless, then, the memory is, as it were, the stomach of the mind, while gladness and sadness are, as it were, sweet and bitter food; when they are entrusted to the memory, they can be stored there, as if passed over into the stomach, but they cannot be tasted. It is ridiculous to think these like those, and yet they are not in every way unlike.

**22**. But behold, I bring forth from memory when I say that there are four perturbations of the mind: desire, gladness, fear, sadness[^51]; and whatever I can argue about these, by dividing each into the species of its own kind and by defining, there I find what to say, and from there I bring it forth; and yet I am disturbed by none of those perturbations when I call them to mind by remembering; and before they were recalled and reconsidered by me, they were there; for that reason they could be drawn out from there by recollection.

Perhaps, then, just as food is brought up from the stomach by chewing the cud, so these things are brought forth from memory by recollection. Why, then, is the sweetness of gladness or the bitterness of sorrow not tasted in the mouth of thought by the one arguing—that is, by the one remembering? Or is it unlike in this, that it is not like in every respect? For who would willingly speak of such things, if, as often as we name sadness or fear, we were compelled to grieve or to be afraid?

And yet we would not speak of them, did we not find in our memory not only the sounds of the names according to the images impressed by the senses of the body, but also the notions of the things themselves, which we received by no door of the flesh; rather, the mind itself, perceiving them through the experience of its own passions, entrusted them to the memory, or the memory retained these for itself even though they were not entrusted to it.

### Chapter 15: It is hard to say whether we remember all things through images

**23**. But whether through images or not, who could easily say? For I name a stone, I name the sun, when the things themselves are not present to my senses; yet in my memory their images are at hand.

I name a bodily pain, and it is not present to me while nothing hurts; yet unless its image were present in my memory, I would not know what I was saying, nor in arguing would I distinguish it from pleasure.

I name bodily health while I am sound in body; the thing itself is indeed present to me; yet unless its image were also present in my memory, I would in no way recall what the sound of this name signified, nor would the sick, when health was named, recognize what was said, unless that same image were held by the power of memory, although the thing itself were absent from their body.

I name the numbers by which we count; and there are present in my memory not their images, but the numbers themselves.

I name the image of the sun, and this is present in my memory; for I recall not an image of its image, but the image itself: it is at hand for me as I remember.

I name memory, and I recognize what I name. And where do I recognize it but in memory itself? Is it too present to itself through its own image, and not through itself?

### Chapter 16: g) forgetfulness itself

**24**. What of this: when I name forgetfulness, and likewise recognize what I name, from where would I recognize it, unless I remembered it? I do not mean the very sound of the name, but the thing it signifies; and if I had forgotten this thing, I would surely be unable to recognize what that sound meant.

Therefore, when I remember memory, memory itself is present to itself through itself; but when I remember forgetfulness, both memory and forgetfulness are present—the memory by which I remember, the forgetfulness which I remember. But what is forgetfulness but the privation of memory? How, then, is it present, that I should remember it, since, when it is present, I cannot remember?

But if we retain by memory what we remember, and if, unless we remembered forgetfulness, we could in no way recognize the thing signified by that name when it was heard—then forgetfulness is retained by memory. It is present, then, lest we forget; yet, when it is present, we forget.

Or is it to be understood from this that forgetfulness is not present in the memory through itself, when we remember it, but through its image—since, if forgetfulness were present through itself, it would bring it about not that we remember, but that we forget? And who, in the end, will track this down? Who will comprehend how it can be?

**25**. I, certainly, Lord, labor here, and labor in myself: I have become to myself a soil of difficulty and too much sweat[^52]. For we are not now scanning the regions of the sky[^53] or measuring the distances of the stars or seeking the balancings of the earth; it is I who remember, I, the mind. It is not so wonderful if whatever I am not is far from me; but what is nearer to me than I myself? And behold, the power of my memory is not comprehended by me, though I cannot even speak of myself apart from it. For what shall I say, when it is certain to me that I remember forgetfulness? Shall I say that what I remember is not in my memory? Or shall I say that forgetfulness is in my memory for this purpose, that I may not forget? Both are most absurd.

What of that third option? How shall I say that the image of forgetfulness is held by my memory, and not forgetfulness itself, when I remember it? How shall I say this either, since, when the image of any thing is impressed on the memory, it is first necessary that the thing itself be present, from which that image could be impressed?

For thus I remember Carthage, thus all the places where I have been, thus the faces of people I have seen, and the reports of the other senses, thus the health or pain of the body itself; when these were present, the memory took images from them, which I might gaze upon as present and reconsider in my mind, when I remembered them even in their absence.

If, then, forgetfulness is held in the memory through its image and not through itself, it must surely have been present, that its image might be taken. But when it was present, how did it inscribe its image in the memory, since forgetfulness, by its very presence, erases even what it finds already noted down?

And yet, in whatever way—though that way be incomprehensible and inexplicable—I am certain that I remember even forgetfulness itself, by which what we remember is buried.

### Chapter 17: God is to be sought above memory

**26**. Great is the power of memory—something awe-inspiring, my God, a deep and boundless manifoldness; and this is the mind, and this I myself am. What, then, am I, my God? What is my nature? A life varied, manifold, and exceedingly immense.

Behold, in the fields and caves and innumerable caverns of my memory, innumerably full of innumerable kinds of things—whether through images, as of all bodies; or through their presence, as of the disciplines; or through certain notions or notations, I know not what, as of the affections of the mind (which the memory holds even when the mind does not undergo them, since whatever is in the memory is in the mind)—

through all these I run about and flit this way and that, and I penetrate as far as I can, and there is no end anywhere; so great is the power of memory, so great is the power of life in a man living mortally!

What, then, shall I do, you my true life, my God? I will pass beyond this power of mine too, which is called memory; I will pass beyond it, that I may press on to you, *sweet light*[^54]. What do you say to me? Behold, ascending through my mind to you, who abide above me, I will pass beyond this power of mine too, which is called memory, wishing to reach you where you can be reached, and to cling to you where you can be clung to.

For beasts and birds also have memory; otherwise they would not return to their dens and nests, nor to many other things to which they grow accustomed; for they could not grow accustomed to anything except through memory. I will pass beyond memory, then, to reach him who separated me from the four-footed beasts and made me wiser than the birds of the air. I will pass beyond memory too—but where shall I find you, truly good one, sure sweetness? Where shall I find you? If I find you beyond my memory, I am unmindful of you. And how shall I now find you, if I am not mindful of you?

### Chapter 18: Memory and forgetfulness

**27**. For the woman had lost a drachma and sought it with a lamp, and unless she had remembered it, she would not have found it[^55]. For when it was found, how would she know whether it was the one, if she did not remember it? I remember having sought and found many things that were lost. From this I know it, because, when I was seeking one of them and it was said to me, "Is it perhaps this?" "Is it perhaps that?" I kept saying, "It is not," until what I was seeking was offered to me. And unless I remembered it—whatever it was—even if it were offered to me, I would not find it, because I would not recognize it.

And so it always happens when we seek and find something lost. Yet if something is lost to the eyes, but not to the memory—any visible body, say—its image is held within and is sought until it is restored to sight. And when it is found, it is recognized from the image that is within. Nor do we say that we have found what was lost[^56] if we do not recognize it; nor can we recognize it if we do not remember it; but this had been lost, indeed, to the eyes, while it was held by the memory.

### Chapter 19: On the things the memory loses

**28**. What of this? When the memory itself loses something, as happens when we forget and seek to recollect, where, in the end, do we seek but in the memory itself?

And there, if one thing happens to be offered for another, we reject it until the one we seek comes to mind. And when it comes, we say, "This is it"; which we would not say unless we recognized it, nor would we recognize it unless we remembered it. Certainly, then, we had forgotten it.

Or had not the whole slipped away, but from the part that was held the other part was sought, because the memory felt that it was not turning over together what it used to turn over together, and, limping as if its accustomed habit were maimed, demanded that what was missing be restored?

It is as when a known person is either seen with the eyes or thought of, and, having forgotten his name, we seek it: whatever else comes to mind is not connected, because it has not been accustomed to be thought of together with him, and so it is rejected, until that name is present at which the knowledge, accustomed to it, may rest evenly, in concord.

And from where is it present, but from the memory itself? For even when we recognize it on being reminded by another, it is from there that it is present. For we do not believe it as something new, but, remembering, we confirm that this is what was said.

But if it is utterly abolished from the mind, we do not recollect it even when reminded. For we have not yet forgotten in every way that which we remember we have forgotten. And so we shall not even be able to seek, as lost, what we have entirely forgotten.

## When we seek God, we seek the happy life

### Chapter 20

**29**. How, then, do I seek you, Lord? For when I seek you, my God, I seek the happy life. Let me seek you, that my soul may live. For my body lives from my soul, and my soul lives from you. How, then, do I seek the happy life? For it is not mine until I can say: "It is enough, it is there."

And here I ought to say how I seek it: whether through recollection, as though I had forgotten it and still held that I had forgotten it, or through a desire to learn what is unknown—either what I never knew, or what I had so forgotten that I do not even remember that I had forgotten it. Is not the happy life the very thing that all wish for, and there is absolutely no one who does not wish it? Where did they come to know it, that they should so wish for it? Where did they see it, that they should love it?

Doubtless we have it somehow, I know not how. And there is a certain other way in which each one, when he has it, is then happy; and there are those who are happy in hope. These have it in a lower way than those who are already happy in the thing itself, yet they are better than those who are happy neither in fact nor in hope. And even these latter, unless they had it in some way, would not so wish to be happy: that they wish it is most certain.

Somehow, I know not how, they have come to know it, and therefore have it in some knowledge, I know not what, about which I am anxious to learn whether it is in the memory; because, if it is there, then we were once happy at some time—whether all of us individually, or in that man who first sinned, in whom we all died and from whom we were all born into misery[^57]—this I do not now ask, but I ask whether the happy life is in the memory.

For we would not love it unless we knew it. We hear this name, and we all confess that we desire the thing itself; for it is not the sound that delights us. For when a Greek hears this word in Latin, he is not delighted, because he does not know what is said; but we are delighted, just as he too would be if he heard it in Greek, since the thing itself is neither Greek nor Latin—the thing which Greeks and Latins, and people of all other tongues, pant to attain. It is known, therefore, to all, who, if they could be asked with one voice whether they wished to be happy, would answer without any doubt that they do. And this would not be so, unless the thing itself, of which this is the name, were held in their memory.

### Chapter 21: The recollection of the happy life

**30**. Is it perhaps in the way that one who has seen Carthage remembers it? No; for the happy life is not seen with the eyes, because it is not a body.

Is it as we remember numbers? No; for one who has these in his knowledge does not still seek to attain them, whereas we have the happy life in our knowledge, and therefore love it, and yet still wish to attain it, that we may be happy.

Is it as we remember eloquence? No; for although, at the hearing of this name too, even those who are not yet eloquent recall the thing itself, and many desire to be eloquent

(from which it appears that it is in their knowledge), yet through the senses of the body they have observed others to be eloquent and have been delighted and desire to be so (although they would not be delighted but from an inner knowledge, nor would they wish to be so unless they were delighted); but the happy life we experience in others by no sense of the body.

Is it as we remember joy? Perhaps so. For I remember my joy even when sad, as I remember the happy life when wretched; and I have never with a bodily sense seen or heard or smelled or tasted or touched my joy, but I experienced it in my mind when I rejoiced, and the knowledge of it clung to my memory, so that I can recall it—sometimes with scorn, sometimes with longing, according to the diversity of the things in which I remember I rejoiced.

For I have been suffused with a certain joy even over shameful things, which now, remembering, I detest and execrate; and sometimes over good and honorable things, which I recall with longing, even though they are perhaps not present; and therefore I recall my former joy with sadness.

#### We all wish to be happy

**31**. Where, then, and when did I experience my happy life, that I should remember it and love it and long for it? And not I alone, or with a few, but absolutely all of us wish to be happy[^58]. And unless we knew it with a certain knowledge, we would not wish it with so certain a will.

But what is this[^59]? If two men are asked whether they wish to be soldiers, it could happen that one answers that he does, the other that he does not; but if they are asked whether they wish to be happy, each at once, without any doubt, says that he desires it; nor does the one wish to be a soldier, nor the other refuse, for any other reason than that they may be happy.

Is it perhaps because one rejoices in one thing, another in another? In the same way all agree that they wish to be happy, just as they would agree, if they were asked this, that they wish to rejoice; and this very joy they call the happy life. And although one attains it from one source, another from another, there is yet one thing at which all strive to arrive: that they may rejoice. And since this is a thing which no one can say he has not experienced, it is therefore found in the memory and recognized when the name of the happy life is heard.

### Chapter 22: God himself is the joy of those who worship him

**32**. Far be it, Lord, far be it from the heart of your servant, who confesses to you, far be it that I should think myself happy with whatever joy I rejoice. For there is a joy that is not given to the ungodly, but to those who worship you freely, whose joy you yourself are.

And this is the happy life: to rejoice toward you, of you, for you; this it is, and there is no other. But those who think there is another pursue another joy, and not the true one. Yet their will is not turned away from some image of joy.

### Chapter 23: All wish for joy in the truth

**33**. It is not certain, then, that all wish to be happy, since those who do not wish to rejoice in you—which alone is the happy life—do not, in truth, wish for the happy life.

Or do all wish for this, but, because *the flesh lusts against the spirit and the spirit against the flesh, so that they do not do what they wish*[^60], do they fall to what they are able to do, and are content with that—because that which they are not able to do they do not wish for so much as would be enough to be able to do it?

For I ask of all whether they would rather rejoice in the truth than in falsehood; they hesitate no more to say that they would rather rejoice in the truth than they hesitate to say that they wish to be happy. For the happy life is joy in the truth. For this is joy in you, who are the Truth[^61], God, *my light*[^62], *the salvation of my countenance, my God*[^63].

This happy life all wish for; this life, which alone is happy, all wish for; joy in the truth all wish for. I have known many who wished to deceive, but no one who wished to be deceived. Where, then, did they come to know this happy life, but where they also came to know the truth?

For they love the truth too, since they do not wish to be deceived; and when they love the happy life, which is nothing other than joy in the truth, they surely love the truth as well; nor would they love it, unless there were some knowledge of it in their memory.

Why, then, do they not rejoice in it? Why are they not happy? Because they are more strongly occupied with other things, which make them wretched rather than that thing, which they faintly remember, makes them happy. For *there is still a little light* in human beings; let them walk, let them walk, *lest the darkness overtake them*[^64].

**34**. But why does *truth beget hatred*[^65], and why has your man, preaching the truth, become an enemy to them[^66], when the happy life is loved, which is nothing but joy in the truth—unless because the truth is loved in such a way that those who love something else wish what they love to be the truth, and, because they would not wish to be deceived, are unwilling to be convinced that they are deceived? And so, for the sake of that very thing which they love in place of the truth, they hate the truth. They love it when it shines, they hate it when it reproves[^67]. For because they do not wish to be deceived, and do wish to deceive, they love it when it reveals itself, and hate it when it reveals them.

Therefore it will repay them thus: that those who do not wish to be revealed by it, it will reveal even against their will, while it itself will not be revealed to them. Thus, thus, even thus the human mind, even thus blind and languid, base and unseemly, wishes to lie hidden, yet does not wish that anything be hidden from it.

The opposite is rendered to it: that it does not lie hidden from the truth, while the truth lies hidden from it. Yet even so, while it is wretched, it would rather rejoice in true things than in false. It will be happy, then, if, with no trouble interrupting, it shall rejoice in that truth alone, through which all things are true.

## God is found in the memory

### Chapter 24

**35**. Behold, how widely I have ranged through my memory seeking you, Lord, and I have not found you outside it. For I have found nothing concerning you that I had not remembered, from the time I learned you. For from the time I learned you, I have not forgotten you. For where I found the truth, there I found my God, the Truth itself[^68]; and from the time I learned it, I have not forgotten it.

And so, from the time I learned you, you abide in my memory, and there I find you when I remember you and delight in you. These are my holy delights, which you have given me, your mercy looking upon my poverty[^69].

### Chapter 25: In what place of the memory does God dwell?

**36**. But where do you abide in my memory, Lord, where do you abide there? What couch have you fashioned for yourself? What sanctuary have you built for yourself? You gave this honor to my memory, that you should abide in it; but in which part of it you abide, this I consider. For I transcended those parts of it which the beasts also have, when I remembered you, since I did not find you there among the images of corporeal things; and I came to those parts of it where I entrusted the affections of my mind, and there too I did not find you.

And I entered into the very seat of my mind, which it has in my memory, since the mind also remembers itself; and you were not there, because, just as you are not a corporeal image nor the affection of a living being—such as it is when we are glad, are saddened, desire, fear, remember, forget, and whatever else of this kind—so neither are you the mind itself, since you are the Lord God of the mind; and all these things are changed, but you remain unchangeable above all things, and you have deigned to dwell in my memory from the time I learned you.

And why do I ask in what place of it you dwell, as if there were really places there? You dwell in it, certainly, since I remember you from the time I learned you, and in it I find you when I recall you.

### Chapter 26: Where is God found, when he is known?

**37**. Where, then, did I find you, that I might learn you? For you were not yet in my memory before I learned you. Where, then, did I find you, that I might learn you, but in yourself, above me? And there is no place; we withdraw and we draw near, and there is no place.

Truth, everywhere you preside over all who consult you, and at the same time you answer all, even those who consult you about diverse things. Clearly you answer, but not all clearly hear. All consult you about what they wish, but they do not always hear what they wish. He is your best servant who looks not so much to hear from you what he himself wished, as rather to wish what he has heard from you.

### Chapter 27: Late did Augustine love God

> **38**. Late have I loved you,  
> Beauty so ancient and so new,  
> late have I loved you!  
> And behold, you were within  
> and I outside, and there I sought you,  
> and upon those lovely things you made  
> I rushed, deformed.  
> You were with me, and I was not with you.  
> Those things held me far from you  
> which, if they were not in you,  
> would not be at all.  
> You called and cried out and broke open my deafness;  
> you flashed, you shone,  
> and you put my blindness to flight;  
> you breathed fragrance, and I drew breath  
> and now pant for you;  
> I tasted[^70], and I hunger and thirst[^71];  
> you touched me, and I burned for your peace.

### Chapter 28: Human life upon earth

**39**. When I shall cling to you[^72] with all of myself, there will nowhere be *pain and toil* for me[^73], and my life will be alive, wholly full of you. But now, since you raise up the one whom you fill, and I am not full of you, I am a burden to myself. My joys that should be wept over contend with my sorrows that should be rejoiced in, and on which side the victory stands I do not know. My evil sorrows contend with my good joys, and on which side the victory stands I do not know. Woe is me! *Lord, have mercy on me*[^74]! Woe is me! Behold, I do not hide my wounds: you are the physician, I am sick; you are merciful, I am wretched. Is not *human life upon earth a temptation*[^75]?

Who would wish for troubles and difficulties? You command that they be endured, not loved. No one loves what he endures, even if he loves to endure. For although he rejoices that he endures, he would still rather there be nothing for him to endure. In adversity I long for prosperity, in prosperity I fear adversity. What middle place is there between these, where human life is not a temptation? Woe to the prosperities of the world, once and again, from the fear of adversity and from the corruption of joy! Woe to the adversities of the world, once and again and a third time, from the longing for prosperity, and because the adversity itself is hard, and lest it shatter endurance! Is not *human life upon earth a temptation* without any interval?

## God commands continence

### Chapter 29

**40**. And all my hope is in nothing but your exceedingly great mercy. Give what you command, and command what you will. You command us continence. *And when I knew*, a certain one says, *that no one could be continent unless God gave it, this very thing was part of wisdom—to know whose gift it was*[^76].

For through continence we are gathered together and brought back into one, from which we had flowed apart into many. For he loves you less who loves anything along with you that he does not love for your sake. O love, who ever burn and are never extinguished, O charity, my God, set me on fire! You command continence: give what you command, and command what you will.

## A) The lust of the flesh, or sensual pleasures: a) sexual lust

### Chapter 30

**41**. You command, certainly, that I abstain from *the lust of the flesh and the lust of the eyes and the ambition of the world*[^77]. You commanded abstinence from intercourse, and concerning marriage itself you counseled something better than what you permitted[^78]. And since you gave it, it was done, even before I became a dispenser of your sacrament.

But there still live in my memory—of which I have spoken much—the images of such things, which my habit fixed there; and they rush upon me, lacking force indeed while I am awake, but in sleep going as far as delight and even as far as consent and a deed most like the act itself. And the illusion of an image has such power in my soul, in my flesh, that false visions persuade me while sleeping to what true ones cannot while I am awake.

Am I not myself then, Lord my God? And yet there is so great a difference between myself and myself, within the moment in which I pass from here into sleep, or pass back again from there to here! Where then is the reason by which, awake, one resists such suggestions and, if the very things are pressed upon him, remains unshaken? Is it closed up with the eyes? Is it lulled to sleep with the senses of the body? And from where comes it that often even in sleep we resist, and, mindful of our resolve and most chastely persevering in it, give no assent to such enticements?

And yet there is so great a difference that, when it happens otherwise, on waking we return to the rest of a good conscience, and by that very distance we find that we did not do what we yet grieve to have been done in us in some way.

**42**. Is not your hand mighty, almighty God, to heal all the *infirmities*[^79] of my soul, and by your more abundant grace to extinguish the lascivious motions even of my sleep? You will increase, Lord, more and more your gifts in me, that my soul may follow me to you, freed from the birdlime of desire; that it may not be in rebellion against itself, and that even in sleep it may not only not perpetrate those shameful corruptions through bestial images, even to the flowing of the flesh, but may not even consent to them.

For that nothing of the kind should give pleasure, or so little that it could be checked by a mere nod, even in the chaste disposition of one sleeping—not only in this life, but even at this present age—is no great thing for the Almighty, *who are able to do beyond what we ask or understand*[^80].

Yet now, what I still am in this kind of my evil, I have told my good Lord, exulting *with trembling*[^81] in what you have given me, and mourning in that I am unfinished, hoping that you will perfect your mercies in me[^82] unto the full peace which my inward and outward being shall have with you, when *death shall be swallowed up in victory*[^83].

## b) taste

### Chapter 31

**43**. There is another evil of the day, which I would were sufficient for it[^84]. For we repair the daily ruins of the body by eating and drinking, until you destroy both food and belly[^85], when you shall slay my want with a wonderful fullness and clothe *this corruptible* with everlasting *incorruption*[^86].

But now this necessity is sweet to me, and against that sweetness I fight[^87], lest I be taken captive, and I wage a daily war, often *by fastings*[^88] bringing *my body into subjection*[^89]; and my pains are driven out by pleasure. For hunger and thirst are a kind of pain; they burn and, like a fever, kill, unless the medicine of nourishment comes to the rescue. And since this is at hand from the consolation of your gifts, in which earth and water and sky serve our weakness, the calamity is called a delight.

**44**. This you have taught me: that I should come to take food as I take medicines. But while I pass from the discomfort of want to the quiet of satiety, in that very passage the snare of desire lies in wait for me. For the passage itself is a pleasure, and there is no other by which to pass, where necessity compels one to pass.

And although health is the cause of eating and drinking, a perilous pleasantness joins itself as an attendant, and for the most part tries to go ahead, so that for its sake may be done what I say, or wish, to do for the sake of health. Nor is the measure of the two the same: for what is enough for health is too little for delight, and often it becomes uncertain whether it is the still-necessary care of the body asking for support, or the voluptuous deceit of greed seeking to be served.

At this uncertainty the unhappy soul grows cheerful, and in it prepares a defense of excuse, rejoicing that it is not clear what is enough for the moderation of health, so that under the pretext of health it may screen the business of pleasure.

These temptations I try daily to resist, and I call upon your right hand, and I bring my turmoils to you, because as yet I have no settled counsel in this matter.

**45**. I hear the voice of my God commanding: *Let not your hearts be weighed down with surfeit and drunkenness*[^90]. Drunkenness is far from me: you will have mercy, that it not draw near to me. But surfeit sometimes creeps up on your servant: you will have mercy, that it be put far from me. For *no one can be continent unless you give it*[^91]. Many things you grant us as we pray, and whatever good we received before we prayed, we received from you; and that we should afterward know this, we received from you. A drunkard I never was, but I have known drunkards made sober by you. Therefore it was your doing that those who never were drunkards should not be such, just as it was your doing that those who were should not always be such, and your doing too that both should know by whom it was done.

I have heard another voice of yours: *Go not after your lusts, and refrain yourself from your appetites*[^92]. I have heard also, by your gift, that one which I have loved much: *Neither if we eat shall we abound, nor if we do not eat shall we lack*[^93]; that is to say: neither will the one thing make me plentiful, nor the other wretched. And I have heard another: *For I have learned, in whatever state I am, to be content; I know how to abound and I know how to suffer want. I can do all things in him who strengthens me*[^94]. Behold, a soldier of the heavenly camp, not the dust which we are.

But remember, Lord, *that we are dust*[^95], and out of dust you made man[^96], and he *was lost and is found*[^97].

Nor could that man do it in himself, since he too was the same dust—he whom, saying such things by the breath of your inspiration, I came to love dearly: *I can do all things*, he says, *in him who strengthens me*.

Strengthen me, that I may be able; give what you command, and command what you will. This man confesses that he has received, and what he glories in, he *glories in the Lord*[^98].

I have heard another asking, that he might receive: *Take from me*, he says, *the desires of the belly*[^99].

From which it appears, my holy God, that you give, when what you command to be done is done.

**46**. You have taught me, good Father: *all things are clean to the clean*[^100], but that *it is evil for the man who eats with offense*[^101]; and that *every creature of yours is good, and nothing is to be cast away that is received with thanksgiving*[^102]; and that *food does not commend us to God*[^103]; and that *no one should judge us in food or in drink*[^104]; and that *he who eats should not despise him who does not eat, and he who does not eat should not judge him who eats*[^105]. I have learned these things—thanks be to you, praise be to you, my God, my master, the knocker at my ears[^106], the enlightener of my heart; rescue me from every temptation[^107]. It is not the uncleanness of meat that I fear, but the uncleanness of greed.

I know that Noah was permitted to eat every kind of flesh that was of use for food[^108]; that Elijah was refreshed with the food of flesh[^109]; that John, endowed with a wonderful abstinence, was not polluted by the living creatures—that is, the locusts—that served him for food[^110]. And I know that Esau was deceived by his craving for lentils[^111], and that David reproached himself over his longing for water[^112], and that our King was tempted not concerning flesh, but concerning bread[^113]. And therefore the people in the desert too deserved to be reproved, not because they desired meat, but because, in their desire for food, they murmured against the Lord[^114].

**47**. Placed, then, among these temptations, I struggle daily against the desire of eating and drinking. For this is not something I can decide to cut off once and not touch thereafter, as I could about intercourse. And so the reins of the throat must be held with a tempered loosening and tightening. And who is there, Lord, who is not carried somewhat beyond the bounds of necessity?

Whoever he is, he is great; let him magnify your name[^115]. But I am not such, *for I am a sinful man*[^116]. Yet I too magnify your name, and he *who has conquered the world*[^117] *intercedes with you for* my *sins*[^118], numbering me among the weak members of his body[^119], because *your eyes have seen* even his *imperfection, and in your book all shall be written*[^120].

## c) smell

### Chapter 32

**48**. About the enticement of odors I am not much concerned: when they are absent, I do not seek them; when they are present, I do not refuse them, ready always to do without them. So it seems to me; perhaps I am deceived.

For these too are darkness to be mourned, in which the faculty that is in me lies hidden from me, so that my mind, questioning itself about its own powers, judges that it must not easily trust itself, because what is within is for the most part hidden unless it is made manifest by experience; and no one ought to be secure in this life, which is called *wholly a temptation*[^121], as to whether he who could become better from worse may not also become worse from better. One hope, one confidence, one firm promise: your mercy.

## d) hearing

### Chapter 33

**49**. The pleasures of the ear had more tenaciously entangled and subdued me, but you loosed and freed me. Now, in the sounds which your words give life to, when they are sung with a sweet and skillful voice, I confess, I find a little repose—not, indeed, so as to cling to them, but so as to rise up when I will.

Yet, together with the very meanings by which they have their life, so as to be admitted to me, they seek in my heart a place of some dignity, and I scarcely grant them a fitting one.

For sometimes I seem to myself to grant them more honor than is fitting, when I feel that our minds are moved to the flame of devotion more religiously and ardently by those holy words when they are sung in this way than if they were not so sung, and that all the affections of our spirit, according to their diversity, have their own proper modes in voice and song, by which, through some hidden affinity I know not, they are stirred.

But the delight of my flesh, to which the mind ought not to be given over to be enfeebled, often deceives me, when the sense does not so accompany the reason as patiently to keep behind it, but, only because it has earned admittance for reason's sake, tries even to run ahead and lead. Thus in these things I sin without noticing, and afterward I notice.

**50**. But sometimes, guarding too immoderately against this very deception, I err by excess of severity—indeed, sometimes so much that I could wish every melody of the sweet songs to which the Psalter of David is set to be removed from my ears, and from those of the Church herself; and it seems safer to me, what I remember was often told me about Athanasius, bishop of Alexandria, who made the reader of the psalm sound it with so slight an inflection of voice that he was nearer to one speaking than to one singing. And yet, when I recall the tears I shed at the songs of the Church in the first days of my recovered faith, and now, when I am moved not by the singing but by the things that are sung—when they are sung with a clear voice and most fitting modulation—I again acknowledge the great usefulness of this practice.

Thus I fluctuate between the peril of pleasure and the experience of its wholesomeness; and I am the more inclined—though not pronouncing an irrevocable opinion—to approve the custom of singing in the Church, that through the delights of the ear a weaker mind may rise up into the affection of devotion. Yet when it happens to me that the singing moves me more than the thing that is sung, I confess that I sin in a way that deserves punishment, and then I would rather not hear the singer.

Behold where I am! Weep with me, and weep for me, you who work some good within yourselves, whence deeds proceed. For you who do not so work, these things do not move you. But you, *Lord my God, hear, look and see and have mercy and heal me*[^122], in whose eyes I have become a question to myself; and this is my infirmity[^123].

## e) sight

### Chapter 34

**51**. There remains the pleasure of these eyes of my flesh, about which I will speak my confessions, which the ears of your temple may hear[^124], the brotherly and devout ears, that we may conclude the temptations of the lust of the flesh[^125], which still beat upon me as I groan and long *to be clothed over with my dwelling that is from heaven*[^126]. The eyes love beautiful and varied forms, bright and pleasant colors. Let these not hold my soul; let God hold it, who made these things *very good*[^127]—but he is my good, not these.

And they touch me, waking, all day long, nor is rest given me from them, as is given from melodious voices—sometimes, in silence, from all of them. For light itself, that queen of colors, flooding all the things we behold, wherever I am through the day, fawns upon me by its manifold approach even when I am doing something else and not attending to it. And it insinuates itself so forcibly that, if it is suddenly withdrawn, it is sought with longing; and if it is long absent, it saddens the mind.

**52**. O light, which Tobit saw when, with these eyes closed, he taught his son the way of life and went before him with the foot of love[^128], nowhere straying; or which Isaac saw, with his bodily eyes weighed down and covered by old age, when he was granted not to recognize his sons and bless them, but, by blessing, to recognize them[^129];

or which Jacob saw, when he too, his eyes failing through great age, with a luminous heart shone upon the kinds of the future people prefigured in his sons, and laid upon his grandsons by Joseph his hands crossed in a mystical way—not as their father, correcting from without, but as he himself discerned within[^130]. This is the light; it is one, and all are one who see and love it.

But that corporeal light, of which I was speaking, seasons the life of the world for its blind lovers with an enticing and perilous sweetness. But those who know how to praise you even for it, *God, Creator of all things*[^131], take it up in your hymn; they are not taken up by it in their own sleep: so I desire to be. I resist the seductions of the eyes, lest my feet, with which I walk your way, be entangled; and I raise up to you my invisible eyes, that you may *pluck my feet out of the snare*[^132]. Again and again you pluck them out, for they are ensnared. You do not cease to pluck them out, but I often stick fast in the snares strewn everywhere, since *you who keep Israel will neither slumber nor sleep*[^133].

**53**. How innumerable are the things—made by various arts and crafts, in clothing, shoes, vessels, and fabrications of every kind, in pictures too and diverse images, and these far exceeding all necessary and moderate use and all devout meaning—that people have added to the enticements of the eyes, outwardly following what they make, inwardly forsaking him by whom they were made, and destroying what they were made to be.

But I, my God and my glory, even from this raise a hymn to you and offer a sacrifice of praise[^134] to my sanctifier, since the beautiful things that pass through souls into artful hands come from that Beauty which is above souls, for which my soul sighs *day and night*[^135]. But the makers and pursuers of outward beauties draw from it the measure of approving, but they do not draw from it the measure of using. And he is there, and they do not see him, that they might not go farther and might *keep their strength for you*[^136], nor scatter it into delicious weariness.

But I, even as I speak of these things and discern them, entangle my steps among these beautiful things; but you pluck me out, Lord, you pluck me out, *for your mercy is before my eyes*[^137]. For I am caught miserably, and you pluck me out mercifully—sometimes without my feeling it, because I had fallen in only lightly, sometimes with pain, because I had already stuck fast.

## B) The lust of the eyes, or superfluous curiosity

### Chapter 35

**54**. To this is added another form of temptation, more variously perilous. For besides the lust of the flesh, which is in the delight of all the senses and pleasures—those serving which, by putting themselves far from you, perish[^138]—there is in the soul, through the same senses of the body, a certain vain and curious desire, not of taking delight in the flesh, but of experiencing through the flesh, cloaked under the name of knowledge and learning. And because it is in the appetite of knowing, and the eyes are foremost among the senses for knowing, it is called in the divine word *the lust of the eyes*[^139].

For seeing properly belongs to the eyes. But we use this word also of the other senses when we direct them to know. For we do not say, "Hear how red it is," or "Smell how it shines," or "Taste how it glistens," or "Touch how it gleams"; all these are said to be seen. But we say not only, "See what shines," which the eyes alone can perceive; but also, "See what sounds, see what smells, see what tastes, see how hard it is." And therefore the general experience of the senses is called, as has been said, *the lust of the eyes*, because the office of seeing, in which the eyes hold primacy, the other senses also usurp for themselves by way of similarity when they are exploring for some knowledge.

**55**. From this is more clearly distinguished what is done through the senses for the sake of pleasure, what for the sake of curiosity: namely, that pleasure pursues things beautiful, melodious, sweet, savory, smooth; but curiosity, for the sake of trying them, pursues even the contraries of these—not to undergo discomfort, but out of a lust of experiencing and knowing.

For what pleasure is there in seeing in a mangled corpse what makes you shudder? And yet, if one lies anywhere, people run together, that they may be saddened, that they may grow pale. They even fear to see it in their sleep, as if anyone had forced them to see it when awake, or as if any report of beauty had persuaded them. So too in the other senses, which it would be long to pursue.

From this disease of greed all sorts of marvels are exhibited at the shows. From this men proceed to scrutinize the hidden things of the nature that lies outside us, the knowing of which is of no profit, and which people desire only to know. From this too, if anything is sought, with the same end of a perverse knowledge, by magical arts. From this even in religion itself God is tempted, when *signs and wonders are demanded*[^140]—desired not for any salvation, but for the experience alone.

**56**. In this so vast forest, full of snares and perils, behold, I have cut off many things and driven them from my heart, as you have granted me to do, *God of my salvation*[^141]; yet when do I dare to say—since on every side so many things of this kind clamor around our daily life—when do I dare to say that no such thing makes me intent on watching it or take me captive with vain concern? Of course the theaters no longer carry me away, nor do I care to know the courses of the stars, nor has my soul ever sought answers from the shades; all sacrilegious rites I detest. From you, my Lord God, to whom I owe humble and simple servitude, with how great machinations of suggestions the enemy works upon me, to make me ask for some sign! But I beseech you, through our King and our homeland Jerusalem, simple, chaste: as consent to such things is far from me, let it be ever far and farther still. And when I ask you for the safety of anyone, the end of my intention is something far different, and you, doing what you will, give and will give me to follow you willingly.

**57**. Yet in how many very tiny and contemptible matters our curiosity is daily tempted, and how often we slip—who can count? How often, when people tell empty tales, we at first put up with them, as it were, lest we offend the weak; and then little by little we listen to them gladly. I no longer watch a dog running after a hare when it is done in the circus; but in the field, if I should happen to pass by, that hunt may turn me away, perhaps even from some great thought, and turn me to itself—not forcing my mount's body off course, but the heart's inclination; and unless you, having already shown me my weakness, quickly admonish me either to rise up to you out of the very sight by some reflection, or to despise the whole thing and pass by, I grow dull in vanity.

What, when, sitting at home, a gecko catching flies, or a spider entangling those that rush into its webs, often makes me intent? Is it because they are small animals that the same thing is not going on? I proceed from there to praise you, the wonderful Creator and orderer of all things; but it is not from there that I begin to be intent. It is one thing to rise quickly, another thing not to fall.

And my life is full of such things, and my one hope is your exceedingly great mercy[^142]. For when our heart becomes a receptacle for things of this kind and bears troops of plentiful vanity, hence too our prayers are often interrupted and disturbed; and before your sight, while we direct the voice of our heart to your ears, the great matter is cut short by trifling thoughts rushing in from I know not where.

## C) The ambition of the world: a) public offices

### Chapter 36: The gentle yoke of God

**58**. Shall we count this too among things to be despised? Or shall anything bring us back into hope but your known mercy, since you have begun to change us? *And you know*[^143] how far you have changed me—you who first heal me of the lust of avenging myself, that you may be *propitious also to all my other iniquities, and heal all my infirmities, and redeem my life from corruption, and crown me with pity and mercy, and satisfy my desire with good things*[^144]; you who have repressed my pride by your fear and tamed my neck by your yoke. And now I bear it, and it is gentle to me, since so you promised[^145] and so you have done; and truly so it was, and I did not know it, when I feared to take it up.

**59**. But, Lord, you who alone rule without pride, since *you alone are the true Lord*[^146], who have no Lord—has this third kind of temptation[^147] ceased in me, or can it cease, in this whole life,

the wish to be feared and loved by people, for no other reason than that there may be from it a joy that is no joy? It is a wretched life and a foul boasting.

Hence comes especially the failure to love you or to fear you chastely; and therefore *you resist the proud, but to the humble you give grace*[^148], and you thunder[^149] upon the ambitions of the world[^150], and *the foundations of the mountains tremble*[^151].

And so, since for certain offices of human society it is necessary that we be loved and feared by people, the adversary of our true happiness presses upon us, scattering everywhere in his snares the cry *Aha, aha!*[^152], so that, while we greedily gather it up, we may be incautiously caught, and may set our joy aside from your truth and place it in human deception; and that it may delight us to be loved and feared not for your sake, but in place of you, and that in this way, having made us like himself, he may have us with him—not for the concord of love, but for fellowship in punishment—he who *resolved to set his throne in the north*[^153], that those who imitate you by a perverse and distorted way might serve him, dark and cold.

But we, Lord, behold, are your *little flock*[^154]; possess us. Stretch out your wings, and let us flee under them. Be our glory; let us be loved for your sake, and let your word be feared in us. He who wishes to be praised by people while you disapprove will not be defended by people when you judge, nor delivered when you condemn.

But when it is not the case that *the sinner is praised in the desires of his soul, nor he who does wrong blessed*[^155], but a man is praised on account of some gift which you have given him—and yet he is more glad to have himself praised than to have the very gift for which he is praised—this man too is praised while you disapprove; and now the one who praised is better than the one who was praised. For to the first the gift of God in the man was pleasing; to the other, the gift of man pleased more than the gift of God.

### Chapter 37: b) the praises of people

**60**. We are tempted by these temptations daily, Lord; without ceasing we are tempted. Our daily furnace[^156] is the human tongue. You command us continence in this kind too: give what you command, and command what you will. You know on this matter the groaning of my heart toward you[^157] and the rivers of my eyes. For I do not easily gather how far I have been cleansed of this plague, and I greatly fear my hidden things[^158], which your eyes know[^159], but mine do not. For in other kinds of temptation I have some means of exploring myself; in this, almost none.

For both from the pleasures of the flesh and from superfluous curiosity to know, I see how far I have advanced in being able to rein in my mind, when I do without those things, either willingly or because they are absent. For then I question myself, how much more or less it troubles me not to have them.

But riches, which are sought for this reason—that they may serve some one of these three desires, or two of them, or all—if the mind cannot fully perceive whether, having them, it despises them, can also be let go, that it may test itself.

But that we may do without praise, and in this test what we can do—must we then live wickedly and so abandonedly and monstrously that no one who knows us does not detest us? What greater madness can be spoken or thought? But if praise both is and ought to be the companion of a good life and of good works, neither its companionship nor the good life itself ought to be deserted. Yet I do not perceive whether I can be without something, either with an even mind or with grief, except when it is absent.

**61**. What, then, do I confess to you in this kind of temptation, Lord? What, but that I take delight in praise? Yet more in the truth itself than in praise. For if it were set before me whether I would rather be praised by all people while raving or erring in all things, or be reproached by all while standing firm and most certain in the truth, I see what I would choose. And yet I would not wish that the support of another's mouth should even add to my joy in any good of mine. But it does add, I confess; not only that, but reproach also lessens it.

And when I am troubled by this misery of mine, an excuse creeps in, of what sort it is, *you know, God*[^160]; for it leaves me uncertain. For because you have commanded us not only continence—that is, from what things we should restrain our love—but also righteousness, that is, where we should bestow it, and have willed that not you only, but also our neighbor, be loved by us[^161],

I often seem to myself to take delight in the progress or hope of my neighbor, when I take delight in the praise of one who understands well; and again, to be saddened by his evil, when I hear him reproach either what he does not know or what is good.

For I am sometimes also saddened by my own praises, when either those things are praised in me in which I displease myself, or even when lesser and slight goods are reckoned at a higher value than they ought to be.

But again, how do I know whether I am so affected on this account—that I do not wish my praiser to differ from me about myself, not because I am moved by his benefit, but because the same goods which please me in me are more pleasant to me when they also please another?

For in a way I am not praised when my own opinion of me is not praised, since either things are praised that displease me, or those more that please me less. Am I, then, uncertain about myself in this?

**62**. Behold, in you, Truth, I see that I ought to be moved by my praises not for my own sake, but for the benefit of my neighbor. And whether I am so, I do not know. In this matter I am less known to myself than I am to you. I beseech you, my God, show me myself also to myself, that I may confess to my brethren who will pray for me what I find wounded in me.

Let me question myself again more carefully. If I am moved in my praises by the benefit of my neighbor, why am I less moved if someone else is unjustly reproached than if I am? Why am I more stung by the contumely that is hurled at me, than by that hurled with the same unfairness against another in my presence? Or do I not know this either? Does it remain that I deceive myself[^162] and do not the truth[^163] before you in heart and tongue? Put this madness, Lord, far from me, lest my mouth *be to me the oil of a sinner to anoint my head*[^164].

### Chapter 38: c) vainglory

**63**. *I am needy and poor*[^165], and the better when in secret groaning I am displeasing to myself and seek your mercy, until my defect is repaired and I am perfected unto the peace which the arrogant eye does not know.

But speech going forth from the mouth, and deeds that become known to people, have a most perilous temptation from the love of praise, which, in pursuit of a certain private excellence, gathers up begged-for endorsements;

it tempts, and when it is convicted within me by me, on that very ground on which it is convicted, and often it glories more vainly in the very contempt of vainglory; and therefore it does not glory any longer in that contempt of glory itself, for it does not despise glory when it glories.

### Chapter 39: d) one's own delight in oneself

**64**. Within, too, within is yet another evil in the same kind of temptation, by which those grow empty who please themselves about themselves, although they may not please others, or may displease them, nor do they care to please others.

But pleasing themselves, they greatly displease you, not only over non-goods as though they were goods, but even over your goods as though they were their own; or even as over your goods, but as from their own merits; or even as from your grace, yet not rejoicing in fellowship, but envying it to others.

In all these things, and in perils and labors of this kind, you see the trembling of my heart; and I feel my wounds being healed by you again and again, rather than not being inflicted upon me.

## What the author has surveyed in seeking God

### Chapter 40

**65**. Where have you not walked with me, Truth, teaching me what to beware of and what to seek, when I referred to you my lower sights, such as I could, and consulted you? I surveyed the world outside with my sense, so far as I could, and considered the life of my body from myself, and my very senses.

From there I entered into the recesses of my memory, manifold and spacious, wonderfully filled with countless abundances; and I considered, and was afraid[^166], and could discern none of these things without you, and I found none of them to be you.

Nor was I myself the finder—I who traversed all things and tried to distinguish them and to value each according to its own dignity,

receiving some from the senses that reported them and questioning them; perceiving others mingled with myself, recognizing and enumerating the very messengers; and now, in the wide riches of memory, handling some, storing some away, drawing some out;

and neither was I myself, when I did these things—that is, the power of mine by which I did them—neither were you yourself this, since you are the abiding light[^167], of whom I asked about all things—whether they were, what they were, of what value they should be reckoned—and I heard you teaching and commanding.

And often I do this; this delights me, and from the actions of necessity, as far as I can be released, I flee back to this pleasure. Nor in all these things which I run through consulting you do I find a safe place for my soul except in you, where my scattered parts may be gathered together and nothing of me depart from you.

And sometimes you admit me, inwardly, to a wholly unwonted feeling, to a sweetness I know not what, which, if it should be perfected in me, will be I know not what—but it will not be this life. But I fall back into these things with troublesome weights and am drawn back into the customary, and I am held, and I weep much; but I am much held. So much is the burden of habit able to do! Here I can be, and do not wish to; there I wish to be, and cannot—wretched in both places.

### Chapter 41: Truth and falsehood

**66**. And so I have considered the infirmities of my sins in the threefold desire, and have called upon your right hand for my salvation[^168]. For I saw your splendor with a wounded heart, and, beaten back, I said: Who can come there? *I have been cast away from the sight of your eyes*[^169].

You are the Truth[^170], presiding over all things. But I, through my greed, did not wish to lose you, but wished to possess a lie along with you—just as no one wishes to speak falsely in such a way as not himself to know what is true. And so I lost you, because you do not deign to be possessed along with a lie.

## False mediators between God and humanity praised by some

### Chapter 42

**67**. Whom should I find to reconcile me to you? Was I to court the angels? With what prayer? With what rites? Many, as I hear, who tried to return to you and were not strong enough by themselves, have tried these things, and fell into the desire of curious visions, and were judged worthy of illusions.

For, lifted up, they sought you, by the haughtiness of learning—rather puffing out than beating their breasts; and they drew to themselves, through the likeness of their own hearts, conspiring with them and the allies of their pride, *the powers of this air*[^171], by which they were deceived through magical powers, seeking a mediator through whom they might be cleansed—and there was none. For it was the devil, *transforming himself into an angel of light*[^172]. And it strongly enticed their proud flesh, that he himself had no fleshly body. For they were mortal and sinners, while you, Lord, to whom they proudly sought to be reconciled, are immortal and without sin.

But a mediator between God and humanity[^173] needed to have something like God and something like humanity—lest, like humanity in both, he be far from God; or, like God in both, he be far from humanity—and so not be a mediator. The false mediator, then, by whom, through your secret judgments, pride deserves to be deluded, has one thing in common with humanity: namely, sin; and wishes to seem to have another thing in common with God, that, since he is not covered with the mortality of the flesh, he may make a show of himself as immortal. But because *the wage of sin is death*[^174], he has this in common with humanity: that he be condemned together with them to death.

### Chapter 43: The true Mediator between God and humanity, the man Christ Jesus

**68**. But the true mediator, whom you in your secret mercy showed to the humble and sent, that by his example they might also learn humility itself—that *Mediator between God and humanity, the man Christ Jesus*[^175]—appeared between mortal sinners and the immortal Just One: mortal with humanity, just with God, so that, since the wage of righteousness is life and peace, through righteousness joined to God he might empty out the death of those once ungodly but now justified[^176]—a death he willed to have in common with them.

He was shown to the saints of old, that they too, by faith in his coming passion, just as we by faith in his past, might be saved[^177]. For as he is man, so he is mediator; but as he is the Word, he is not in the middle, because he is equal to God[^178] and *God with God*[^179], and at the same time one God.

**69**. How you have loved us, good Father, *who did not spare your only Son, but handed him over for us* ungodly ones[^180]! How you have loved us, for whom he *did not consider it robbery to be equal* to you, and yet *became obedient unto the death of the cross*[^181]—he, the one *free among the dead*[^182], *having power to lay down his life and having power to take it up again*[^183]; for us, before you, victor and victim, and victor because victim; for us, before you, priest and sacrifice, and priest because sacrifice, making us your sons in place of servants by being born from you, by serving us! Rightly is my hope strong in him, that you will heal all my *infirmities*[^184] through him who sits *at your right hand and intercedes for us*[^185]; otherwise I would despair. For these infirmities are many and great, many and great; but greater is your medicine. We might have thought that your Word was removed from union with humanity and have despaired of ourselves, had he not become *flesh and dwelt among us*[^186].

**70**. Terrified by my sins and by the mass of my misery, I had stirred up my heart and meditated flight into solitude; but you forbade me and strengthened me, saying: *Therefore Christ died for all, that those who live may no longer live for themselves, but for him who died for them*[^187]. Behold, Lord, *I cast my care upon you*[^188], that I may live, and *I will consider the wonders of your law*[^189]. *You know*[^190] my ignorance and my weakness[^191]; *teach me*[^192] and *heal me*[^193]. That Only-begotten of yours, *in whom are all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge hidden*[^194], has redeemed me with his blood[^195]. *Let not the proud bring false charges against me*[^196], since I am thinking on my *ransom*[^197], and I eat and drink[^198], and dispense it; and, poor, I desire to be satisfied[^199] from it, among those who eat and are satisfied: *and they shall praise the Lord who seek him*[^200].
