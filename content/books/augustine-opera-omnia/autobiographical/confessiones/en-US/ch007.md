# Book 6: The Thirtieth Year of His Age

## Slow progress toward the catholic faith

### Chapter 1: His mother followed him to Milan

**1**. *My hope from my youth*[^1], where were you to me, and where had you withdrawn[^2]? Had you not made me, and set me apart *from the beasts of the field, and made me wiser than the birds of the air*? And I was walking through darkness and on slippery ground[^3], and I sought you outside myself, and did not find the God of my heart[^4]; and I had come *into the depths of the sea*[^5], and I distrusted and despaired of finding the truth.

By now my mother had come to me, strong in piety, following me by land and sea, and secure in you amid all dangers. For even through the perils of the sea she comforted the very sailors—by whom inexperienced travelers of the deep are usually comforted when they are alarmed—promising them safe arrival, because you had promised her this in a vision[^6].

And she found me in grave peril, through my despair of tracking down the truth; but when I told her that I was no longer a Manichee, though not yet a catholic Christian, she did not leap up for joy as though she had heard something unexpected, since she was already secure about that part of my misery in which she wept for me to you as one dead but to be raised, and offered me on the bier of her thought, that you might say to the widow's son, *Young man, I say to you, arise*[^7], and he should revive and begin to speak, and you should give him back to his mother.

So her heart was shaken by no turbulent exultation when she heard that so great a part was already done of what she daily wept to you that it might be done—that I had not yet attained the truth, but was already rescued from falsehood. Indeed, because she was certain that you, who had promised the whole, would give what remained, she answered me most calmly, and with a heart full of confidence, that she believed in Christ that, before she departed from this life, she would see me a faithful catholic.

This she said to me. But to you, fountain of mercies, she poured out denser prayers and tears, that you would hasten your help[^8] and lighten my darkness[^9]; and she ran the more eagerly to the church and hung upon Ambrose's lips, to the fountain *of water springing up to eternal life*[^10]. For she loved that man like an angel of God[^11], because she had learned that it was through him that I had meanwhile been brought to that wavering hesitation through which she was certain I would pass from sickness to health, with a sharper danger intervening—as it were, the crisis the physicians call "critical."

### Chapter 2: How religiously Monica revered and honored Ambrose

**2**. And so, when she had brought to the memorials of the saints, as she used to do in Africa, porridge and bread and wine, and was forbidden by the doorkeeper, as soon as she learned that the bishop had prohibited this, she embraced it so devoutly and obediently that I myself wondered how easily she became an accuser of her own custom rather than a disputer of his prohibition.

For wine-bibbing did not besiege her spirit, nor did a love of wine provoke her to hatred of the truth, as it does many men and women, who grow queasy at a song of sobriety as drunkards do at a watered draught;

but she, when she had brought her basket with the festal foods to be tasted and then given away, would set out no more than a single little cup, watered to suit her own quite sober palate, from which she would take a sip for courtesy's sake; and if there were many memorials of the dead that seemed to be honored in that way, she carried around that same single cup to set down everywhere, and shared it—by now not only most watered but most tepid—in tiny sips with those present; for she sought devotion there, not pleasure.

And so, when she found that it had been forbidden by that renowned preacher and bishop of devotion, that these things should be done even by those who did them soberly—lest any occasion of gorging be given to drunkards, and because those rites, like funeral feasts, were most like the superstition of the pagans—she most gladly abstained;

and instead of a basket full of earthly fruits, she had learned to bring to the memorials of the martyrs a breast full of purer prayers, so as both to give what she could to the needy, and that the communion of the Lord's Body might be celebrated there, in imitation of whose passion the martyrs were sacrificed and crowned.

But still it seems to me, O Lord my God (and so my heart stands in your sight[^12] on this matter), that perhaps my mother would not easily have yielded about cutting off this custom, had it been forbidden by another whom she did not love as she loved Ambrose.

She loved him most of all for the sake of my salvation; and he loved her for her most religious manner of life, by which, *fervent in spirit*[^13], she frequented the church *in good works*[^14]—so that often, when he saw me, he would break out in praise of her, congratulating me that I had such a mother, not knowing what sort of son she had, who doubted of all these things and did not think the way of life[^15] could be found at all.

## Ambrose, an admirable man

### Chapter 3

**3**. And now I no longer groaned in prayer that you would help me, but my mind was intent on seeking and restless for disputing; and I thought Ambrose himself a happy man by the world's reckoning, since such great powers honored him: only his celibacy seemed to me burdensome. But what hope he bore, and what struggle he had against the temptations of his very eminence, and what solace in adversity, and the hidden mouth that was in his heart—what savory joys he chewed over from your bread—I could neither conjecture nor had I experienced.

Nor did he know my own seethings, or the pit of my danger. For I could not ask him what I wished, as I wished, since crowds of busy men, to whose infirmities he ministered, shut me off from his ear and mouth. And when he was not with them—which was a very small space of time—he refreshed either his body with necessary sustenance or his mind with reading. But when he read, his eyes were drawn over the pages and his heart searched out the sense, while his voice and tongue were at rest.

Often, when we were present—for no one was forbidden to enter, nor was it the custom for anyone coming to be announced to him—we saw him reading silently, and never otherwise; and after sitting in long silence (for who would dare to be a burden to one so intent?) we would depart, and we guessed that in that small time he gained for repairing his mind, free from the din of others' affairs, he did not wish to be called away to anything else;

and perhaps he was being careful lest, if the author he was reading had put anything obscurely, an attentive and intent listener would require him to expound it, or to discuss some more difficult questions, and so, with time spent on this work, he would get through fewer volumes than he wished—though the preservation of his voice, which was very easily strained, could also be a juster cause of reading silently. But with whatever intention he did it, that man certainly did it for a good one.

#### Catholics do not believe that God is bounded by the form of a human body

**4**. But certainly no opportunity was given me to inquire as I desired of that most holy oracle of yours, his breast, except when something was to be briefly heard. Those seethings of mine required him quite at leisure, to have them poured out to him, and never found him so. And indeed I heard him every Lord's Day *rightly handling the word of truth*[^16] among the people, and I was more and more confirmed that all the knots of the cunning calumnies which those deceivers of ours wove against the divine Books could be untied.

But when I also found that man, made by you to your image[^17], was not so understood by your spiritual sons, whom you have regenerated of the catholic mother through grace, as to believe and think him bounded by the form of a human body—although how a spiritual substance might be, I had not even faintly or in a riddle conjectured[^18]—yet, rejoicing, I blushed that for so many years I had barked not against the catholic faith, but against the figments of carnal thoughts. For I had been rash and impious in this, that what I ought to have said by way of inquiry, I had said by way of accusation.

For you, most high[^19] and most near, most hidden and most present, who have no members some greater and some smaller, but are wholly everywhere and nowhere in place, are surely not this bodily form; and yet you made man to your image, and behold, he, from head to foot, is in place.

## The sound doctrine of God's one Church became clear to Augustine

### Chapter 4

**5**. Since, then, I did not know how this image of yours could subsist, I should have knocked[^20] and proposed the question how it was to be believed, not insultingly opposed it as though it were so believed.

So much the sharper, then, was the care that gnawed my inmost being—what I might hold for certain—the more I was ashamed that I had been so long deluded and deceived by the promise of certainties, and, with childish error and animosity, had prattled so many uncertain things as though they were certain.

For that they were false became clear to me later. Yet it was certain that they were uncertain, and that I had at one time held them for certain, when with blind contentions I accused your catholic Church—which, though I had not yet found that she taught the truth, I now saw at least did not teach the things of which I gravely accused her.

And so I was confounded and turned about, and I rejoiced, my God, that the one Church, the body of your only Son[^21], in which the name of Christ had been set upon me as an infant, did not savor of infantile trifles, nor held this in her sound doctrine: to cram you, the Creator of all, into a space of place—however high and ample, yet on every side bounded—by the figure of human members.

#### Ambrose taught Augustine the spiritual understanding of the Scriptures

**6**. I rejoiced also that the old writings of the Law and the Prophets[^22] were now set before me to be read with a different eye than that with which they had earlier seemed absurd, when I found fault with your holy ones as thinking so, though in truth they did not think so. And I gladly heard Ambrose, in his sermons to the people, often commending this as a rule most diligently: *the letter kills, but the spirit gives life*[^23], when, removing the mystical veil, he opened spiritually those things which, taken to the letter, seemed to teach perversity—saying nothing that offended me, though he said things whose truth I did not yet know.

For I held my heart back from all assent, fearing a headlong fall, and I was the more killed by hanging in suspense. For I wanted to be made as certain of the things I did not see as I was certain that seven and three are ten. For I was not so insane as to think that even this could not be comprehended; but as this, so I desired the rest—whether bodily things, which were not present to my senses, or spiritual things, of which I knew no way to think except bodily.

And I could have been healed by believing, so that the cleansed gaze of my mind might be directed in some way toward your truth, which abides forever[^24] and fails in nothing; but, as it usually happens that one who has tried a bad physician is afraid to trust himself even to a good one, so was the health of my soul, which could in no way be healed except by believing, and yet refused to be cured lest it believe falsehoods, resisting your hands[^25], you who have compounded the medicines of faith and sprinkled them over the diseases of the world, and given them so great an authority.

### Chapter 5: The slow and slender growth of faith

**7**. From this, too, now preferring the catholic teaching, I felt that there it was more modestly and least deceitfully commanded that what was not demonstrated should be believed (whether there was something, though to someone it perhaps could not be demonstrated, or whether there was not even anything that could be),

than that, there among the Manichees, credulity should be mocked by a rash promise of knowledge, and afterward so many most fabulous and absurd things commanded to be believed, because they could not be demonstrated.

Then, little by little, you, Lord, handling and composing my heart with a most gentle and merciful hand, persuaded me—when I considered how innumerable were the things I believed which I had not seen, nor had been present when they were done, such as so many things in the history of the nations, so many things about places and cities I had not seen, so many things about friends, so many about physicians, so many about ever so many other men, which, unless believed, we should do nothing at all in this life—

and lastly, how unshakably fixed in faith I held from what parents I had sprung, which I could not know unless I had believed by hearing—you persuaded me that it was not those who believed your Books, which you have established with such authority among almost all nations, but those who did not believe them, who were to be blamed,

and that those were not to be listened to who might perhaps say to me, "How do you know that those Books were ministered to the human race by the Spirit of the one true and most truthful God?"

For this very thing was most of all to be believed, since no pugnacity of calumnious questions—among the many philosophers I had read, conflicting with one another—could ever wrest from me the belief that you exist, whatever you might be (which I did not know), and that the governance of human affairs belongs to you[^26].

**8**. But this I believed sometimes more robustly, sometimes more faintly; yet I always believed both that you exist and that you have care of us, even though I did not know either what was to be thought about your substance, or what way led or led back to you. And so, since we were *too weak* to find the truth by clear reasoning, and for this reason needed the authority of the Holy Scriptures, I had now begun to believe that in no way would you have granted such excellent authority to that Scripture throughout all the world, unless you had willed both that you should be believed through it, and that you should be sought through it.

For now, when I had heard many things in those writings expounded plausibly, I referred the absurdity that used to offend me there to the depth of the mysteries; and that authority appeared to me the more venerable and the more worthy of a sacrosanct faith, in that, while it was open to all for reading, it yet preserved the dignity of its secret in a more profound understanding—offering itself to all in the plainest words and the humblest kind of speech, and yet exercising the attention of those who are not *light of heart*[^27]—so that it might receive all in its popular bosom, and through narrow openings pass a few through to you[^28], yet far more than if it did not stand out with so high a peak of authority, nor draw the crowds into the bosom of holy humility.

I pondered these things, and you were present to me; I sighed, and you heard me; I wavered, and you steered me; I went along the broad way of the world[^29], and you did not forsake me.

## The carefree gladness of a certain beggar compared with Augustine's misery

### Chapter 6

**9**. I gaped after honors, gains, marriage, and you laughed at me[^30]. In those desires I suffered most bitter difficulties, the more graciously by you, the less you let what was not you grow sweet to me. Look at my heart, Lord[^31], you who willed that I should recall this and confess it to you. Now let my soul cleave to you[^32], whom you have stripped from the glue of death so tenacious. How wretched it was! And you pricked the feeling of its wound, that, leaving all things, it might turn to you[^33], *who are over all things*[^34] and without whom all things would be nothing—that it might turn and be healed[^35].

How wretched, then, I was; and how you dealt with me, that I might feel my misery, on that day when, as I was preparing to recite praises of the emperor, in which I would tell many lies and, lying, be applauded by those who knew I lied—and my heart panted with these cares and seethed with the fevers of consuming thoughts—as I passed through a certain street of Milan, I noticed a poor beggar, by now full, I suppose, joking and merry. And I groaned, and spoke with the friends who were with me of the many sorrows of our madnesses, because in all such efforts of ours, such as I was then laboring at—dragging the burden of my unhappiness under the goads of desire, and by dragging making it heavier—we wished for nothing else but to arrive at a carefree gladness, where that beggar had already gone before us, and we perhaps would never come. For what he had already attained with a few little begged coins, I was scheming for by such toilsome windings and roundabout ways: namely, the gladness of a temporal felicity.

For he did not have true joy; but I, with those ambitions, was seeking one far more false. And surely he was glad, while I was anxious; he was carefree, I was trembling. And if anyone asked me whether I would rather exult or fear, I would answer, "Exult"; but again, if he asked whether I would rather be such as he was, or such as I then was, I would choose myself, worn out with cares and fears—but out of perversity; was it out of truth?

For I ought not to have preferred myself to him because I was more learned, since I took no joy from that, but only sought to please men by it—not to teach them, but only to please. And for this reason you too were breaking my bones[^36] with the rod of your discipline[^37].

**10**. Let them depart, then, from my soul[^38] who say to it: "It matters from what source one rejoices. That beggar rejoiced in his drunkenness; you desired to rejoice in glory." What glory, Lord? One that is not in you. For just as it was no true joy, so neither was that true glory, and it twisted my mind the more. And he would that very night digest his drunkenness, while I had slept and risen with mine, and was to sleep and rise again[^39]—see how many days!

It does matter, indeed, from what source one rejoices, I know; and the joy of a faithful hope stands incomparably apart from that vanity. But even then there was a distance between us: for he was without doubt the happier—not only because he was steeped in cheerfulness while I was disemboweled with cares, but also because he had got his wine by wishing people well, while I was seeking conceit by lying.

I said many things to this effect to my dear ones at that time, and often I noticed in them how it was with me, and I found it ill with me, and grieved, and doubled the very ill; and if anything prosperous had smiled on me, it wearied me to grasp it, because almost before it was held it flew away.

## How Alypius heard Augustine as his teacher and came to love him

### Chapter 7: On his friends, and the counsels taken with them

**11**. We groaned together, those of us who lived as friends together; and most of all, and most intimately, I talked of these things with Alypius and Nebridius. Of these, Alypius was born of the same town as I, of parents who were leading men of the municipality, younger than I. For he had studied under me, both when I began to teach in our town and afterward at Carthage; and he loved me much, because I seemed to him good and learned, and I loved him for the great natural bent toward virtue which, at no great age, stood out plainly enough.

Yet the whirlpool of Carthaginian morals, in which the trifling shows seethe, had swallowed him into a madness for the circus games. But while he was being wretchedly tossed in it, and I, professing rhetoric there, was conducting a public school, he was not yet hearing me as his teacher, because of a certain quarrel that had arisen between me and his father. And I had learned that he loved the circus ruinously, and I was gravely distressed, because he seemed about to lose—or even to have already lost—so great a hope.

But there was no means of warning him and recalling him by some restraint, neither the goodwill of friendship nor the right of a teacher. For I thought he felt about me as his father did; but he was not so. And so, setting aside his father's wish in this matter, he began to greet me, coming into my lecture-room, hearing something, and going away.

**12**. But it had slipped from my memory to deal with him, lest by a blind and headlong zeal for empty games he should destroy so good a talent. But you, Lord, who preside over the helm of all you have created, had not forgotten him, who was to be among your sons a bishop of your sacrament; and, that his correction might be openly ascribed to you, you worked it through me indeed, but unknowing. For one day, as I sat in my usual place and my pupils were present before me, he came, greeted me, sat down, and bent his mind to what was being treated.

And it happened that the text in hand was one which, while I expounded it, a comparison with the circus games seemed opportune to bring in, to make what I was insinuating both more pleasant and plainer, with a biting mockery of those whom that madness had taken captive. You know, our God[^40], that I was not then thinking of curing Alypius of that plague.

But he seized it to himself, and believed I had said it only for his sake; and what another would have taken as a reason to be angry with me, that honest young man took as a reason to be angry with himself, and to love me the more ardently. For you had said long ago, and woven it into your Scriptures: *Rebuke a wise man, and he will love you*[^41]. But I had not rebuked him; rather, using all, both the knowing and the unknowing, in the order which you know (and that order is just), out of my heart and tongue you made burning coals[^42], by which you might cauterize and heal the mind of good hope as it wasted away[^43].

Let him be silent of your praises who does not consider your mercies, which confess to you out of my marrow[^44]. For indeed, after those words, he flung himself out of that pit so deep, into which he was gladly sinking and being blinded with a wondrous pleasure; and he shook his mind with a strong temperance, and all the filth of the circus games sprang away from him, and he went there no more.

Then he prevailed over his reluctant father, that he might have me as his teacher: the father yielded and gave consent. And beginning to hear me again, he was wrapped up with me in that superstition, loving in the Manichees the ostentation of continence, which he thought true and genuine. But it was a senseless and seductive thing, snaring precious souls[^45] that did not yet know how to touch the height of virtue, and were easily deceived by the surface—the surface of a shadowed and counterfeit virtue.

### Chapter 8: Alypius swept away by the bloody pleasure of the circus games

**13**. Not, indeed, abandoning the earthly way enchanted upon him by his parents, he had gone ahead to Rome to study law; and there he was carried away, with an incredible eagerness and incredibly, by the craze for gladiatorial shows.

For when he was turning away from and detesting such things, certain of his friends and fellow students, meeting him by chance as they returned from dinner, with a friendly violence dragged him—vehemently refusing and resisting—into the amphitheater, on a day of those cruel and deadly games, while he said this: "Though you drag my body to that place and set it there, can you fasten my mind and my eyes on those shows? I shall be there, then, absent, and so I shall overcome both you and them."

Hearing this, they nonetheless brought him along with them, perhaps desiring to test this very thing, whether he could do it. When they had come and taken what seats they could, everything was seething with the most savage pleasures.

He, closing the doors of his eyes, forbade his mind to go forth into such evils. And would that he had stopped his ears too! For at a certain turn of the fight, when a huge shout of the whole crowd struck him violently, overcome by curiosity, and as if prepared to despise and overcome whatever it might be even when seen, he opened his eyes, and was struck with a graver wound in his soul than the man he longed to look at was in his body,

and he fell more wretchedly than that man at whose fall the shout arose—a shout that entered through his ears and unlocked his eyes, so that there might be a way to strike and cast down a mind still bold rather than strong, and the weaker for this, that it had presumed upon itself, when it ought to have presumed upon you[^46].

For as soon as he saw that blood, he drank in cruelty along with it, and did not turn away, but fixed his gaze and drank in the frenzy, and did not know it, and delighted in the wickedness of the contest, and was made drunk with the bloody pleasure. And he was no longer the man who had come, but one of the crowd to which he had come, and a true companion of those who had brought him. Why say more? He watched, he shouted, he caught fire, he carried away from there a madness by which he would be goaded to return—not only with those by whom he had first been dragged, but even ahead of them, and dragging others.

And yet from there, with a most strong and most merciful hand, you plucked him out, and taught him to have confidence not in himself but in you[^47]—but long afterward.

### Chapter 9: Alypius arrested at Carthage as a thief

**14**. Yet this too was now being stored up in his memory for a future medicine. For that other thing as well—that when he was still studying, by now hearing me at Carthage, and was thinking at midday in the forum about what he was to recite, as students are wont to practice, you allowed him to be seized by the guards of the forum as a thief—I do not think you permitted, our God, for any other reason than that the man who was to be so great might already begin to learn how a man is not lightly, in judging cases, to be condemned by another through rash credulity.

For he was walking alone before the tribunal with his tablets and stylus, when behold, a certain young man of the student set, a real thief, secretly bringing an axe, entered—Alypius not noticing—up to the leaden gratings that overhang the silversmiths' street, and began to cut the lead. At the sound of the axe the silversmiths below muttered, and sent men to seize whomever they might find. He, hearing their voices, dropped the tool and made off, fearing to be caught with it. But Alypius, who had not seen him enter, became aware of him going out, and quickly saw him departing; and wishing to know the reason, he entered the place, and found the axe, and stood considering and wondering at it, when behold, those who had been sent find him alone, holding the iron at whose sound they had been roused; they seize him, drag him off, and, the tenants of the forum being gathered, they boast that they have caught a thief red-handed; and from there he was being led off to be handed over to the courts.

**15**. But thus far was he to be taught. For at once, Lord, you came to the aid of his innocence, of which you alone were the witness[^48]. For as he was being led off either to prison or to punishment, a certain architect met them, who had the chief charge of the public buildings. They were glad to have met him especially, since he was the one to whom they used to come under suspicion of things stolen that had gone missing from the forum, as if now at last he might learn by whom these things were done.

But the man had often seen Alypius at the house of a certain senator, to whom he used to come to pay his respects; and recognizing him at once, he took him by the hand, drew him aside from the crowds, and, asking the cause of so great an evil, heard what had happened, and ordered all the rioters who were present, snarling threateningly, to come with him.

And they came to the house of that young man who had committed the deed. Now there was a boy before the door, so small that, fearing nothing thereby for his master, he could easily disclose the whole; for he had been with him in the forum as his attendant. When Alypius recalled this, he told the architect; and the architect showed the axe to the boy, asking him whose it was. He at once said, "Ours"; then, being questioned, he disclosed the rest.

So, the case being transferred to that house, and the crowds confounded who had already begun to triumph over him, the future steward of your word and examiner of many cases in your Church departed more experienced and better instructed.

### Chapter 10: Alypius at Rome discharged the office of assessor with integrity

**16**. This man, then, I had found at Rome, and he clung to me with the strongest bond, and set out with me to Milan, both that he might not desert me and that he might do something with the law he had learned—more to satisfy his parents' wish than his own. And he had now three times sat as assessor, with a continence that astonished the rest, while he himself rather marveled at those who would prefer gold to innocence.

His character was tested, too, not only by the allurement of greed but also by the goad of fear. At Rome he was assessor to the count of the Italian treasury. There was at that time a certain most powerful senator, by whose benefits many were bound and to whose terror many were subject. He wished, by the customary license of his power, to have something allowed him that was unlawful by the laws; Alypius resisted. A reward was promised; he laughed at it in his heart. Threats were held out; he trod them underfoot—while all marveled at so unusual a soul, which neither desired as a friend nor dreaded as an enemy a man so great, and celebrated with vast fame for his countless ways of helping and harming.

But the very judge whose counselor he was, although he too did not wish it done, yet did not openly refuse, but, transferring the matter onto Alypius, asserted that he was not permitted by him—since in truth, if the judge had done it, Alypius would have withdrawn.

By this one thing alone was he almost enticed, through his love of literature: that he might have manuscripts copied for himself at praetorian prices. But, having consulted justice, he turned his deliberation to the better, judging the equity that forbade more useful than the power that permitted. This is a small thing; but *he who is faithful in a little is faithful also in much*[^49]; nor will that be void in any way which proceeded from the mouth of your truth[^50]: *If you have not been faithful in the unrighteous mammon, who will entrust to you what is true? And if you have not been faithful in what is another's, who will give you what is your own?*[^51]

Such was the man who then clung to me, and wavered with me in counsel as to what manner of life was to be held.

## Nebridius too came to Milan to live with Augustine

**17**. Nebridius also—who, leaving his homeland near Carthage, and Carthage itself, where he was very often, leaving his excellent paternal estate, leaving his home and a mother who would not follow him, had come to Milan for no other reason than to live with me in a most ardent zeal for truth and wisdom—sighed alike and wavered alike, a burning seeker of the happy life and a most keen scrutinizer of the most difficult questions. And there were the mouths of three needy men, breathing out their want to one another, and waiting upon you, that you might give them *food in due season*[^52].

And in all the bitterness that followed our worldly acts by your mercy, as we looked toward the end—why we suffered these things—darkness met us, and we turned away groaning, and said, "How long these things?" And this we said often, and saying it we did not leave them, because nothing certain shone forth which, leaving them, we might lay hold of.

## Augustine seethes in doubt and hangs in suspense

### Chapter 11

**18**. And I marveled most of all, busily recalling how long a time it was from the nineteenth year of my age, in which I had begun to burn with zeal for wisdom, resolving, once it was found, to leave behind all the empty hopes of vain desires and the lying madnesses[^53]. And behold, I was now bearing my thirtieth year, stuck in the same mire through a greed for enjoying things present, which were fleeing and scattering me, while I said, "Tomorrow I shall find it; behold, it will appear plainly, and I shall hold it; behold, Faustus will come and explain everything.

"O great men, the Academics! Nothing for the conduct of life can be grasped as certain[^54]. No—let us seek the more diligently, and not despair. Behold, the things in the Church's books that seemed absurd are now not absurd, and can be understood otherwise and honorably. I will fix my feet on that step where, as a boy, I was placed by my parents, until the clear truth be found.

"But where shall it be sought? When shall it be sought? Ambrose has no leisure; there is no leisure for reading. Where do we look for the very manuscripts? From where, or when, do we obtain them? From whom do we get them?

"Let times be appointed, let hours be apportioned, for the health of the soul[^55]. A great hope has arisen: the catholic faith does not teach what we thought, and vainly accused. Its learned ones count it a sin to believe God bounded by the figure of a human body. And do we hesitate to knock, that the rest may be opened[^56]? The forenoon hours my pupils occupy; what do we do with the rest? Why do we not do this?

"But when do we pay our respects to our greater friends, whose support we need? When do we prepare what the students buy? When do we restore ourselves, relaxing the mind from the strain of cares?"

**19**. "Let all things perish, and let us dismiss these vain and empty things; let us betake ourselves to the search for truth alone. Life is wretched, death is uncertain; it may steal upon us suddenly: how shall we depart hence? And where are we to learn what here we have neglected? Or rather, must we not pay the penalties of this neglect?

"What if death itself cuts off and ends all care, together with feeling? This too, then, must be inquired into.

"But far be it that it should be so. It is not in vain, not empty, that so eminent a height of the authority of the Christian faith is spread throughout the whole world. Never would such great things be done for us divinely, if with the death of the body the life of the soul were also consumed. Why, then, do we delay to abandon the hope of the world and betake ourselves wholly to seeking God and the happy life?

"But wait: these things too are pleasant; they have no small sweetness of their own; the attention must not easily be cut off from them, because it is shameful to return to them again. See, how much it is, that some honor be obtained. And what more is to be desired in these things? There is a supply of greater friends: if nothing else, and we hurry hard, a governorship may be granted. And a wife must be taken with some money, lest she burden our expense, and that will be the limit of desire. Many great men, most worthy of imitation, have given themselves to the study of wisdom together with wives."

**20**. While I said these things, and these winds shifted and drove my heart this way and that, the times passed on, and I was slow *to turn to the Lord*, and put off *from day to day*[^57] living in you, and did not put off dying daily in myself; loving the happy life, I feared it in its own seat, and, fleeing from it, I sought it.

For I thought I should be too wretched if I were deprived of a woman's embraces, and I did not think of the medicine of your mercy[^58] to heal that very weakness, because I had not tried it, and believed continence to be a matter of one's own strength, of which I was not conscious in myself—being so foolish that I did not know that, as it is written, no one can be continent unless you grant it[^59]. And you would surely have granted it, if with an inward groan I had knocked at your ears[^60], and with solid faith had cast my care upon you[^61].

### Chapter 12: Augustine did not despise marriage, and Alypius himself came to desire it

**21**. Alypius, indeed, kept holding me back from taking a wife, chanting that in no way could we live together in carefree leisure in the love of wisdom, as we had long desired, if I did so. For he himself was even then most chaste in that matter, so that it was a wonder—since at the entrance of his youth he had taken some experience of intercourse, but had not clung to it, but rather grieved over it and despised it, and from then on lived most continently.

But I resisted him with the examples of those who, married, had cultivated wisdom and deserved well of God[^62], and had kept faith with their friends and loved them. From the greatness of soul of these men I was far indeed; and, bound by the disease of the flesh, with its deadly sweetness I dragged my chain, afraid to be loosed, and—as if my wound had been struck—I repelled the words of one counseling me well, as the hand of one loosing my bonds. Moreover, through me the serpent spoke[^63] to Alypius too, weaving and scattering through my tongue sweet snares in his path[^64], in which his honorable and unfettered feet might be entangled.

**22**. For when he marveled that I, whom he esteemed not slightly, should so stick to the birdlime of that pleasure as to affirm, whenever we discussed it between us, that I could in no way lead a celibate life—and when, seeing him marvel, I defended myself by saying that there was a great difference between what he had taken hastily and by stealth, which he now scarcely even remembered and therefore easily despised without any trouble, and the delights of my habit, to which, if the honorable name of marriage were added, he ought not to wonder why I could not despise that life—he too began to desire marriage, in no way overcome by the lust of such pleasure, but by curiosity.

For he said that he wanted to know what that thing was, without which my life, which so pleased him, seemed to me not life but a punishment. For his mind, free from that bond, was astonished at my servitude; and by its astonishment it was going on into a desire of trying it, and would come into the experience itself, and from there perhaps would fall into that very servitude at which he was astonished—since he was willing to make a covenant with death[^65], and he who loves danger will fall into it[^66].

For neither of us was drawn, except slightly, by whatever marital honor there is in the office of governing a marriage and rearing children. But in great part, and vehemently, the habit of sating an insatiable concupiscence held me captive and tormented, while astonishment was dragging him along to be captured.

Such we were, until you, O Most High[^67], not forsaking our dust, in mercy came to the aid of us wretched ones in wonderful and hidden ways.

## A girl not yet of age was betrothed to Augustine

### Chapter 13

**23**. And I was pressed unrelentingly to take a wife. Already I was asking, already it was being promised, my mother especially taking pains, so that, once married, the saving baptism might wash me—for which she rejoiced to see me being fitted day by day, and noticed that her vows and your promises were being fulfilled in my faith. And although, both at my request and out of her own strong desire, she daily beseeched you with a great cry of her heart to show her something through a vision about my future marriage, you never willed it.

And she saw certain vain and fantastic things, such as the press of the human spirit, busy about this matter, forced up; and she told them to me, not with the confidence she usually had when you showed her things, but despising them. For she said that she could discern, by some savor she could not put into words, the difference between you revealing and her own soul dreaming.

Yet the matter was pressed on, and the girl was asked for, whose age was nearly two years short of marriageable; and because she was pleasing, she was waited for.

## Augustine and his friends planned to form a community, which did not come to be

### Chapter 14

**24**. And many of us friends had turned it over in mind, and, conferring and detesting the turbulent troubles of human life, had almost settled to live apart from the crowds at leisure—planning that leisure thus: that whatever we could have, we would bring into a common store, and out of all make one household,

so that, through the sincerity of friendship, there should not be one thing belonging to this man and another to that, but what was made out of all should be one, and the whole belong to each, and all things to all,

since it seemed to us that there could be perhaps ten men in the same fellowship; and there were among us some very rich men, especially Romanianus, our townsman, whom heavy storms of his own affairs had then drawn to court, most intimate with me from my earliest years. He pressed this matter most of all, and had great authority in urging it, because his ample wealth far surpassed the rest.

And it had pleased us that two each year, like magistrates, should see to all that was needed, while the rest were at rest. But after it began to be considered whether the little wives—whom some of us already had, and we others wished to have—would allow this, that whole plan, which we were forming so well, fell to pieces in our hands, and was broken and cast aside. From there it was back to sighs and groans, and our steps to following the broad and beaten ways of the world[^68]; for *many were the thoughts in our heart*[^69], *but your counsel abides forever*[^70]. By that counsel you laughed at ours, and prepared your own for us, ready to give us *food in due season, and to open your hand and fill our souls with blessing*[^71].

## His concubine, by whom he had had a son, having departed to Africa, Augustine procured another woman

### Chapter 15

**25**. Meanwhile my sins were being multiplied[^72], and when she with whom I was accustomed to share my bed was torn from my side as a hindrance to marriage, my heart, where it clung to her, was cut and wounded, and trailed blood. And she had returned to Africa, vowing to you that she would know no other man[^73], leaving with me the natural son I had had by her.

But I, unhappy, no imitator of a woman, impatient of delay—since it was only after two years that I was to receive the girl I sought—because I was no lover of marriage but a slave of lust, procured another woman, no wife of course, so that, as it were, the disease of my soul might be sustained and carried on, whole or even increased, under the escort of an enduring habit, into the kingdom of marriage.

Nor was that wound of mine healed which had been made by the cutting away of my former companion; but after the burning and the sharpest pain it festered, and pained, as it were, the more coldly, but the more hopelessly.

### Chapter 16: A discussion with Alypius and Nebridius on the ends of good and evil

**26**. To you be praise, to you be glory[^74], fountain of mercies! I was becoming more wretched, and you nearer. Already, again and again, your right hand was at hand to snatch me from the mire[^75] and wash me away, and I did not know it. Nor did anything call me back from a deeper whirlpool of carnal pleasures except the fear of death and of your judgment to come, which, through various opinions indeed, yet never departed from my breast.

And I disputed with my friends Alypius and Nebridius about the ends of good and evil[^76]. Epicurus would have carried off the palm in my mind, had I not believed that after death there remains life for the soul, and the consequences of merit—which Epicurus refused to believe.

And I asked: if we were immortal, and lived in perpetual bodily pleasure with no fear of losing it, why should we not be happy, or what else should we seek?—not knowing that this very thing belonged to great misery: that, so sunk and blind, I could not conceive the light of integrity and of a beauty to be embraced for its own sake, which the eye of the flesh does not see, and which is seen from within.

Nor did I, wretched, consider from what vein it flowed to me that even these foul things I yet discussed sweetly with friends, and could not be happy without friends—even according to the sense I then had—amid however great an abundance of carnal pleasures. These friends, surely, I loved freely, and freely I felt myself loved by them in turn.

O twisting ways! *Woe to the audacious soul*[^77] that hoped, if it withdrew from you, it would have something better! It turned and turned again, onto its back and sides and belly, and all was hard, and you alone are rest. And behold, you are at hand[^78], and you free us from our wretched errors, and set us on your way[^79], and console us, and say: "Run[^80], *I will carry you*[^81], and I will bring you through, and there too *I will carry you*."
