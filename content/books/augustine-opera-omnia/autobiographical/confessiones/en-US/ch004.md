# Book 3: Given Over to Honorable Studies at Carthage

## Student amusements

### Chapter 1

**1**. I came to Carthage, and a cauldron of shameful loves crackled all about me. I was not yet in love, but I was in love with loving, and out of a more hidden want I hated myself for wanting too little.

I sought what I might love, in love with loving; and I hated safety and a path without snares[^1], for within me there was a hunger from the inner food, you yourself, my God—and by that hunger I was not hungry, but was without any desire for incorruptible nourishment: not because I was full of it, but the emptier I was, the more I loathed it.

And for that reason my soul was not in good health, and, covered with sores, it flung itself outward, miserably eager to be scratched by the touch of sensible things. Yet if these had no soul, they would surely not be loved. To love and to be loved was the sweeter to me if I might also enjoy the body of the one who loved me. So I fouled the spring of friendship with the filth of concupiscence, and I clouded its brightness with the hell of lust; and yet, foul and dishonorable as I was, in my overflowing vanity I was eager to be thought elegant and urbane.

I rushed headlong into the love by which I longed to be caught. *My God, my mercy*[^2], with how much gall, in your goodness, you sprinkled that sweetness for me! For I was loved, and I came secretly to the bond of enjoyment, and I was joyfully bound with troublesome bonds, only to be beaten afterward with the burning iron rods[^3] of jealousy and suspicions and fears and angers and quarrels[^4].

### Chapter 2: An insane craving for theatrical shows

**2**. Theatrical shows carried me away, full of images of my own miseries and of tinder for my fire.

Why is it that a man wants to grieve there, watching mournful and tragic things that he himself would not want to suffer? And yet the spectator does want to feel grief from them, and that very grief is his pleasure.

What is this but a pitiable madness? For the more a man is moved by such things, the less healthy he is from such feelings—though when he suffers them himself it is usually called misery, and when he suffers them with others, compassion.

But what sort of compassion, after all, is there in things feigned and staged? For the listener is not called on to help, but only invited to grieve; and he favors the author of those images the more, the more he grieves. And if those calamities of men—whether ancient or false—are so acted that the spectator does not grieve, he goes away disgusted and faultfinding; but if he grieves, he stays intent and weeps with joy.

#### Tears and sorrows are sometimes loved by men

**3**. So tears and sorrows are loved. Surely every man wants to rejoice. Or is it that, while no one likes to be miserable, one does like to be compassionate—and since this cannot be without grief, is it for this one reason alone that sorrows are loved?

This too is from that spring of friendship. But where does it go? Where does it flow? Why does it run down into a torrent of boiling pitch, the monstrous surges of foul lusts, into which it is itself changed and turned, by its own choice twisted away and cast down from heavenly serenity?

Is compassion, then, to be rejected? By no means. So let sorrows sometimes be loved. But beware uncleanness, O my soul, under the guardianship of my God, the God of our fathers, to be praised and exalted above all forever[^5]—beware uncleanness.

For even now I do not fail to feel compassion; but then, in the theaters, I rejoiced with lovers when they wickedly enjoyed one another, although they were doing this only in the make-believe of a show; and when they lost one another, I grew sad, as if in compassion; and yet in both I took delight.

But now I have more pity for one who rejoices in wickedness than for one who suffers, as it were, hard usage by the loss of some ruinous pleasure or the forfeit of a wretched happiness. This, surely, is the truer compassion; but in it grief gives no delight.

For although one who grieves for the wretched is approved for the office of love, still anyone who is genuinely compassionate would prefer that there be nothing to grieve over.

For if there were a malevolent benevolence—which cannot be—then one who truly and sincerely felt compassion could wish that there be wretched people, so that he might pity them.

Some sorrow, therefore, may be approved; none is to be loved. For this is how you, O Lord God, who love souls, feel compassion—far more purely and incorruptibly than we, since you are wounded by no sorrow. *And who is sufficient for these things?*[^6]

#### Then, wretched, he loved to grieve

**4**. But I then, wretch that I was, loved to grieve, and I sought something to grieve over, when, in another's misery—a feigned and theatrical misery—the actor's performance pleased me the more and drew me the more strongly, the more it wrung tears from me. What wonder that I, an unhappy sheep[^7] straying from your flock[^8] and impatient of your keeping, was disfigured by a foul mange[^9]?

And from this came my loves of sorrows—not such as would pierce me deeply (for I did not love to suffer such things as I loved to watch), but such as, heard and feigned, would scratch me as on the surface; yet, like the nails of those who scratch, a burning swelling and corruption and horrid pus followed after. Such was my life—was it any life at all, my God?

### Chapter 3: God's mercy did not abandon him even amid grave punishments

**5**. And your faithful mercy hovered over me from afar. Into what great iniquities did I waste away! I followed a sacrilegious curiosity, so that, deserting you, it might bring me down to the treacherous depths and the beguiling services of demons, to whom I sacrificed[^10] my evil deeds; and in all of it you scourged me[^11]!

I even dared, amid the celebration of your solemnities, within the walls of your church, to lust and to set about the business of procuring the fruits of death[^12]; for which you struck me with grave punishments, though nothing compared to my guilt, O you my exceedingly great *mercy, my God, my refuge*[^13] from the terrible powers that do harm, among whom I wandered with stiff neck, withdrawing far from you, loving my own ways and not yours, loving a fugitive liberty.

## He always shrank from the disruptions of his fellow students

**6**. Those studies too, which were called honorable, had their aim set on the wrangling of the lawcourts[^14], that I might excel in them, the more praised the more deceitful. So great is the blindness of men, who even glory in their blindness. And I was now senior in the school of rhetoric, and I was proudly glad and swollen with conceit—though far quieter, *Lord, you know*[^15], and wholly removed from the disruptions that the "wreckers" carried out (for that sinister and devilish name passed as a badge of sophistication), among whom I lived with a shameless shame, because I was not such as they. And I was with them, and at times took delight in their friendships, though I always shrank from their deeds—that is, from the disruptions with which they would wantonly harass the modesty of strangers, troubling it by gratuitous mockery and feeding on it their own malicious mirth. Nothing is more like the doings of demons than that conduct. What, then, could be truer than that they should be called wreckers—wrecked themselves, plainly, and perverted first, by the deceiving spirits who secretly mock and seduce them in the very thing in which they love to mock and deceive others?

## He reads Cicero's Hortensius, which changes his affections

### Chapter 4

**7**. Among such men, at that feeble age, I was learning books of eloquence, in which I longed to excel for a damnable and windy end, in the joys of human vanity; and following the usual order of study, I had come to a book of a certain Cicero, whose tongue nearly everyone admires, though not so his heart. But that book of his contains an exhortation to philosophy, and is called the *Hortensius*.

That book, indeed, changed my affections, and changed my prayers to you yourself, Lord, and made my vows and desires other than they had been. Suddenly every empty hope grew worthless to me, and with an incredible burning of heart I longed for the immortality of wisdom, and I began to rise up that I might return to you[^16].

For it was not to sharpen my tongue—which is what I seemed to be buying with my mother's payments, since I was in my nineteenth year, my father having died two years before—it was not, then, to sharpen my tongue that I applied that book; it had won me over not by its manner of speaking, but by what it said.

**8**. How I burned, my God, how I burned to fly back from earthly things to you, and I did not know what you were doing with me! *For with you is wisdom*[^17]. But the love of wisdom has the Greek name "philosophy," with which those writings set me on fire. There are some who seduce by way of philosophy, coloring and rouging their errors with a great and flattering and honorable name; and nearly all who in those times and before were of that sort are marked and pointed out in that book; and there is made plain that saving warning of your Spirit through your good and devout servant: *See that no one deceives you through philosophy and empty seduction, according to the tradition of men, according to the elements of this world, and not according to Christ; for in him dwells all the fullness of the godhead bodily*[^18].

And I at that time—*you know*[^19], light of my heart—since these apostolic words were not yet known to me, took delight in that exhortation on this account alone: that it stirred me, kindled me, and set me ablaze to love and seek and attain and hold and embrace with all my strength not this or that sect, but wisdom itself, whatever it might be. And one thing only checked me in so great a blaze—that the name of Christ was not there. For this name, *according to your mercy, Lord*[^20], this name of my Savior, your Son, my tender heart had devoutly drunk in with my mother's very milk and held deep within; and whatever lacked this name, however learned and polished and truthful, could not wholly carry me away.

## He began to read the holy Scriptures and despised them

### Chapter 5

**9**. And so I resolved to turn my attention to the holy Scriptures and to see what they were like. And behold, I see a thing not grasped by the proud, nor laid bare to children[^21], but lowly in its gait, lofty in its advance, and veiled in mysteries; and I was not such as could enter into it or bend my neck to its steps.

For I did not feel, when I turned my attention to that Scripture, as I now speak; rather, it seemed to me unworthy to be compared with the dignity of Cicero. For my swelling pride recoiled from its manner, and my sharp gaze did not penetrate its inner parts[^22]. Yet it was the kind of thing that grows up with little ones; but I disdained to be a little one[^23], and, puffed up with pride, I seemed to myself a grown man.

## He took up the teaching of the Manichees

### Chapter 6: While seeking truth, he stumbled into falsehoods

**10**. And so I fell in among men proudly raving, exceedingly carnal and wordy, in whose mouths were the snares of the devil[^24] and a birdlime compounded by mixing together the syllables of your name, and of the Lord Jesus Christ, and of the Paraclete, our comforter, the Holy Spirit[^25]. These names did not leave their mouths[^26], but only as far as the sound and the rattle of the tongue; for the rest, their heart was empty of truth.

And they kept saying, "Truth, truth," and said it to me often, and nowhere was it in them; rather, they spoke falsehoods—not only about you, who truly are Truth[^27], but even about these elements of this world, your creation, concerning which I ought to have passed beyond even the philosophers who spoke true things, for love of you, my Father, supremely good, beauty of all things beautiful.

O Truth, Truth, how inwardly even then did the very marrow of my mind sigh for you, while they sounded your name to me often and in manifold ways, with the voice alone and in many huge books! And these were the dishes on which, while I hungered for you, they served me, in your place, the sun and the moon—your beautiful works, but works of yours all the same, not you yourself, nor even your first works. For your spiritual works come before these bodily ones, however shining and heavenly.

But I hungered and thirsted[^28] not even for those prior works, but for you yourself, O Truth, in whom *there is no change, nor shadow of alteration*[^29]. And still they set before me, on those dishes, splendid phantasms—better than which it would have been to love this very sun, at least real to these eyes, than those false things that deceived the mind through the eyes.

And yet, because I supposed them to be you, I ate—not greedily, indeed, for you did not taste in my mouth as you are (nor were you those empty figments), nor was I nourished by them, but rather drained the more. Food in dreams is exactly like the food of waking men, yet sleepers are not nourished by it: for they are asleep.

But those things were in no way like you, as you have now spoken to me, because they were bodily phantasms, false bodies; more certain than these are the real bodies that we see with the eye of the flesh, whether heavenly or earthly. We see these along with the beasts and the birds, and they are more certain than when we imagine them.

And again, we imagine them more certainly than, from them, we conjecture other things greater and infinite, which have no existence at all. On such empty things was I then fed, and was not fed.

But you, my love, for whom I faint[^30] that I may be strong[^31], are neither those bodies that we see, though in heaven, nor those that we do not see there, because you founded them and do not hold them among your highest works. How far, then, are you from those phantasms of mine, the phantasms of bodies that do not exist at all!

More certain than these are the images of those bodies that do exist; and more certain than the images are the bodies themselves, which, even so, you are not. Nor are you the soul, which is the life of bodies (and so the life of bodies is better and more certain than the bodies); but you are the life of souls, the life of lives, living of your own self, and you do not change[^32], O life of my soul[^33].

**11**. Where, then, were you to me at that time, and how far away? Far indeed was I wandering, an exile from you, and barred even from the husks of the pigs that I fed with husks[^34]. For how much better were the fables of the grammarians and poets than those traps!

For verse and song, and "Medea flying,"[^35] are surely more useful than the five elements, disguised in various ways on account of the five caves of darkness—which have no existence at all and slay the one who believes in them.

For verse and song I can even turn to real nourishment; but as for "Medea flying," though I would chant it, I did not assert it, and though I heard it chanted, I did not believe it. But those other things I did believe. Woe, woe!

By what steps was I led down into the depths of hell[^36], toiling and seething for want of truth—since I sought you, my God (for to you I confess, who had mercy on me even when I did not yet confess), I sought you not according to the understanding of the mind, by which you willed me to surpass the beasts, but according to the sense of the flesh! But you were more inward than my inmost self, and higher than my highest.

I had stumbled upon that bold woman, devoid of prudence, the riddle of Solomon, sitting on a stool at the door and saying, *Eat hidden bread gladly, and drink sweet stolen water*[^37]. She seduced me, because she found me dwelling abroad in the eye of my flesh and chewing over within myself such things as I had devoured through it.

### Chapter 7: The Manichees, foolish deceivers

**12**. For I did not know that other reality, which truly is, and I was as if shrewdly moved to side with those foolish deceivers when they asked me where evil comes from, and whether God is bounded by a bodily form and has hair and nails, and whether those were to be reckoned righteous who had many wives at once, and killed men, and offered sacrifices of animals.

Ignorant of these matters, I was troubled, and, withdrawing from the truth, I seemed to myself to be going toward it, because I did not yet know that evil is nothing but the privation of good, down to that which is not at all. How was I to see this, whose seeing reached, with the eyes, only as far as body, and, with the mind, only as far as a phantasm?

And I did not know that God is a spirit[^38], who has no members extended in length and breadth, and whose being is not a mass; for a mass is less in a part than in its whole, and even if it be infinite, it is less in any part bounded by a fixed space than throughout the infinite, and is not wholly everywhere, as a spirit is, as God is.

And what it was in us according to which we are, and are rightly said in Scripture to be *in the image of God*[^39], I was utterly ignorant.

#### Different things are permitted to men at different times

**13**. And I did not know the true inner justice, which judges not from custom but from the most upright law of God almighty—the law by which the morals of regions and days were shaped to suit those regions and days, while the law itself was everywhere and always the same, not one thing in one place and another elsewhere, nor one thing at one time and otherwise at another;

according to which Abraham and Isaac and Jacob and Moses and David, and all those praised by the mouth of God, were righteous—though they are judged unrighteous by the ignorant, who judge *by a human day*[^40] and measure all the morals of the human race by the standard of their own.

It is as if someone ignorant of armor, of what is fitted to which limb, should want the head covered with a greave and the foot shod with a helmet, and then grumble that they do not fit well;

or as if, on a single day when a court recess has been declared from the afternoon hours, someone should be annoyed that he is not allowed to put goods up for sale, because it was allowed in the morning;

or as if, in one house, he should see something handled by one servant which the one who serves the cups is not permitted to do, or something done behind the stalls which is forbidden before the table, and should be indignant that, though it is one dwelling and one household, the same thing is not granted everywhere and to everyone.

Such are those who are indignant when they hear that something was permitted to the righteous in that earlier age which is not permitted to the righteous in this one, and that God commanded one thing to those and another to these for reasons belonging to the times, although both served the same justice;

while in one man, and in one day, and in one house, they see that one thing suits one limb and another another, that what was allowed a while ago is not allowed after an hour, and that something is permitted or commanded in that corner which is forbidden and punished in the one next to it.

Is justice, then, variable and changeable[^41]? No—but the times over which it presides do not run all alike; for they are times. But men, whose life on earth is short[^42], because they cannot by their senses connect the causes of earlier ages and of other nations, which they have not experienced, with those they have experienced—while in one body, or day, or house they can easily see what suits which limb, which moment, which parts or persons—take offense at the former, but submit to the latter.

**14**. These things I then did not know, and did not notice, and they struck my eyes on every side, and I did not see.

I would sing songs, and I was not allowed to place just any foot anywhere, but in one meter one way and in another differently, and even in some single verse not the same foot in every place; and the very art by which I sang did not have one rule in one place and another elsewhere, but all its rules together at once.

And I did not perceive that the justice which good and holy men served held, far more excellently and sublimely, all that it prescribes together at once, and is in no part varied, and yet, at various times, did not distribute everything at once, but apportioned and prescribed what was proper to each.

And, blind, I blamed the devout fathers, not only for using present things as God commanded and inspired, but also for foretelling things to come as God revealed them.

### Chapter 8: Everywhere and always, deeds against nature are to be detested, and the Ruler is to be served

**15**. Is it ever, or anywhere, unjust to love God *with the whole heart and with the whole soul and with the whole mind*, and to love *your neighbor as yourself*[^43]? Therefore disgraceful deeds that are against nature are everywhere and always to be detested and punished, such as those of the men of Sodom were. Even if all nations should do them, they would be held guilty of the same crime by the divine law, which did not make men so as to use one another in that way. For the very fellowship that ought to be between us and God is violated when that same nature, of which he is the author, is polluted by the perversity of lust.

But deeds that are disgraceful against the customs of men are to be avoided according to the diversity of customs, so that an agreement established among themselves by a city or nation, whether by custom or by law, may not be violated by the lust of any citizen or stranger. For any part that does not harmonize with its whole is base.

But when God commands something against the custom or compact of any people, even if it has never been done there, it must be done; and if it was neglected, it must be restored; and if it had not been established, it must be established.

For if a king is permitted, in the city over which he reigns, to command something that neither anyone before him nor he himself had ever commanded, and obeying him is not against the fellowship of his city—indeed, not to obey him is against that fellowship (for it is a general compact of human society to obey one's kings)—how much more must God, the Ruler of all his creation, be served without hesitation in whatever he commands! For just as among the powers of human society the greater power is set above the lesser to be obeyed, so is God above all.

**16**. Likewise in crimes, where there is a lust to do harm, whether by insult or by injury, and either of these for the sake of revenge, as an enemy against an enemy;

or to obtain some advantage outside oneself, as a robber against a traveler; or to avoid an evil, as against one who is feared;

or out of envy, as a more wretched man against a happier one, or one prosperous in some respect against one whom he fears to be made his equal, or grieves to be already his equal;

or for the sole pleasure of another's harm, as the spectators of gladiators, or the mockers and scoffers of anyone at all.

These are the chief kinds of iniquity, which sprout from the lust of domineering, of looking, and of sensual feeling[^44]—from one, or two of them, or all together; and men live evilly against the three and the seven, the psaltery of ten strings[^45], your Decalogue, O God most high[^46] and most sweet.

But what disgraceful deeds can there be against you, who cannot be corrupted? Or what crimes against you, who cannot be harmed? But you avenge what men perpetrate against themselves, because even when they sin against you, they act impiously against their own souls, and their iniquity lies to itself[^47]—whether by corrupting and perverting their nature, which you made and ordered, or by using immoderately things allowed, or by blazing after things not allowed, *in that use which is against nature*[^48];

or they are held guilty when they rage in mind and word against you, and *kick against the goad*[^49]; or when, the bounds of human society broken, they take bold delight in private leagues or feuds, according as anything has pleased or offended them. And these things are done when you are forsaken, O fountain of life[^50], the one and true Creator and ruler of the universe, and when, by private pride, a single false thing is loved in a part.

And so, by humble devotion we return to you, and you cleanse us from evil custom, and are merciful to the sins[^51] of those who confess, and you hear *the groaning of the prisoners*[^52] and loose us from the bonds we have made for ourselves—if only we no longer lift up against you the horns[^53] of a false liberty, through avarice to have more and the risk of losing all, by loving our own private good more than you, the good of all.

### Chapter 9: Many things men disapprove, God approves; and many are praised that God condemns

**17**. But amid disgraceful deeds and crimes and so many iniquities, there are the sins of those making progress, which, by those who judge well, are both blamed by the rule of perfection and praised in the hope of harvest, like the blade of standing grain.

And there are some things like a disgraceful deed or a crime, and yet they are not sins, because they offend neither you, our Lord God, nor the social fellowship—as when something is procured for the use of life, suited to the time (though it is uncertain whether out of a lust for having), or is punished out of zeal to correct, by an ordered authority (and it is uncertain whether out of a lust to do harm).

Many deeds, therefore, that would seem to men to deserve disapproval are approved by your testimony, and many praised by men are condemned with you as witness—since often the appearance of a deed is one thing, and the mind of the doer and the hidden articulation of the moment are another.

But when you suddenly command something unusual and unforeseen, even if you once forbade it, though for the time you conceal the reason for your command, and though it be against the compact of some men's society—who can doubt that it must be done, since that society of men is just which serves you? But blessed are those who know that you have commanded it. For all things are done by those who serve you either to display what the present need requires, or to foretell things to come.

### Chapter 10: What foolish and ridiculous things the Manichees proclaim

**18**. Not knowing these things, I mocked those holy servants and prophets of yours. And what was I doing in mocking them, but earning your mockery of me, as little by little I was led on to such trifles that I believed a fig wept when it was plucked, and its mother, the tree, with milky tears?

Yet if some holy one—by another's crime, of course, not his own—should eat that fig once it was plucked, he would mingle it with his vitals and breathe out from it angels; indeed, he would belch out particles of God by groaning in prayer—particles of the most high and true God which would have stayed bound in that fruit, had they not been released by the tooth and belly of an "elect" holy one.

And I, wretched, believed that more mercy was owed[^54] to the fruits of the earth than to the human beings for whose sake they are born. For if anyone hungry, who was not a Manichee, were to ask for a morsel, it seemed the mouthful deserved to be condemned, as it were, to capital punishment, if it were given to him.

## Monica saw a dream that showed her, long before, her son's conversion

### Chapter 11

**19**. And you sent your hand from on high[^55], and *you drew my soul*[^56] out of this deep darkness, when my mother, your faithful one, wept for me to you more than mothers weep over bodily deaths. For by the faith and the spirit[^57] that she had from you, she saw my death; and you heard her, Lord. You heard her, and did not despise her tears, when, streaming down, they watered the ground beneath her eyes in every place of her prayer: you heard her.

For whence came that dream by which you consoled her, so that she agreed to live with me and to share the same table in the house?—which she had begun to refuse, turning away from and detesting the blasphemies of my error.

For she saw herself standing on a certain wooden rule, and a radiant youth coming to her, cheerful and smiling upon her, while she was mournful and worn down with grief[^58]. When he asked her the reasons for her sadness and her daily tears—asking, as is the way, to teach, not to learn—and she answered that she was lamenting my perdition, he bade her be at ease, and advised her to look and see[^59] that where she was, there was I also. And when she looked, she saw me standing beside her on the same rule.

Whence came this, except that your ears were turned to her heart[^60], O you good and almighty one, who so care for each one of us as though you cared for him alone, and so for all as for each?

**20**. And whence also came this: that when she had told me the vision, and I tried to twist it to mean that she rather should not despair of one day being what I was, she said at once, without any hesitation, "No—for it was not said to me, 'Where he is, there shall you be also,' but, 'Where you are, there shall he be also.'" I confess to you, Lord, my remembrance—so far as I recall it, and I have often spoken of it—that I was more moved by that answer of yours, given through my watchful mother (who was not troubled by the falsity of so plausible an interpretation, and so quickly saw what was to be seen, which I certainly had not seen before she said it), than by the dream itself, by which the joy of that devout woman, to come so long afterward, was foretold so long before for the consolation of her present anxiety.

For nearly nine years followed, in which I wallowed *in the deep mire*[^61] and the darkness of falsehood, often trying to rise but being dashed down the harder; while all the while that chaste, devout, and sober widow[^62]—such as you love—now indeed more cheerful in hope, but no less unflagging in weeping and groaning, did not cease at every hour of her prayers to lament over me to you; and *her prayers entered into your presence*[^63], and yet you still let me go on being rolled and wrapped about in that darkness.

## Another answer of God, given to Monica through a certain bishop

### Chapter 12

**21**. And meanwhile you gave another answer, which I recall. For I pass over many things, because I hasten to those that more press me to confess to you, and many I do not remember. You gave, then, another answer through your priest, a certain bishop nourished in the Church and well exercised in your books.

When that woman begged him to deign to talk with me, and to refute my errors and unteach me evil and teach me good (for he used to do this, when he chanced to find people fit for it), he refused—wisely indeed, as I afterward understood. For he answered that I was as yet unteachable, because I was puffed up with the novelty of that heresy and had already unsettled many untrained people with some petty little questions, as she had told him. "But," he said, "let him be where he is. Only pray to the Lord for him; he will himself, by reading, discover what that error is and how great its impiety."

At the same time he told her that he too, as a small boy, had been handed over by his seduced mother to the Manichees, and had not only read but even copied out almost all their books; and it had become clear to him, with no one arguing against him or convincing him, how greatly that sect was to be fled: and so he had fled it.

When he had said this, and she would not be content, but pressed the more with entreaties and abundant tears, that he would see me and discuss things with me, he, now a little vexed with weariness, said, "Go away from me; as you live, it cannot be that the son of these tears should perish." Which words she often recalled, in her conversations with me, that she had taken as though they had sounded from heaven.
