# Book 8: Conversion to the One True God

## The conversation with Simplicianus

### Chapter 1: Simplicianus, a servant of God

**1**. My God, let me remember, in thanksgiving to you, and confess your mercies upon me[^1]. Let my bones be steeped in your love, and say: *Lord, who is like you?*[^2] *You have burst my bonds: I will sacrifice to you a sacrifice of praise*[^3]. How you burst them, I will tell; and all who adore you will say, when they hear these things: *Blessed be the Lord*[^4], *in heaven and on earth*[^5]; *great and wonderful is his name*[^6].

Your words had clung fast in my vitals, and on every side I was hedged about by you[^7]. Of your eternal life I was certain, although I had seen it *in a riddle and as through a mirror*[^8]; yet all doubt about the incorruptible substance—that from it is every substance—had been taken from me, and I desired not to be more certain of you, but more steadfast in you.

But concerning my temporal life all things were wavering, and my heart had to be cleansed of the old leaven[^9]; and the Way itself, the Savior, pleased me, but as yet it irked me to go through its narrows[^10].

And you put into my mind, and it seemed good *in my sight*[^11], to go to Simplicianus, who seemed to me a good servant of yours, and your grace shone in him. I had heard, too, that from his youth he had lived most devoutly to you; and by now he had grown old, and in so long a life, in so good a zeal for following your way, he seemed to me to have experienced much and learned much—and truly so it was. Whence I wished that he would bring out to me, as I conferred my seethings with him, what was the fitting manner, for one affected as I was, of walking in your way[^12].

#### Augustine was even then tenaciously bound by a woman

**2**. For I saw the Church full, and one went this way, another that[^13]. But it displeased me, what I was doing in the world, and it was a great burden to me, now that the desires no longer inflamed me, as they used to, with the hope of honor and money, to bear that servitude so heavy.

For those things no longer delighted me, before your sweetness and the beauty of *your house, which I loved*[^14]; but I was still tenaciously bound by a woman; nor did the Apostle forbid me to marry, though he exhorted to a better state, wishing most of all that all men be as he himself was[^15]. But I, the weaker, was choosing the softer place, and for this one thing I was tossed languid in the rest, fainting with withering cares, because in the other matters too, which I was unwilling to suffer, I was forced to conform to married life, to which, being given over, I was bound. I had heard from the mouth of truth[^16] that there are *eunuchs who made themselves eunuchs for the kingdom of heaven; but let him who can receive it*, he says, *receive it*[^17].

*Vain indeed are all men in whom there is no knowledge of God, and who, from the good things that are seen, could not find out him who is*[^18]. But I was no longer in that vanity; I had transcended it, and, with all creation bearing witness, I had found you, our Creator, and your Word, God with you, and one God with you, through which you created all things[^19].

And there is another kind of the impious, who, *knowing God, did not glorify him as God or give thanks*[^20]. Into this too I had fallen, *and your right hand took me up*[^21], and, taking me from there, set me where I might recover; for you said to man: *Behold, piety is wisdom*[^22], and: *Do not wish to seem wise*[^23], since *those saying they are wise became fools*[^24]. And I had now found the good pearl, and, all I had being sold, it was to be bought[^25]—and I hesitated.

## The conversion of the rhetorician Victorinus, related by Simplicianus

### Chapter 2

**3**. I went, then, to Simplicianus, father of the then bishop Ambrose in the receiving of grace, and whom Ambrose truly loved as a father. I told him the windings of my error. But when I mentioned that I had read certain books of the Platonists, which Victorinus—once a rhetorician of the city of Rome, who, I had heard, had died a Christian—had translated into the Latin tongue, he congratulated me that I had not fallen upon the writings of other philosophers, full of fallacies and deceptions *according to the elements of this world*[^26], whereas in these, in every way, God and his Word are insinuated.

Then, to exhort me to the humility of Christ, *hidden from the wise and revealed to little ones*[^27], he recalled Victorinus himself, whom, when he was at Rome, he had known most intimately; and about him he told me what I will not pass over in silence. For it holds great praise of your grace, to be confessed to you—how that most learned old man,

most skilled in all the liberal disciplines, who had read and judged so many of the philosophers, the teacher of so many noble senators, who even, for the distinction of his renowned teaching, had merited and received a statue in the Roman forum (which the citizens of this world think a great thing)—up to that age a worshiper of idols and a sharer in sacrilegious rites, with which nearly all the Roman nobility was then puffed up and breathed—the people's Pelusian, and

> *the monstrous gods of every kind,  
> and barking Anubis,  
> who once  
> against Neptune and Venus and against Minerva*[^28]

had held their weapons, and to whom Rome now made supplication, though once conquered by her—which that old man Victorinus had for so many years defended with earth-thundering mouth—he did not blush to become a boy of your Christ and an infant of your font[^29], bending his neck[^30] to the yoke of humility[^31], and submitting his brow to the reproach of the cross[^32].

**4**. O Lord, Lord, *who bowed the heavens and came down, who touched the mountains and they smoked*[^33], by what means did you insinuate yourself into that breast? He read—so Simplicianus said—Holy Scripture, and investigated and searched out all the Christian writings most studiously, and said to Simplicianus, not openly, but more secretly and familiarly: "Know that I am now a Christian."

And he answered: "I will not believe it, nor count you among Christians, until I see you in the Church of Christ." But he laughed, saying: "Do walls, then, make Christians?" And this he often said—that he was already a Christian—and Simplicianus often gave that answer, and often the joke about the walls was repeated by him.

For he feared to offend his friends, proud demon-worshipers, from the height of whose Babylonian dignity, as from the cedars of Lebanon which the Lord had not yet crushed[^34], he thought enmities would fall heavily upon him.

But after, by reading and longing, he drank in firmness, and feared to be denied by Christ before the holy angels if he feared to confess him before men[^35], and appeared to himself guilty of a great crime in being ashamed of the sacraments of the humility of your Word and not ashamed of the sacrilegious rites of the proud demons, which, a proud imitator, he had received—he ceased to be shameless toward vanity and grew ashamed before the truth, and suddenly and unexpectedly said to Simplicianus, as he himself related: "Let us go to the church: I want to become a Christian."

And he, not containing himself for joy, went with him. And when he had been steeped in the first sacraments of instruction, not long after he also gave in his name, that he might be regenerated through baptism—Rome marveling, the Church rejoicing. The proud saw and were angry; they gnashed their teeth and wasted away[^36]. But for your servant the Lord God was *his hope, and he did not look* upon *vanities and lying follies*[^37].

**5**. Finally, when the hour came for professing the faith—which at Rome is wont to be rendered, in words framed and held by memory, from a more eminent place in the sight of the faithful people, by those who are about to come to your grace—Simplicianus said that it was offered to Victorinus by the presbyters that he might render it more privately, as it was the custom to offer to some who seemed likely to be alarmed by bashfulness; but he chose rather to profess his salvation in the sight of the holy multitude.

For there was no salvation in the rhetoric he taught, and yet he had professed that publicly. How much less, then, ought he to have feared your gentle flock, in pronouncing your word, who did not fear the crowds of madmen in his own words?

And so, when he went up to render it, all, each as he knew him, murmured his name to one another with a din of congratulation. And who there did not know him? And there sounded, in a low sound, through the mouths of all rejoicing together: "Victorinus, Victorinus." Quickly they sounded with exultation because they saw him, and quickly they fell silent with attention to hear him. He pronounced the true faith with splendid confidence, and all wished to seize him into their very heart. And they did seize him, by loving and rejoicing: these were the hands of those who seized him.

### Chapter 3: Greater is a man's joy at being freed from a greater peril

**6**. Good God, what happens in a man, that he should rejoice more over the salvation of a soul despaired of and freed from a greater peril than if hope had always been present to it, or the peril had been less? For you too, merciful Father, rejoice more *over one penitent than over ninety-nine righteous who have no need of penitence*[^38].

And we also hear it with great gladness, when we hear with how exultant the shepherd's shoulders the sheep that had strayed is carried back[^39], and the coin is brought back into your treasuries, the neighbor women rejoicing with her who found it[^40]; and the joy of the solemnity of your house wrings out tears[^41], when it is read in your house about your younger son, *that he was dead and is alive again, was lost and is found*[^42]. For you rejoice in us and in your angels, holy by holy love. For you are ever the same[^43], you who *know all things*[^44] that are not always nor in the same way, in the same way always.

**7**. What, then, happens in the soul, when it is more delighted at things found or restored which it loves than if it had always had them? For other things too bear witness, and all are full of testimonies crying out: "It is so." The victorious commander triumphs, and would not have conquered had he not fought; and the greater the peril in the battle, the greater the joy in the triumph. The storm tosses sailors and threatens shipwreck; all grow pale at coming death[^45]: the sky and sea grow calm, and they exult exceedingly, because they feared exceedingly.

One dear to us is sick, and his pulse announces ill; all who desire him well are sick at once in mind: he recovers, and does not yet walk with his former strength, and already there is such joy as there was not when before he walked safe and strong.

And the very pleasures of human life men acquire even through troubles not unforeseen and bursting in against their will, but appointed and voluntary. There is no pleasure in eating and drinking unless the trouble of hunger and thirst go before. And drunkards eat certain salty things, that a troublesome burning may arise, which, when drinking quenches it, becomes delight. And it is appointed that brides already betrothed not be handed over at once, lest, as one given cheaply, the husband should hold her whom, as a bridegroom, he had not sighed for in her delay.

**8**. This in base and execrable joy; this in that joy which is granted and lawful; this in the most sincere honorableness of friendship; this in him who *was dead and is alive again, was lost and is found*: everywhere the greater joy is preceded by a greater trouble. *What is this*[^46], O Lord my God, since you are an eternal joy to yourself, you yourself, and certain things about you rejoice ever around you? What is it that this part of things alternates between failing and advancing, between offenses and reconciliations? Is this their measure, and is this what you gave them, when *from the heights of the heavens*[^47] down to the depths of the earth, from the beginning to the end of the ages, from the angel to the little worm, from the first motion to the last, you placed all kinds of good things and all your just works, each in its own seat, and acted each in its own time?

Woe is me, how high you are in the heights, and how deep in the depths[^48]! And nowhere do you withdraw, and scarcely do we return to you.

### Chapter 4: The conversion of eminent men brings joy to many and is for their salvation

**9**. Come, Lord, act: rouse us and call us back, kindle us and seize us, be fragrant, grow sweet: let us love, let us run. Do not many return to you from a deeper Tartarus of blindness than Victorinus, and draw near, and are enlightened[^49], receiving the light which, if any receive, they receive from you the power to become your sons[^50]? But if they are less known to the peoples, even those who know them rejoice less over them. For when joy is shared with many, even in individuals the joy is more abundant, because they warm and inflame one another.

Then, because they are known to many, they are an authority to many for salvation, and go before many who will follow; and therefore those also who went before them rejoice much over them, because they do not rejoice over them alone.

For far be it that in your tabernacle the persons of the rich should be accepted before the poor, or the noble before the lowly[^51], since rather you chose *the weak things of the world, that* you might confound *the strong; and the lowly things of this world* you chose, *and the contemptible, and the things that are not, as though they were, that you might bring to nothing the things that are*[^52].

And yet that same *least of your apostles*[^53], through whose tongue you sounded these words of yours, when Paul the proconsul—his pride beaten down by Paul's service and sent under the gentle yoke of your Christ[^54], made a provincial of the great King[^55]—he too, from the former Saul, loved to be called Paul, for the distinction of so great a victory.

For the enemy is more conquered in one whom he holds the more, and through whom he holds the more. But he holds the proud the more by the name of nobility, and through them holds the more by the name of authority.

By how much the more gratefully, then, was the heart of Victorinus thought of, which the devil had held as an impregnable stronghold, and the tongue of Victorinus, with which, a great and sharp weapon, he had slain many—so much the more abundantly ought your sons to exult, because our King bound the strong one[^56], and they saw his vessels snatched away, cleansed, and fitted for your honor, and made useful *to the Lord for every good work*[^57].

## Two wills were tearing apart Augustine's soul, and evil habit held it

### Chapter 5

**10**. But when your man Simplicianus told me these things about Victorinus, I was on fire to imitate him; and for this indeed he had told them. But after he had added this too—that in the times of the emperor Julian, by a law given, Christians were forbidden to teach literature and oratory (which law he embraced, choosing to desert the wordy school rather than your word, by which you make *the tongues of infants eloquent*[^58])—he seemed to me not more brave than fortunate, because he found an occasion of being free for you.

For which thing I sighed, bound not by another's iron, but by my own iron will. The enemy held my will, and from it had made a chain for me, and bound me fast. For from a perverse will came lust, and while lust was served, it became habit, and while habit was not resisted, it became necessity. By these links, as it were, interlinked one with another (whence I called it a chain), a hard servitude held me bound.

But the new will, which had begun to be in me—that I should freely worship you and wish to enjoy you, O God, the only sure delight—was not yet able to overcome the former, strengthened by age. Thus my two wills, one old, the other new[^59], the one carnal, the other spiritual[^60], struggled with each other, and by their discord tore apart my soul.

**11**. Thus I understood, by my own experience, that which I had read: how *the flesh* lusts *against the spirit, and the spirit against the flesh*[^61]. I was indeed in both, but more I in that which I approved in myself than in that which I disapproved in myself. For there it was now more not I, since in great part I suffered it unwilling rather than did it willing. But still, the habit that had been made against me was made out of me, since by willing I had come where I did not will. And who could justly contradict, when a just penalty followed the sinner?

And there was no longer that excuse by which I used to seem to myself not yet to serve you, having not yet despised the world, because my perception of the truth was uncertain: for now it too was certain. But I, still bound to the earth, refused to enlist for your service[^62], and feared to be freed of all hindrances as one ought to fear being hindered.

#### The burden of the world

**12**. So by the burden of the world, as is wont to happen in sleep, I was sweetly pressed down; and the thoughts by which I meditated on you were like the efforts of those wishing to wake, who yet, overcome by the depth of their drowsiness, sink back.

And just as there is no one who would wish to sleep forever, and in the sound judgment of all to be awake is better, yet a man usually puts off shaking off sleep when a heavy torpor is in his limbs, and, though the time to rise has come, takes it up the more gladly even while it displeases him;

so I held it for certain that it was better to give myself to your love than to yield to my desire; but the one pleased and conquered, the other delighted and bound me.

For there was nothing I could answer you, as you said to me, *Rise, you who sleep, and arise from the dead, and Christ will enlighten you*[^63], and showed on every side that you spoke truly; there was nothing at all I could answer, convicted by the truth, except only slow and drowsy words: "Presently," "See, presently," "Leave me a little while." But "presently and presently" had no present, and "leave me a little while" went on for long.

In vain I delighted in your law *according to the inner man*, while another law *in my members* warred against *the law of my mind* and led me captive *in the law of sin which was in my members*. For the law of sin is the violence of habit, by which even the unwilling mind is dragged and held, deserving it, since by willing it slipped into that habit. *Who*, then, would free wretched me *from the body of this death, but* your *grace, through Jesus Christ our Lord*?[^64]

## What Ponticianus related to Augustine and Alypius

### Chapter 6: On the leisure and business of Augustine, Alypius, and Nebridius

**13**. And how you took me out of the bond of the desire for intercourse, by which I was most tightly held, and out of the servitude of worldly business, I will tell, *and I will confess to your name, O Lord*[^65], *my helper and my redeemer*[^66].

I was going about my usual affairs, with anxiety growing, and daily I sighed to you; I frequented your church, as much as I had leisure from the business under whose weight I groaned. With me was Alypius, idle from the work of the legal experts after his third assessorship, awaiting to whom he might again sell his counsels, just as I sold the faculty of speaking, if any can be furnished by teaching.

But Nebridius had yielded to our friendship, to teach under Verecundus, a citizen and grammarian of Milan, most intimate with us all—who vehemently desired, and by the right of friendship demanded, faithful help from our number, of which he was in great need.

It was not, then, the desire of advantages that drew Nebridius there (for he could have done greater things with letters, if he wished), but, out of a duty of goodwill, that most sweet and gentle friend would not despise our petition.

He did it most prudently, careful not to become known to persons great *according to this world*[^67], avoiding in them all disquiet of mind, which he wished to keep free and at leisure for as many hours as he could, to seek or read or hear something of wisdom.

#### Ponticianus's discourse about Antony the monk

**14**. One day, then (I do not recall the cause why Nebridius was absent), behold, there came to us at the house, to me and Alypius, a certain Ponticianus, our countryman insofar as he was African, serving with distinction at the palace. He wanted something of us; and we sat down to talk. And by chance he noticed, on the gaming table that was before us, a book: he took it, opened it, and found the apostle Paul—quite unexpectedly, for he had supposed it to be something from the books over which I was wearing myself out in my profession. Then, smiling and looking at me, he marveled with congratulation that he had suddenly found these writings, and these alone, before my eyes.

For he was a Christian and one of the faithful, and often prostrated himself before you, our God, in church, in frequent and lengthy prayers. When I had told him that I bestowed the greatest care on those Scriptures, a conversation arose, he telling of Antony, the Egyptian monk, whose name shone with excellence among your servants, though it lay hidden from us until that hour. When he learned this, he dwelt on that subject, making so great a man known to those who knew him not, and marveling at our very ignorance. But we were astonished, hearing of your *wonderful works*[^68], so well attested by a memory so recent, almost in our own times, in the right faith and the catholic Church. We all marveled—we, because they were so great, and he, because they were unheard of by us.

## What befell Ponticianus and three of his friends as they walked in the gardens at Trier

**15**. From there his speech rolled on to the flocks of the monasteries, and the ways of your sweet-smelling fragrance, and the fruitful wastes of the desert, of which we knew nothing. And there was a monastery at Milan, full of good brothers, outside the city walls, under the fostering of Ambrose, and we did not know it.

He went on and kept speaking, and we, intent, were silent. Whence it came about that he told how, at some time, he and three of his comrades—at Trier, no doubt, when the emperor was occupied with the afternoon show of the circus—had gone out to walk in the gardens adjoining the walls; and there, as they happened to be strolling two by two, one went apart with him, and the other two likewise went apart together; but those two, wandering, came upon a certain cottage where dwelt some servants of yours, *poor in spirit*, of whom *is the kingdom of heaven*[^69], and found there a book in which was written the life of Antony.

One of them began to read it, and to marvel, and to be kindled, and, as he read, to meditate on seizing such a life and, leaving worldly soldiering, to serve you. For they were of those they call agents in affairs.

Then suddenly, filled with holy love and a sober shame, angry at himself[^70], he cast his eyes on his friend and said to him: "Tell me, I beg you, by all these labors of ours, where are we striving to arrive? What do we seek? For what cause do we serve? Can our hope at the palace be any greater than to become friends of the emperor? And there, what is not fragile and full of perils? And through how many perils does one come to a greater peril? And when will that be? But a friend of God, if I wish, behold, I become now[^71]."

He said this, and, troubled with the birth-pangs of a new life, he turned his eyes back to the pages: and he read, and was changed within, where you saw, and his mind was stripped of the world, as soon appeared.

For while he read, and turned over the waves of his heart, he raged for a time, and discerned and decreed better things; and now yours, he said to his friend: "I have now torn myself away from that hope of ours, and have resolved to serve God, and this I begin from this hour, in this place. If it irks you to imitate me, do not oppose me."

He answered that he would cleave to him as a companion of so great a reward and so great a service. And both, now yours, were building a tower at fitting cost[^72], by leaving all their own things and following you[^73].

Then Ponticianus and the one who was walking with him through other parts of the garden, seeking them, came to the same place, and, finding them, admonished them to return, for the day had now declined[^74]. But they, having told of their resolve and purpose, and how such a will had arisen and been confirmed in them, begged them not to be troublesome if they refused to join them.

But these two, in no way changed from their former selves, wept for themselves, however (as he said), and devoutly congratulated them, and commended themselves to their prayers, and, dragging their heart along the ground, went off to the palace; while those others, fixing their heart on heaven, remained in the cottage. And both had betrothed brides: who, after they heard this, also dedicated their virginity to you.

### Chapter 7: Augustine, wretched and trembling

**16**. Ponticianus was telling these things. But you, Lord, amid his words, were turning me back toward myself, taking me from behind my own back, where I had placed myself while I refused to attend to myself[^75], and setting me before my own face, that I might see how foul I was, how distorted and sordid, spotted and ulcerous. And I saw, and was horrified, and there was nowhere to flee from myself[^76]. And if I tried to turn my gaze from myself, he kept telling what he told, and you again set me over against myself, and thrust me into my own eyes, that I might find my iniquity and hate it[^77]. I had known it, but I dissembled, and held it back, and forgot it.

**17**. But then, the more ardently I loved those of whom I heard such wholesome affections—that they had given themselves wholly to you to be healed—so much the more execrably did I hate myself compared with them, since many of my years had flowed away with me (perhaps twelve years), from the time when, in my nineteenth year, having read Cicero's *Hortensius*, I had been roused to the zeal of wisdom,

and I kept putting off, with earthly felicity despised, to be free for searching it out—the mere search for which, not to say the finding, was already to be preferred even to treasures found and the kingdoms of the nations, and to bodily pleasures flowing about me at a nod.

But I, a most wretched young man, wretched at the very outset of my youth, had even begged chastity of you, and had said: "Give me chastity and continence, but not yet." For I feared that you would hear me quickly, and quickly heal me of the disease of concupiscence, which I preferred to be glutted rather than extinguished. And I had gone *by crooked ways*[^78] in sacrilegious superstition—not indeed certain in it, but as it were preferring it to the others, which I did not devoutly seek but assailed as an enemy.

**18**. And I had supposed that I was therefore putting off *from day to day*[^79] following you alone, with the hope of the world despised, because there appeared to me nothing certain by which to direct my course. And the day had come when I was laid bare to myself, and my conscience upbraided me: "Where is your tongue? Surely you used to say that, on account of the uncertainty of the truth, you would not cast off the burden of vanity. Behold, now it is certain, and that burden still presses you, while wings are received on the freer shoulders of those who have neither been so worn out in seeking, nor meditated on these things for a decade and more."

So I was gnawed within, and was confounded with a horrible shame, vehemently, while Ponticianus spoke such things. And the conversation being ended, and the cause for which he had come, he went away, and I to myself.

What did I not say against myself? With what scourges of sentences did I not lash my soul, that it might follow me as I tried to go after you[^80]? And it resisted, it refused, and did not excuse itself. All arguments were used up and refuted; there remained a mute trembling, and it dreaded, as it were death, to be restrained from the flux of habit, by which it was wasting away unto death.

## What followed in the little garden of the house

### Chapter 8: Augustine went up into the little garden of the house with Alypius

**19**. Then, in that great quarrel of my inner house, which I had strongly stirred up with my soul in our chamber[^81], my heart—disturbed in countenance as in mind—I burst in upon Alypius and cried out: "What are we suffering? What is this? What have you heard? The unlearned rise up and seize heaven[^82], and we, with our learning, without heart—behold, where we wallow in flesh and blood[^83]! Is it because they have gone before that we are ashamed to follow, and not ashamed not even to follow?"

I said something of the sort, and my fever tore me away from him, while he kept silent, astonished, gazing at me. For I did not sound as usual. My brow, cheeks, eyes, color, and the manner of my voice spoke my mind more than the words I uttered.

There was a little garden belonging to our lodging, which we used as we did the whole house; for the host, the owner of the house, did not live there. To it the tumult of my breast had carried me, where no one might hinder the burning suit which I had entered upon with myself, until it should come out—how, you knew, but I did not; but I was only raging wholesomely and dying vitally, knowing what evil I was, and not knowing what good I was about to be after a little while.

So I withdrew into the garden, and Alypius, step after step. For my secret was no less secret where he was present. Or when would he desert me, so affected? We sat down as far as we could from the buildings. I was groaning in spirit[^84], indignant with a most turbulent indignation, because I did not go over into your covenant and pact[^85], my God, into which *all my bones*[^86] cried out that I must go, and extolled it to heaven with praises; and one did not go there by ships or chariots or feet, as far even as I had gone from the house to the place where we were sitting. For not only to go, but also to arrive there, was nothing other than to will to go—but to will strongly and wholly, not to turn and toss a half-wounded will this way and that, struggling, with one part rising while another part fell.

**20**. Finally, I did so many things with my body, in the very tossings of hesitation, which men sometimes will and cannot do, if they either do not have the limbs themselves, or have them bound by chains, or loosened by weakness, or in some way hindered. If I tore my hair, if I struck my forehead, if, with fingers interlocked, I clasped my knee—I did it because I willed. But I could have willed and not done it, if the mobility of my limbs had not obeyed.

So I did many things where to will was not the same as to be able; and I did not do what pleased me with an incomparably greater affection, and which soon, as soon as I should will it, I would be able to do—because, as soon as I should will it, I would surely will it. For there the faculty was the same as the will, and the very willing was already the doing;

and yet it was not done; and the body more easily obeyed the slightest will of the soul, that the limbs should move at its nod, than the soul itself obeyed itself, to accomplish its own great will in the will alone.

### Chapter 9: When the will does not command fully, what it commands is not done

**21**. Whence this monstrous thing? And why this? Let your mercy shine, and let me ask, whether perhaps the hidden places of men's punishments and the most darksome contritions of the sons of Adam can answer me. Whence this monstrous thing? And why this?

The mind commands the body, and it obeys at once: the mind commands itself, and it is resisted. The mind commands that the hand be moved, and there is such ease that the command is scarcely distinguished from the obeying; and the mind is mind, but the hand is body.

The mind commands that the mind will, and it is no other, and yet it does not do it. Whence this monstrous thing? And why this?

It commands, I say, that it will, which would not command unless it willed, and it does not do what it commands.

But it does not will wholly: therefore it does not command wholly. For it commands insofar as it wills, and insofar as it does not will, what it commands is not done—since the will commands that there be a will, not another, but itself.

Therefore it does not command fully; and so what it commands is not. For if it were full, it would not even command that it be, because it would already be.

It is not, then, a monstrous thing partly to will, partly to be unwilling, but a sickness of the mind, because it does not rise wholly, lifted up by truth, weighed down by habit. And so there are two wills, because one of them is not whole, and what is lacking to the one is present in the other.

### Chapter 10: Two contrary natures do not war within a man; rather, the same soul sometimes wills with less than its whole will

**22**. *Let them perish from your face*[^87], O God, as they perish, *the vain talkers and seducers* of the mind[^88], who, when they have noticed two wills in deliberating, assert that there are two natures of two minds, one good, the other evil. They themselves are truly evil, when they hold these evil things; and these same will be good, if they hold true things and consent to the true, so that your Apostle may say to them: *You were once darkness, but now light in the Lord*[^89]. For they, while they wish to be light not in the Lord but in themselves, supposing the nature of the soul to be what God is, have thus become denser darkness, because by a horrible arrogance they withdrew farther from you, the true light, which enlightens *every man coming into this world*[^90]. Attend to what you say, and blush, and *draw near to him and be enlightened, and your faces shall not blush*[^91].

When I deliberated about now serving the Lord my God[^92], as I had long resolved, it was I who willed, I who was unwilling; it was I. I neither willed fully nor was fully unwilling.

Therefore I strove with myself and was torn apart by myself; and that very tearing happened against my will, yet did not show the nature of an alien mind, but the punishment of my own. And so it was now no longer I that did it, *but the sin* that dwelt *in me*[^93], from the punishment of a sin more freely committed, because I was a son of Adam.

**23**. For if there are as many contrary natures as there are wills resisting one another, there will be not two, but more. If anyone deliberates whether he should go to their conventicle or to the theater, those men cry out: "Behold, two natures—one good leads this way, the other evil leads back that way. For whence this hesitation of wills opposing one another?"

But I say that both are evil—both that which leads to them and that which leads back to the theater. But they do not believe that the one by which one goes to them is anything but good.

What, then? If one of us deliberates, and wavers, with two wills contending within him, whether he should go to the theater or to our church—will not these men too waver as to what to answer? For either they will confess, what they do not wish, that one goes to our church by a good will, as those go to it who have been steeped in its sacraments and are held by them; or they will think that two evil natures and two evil minds clash in one man, and then it will not be true, as they are wont to say, that one is good, the other evil; or they will be converted to the truth, and will not deny that, when anyone deliberates, one soul seethes with diverse wills.

**24**. Let them, then, no longer say, when they perceive two wills in one man opposing each other, that two contrary minds, of two contrary substances and two contrary principles, are contending, one good, the other evil. For you, *truthful God*[^94], disprove them and refute and convict them—as in the case of two evil wills, when someone deliberates whether to kill a man by poison or by the sword; whether to seize this estate of another's or that, when he cannot both; whether to buy pleasure by self-indulgence or keep his money by avarice; whether to go to the circus or to the theater, if both are put on the same day. I add even a third: whether to go to steal from another's house, if the occasion is at hand; I add a fourth too: whether to commit adultery, if the chance for that also opens at the same time—if all converge upon one point of time, and all are equally desired which cannot be done together;

for they tear the mind apart with four mutually opposing wills, or even more, in so great a supply of things that are desired; and yet they are not wont to say that there is so great a multitude of diverse substances.

So too in good wills. For I ask them whether it is good to be delighted by the reading of the Apostle, and whether it is good to be delighted by a sober psalm, and whether it is good to expound the Gospel. To each they will answer: "Good." What, then? If all equally delight, and at one and the same time, do not diverse wills stretch the heart of man, while we deliberate what we should most of all seize upon? And all are good, and they contend among themselves, until one is chosen, to which the whole will, which was divided into many, may be borne. So too, when eternity delights on high and the pleasure of a temporal good holds below, it is the same soul, willing this or that with less than its whole will, and therefore it is torn apart by a heavy trouble, while by truth it prefers the one, but by familiarity does not put away the other.

### Chapter 11: Augustine hesitating and held back

**25**. So I was sick and tormented, accusing myself far more bitterly than usual, and rolling and turning in my chain, until the whole should be broken, by which I was now but slightly held. But held I still was. And you pressed upon me in my hidden depths, Lord, with a severe mercy, redoubling the scourges of fear and shame, lest I should again give way and that very slight and thin remnant that was left should not be broken, but recover strength again and bind me more strongly. For I said within myself: "Behold, let it be done now, let it be done now," and with the word I was already going into the resolve. I almost did it, and did not do it; yet I did not slip back into my former state, but stood close by and caught my breath. And again I tried, and was a little less far off, and a little less, and now, now I was all but touching it and holding it; and I was not there, nor touching, nor holding—hesitating to die to death and to live to life, and the worse, ingrained in me, prevailed more than the better, to which I was unaccustomed; and the very point of time at which I was to become other, the nearer it drew, the greater horror it struck into me; but it did not strike me back, nor turn me away, but held me in suspense.

**26**. The trifles of trifles, and vanities of vanities[^95], my old loves, held me back, and plucked at my garment of flesh, and murmured softly: "Are you dismissing us?" and: "From this moment we shall not be with you anymore, forever," and: "From this moment this and that will not be allowed you anymore, forever." And what they suggested in what I called "this and that"—what they suggested, my God! Let your mercy turn it away from the soul of your servant! What filth they suggested, what shameful things!

And I heard them now far less than half, not as if openly contradicting me by going to meet me face to face, but as if muttering behind my back, and, as I departed, furtively plucking at me to look back. Yet they delayed me, hesitating to tear myself away and shake free of them and leap across to where I was called, while violent habit said to me: "Do you think you can do without these?"

#### The exhortation of Continence

**27**. But by now it said this very tepidly. For on that side toward which I had set my face, and to which I trembled to cross, there was revealed the chaste dignity of Continence, serene and not dissolutely cheerful, honorably coaxing me to come and not to hesitate, and stretching out to receive and embrace me her devout hands, full of flocks of good examples.

There were so many boys and girls, there much youth and every age, and grave widows and aged virgins; and in all of them Continence herself was by no means barren, but a fruitful mother of children of joys[^96] by you, her husband, Lord.

And she mocked me with an encouraging mockery, as if to say: "Can you not do what these men and women can? Or can these men and women do it in themselves, and not in the Lord their God? The Lord their God gave me to them. Why do you stand in yourself, and so do not stand? Cast yourself upon him, do not be afraid; he will not withdraw, that you should fall: cast yourself securely; he will receive and heal you."

And I blushed exceedingly, because I still heard the murmurs of those trifles, and, hesitating, I hung back. And again she, as if to say: "Be deaf toward those unclean members of yours upon the earth[^97], that they may be mortified. They tell you of *delights, but not as the law* of the Lord your God[^98]."

This controversy in my heart was nothing but myself against myself. But Alypius, fixed at my side, silently awaited the outcome of my unwonted agitation.

### Chapter 12: With many tears he implored the mercy of God

**28**. But when a deep consideration drew up, from the secret bottom, and heaped together my whole misery in the sight of my heart[^99], there arose a huge storm, bearing a huge shower of tears. And, that I might pour it all out with its own cries, I rose up from Alypius (solitude seemed to me fitter for the business of weeping) and withdrew far enough that even his presence could not be a burden to me. So I was then, and he perceived it; for I had said something, I think, in which the sound of my voice already appeared heavy with weeping, and thus I had risen. He remained, then, where we had been sitting, utterly astonished.

I flung myself down, somehow, under a certain fig tree, and gave free rein to my tears, and the rivers of my eyes burst forth, an acceptable sacrifice to you[^100]; and, not indeed in these words, but to this effect, I said many things to you: *And you, Lord, how long?*[^101] *How long, Lord, will you be angry forever? Remember not our old iniquities*[^102]. For I felt that I was held by them. I cast out wretched cries: "How long, how long? 'Tomorrow and tomorrow'? Why not now? Why not, this very hour, an end to my foulness?"

## "Take and read"

**29**. I was saying these things and weeping in the most bitter contrition of my heart. And behold, I hear a voice from a neighboring house, of one saying and often repeating in a chant, as of a boy or a girl, I know not which: "Take and read, take and read." And at once, with changed countenance, I began most intently to think whether children were wont to chant some such thing in any kind of game, and it did not occur to me that I had heard it anywhere at all; and, checking the rush of my tears, I rose up, interpreting it as nothing other than a divine command to me to open the book and read the first chapter I should find. For I had heard of Antony[^103] that, admonished by a Gospel reading upon which he had chanced, as though what was read were said to him, *Go, sell all that you have, and give to the poor, and you shall have treasure in heaven; and come, follow me*[^104], he had been converted to you at once by such an oracle[^105].

So, stirred, I returned to the place where Alypius was sitting; for there I had put down the book of the Apostle when I rose from there. I seized it, opened it, and read in silence the chapter on which my eyes first fell: *not in revelings and drunkenness, not in chamberings and wantonness, not in strife and envying; but put on the Lord Jesus Christ, and make no provision for the flesh in its concupiscences*[^106]. I would read no further, nor was there need; for instantly, with the end of this sentence, as though a light of security had been poured into my heart, all the shadows of doubt fled away.

## The conversion of Augustine and Alypius, and the joy of his mother

**30**. Then, marking the place with a finger, or with some other sign I forget, I closed the book, and with a now tranquil countenance made it known to Alypius. And he, in turn, disclosed what was going on in him, which I did not know. He asked to see what I had read: I showed him, and he looked even beyond what I had read. And I did not know what followed. There followed: *Receive him who is weak in faith*[^107]. He applied this to himself, and disclosed it to me. And by that admonition he was strengthened; and by a good resolve and purpose, most congruent with his character (in which he had long differed from me, very much and far for the better), without any turbulent hesitation he was joined with me.

From there we go in to my mother; we tell her: she rejoices. We relate how it was done: she exults and triumphs, and she blessed you, *who are able to do beyond what we ask or understand*[^108], because she saw that you had granted her so much more concerning me than she was wont to ask with her pitiable and tearful groanings. For you converted me to you[^109], so that I sought neither a wife nor any hope of this world, standing in that rule of faith in which you had shown me to her so many years before; and *you turned* her *mourning into joy*[^110] far more abundantly than she had wished, and far dearer and chaster than she had sought in grandchildren of my flesh.
