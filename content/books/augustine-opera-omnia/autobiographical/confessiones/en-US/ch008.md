# Book 7: The Ascent to the Truth

## Augustine's thoughts about God

### Chapter 1: The question of the nature of evil

**1**. By now my evil and wicked youth was dead, and I was passing into young manhood; and the older I was in age, the baser in vanity, since I could not conceive any substance except such as is wont to be seen through these eyes.

I did not think of you, God, in the figure of a human body, from the time I began to hear anything of wisdom; this I always fled, and I rejoiced to have found it in the faith of our spiritual mother, your catholic Church. But what else to think of you did not occur to me. And I, a man, and such a man, tried to think of you, the supreme and *only and true God*[^1];

and with all my marrow I believed you incorruptible and inviolable and unchangeable, because, though not knowing whence or how, I yet plainly saw and was certain that what can be corrupted is worse than what cannot, and what cannot be violated I unhesitatingly set before the violable, and what suffers no change is better than what can be changed.

My heart cried out violently[^2] against all my phantasms, and with this one blow I tried to drive away the swarm of uncleanness fluttering about the gaze of my mind; and scarcely was it dispersed when, in the twinkling of an eye[^3], behold, it was massed together again, and rushed upon my sight and clouded it, so that, although not in the form of a human body, I was yet forced to think of something bodily, set through spaces of place—whether infused into the world, or even diffused through the infinite outside the world;

even that incorruptible and inviolable and unchangeable thing, which I set before the corruptible and violable and changeable—since whatever I deprived of such spaces seemed to me to be nothing, but utterly nothing, not even a void: as if, when a body is taken away from a place, the place should remain emptied of every body, earthly and watery and airy and heavenly, yet still be an empty place, a kind of spacious nothing.

**2**. I therefore, gross of heart[^4], and not even clear to myself, thought that whatever was not stretched or diffused or massed or swollen through some extent of space, or did not or could not take on something of the kind, was nothing whatever. For over such forms as my eyes are wont to range, my heart ranged in such images; and I did not see that this very attention, by which I formed those images, was not anything of the kind—and yet it could not have formed them unless it were something great.

So too I thought of you, life of my life, as vast through infinite spaces, penetrating on every side the whole mass of the world, and beyond it, on all sides, through the immeasurable without end; so that the earth should have you, the heaven should have you, all things should have you, and they should be bounded in you, but you nowhere. For just as the body of this air, the air that is above the earth, does not block the light of the sun from passing through it—penetrating it, not by breaking or cutting it, but by filling it wholly—so I thought the body not only of heaven and air and sea but also of earth pervious to you, and penetrable in all its greatest and least parts, to receive your presence, which by a hidden inspiration, within and without, administers all things that you have created.

So I conjectured, because I could not think otherwise; for it was false. For in that way a greater part of the earth would have a greater part of you, and a lesser a lesser; and so all things would be full of you in such a way that the body of an elephant would hold more of you than that of a sparrow, by as much as it was larger than it and occupied a larger place; and so, piece by piece, you would make your parts present to the parts of the world—great parts to great, small to small. But it is not so. But you had not yet lightened my darkness[^5].

### Chapter 2: By what argument Nebridius refuted the Manichee teaching about the substance of God

**3**. It was enough for me, Lord, against those deceived deceivers and speechless chatterers[^6] (since your word did not sound from them)—enough, then, was that which Nebridius had long been wont to propose, from as far back as Carthage, and by which all of us who heard it were shaken: what would that nation of darkness of theirs, which they are wont to set against you out of an opposing mass, have done to you, if you had refused to fight with it?

For if it were answered that it would have done you some harm, then you would be violable and corruptible. But if it were said that it could do you no harm, then no cause would be brought for fighting—

and for fighting in such a way that some portion of you, and a member of you, or an offspring of your very substance, should be mingled with hostile powers and natures not created by you, and be corrupted and changed by them so far for the worse that it was turned from blessedness into misery, and needed help by which it might be rescued and cleansed—

and that this is the soul, to which your word, itself enslaved, would come to aid as free, and as pure to it defiled, and as whole to it corrupted; but itself corruptible too, since it is of one and the same substance.

And so, if they said that you—whatever you are, that is, your substance by which you exist—are incorruptible, then all those things were false and execrable; but if they said you are corruptible, that itself was already false, and to be abominated at the first utterance.

Enough, then, was this against those men, to be utterly vomited out from the oppression of the breast, because they had no way out without a horrible sacrilege of heart and tongue, in thinking such things of you and speaking them.

## Free choice of the will is the cause of evil

### Chapter 3

**4**. But I too, although I said and firmly held that our God, the true God, is uncontaminable and unconvertible and in no part changeable—you who made not only our souls but also our bodies, and not only our souls and bodies but all souls and all things—yet did not hold an unfolded and disentangled cause of evil. Whatever it might be, however, I saw that it must be sought in such a way that I should not, through it, be constrained to believe the unchangeable God changeable, lest I myself become the very thing I was seeking. And so I sought it free from care, and certain that what those men said was not true, whom I fled with all my soul, because I saw that, in asking whence evil came, they were filled with malice[^7], by which they supposed that your substance rather suffered evil than that their own did evil.

**5**. And I bent myself to discern what I had heard—that the free choice of the will is the cause of our doing evil, and *your just judgment*[^8] the cause of our suffering it—and I could not discern it clearly. And so, trying to draw the gaze of my mind up out of the deep, I was plunged back again; and often trying, I was plunged back again and again. For it raised me toward your light, that I knew I had a will as surely as I knew I was alive. And so, when I willed or did not will anything, I was most certain that it was no one but myself who willed and did not will; and there, I was now coming to perceive, was the cause of my sin. But what I did unwillingly, I saw that I rather suffered than did, and I judged it to be not fault but punishment—by which, thinking you just, I quickly confessed I was not unjustly chastised.

But again I said: "Who made me? Was it not my God, not only good, but goodness itself? Whence, then, comes it that I will evil and refuse good, so that there is a reason why I should justly pay penalties? Who put this in me, and grafted into me this nursery of bitterness[^9], when I was wholly made by my most sweet God? If the devil is the author, whence is the devil himself? But if he too, by a perverse will, was made a devil from a good angel, whence, again, is the evil will in him by which he became a devil, when the whole angel had been made by a most good Creator?"

By these thoughts I was again pressed down and choked, but not dragged down to that hell of error where no one confesses to you[^10], while you are thought rather to suffer evil than man to do it.

### Chapter 4: God is in no way corrupted

**6**. For I strove to find the rest in the same way as I had already found that the incorruptible is better than the corruptible; and so I confessed that you, whatever you were, were incorruptible. For no soul ever could, or ever will be able to, think of anything better than you, who are the supreme and best good. But since, most truly and most certainly, the incorruptible is to be set before the corruptible—as I already set it—I could now in thought reach something better than my God, unless you were incorruptible.

Where, then, I saw the incorruptible to be preferred to the corruptible, there I ought to have sought you, and thence to take note where evil is—that is, whence comes that very corruption by which your substance can in no way be violated. For corruption in no way at all violates our God: by no will, by no necessity, by no unforeseen chance, since he himself is God, and what he wills for himself is good, and he himself is that same good; but to be corrupted is not good. Nor are you forced unwilling to anything, because your will is not greater than your power. It would be greater, if you yourself were greater than yourself: for the will and the power of God are God himself. And what is unforeseen to you, who know all things? And no nature is, except because you know it. And why do we say many things about why the substance which is God is not corruptible, when, if it were so, it would not be God?

### Chapter 5: What is the root of evil, since God made all things good?

**7**. And I sought whence evil is, and I sought it badly, and in my very search I did not see the evil. And I set in the sight of my spirit[^11] the whole creation, whatever in it we can discern—such as earth and sea and air and stars and trees and mortal animals—and whatever in it we do not see, such as the firmament of heaven above, and all the angels and all its spiritual beings; but these too, as if they were bodies, my imagination set in places, place by place. And I made one great mass of your creation, distinguished by kinds of bodies—whether they really were bodies, or those I myself had feigned in place of spirits—and I made it great, not as great as it was (which I could not know), but as great as I pleased, yet on every side finite; but you, Lord, I supposed surrounding and penetrating it on every side, but everywhere infinite—as if there were a sea everywhere and on every side, through the immeasurable, an infinite sea, and only sea, and it had within it some sponge, however great, but finite all the same, and that sponge would be filled in its every part from the immeasurable sea;

so I supposed your finite creation full of you the infinite, and I said: "Behold God, and behold what God has created; and God is good, mightily and by far surpassing these; but still, being good, he created good things; and see how he surrounds and fills them. Where, then, is evil, and whence, and by what way did it creep in here? What is its root, and what its seed? Or does it not exist at all? Why, then, do we fear and beware of what does not exist? Or if we fear it in vain, surely that very fear is an evil, by which the heart is goaded and tortured for nothing—and an evil so much the graver as there is nothing we should fear, and yet we fear. Therefore, either there is an evil that we fear, or the very fact that we fear is the evil."

"Whence, then, is it, since God, being good, made all these good things[^12]? The greater and supreme good, indeed, made lesser goods; but still both the creating and the created are all good. Whence is evil? Or was there some evil matter from which he made them, and he formed and ordered it, yet left something in it which he did not turn into good? But why this? Was he powerless to turn and change the whole, so that no evil should remain, when he is omnipotent?"

"Finally, why did he will to make anything from it, and not rather, by that same omnipotence, bring it about that it should not exist at all? Or could it indeed exist against his will? Or if it were eternal, why did he allow it to exist so long, through infinite spaces of time backward, and only so long after was pleased to make something from it? Or, if he suddenly willed to do something, would not the Omnipotent rather have brought this about: that it should not exist, and that he himself alone should be the whole, the true and supreme and infinite good?"

"Or, if it was not well that he who was good should not also fashion and found something good, then, that evil matter being removed and reduced to nothing, might he not himself have established the good matter from which he would create all things? For he would not be omnipotent, if he could not found something good unless aided by a matter which he had not himself founded."

Such things I turned over in my wretched breast, weighed down with the most gnawing cares from the fear of death and the truth not found; yet steadfastly there clung in my heart, in the catholic Church, the faith of *your Christ, our Lord and Savior*[^13]—in many respects still unformed, and fluctuating outside the norm of doctrine, but yet my mind did not leave it, and day by day drank it in more and more.

## The refutation of astrology

### Chapter 6

**8**. By now I had also rejected the lying divinations and impious ravings of the astrologers. Let your mercies confess to you for this too, my God, out of the inmost depths of my soul[^14]! For you, you altogether (for who else calls us back from the death of all error but the life that knows not how to die, and the wisdom that enlightens minds in need, itself needing no light, by which the world is administered down to the fluttering leaves of the trees?)—

you provided for my obstinacy, by which I struggled against Vindicianus, the shrewd old man, and Nebridius, the young man of wonderful soul—the one vehemently affirming, the other saying with some doubt, indeed, but yet often, that there is no such art of foreseeing the future, but that men's conjectures often have the force of chance, and that, by saying many things, many that are to come are said, though unknown to those who say them, who hit upon them by not keeping silent. You provided, then, a friend who was no lazy consulter of the astrologers, nor well versed in those writings, but, as I said, a curious consulter, and yet one who knew a certain thing which he said he had heard from his father—how much it availed to overthrow the credit of that art, he did not know.

This man, then, by name Firminus, liberally educated and polished in eloquence, when he consulted me as his dearest friend about certain of his affairs, in which his worldly hope had swelled—as to what seemed to me according to his "constellations," as they call them—I, who had now begun to be turned to Nebridius's opinion on this matter, did not indeed refuse to conjecture and say what occurred to me as I wavered, but yet added that I was now nearly persuaded that those things were ridiculous and empty,

whereupon he told me that his father had been most curious about such books, and had had a friend equally pursuing them along with him. By equal zeal and comparison they fanned into those trifles the fire of their hearts, so that they would observe the moments of birth even of dumb animals, if any were born in their house, and note the position of the heavens at them, from which to gather, as it were, the experiments of that art.

And so he said he had heard from his father that, when Firminus's mother was pregnant, a certain maidservant of that paternal friend was likewise growing big in the womb. This could not be hidden from her master, who took care to know the births even of his dogs with the most exact diligence; and so it came about that, when the one for his wife and the other for his maidservant counted the days and hours and the smaller fractions of the hours with the most careful observation, both were delivered at the same time, so that both were forced to cast the same constellations down to the same minutes—the one for his son, the other for his little slave.

For when the women began to be in labor, they each indicated to the other what was being done in his own house, and had prepared messengers to send to one another, so that the birth of what was being delivered might be announced to each as soon as it happened; and that it should be announced at once, they had easily arranged, each in his own domain. And so, he said, those who were sent by the one and the other met each other at so equal an interval from the two houses that neither of them could note any different position of the stars or any different fractions of the moments.

And yet Firminus, born in an ample station among his own, ran the whiter ways of the world, was increased in riches, raised in honors; while that slave, the yoke of his condition in no way relaxed, served his masters—as the man who knew him related.

**9**. When I had heard and believed these things—for such was the manner of his telling—all that resistance of mine collapsed and dissolved. And first I tried to call Firminus himself back from that curiosity, by saying that, on inspecting his constellations, in order to pronounce truly I ought certainly to have seen there that his parents were among the leading men of their people, a noble family of his own city, freeborn rank, an honorable upbringing, and liberal learning.

But if that slave had consulted me from the same constellations (since they were his own as well), so that I might bring out true things for him too, I ought again to have seen there a most abject family, a servile condition, and the rest, far removed from the former and far apart from it.

Whence, then, would it come that, inspecting the same things, I should say different things, if I spoke truly (whereas, if I said the same things, I should speak falsely)? From this I gathered most certainly that what was said truly upon inspecting the constellations was said not by art but by chance; and what was said falsely, not by lack of skill in the art, but by the lie of chance.

**10**. Having gained this approach, I myself, ruminating on such things, lest any of those same ravers who follow such gain—whom I now longed at once to attack and to refute with mockery—should so resist me, as if either Firminus had told me, or his father him, falsehoods, turned my consideration to those who are born twins, most of whom are poured from the womb so close after one another that the small interval of time between them—however great a force they contend it has in the nature of things—cannot be gathered by human observation, and can in no way be marked in the figures which the astrologer is to inspect in order to pronounce the truth.

And they will not be true, because, inspecting the same figures, he ought to have said the same things about Esau and about Jacob; but the same things did not befall them both. So he would say false things; or, if he said true things, he would not say the same things—yet he would be inspecting the same figures. Not by art, then, but by chance, would he say true things.

For you, Lord, most just governor of the universe, while both those who consult and those consulted know it not, act by a hidden instinct, so that, when anyone consults, he hears what he ought to hear, by the hidden merits of souls, out of the abyss of your just judgment[^15]. To which let no man say, "What is this?"[^16] "Why this?" Let him not say it, let him not say it; for he is a man.

## Augustine sought, without finding a way out, whence evil came

### Chapter 7

**11**. And so by now, my helper[^17], you had loosed me from those bonds; and I sought whence evil is, and there was no way out. But you did not let me be carried away by any waves of thought from that faith by which I believed both that you exist, and that your substance is unchangeable, and that there is care and judgment of yours over men, and that in Christ, your Son, our Lord, and in the Holy Scriptures, which the authority of your catholic Church commended, you had set the way of human salvation to that life which is to be after this death. These things, then, being kept safe and unshakably strengthened in my mind, I sought, seething, whence evil is.

What torments of my heart in travail, what groans, my God! And there were your ears, though I knew it not. And when in silence I sought strongly, the silent contritions of my soul were great voices to your mercy. You knew what I suffered, and no man did. For how little was it that was distilled through my tongue into the ears of my most intimate friends! Did the whole tumult of my soul, for which neither time nor my mouth sufficed, sound to them? Yet the whole went into your hearing—the whole that *I roared from the groaning of my heart, and my desire was before you, and the light of my eyes was not with me*[^18]. For it was within, and I was without; nor was it in place. But I was attending to the things contained in places, and there I found no place to rest, nor did they receive me so that I might say, "It is enough," and, "It is well," nor did they let me return where it might be well enough with me.

For I was higher than these, but lower than you; and you were my true joy, I being subject to you, and you had subjected to me the things you created below me[^19]. And this was the right balance and the middle region of my health: that I should remain in your image[^20], and, serving you, have dominion over the body. But when I rose proudly against you, and ran against the Lord *with the thick neck of my shield*[^21], even these lowest things were made above me, and pressed me down, and nowhere was there relief or breathing-space.

They met me on every side, heaped and massed together as I looked; and as I thought, the images of bodies set themselves against me as I tried to return, as if it were said, "Where are you going, unworthy and foul?" And these things had grown out of my wound, because *you humbled the proud like one wounded*[^22], and by my swelling I was separated from you, and my too-bloated face closed my eyes.

### Chapter 8: The sharp but wholesome medicine of God

**12**. *But you, Lord, abide forever*[^23], and you are not *angry with us forever*[^24], since you have had mercy on dust and ashes[^25], and it pleased you in your sight[^26] to reform my deformities. And by inward goads you stirred me, that I might be impatient until you were made certain to me through the inward gaze. And my swelling subsided under the hidden hand of your medicine, and the gaze of my mind, confused and darkened, was healed day by day[^27] by the stinging salve of wholesome pains.

## The teaching of the Platonists: Augustine found many true things in the books of the Platonists

### Chapter 9

**13**. And first, willing to show me how *you resist the proud, but give grace to the humble*[^28], and with how great a mercy the way of humility was shown to men—that *your Word was made flesh and dwelt* among men[^29]—you procured for me, through a certain man swollen with a most monstrous conceit, certain books of the Platonists, translated from the Greek tongue into Latin.

And there I read—not indeed in these words, but this very same thing, urged by many and manifold reasonings—that *in the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God; the same was in the beginning with God; all things were made through him, and without him was made nothing that was made; in him is life, and the life was the light of men; and the light shines in the darkness, and the darkness did not comprehend it*; and that the soul of man, although it *bears witness of the light*, is yet not *itself the light*, but the Word, God, is *the true light that enlightens every man coming into this world*; and that *he was in this world, and the world was made through him, and the world did not know him*. But that *he came unto his own, and his own did not receive him; but to as many as received him he gave power to become sons of God, to those who believe in his name*[^30]—that I did not read there.

**14**. Again, I read there that the Word, God, *was born not of flesh, not of blood, nor of the will of man, nor of the will of the flesh, but of God*; but that *the Word was made flesh and dwelt among us*[^31]—that I did not read there. For I traced out in those writings, said variously and in many ways, that the Son was *in the form of the Father, and did not think it robbery to be equal with God*, because by nature he is that very thing; but that *he emptied himself, taking the form of a servant, made in the likeness of men, and found in fashion as a man; he humbled himself, becoming obedient unto death, even the death of the cross; wherefore God exalted him* from the dead *and gave him a name which is above every name, that at the name of Jesus every knee should bow, of things in heaven and on earth and under the earth, and every tongue confess that the Lord Jesus is in the glory of God the Father*[^32]—those books do not have.

For that before all times and above all times your only-begotten Son abides unchangeably, coeternal with you, and that souls receive *of his fullness*[^33] that they may be blessed, and that by participation in the wisdom abiding in itself[^34] they are renewed that they may be wise—that is there; but that *in due time he died for the ungodly*[^35], and that *you did not spare your only Son, but delivered him up for us all*[^36]—that is not there. For *you hid these things from the wise and revealed them to little ones*[^37], that those *who labor and are burdened* might come to him, and he might refresh them, *since he is gentle and humble of heart*[^38], and *he directs the meek in judgment and teaches the gentle his ways*[^39], seeing our *humility and our toil*, and forgiving all our *sins*[^40]. But those who, lifted up as on the buskin of a loftier teaching, do not hear him saying, *Learn from me, for I am gentle and humble of heart, and you will find rest for your souls*[^41]—even if they know God, they do not glorify him *as God or give thanks, but* become vain *in their thoughts, and their foolish heart is darkened; saying that they are wise, they became fools*[^42].

**15**. And therefore I read there also that *the glory of your incorruption* was changed into idols and various images, *into the likeness of the image of corruptible man, and of birds and four-footed beasts and serpents*[^43]—namely, that Egyptian food for which Esau lost his birthright[^44]; since the firstborn people honored a four-footed head in your place[^45], turned in heart toward Egypt[^46], and bowing down your image, their own soul[^47], before the image of *a calf that eats hay*[^48].

I found these things there, and I did not eat. For it pleased you, Lord, to take away the reproach[^49] of his lessening from Jacob, that the elder should serve the younger[^50], and you called *the gentiles into your inheritance*[^51]. And I had come to you from the gentiles, and I fixed my attention on the gold which you willed your people to carry off from Egypt[^52], since it was yours, wherever it was. And you said to the Athenians, through your Apostle, that in you *we live and move and have our being, as also certain* of their own *have said*[^53]; and surely those books were from there. And I did not attend to the idols of the Egyptians, whom they served with your gold, *who changed the truth of God into a lie, and worshiped and served the creature rather than the Creator*[^54].

## He returns into himself and, above his own mind, sees the light of the Lord

### Chapter 10

**16**. And admonished from there to return to myself, I entered into my inmost being, with you as my guide; and I was able, because *you had become my helper*[^55]. I entered, and saw, with the eye of my soul such as it was, above that same eye of my soul, above my mind, an unchangeable light—not this common light, visible to all flesh, nor, as it were, a greater one of the same kind, as though it should shine far more brightly and fill all things with its greatness. Not such was that light, but other, far other than all these. Nor was it above my mind as oil is above water, or as heaven above earth; but higher, because it made me, and I lower, because made by it. He who knows the truth knows it, and he who knows it knows eternity. Love knows it.

O eternal truth, and true love, and dear eternity! You are my God[^56]; to you I sigh day and night[^57]. And when I first knew you, you took me up[^58], that I might see there was something to see, and that I was not yet one who could see it. And you beat back the weakness of my gaze, blazing upon me vehemently, and I trembled with love and dread; and I found myself to be far from you, in the region of unlikeness[^59], as if I heard your voice from on high[^60]: "I am the food of the grown; grow, and you shall eat me. Nor shall you change me into yourself, like the food of your flesh, but you shall be changed into me." And I knew that *for iniquity you chastise man, and you made my soul waste away like a spider's web*[^61]; and I said, "Is truth, then, nothing, since it is diffused neither through finite nor through infinite spaces of place?" And you cried from afar[^62]: "No—rather, *I am who I am*[^63]."

And I heard, as one hears in the heart, and there was no way at all left for me to doubt; and I would more easily doubt that I am alive than that there is not truth, *which is understood and seen through the things that are made*[^64].

## Things neither altogether are, nor altogether are not

### Chapter 11

**17**. And I looked upon the other things below you, and I saw that they neither altogether are, nor altogether are not: they are, indeed, since they are from you; but they are not, since they are not what you are. For that truly is which abides unchangeably. *But for me it is good to cleave to God*[^65], because if I shall not abide in him, neither shall I be able to abide in myself. But he, *abiding in himself, makes all things new*[^66]; and *you are my Lord, since you have no need of my goods*[^67].

### Chapter 12: As long as they exist, things are good

**18**. And it was made manifest to me that the things which are corrupted are good—things which could not be corrupted either if they were supreme goods, or unless they were goods: for if they were supreme goods, they would be incorruptible; but if they were no goods at all, there would be nothing in them to be corrupted. For corruption does harm, and unless it lessened the good, it would do no harm. Therefore, either corruption does no harm—which cannot be—or, what is most certain, all things that are corrupted are deprived of good.

But if they shall be deprived of all good, they will not exist at all. For if they shall exist and can no longer be corrupted, they will be better, because they will abide incorruptibly. And what is more monstrous than to say that things, having lost all good, are made better? Therefore, if they shall be deprived of all good, they will be nothing at all; therefore, as long as they exist, they are good.

Therefore whatever things exist are good; and that evil whose source I sought is not a substance, because, if it were a substance, it would be good. For either it would be an incorruptible substance—a great good, surely—or a corruptible substance, which, unless it were good, could not be corrupted.

And so I saw, and it was made manifest to me, that you made all things good, and that there are no substances at all which you did not make. And because you did not make all things equal, therefore all things exist: because each thing is good, and all things together are very good, since our God made *all things very good*[^68].

### Chapter 13: Neither in God nor in his whole creation is there evil

**19**. And to you there is no evil at all—not only to you, but to your whole creation either, because outside you there is nothing that could break in and corrupt the order you imposed on it. But in its parts, certain things, because they do not agree with certain others, are thought evil; and those same things agree with others, and are good, and are good in themselves. And all these things that do not agree with one another agree with the lower part of things, which we call earth, having its own cloudy and windy sky suited to it.

And far be it that I should now say, "Let these things not exist," because, even if I saw these things alone, I would indeed desire better ones, but even for these alone I ought to praise you—since you are shown to be praiseworthy by *the dragons from the earth, and all deeps; fire, hail, snow, ice, the storm-wind that does your word; the mountains and all hills, the fruit trees and all cedars; the beasts and all cattle, creeping things and winged birds; the kings of the earth and all peoples, princes and all judges of the earth; young men and maidens, the old with the younger—let them praise your name*[^69].

But since they praise you also from the heavens, let them praise you, our God, *in the heights: all your angels, all your powers, sun and moon, all the stars and light, the heavens of heavens, and the waters that are above the heavens—let them praise* your *name*[^70]. I no longer desired better things, because I conceived all things; and the higher I judged better than the lower, indeed, but by a sounder judgment I weighed all things together as better than the higher alone.

### Chapter 14: The insane opinion of the Manichees about two substances

**20**. *There is no health*[^71] in those to whom something of your creation is displeasing—as there was none in me, when many things you made were displeasing to me. And because my soul did not dare that my God should be displeasing to it, it refused to count as yours whatever was displeasing to it. And from there it had gone off into the opinion of two substances, and found no rest, and spoke things not its own.

And returning from there, it had made itself a God through the infinite spaces of all places, and had supposed him to be you, and had set him in its heart, and had again become the temple of its own idol[^72], abominable to you. But after you cherished the head of one who knew it not, and closed *my eyes, lest they see vanity*[^73], I ceased a little from myself, and my madness was lulled to sleep; and I awoke in you, and saw you infinite in another way—and that sight was not drawn from the flesh.

### Chapter 15: All things are true insofar as they exist

**21**. And I looked back on other things, and saw that they owe it to you that they exist, and that all are bounded in you, but in another way—not as in a place, but because you are the all-holding one, holding all in your hand by truth; and all things are true insofar as they exist, nor is anything falsehood, except when what is not is thought to be.

And I saw that not only do all things agree with their own places, but also with their own times; and that you, who alone are eternal, did not begin to work after innumerable spaces of time, since all spaces of time, both those that have passed and those that will pass, would neither go nor come unless you were working and abiding.

### Chapter 16: Iniquity is the perversity of a will turned awry

**22**. And I felt, by experience, that it was no wonder that bread which is pleasant to a healthy palate is a torment to one not healthy, and that light, lovely to sound eyes, is hateful to sick ones. And your justice is displeasing to the wicked—much more so the viper and the little worm, which you created good, fitted to the lower parts of your creation, to which the wicked themselves are fitted, the more unlike they are to you; but they are fitted to the higher parts, the more like to you they become.

And I asked what iniquity is, and I found it to be no substance, but a perversity of will, turned aside from the supreme substance, you, O God, toward the lowest things—casting away its inmost parts[^74] and swelling outward.

## Augustine ascends step by step from bodies to God

### Chapter 17

**23**. And I marveled that I now loved you, and not a phantasm in your place; and I did not stand to enjoy my God, but was caught up to you by your beauty, and soon torn away from you by my own weight, and fell back with a groan into these lower things; and that weight was carnal habit. Yet the memory of you remained with me, and in no way did I doubt that there was one to whom I should cleave—but that I was not yet one who could cleave, since *the body that is corrupted weighs down the soul, and the earthly dwelling presses down the mind that ponders many things*[^75]; and I was most certain that your *invisible things, from the creation of the world, are understood and seen through the things that are made—your everlasting power also and divinity*[^76].

For, seeking whence I approved the beauty of bodies, whether heavenly or earthly, and what was at hand to me as I judged soundly about changeable things and said, "This ought to be so, that not so"—seeking, then, whence I judged, when I so judged, I had found the unchangeable and true eternity of truth above my changeable mind.

And so, step by step, from bodies to the soul that perceives through the body, and from there to its inner power, to which the body's sense announces external things—and as far as the beasts can go—

and from there again to the reasoning power, to which what is taken from the bodily senses is referred to be judged; which, finding itself too to be changeable in me, raised itself to its own understanding, and drew *my thought away from habit*[^77], withdrawing itself from the contradicting crowds of phantasms,

that it might find by what light it was sprinkled, when, without any doubt, it cried out that the unchangeable is to be preferred to the changeable—whence it knew the unchangeable itself (for unless it knew it in some way, it would in no way set it with certainty before the changeable)—and it arrived at That Which Is, in the stroke of a trembling glance.

Then indeed I beheld your *invisible things, understood through the things that are made*; but I was not able to fix my gaze, and, my weakness being beaten back, I was returned to my usual ways, carrying with me nothing but a loving memory and, as it were, a longing for what I had smelled but could not yet eat.

## Christ is the Mediator of God and men

### Chapter 18

**24**. And I sought a way of gaining the strength that would be fit for enjoying you, and I did not find it, until I embraced *the mediator of God and men, the man Christ Jesus*[^78], *who is over all things, God blessed forever*[^79], calling and saying, *I am the Way and the Truth and the Life*[^80], and mingling with flesh the food which I was too weak to take—since *the Word was made flesh*[^81], that your Wisdom, through which you created all things, might become milk for our infancy.

For I did not hold fast my God, the humble Jesus, being humble myself, nor did I know what his weakness had to teach. For your Word, the eternal truth, towering above the higher parts of your creation, raises up to itself those who are subject; but in the lower parts it *built itself a humble house*[^82] of our clay, by which to bring down from themselves those who were to be made subject, and to draw them across to itself, healing their swelling and nourishing their love—lest, through confidence in themselves, they go farther off, but rather grow weak, seeing before their feet the Godhead made weak by sharing our coat of skin[^83], and, wearied, throw themselves down upon it, while it, rising, lifts them up.

### Chapter 19: What Augustine and Alypius falsely thought about Christ

**25**. But I thought otherwise, and felt about my Lord Christ only as much as about a man of surpassing wisdom, to whom none could be equal—especially because, wondrously born of a Virgin, he seemed to have merited so great an authority of teaching, as an example of despising temporal things for the sake of attaining immortality, by his care for us. But what mystery the *Word made flesh*[^84] held, I could not so much as suspect.

I had learned only this much from what was handed down in writing about him—that he ate and drank, slept, walked, was glad, was saddened, conversed—that that flesh did not cleave to your Word except along with a human soul and mind. Everyone knows this who knows the unchangeableness of your Word, which I now knew as far as I could, nor did I doubt about it at all.

For now to move the members of the body by will, now not to move them; now to be moved by some feeling, now not; now to utter wise sentences through signs, now to be silent—these are proper to the changeableness of soul and mind. And if these things had been written falsely of him, all the rest too would be imperiled by the lie, nor would any saving faith for the human race remain in those writings. Since, therefore, they were written truly, I acknowledged a whole man in Christ—not only the body of a man, or, with the body, a soul without mind, but the very man; and I judged him to be set before others, not as the person of truth, but by some great excellence of human nature and a more perfect participation in wisdom.

But Alypius supposed that the catholic belief was that God was so clothed with flesh that in Christ there was nothing besides God and flesh; he did not think that a human soul and mind were preached in him. And because he firmly held it well persuaded that the things handed down to memory about him could not be done without a living and rational creature, he was the more sluggish in moving toward the Christian faith itself. But afterward, learning that this was the error of the Apollinarist heretics, he rejoiced with the catholic faith and was brought into accord with it.

But I confess that I learned somewhat later, in the matter of *the Word made flesh*, how the catholic truth is distinguished from the falsehood of Photinus. For the rejection of heretics makes it stand out what your Church holds, and what sound doctrine has[^85]. For *there had to be heresies also, that the approved*[^86] might be made manifest among the weak.

## Augustine had attained vanity, not humility

### Chapter 20

**26**. But then, having read those books of the Platonists, after I had been admonished from them to seek incorporeal truth, I beheld your *invisible things, understood through the things that are made*[^87], and, being repulsed, I perceived what, through the darkness of my soul, I was not allowed to contemplate—

certain that you exist, and that you are infinite, yet not diffused through finite or infinite places, and that you truly are, who are ever the same[^88], in no part and by no motion other or otherwise, while all other things are from you[^89], by this one most firm proof: that they exist. Of these things I was indeed certain, yet too weak to enjoy you.

I prattled plainly as though expert; and unless I had sought your way in *Christ our Savior*[^90], I would have been not expert but extinct. For I had now begun to wish to seem wise, full of my punishment, and I did not weep; rather, I was even puffed up with knowledge. For where was that love which builds upon the foundation of humility, which is Christ Jesus[^91]? Or when would those books teach me it? Into them, I believe, you willed me to fall before I considered your Scriptures, that it might be imprinted on my memory how I was affected by them, and that, when afterward I had been made gentle by your books, and my wounds were handled by your healing fingers, I might discern and distinguish what difference there is between presumption and confession—between those who see whither they must go but do not see the way, and the way that leads to the beatific homeland, not only to be beheld but to be dwelt in.

For if I had first been formed by your holy Writings, and you had grown sweet to me in familiarity with them, and afterward I had fallen upon those volumes, perhaps they would either have snatched me from the solid ground of devotion, or, if I had persisted in the wholesome affection I had drunk in, I might have thought that it could be conceived from those books too, if anyone had learned them alone.

## He read the apostle Paul most eagerly and fruitfully

### Chapter 21

**27**. And so I most eagerly seized the venerable pen of your Spirit, and before all the apostle Paul; and those questions vanished, in which the text of his discourse once seemed to me to contradict itself and not to agree with the testimonies of the Law and the Prophets[^92]; and the one face of those chaste eloquences[^93] appeared to me, and I learned to *exult with trembling*[^94].

And I began, and found that whatever truth I had read there was here said with the commendation of your grace—that he who sees should not so glory as if he had not received not only what he sees, but even the power to see (for what does he have that he has not received?[^95])—and that he should not only be admonished to see you, who are ever the same[^96], but also be healed to hold you,

and that he who cannot see from afar may yet walk the way by which he may come and see and hold; for although a man delights in *the law of God according to the inner man*, what shall he do about another law *in his members*, warring against *the law of his mind* and leading him captive *in the law of sin which is in his members*[^97]? *For you are just, O Lord*; but we *have sinned, have done wickedly*[^98], have acted impiously, and *your hand has grown heavy upon us*[^99], and we have been justly handed over to the ancient sinner, the lord of death, because he persuaded our will into the likeness of his own will, by which *he did not stand in* your *truth*[^100].

What shall *wretched man* do? *Who shall free* him *from the body of this death, but* your *grace, through Jesus Christ our Lord*[^101], whom you begot coeternal and created *in the beginning of your ways*[^102], in whom *the prince of this world*[^103] found nothing worthy of death[^104], yet killed him—and the *bond that was against us was canceled*[^105]?

These things those writings do not have. Those pages do not have the face of this devotion, the tears of confession, your *sacrifice, a troubled spirit, a contrite and humbled heart*[^106], the salvation of a people, the *bride, the city*[^107], the *pledge of the Holy Spirit*[^108], the cup of our ransom. No one there sings: *Shall not my soul be subject to God? For from him is my salvation; for he himself is my God and my salvation, my upholder; I shall be moved no more*[^109]. No one there hears him calling: *Come to me, you who labor*. They disdain to learn from him, *since he is gentle and humble of heart*[^110]. For *you hid these things from the wise and prudent, and revealed them to little ones*[^111].

And it is one thing, from a wooded summit, to see the homeland of peace[^112] and not find the way to it, and to struggle in vain over trackless places, beset and waylaid by fugitive deserters with their prince, the lion and the dragon[^113]; and another to hold to the way that leads there, fortified by the care of the heavenly Commander, where those who deserted the heavenly army do not lie in wait as robbers—for they avoid it like a punishment.

These things were sinking wondrously into my vitals as I read the least of your apostles[^114], and I had considered your works and was afraid[^115].
