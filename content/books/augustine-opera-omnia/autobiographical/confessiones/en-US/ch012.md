# Book 11: A meditation on the "beginning" in which God is said to have created heaven and earth

## A new desire takes hold of him

### Chapter 1: Augustine makes these confessions out of love for the love of God

**1**. Lord, since eternity is yours, do you not know what I say to you, or do you see in time what comes to be in time?

Why, then, do I set out to you so many narratives of things? Not, certainly, that you may know them through me, but I stir up my own affection toward you, and that of those who read these things, that we may all say: *Great is the Lord, and very much to be praised*[^1]. I have already said, and I will say: out of love of your love I do this. For we pray, and yet the Truth[^2] says: *Your Father knows what is needful for you before you ask him*[^3].

We open our affection to you, then, by confessing to you our miseries and your mercies *over us*[^4], that you may free us altogether—since you have begun—so that we may cease to be wretched in ourselves and be made blessed in you, since you have called us to be poor in spirit and meek and mourning and hungering and thirsting for righteousness and merciful and pure of heart and peacemakers[^5].

Behold, I have told you many things—what I could and what I willed—since you first willed that I should confess to you, *my Lord God, because you are good, because your mercy is forever*[^6].

### Chapter 2

**2**. But when shall I be sufficient, with the tongue of the pen[^7], to declare all your exhortations and all your terrors and consolations and governings, by which you have led me on to preach your word and to dispense your sacrament to your people?

And if I am sufficient to declare these things in order, the drops of time are precious to me. And for a long time now I have been burning to meditate on your law[^8], and to confess to you in it my knowledge and my ignorance, the beginnings of your illumination and the remnants of my darkness[^9], until weakness is swallowed up by strength. And I do not want the hours that I find free from the necessities of refreshing the body and the application of the mind and the service which we owe to people, and which we do not owe and yet pay, to flow off into anything else.

#### He prays to God

> **3**. Lord my God, *attend to my prayer*[^10],  
> and let your mercy hear my longing[^11];  
> for it burns not for me alone,  
> but wishes to be of use to brotherly love;  
> and you see in my heart that it is so.  
> Let me sacrifice to you  
> the service of my thought and tongue,  
> and give what *I may offer you*[^12].  
> For *I am needy and poor*[^13];  
> you are *rich toward all who call upon you*[^14],  
> who without anxiety bear care for us.  
> Circumcise from all rashness  
> and from all falsehood, within and without,  
> my lips[^15].

> Let your Scriptures be my chaste delights;  
> let me not be deceived in them,  
> nor deceive from them.  
> *Lord, attend*[^16], and *have mercy, Lord*[^17] my God,  
> light of the blind and strength of the weak,  
> and at the same time  
> light of the seeing  
> and strength of the strong,  
> attend to my soul  
> and hear it crying from the deep[^18].  
> For unless your ears are present even in the deep,  
> where shall we go?[^19] Where shall we cry out?  
> *Yours is the day, and yours is the night*[^20]:  
> at your nod the moments fly by.  
> Grant from there a space  
> for our meditations in the hidden things of your law,  
> and do not close it against those who knock[^21].

> For not in vain  
> have you willed  
> the dark secrets of so many pages to be written;  
> nor do those forests  
> lack their stags,  
> retiring into them and recovering themselves,  
> walking and grazing,  
> lying down and chewing the cud[^22].  
> O Lord, *perfect me*[^23]  
> and reveal them to me.

> Behold, your voice is my joy,  
> your voice above the abundance of pleasures.  
> Give what I love: for I love.  
> And this too you have given.  
> Do not forsake your gifts,  
> nor despise your grass when it thirsts.  
> Let me confess to you  
> whatever I find in your books,  
> and *let me hear the voice of praise*[^24],  
> and drink you,  
> and consider *the wonders of your law*[^25]  
> from the very beginning,  
> in which you made *heaven and earth*[^26],  
> even to the everlasting reign with you[^27]  
> of your holy city[^28].

**4**. *Lord, have mercy on me, and hear*[^29] my longing. For I think that it is not for the earth, not for gold and silver and gems or rich garments or honors and powers or pleasures of the flesh, nor for the necessities of the body and of this life of our pilgrimage—all which things are added to us as we seek *your kingdom and your righteousness*[^30].

*See*[^31], my God, what my longing is from. *The unjust have told me of delights, but not as your law*[^32], Lord. Behold, that is what my longing is from.

See, Father, look and see and approve; and let it please in the sight of your mercy[^33] that I find grace before you, that the inner parts of your words may be opened to me as I knock[^34].

I beseech you through our Lord Jesus Christ, your Son, *the man of your right hand, the Son of man, whom you have strengthened for yourself*[^35], your Mediator and ours[^36], through whom you sought us who were not seeking you—yet sought us that we might seek you—your Word, through whom you made all things[^37], among them also me; your Only-begotten, through whom you have called into adoption the people of believers[^38], among them also me. Through him I beseech you, who sits *at your right hand and intercedes for us*[^39], *in whom are hidden all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge*[^40]. These I seek in your books. Moses wrote of him: he himself says it[^41]; the Truth says it[^42].

## In his Word God made all things

### Chapter 3

**5**. Let me hear and understand how *in the beginning you made heaven and earth*[^43]. Moses wrote this; he wrote it and went away; he passed from here from you to you, and now he is not before me. For if he were, I would hold him and ask him and beseech him through you to unfold these things to me, and I would offer the ears of my body to the sounds breaking forth from his mouth; and if he spoke in the Hebrew tongue, in vain would it knock[^44] at my sense, nor would anything from there touch my mind; but if in Latin, I would know what he said.

But how would I know whether he was speaking the truth? And if I should know this, would I know it from him? Within me, surely—within, in the dwelling place of thought, neither Hebrew nor Greek nor Latin nor barbarian, the Truth, without organs of mouth and tongue, without the noise of syllables, would say: "He speaks truth"; and I, at once certain, would confidently say to that man of yours: "You speak truth."

Since, then, I cannot question him, I ask you, Truth—you, full of whom he spoke true things—I ask you[^45], my God, *spare my sins*[^46]; and you who granted that servant of yours to say these things, grant me also to understand them[^47].

### Chapter 4: That heaven and earth exist and were made is plain

**6**. Behold, heaven and earth are; they cry out that they were made, for they are changed and varied. But whatever has not been made and yet is—in that there is nothing which was not there before, which is to be changed and varied.

They cry out also that they did not make themselves: "We are, because we have been made; we were not, then, before we were, that we could be made by ourselves." And the voice of those who say it is the very evidence.

You, then, Lord, made them, you who are beautiful: for they are beautiful; you who are good: for they are good; you who are: for they are.

They are not beautiful, nor good, nor are they, as you, their Maker, are; compared with whom they are neither beautiful nor good, nor are they. We know these things, thanks be to you; and our knowledge, compared with your knowledge, is ignorance.

## A human shapes, but God creates

### Chapter 5

**7**. But how did you make *heaven and earth*[^48], and what was the engine of so great a work of yours? For you did not work like a human artisan, who forms one body from another at the discretion of a soul able to impose, somehow, the form which it discerns within itself with the inner eye (and from where would this come, except that you made it?), and imposes the form on something already existing and having to be—earth, say, or stone, or wood, or gold, or any thing of that kind.

And from where would these be, unless you had appointed them? You made the artisan's body; you made the soul that commands the limbs; you made the matter from which he makes something; you made the genius by which he grasps an art and sees within what he makes without;

you made the sense of the body, through which, as an interpreter, he transfers from the mind to the matter what he is making, and reports back to the mind what has been made, that within he may consult the truth presiding over him, whether it has been well made. All these things praise you, the Creator of all.

But how do you make these things? How did you, God, make *heaven and earth*? Certainly not in heaven nor on earth did you make heaven and earth, nor in air or in waters, since these too belong to heaven and earth; nor in the universal world did you make the universal world, since there was no place where it could be made, before it was made, that it might be.

Nor did you hold anything in your hand from which to make heaven and earth: for from where would there be for you this—not made by you—from which to make something? For what is there, except because you are?

Therefore *you spoke, and they were made*[^49], and in your word you made them[^50].

### Chapter 6: Human words sound in time, but the Word of God abides forever

**8**. But how did you speak? Was it in the way in which *a voice was made from the cloud, saying: This is my beloved Son*[^51]? For that voice was performed and ended, begun and finished. The syllables sounded and passed, the second after the first, the third after the second, and so in order, until the last after all the rest, and silence after the last.

From this it is clear and stands out that the motion of a creature expressed that voice, serving your eternal will while itself temporal. And these your words, made for a time, the outer ear announced to a prudent mind, whose inner ear is set toward your eternal Word. But the mind compared these words sounding temporally with your eternal Word in silence, and said: "It is far different, far different. These are far beneath me, and are not, because they flee and pass away: but *the word* of my God above me *abides forever*[^52]."

If, then, you spoke with sounding and passing words, that heaven and earth might be made, and so made heaven and earth, then there was already some bodily creature before heaven and earth, by whose temporal motions that voice would run through in time.

But there was no body before heaven and earth; or if there was, you had surely made it without any passing voice, from which to make the passing voice by which you would say that heaven and earth should be made.

For whatever it was from which such a voice would be made, unless it had been made by you, it would not be at all. So that a body might come to be, from which these words could be made, by what word was it spoken by you?

## The eternal Word of God

### Chapter 7

**9**. You call us, then, to understand the Word, *God with you, God*[^53], which is sempiternally spoken, and by which all things are sempiternally spoken.

For what was being spoken does not come to an end, and another is then spoken, that all things may be said; rather, all things are said together and sempiternally. Otherwise there would already be time and change, and no true eternity nor true immortality.

This I know, my God, and I *give thanks*[^54]. I know it, Lord, I confess to you; and with me he knows it and blesses you, whoever is not ungrateful toward sure truth. We know, Lord, we know, since insofar as anything is not what it was and is what it was not, so far it dies and arises. Nothing of your Word, then, gives way and succeeds, since it is truly immortal and eternal.

And therefore by a Word coeternal with you, you say at once and sempiternally all the things you say; and whatever you say should be made, comes to be; nor do you make in any other way than by speaking; and yet not all things that you make by speaking come to be at once and sempiternally.

## The voice of the Lord speaks to us in the Gospel

### Chapter 8

**10**. Why, I ask, Lord my God? Somehow I see it, but how to express it I do not know—except that everything that begins to be and ceases to be then begins to be and then ceases when, in the eternal reason, it is known that it ought to have begun or ceased—where nothing begins or ceases. That is your Word, which is also the Beginning, because *he also speaks to us*[^55]. So in the Gospel he says it through the flesh, and this resounded outwardly in the ears of people, that it might be believed and inwardly sought and found in the eternal truth, where the good and only Master teaches all his disciples[^56].

There I hear your voice, Lord, telling me that he speaks to us who teaches us; while one who does not teach us, even if he speaks, does not speak to us. Who, indeed, teaches us, but the stable Truth? For even when we are admonished through a mutable creature, we are led to the stable Truth, where we truly learn, when we stand and hear him, *and rejoice with joy because of the bridegroom's voice*[^57], restoring ourselves to where we come from.

And therefore the Beginning: for unless it remained when we strayed, there would be nothing to return to. But when we return from error, surely we return by knowing; and that we may know, he teaches us, because he is the Beginning and *speaks to us*[^58].

### Chapter 9: Let us hear God speaking to us within

**11**. In this Beginning, God, you made *heaven and earth*[^59], in your Word, in your Son, in your Power, in your Wisdom, in your Truth—speaking in a wonderful way and making in a wonderful way. Who will comprehend? Who will narrate? What is it that shines through to me and strikes my heart without injury? And I shudder and I burn: I shudder, insofar as I am unlike it; I burn, insofar as I am like it.

Wisdom, Wisdom herself it is, who shines through to me, cleaving my cloud, which again covers me, fainting back from her in the darkness and the heap of my punishments; for so *my strength is weakened in poverty*[^60], that I cannot bear my good, until you, Lord, who have become propitious to all my iniquities, also heal all my infirmities, since you will also redeem my life from corruption and crown me with pity and mercy and satisfy my desire with good things, so that *my youth shall be renewed like the eagle's*[^61]. For *we are saved by hope*, and we await your promises *through patience*[^62].

Let him hear you speaking within who can; I, confidently, will cry out from your oracle: *How magnified are your works, Lord! In Wisdom you have made all things*[^63]! And that Wisdom is the Beginning, and *in that Beginning you made heaven and earth*.

## What was God doing before he made heaven and earth?

### Chapter 10

**12**. Behold, are not those full of their old self[^64] who say to us: "What was God doing before he made *heaven and earth*[^65]? For if he was at leisure," they say, "and was working nothing, why not so always henceforth, just as he always rested *from work*[^66] before? For if any new motion arose in God, and a new will, that he might found a creature which he had never founded before, how is there any true eternity, where a will arises that was not? For the will of God is not a creature, but before every creature, since nothing would be created unless the will of the Creator preceded. Therefore his will pertains to the very substance of God. But if anything has arisen in God's substance which was not there before, that substance is not truly called eternal; on the other hand, if God's will that there should be a creature was sempiternal, why is the creature not sempiternal too?"

### Chapter 11: Time passes; eternity stands

**13**. Those who say these things do not yet understand you, *O Wisdom of God*[^67], Light of minds; they do not yet understand how the things that are made through you and in you come to be, and they try to taste of eternal things; but their *heart* still flits among the past and future motions of things and is still *vain*[^68].

Who will hold the heart and fix it, that it may stand a little and snatch a little of the splendor of ever-standing eternity, and compare it with the never-standing times, and see that they are incomparable; and see that a long time does not become long except by many passing motions, which cannot be extended together; that nothing passes away in the eternal, but the whole is present; while no time is wholly present; and see that every past is pushed away by the future, and every future follows from the past, and that every past and future is created and runs forth from that which is always present?

Who will hold the human heart, that it may stand and see how, while standing, eternity dictates future and past times, though it is itself neither future nor past? Is *my hand strong enough*[^69] for this, or does the hand of my mouth, through speech, perform so great a thing?

### Chapter 12: God did nothing before time

**14**. Behold, I answer the one who says: "What was God doing before he made *heaven and earth*[^70]?" I do not answer what someone is said to have answered, jestingly eluding the force of the question: "He was preparing hells," he said, "for those scrutinizing such deep things." It is one thing to see, another to mock. I do not answer thus. For I would rather answer, "I do not know what I do not know," than answer in a way by which the one who asked deep things is mocked, and the one who answered falsely is praised.

But I say that you, our God, are the Creator of every creature; and if by the name "heaven and earth" every creature is understood, I say boldly: "Before God made heaven and earth, he was making nothing." For if he was making anything, what was he making but a creature? And would that I knew, of whatever I usefully desire to know, as I know that no creature was being made before any creature was made.

### Chapter 13: There is no time that God does not make

**15**. But if anyone's flighty sense wanders through the images of times past, and marvels that you, God almighty and all-creating and all-holding, the artisan of heaven and earth, ceased *from work*[^71] so great, before you did it, through countless ages—let him wake up and consider, since he marvels at falsehoods.

For from where could countless ages pass, which you yourself had not made, since you are the author and founder of all ages? Or what times could have been which had not been founded by you? Or how would they pass, if they had never been? Since, then, you are the maker of all times, if there was any time before you made *heaven and earth*[^72], why is it said that you were ceasing from work? For that very time you had made; nor could times pass before you made times. But if before heaven and earth there was no time, why is it asked what you were then doing? For there was no "then," where there was no time.

#### God precedes the times by eternity

**16**. Nor do you precede the times in time: otherwise you would not precede all times. But you precede all past things by the loftiness of an ever-present eternity, and you surpass all future things, because they are future, and when they have come, they will be past; but *you are the same, and your years do not fail*[^73].

Your years neither go nor come: for these of ours go and come, that all may come. Your years all stand together, because they stand; nor are those that go shut out by those that come, because they do not pass; but ours will all be only when none are.

*Your years are one day*[^74], and your day is not "daily," but "today," because your today does not give way to tomorrow; nor does it succeed yesterday. Your today is eternity: therefore you have begotten one coeternal, to whom you said, *Today I have begotten you*[^75]. You made all times, and you are before all times; nor was there any time when there was no time.

## What time is

### Chapter 14

**17**. There was no time, then, when you had not made something, because you had made time itself. And no times are coeternal with you, because you abide; whereas if they abided, they would not be times.

For what is time? Who could easily and briefly explain it? Who could comprehend it even in thought, so as to put it into a word? And yet what do we mention in speech more familiarly and known than time? And we understand it, certainly, when we speak of it; we understand also when we hear another speak of it. What, then, is time? If no one asks me, I know; if I wish to explain to one who asks, I do not know. Yet I confidently say that I know that, if nothing passed by, there would be no past time; and if nothing were coming, there would be no future time; and if nothing existed, there would be no present time.

Those two times, then, past and future, how are they, when the past is no more and the future is not yet? But if the present always were present and did not pass into the past, it would no longer be time, but eternity. If, then, the present, in order to be time, becomes so precisely because it passes into the past, how can we say that it too is, when its cause for being is this—that it will not be—so that we cannot truly say that time is, except because it tends not to be?

### Chapter 15: By what reckoning time is long or short

**18**. And yet we say a long time and a short time, and we say this only of the past or the future. For example, we call a long past time "a hundred years ago"; likewise a long future time "a hundred years hence"; a short past, say, "ten days ago," and a short future "ten days hence." But in what way is that long or short which is not? For the past is no longer, and the future is not yet. Therefore let us not say, "It is long," but say of the past, "It was long," and of the future, "It will be long."

My Lord, *my Light*[^76], will not your truth deride a man here too? For when a past time was long, was it long when it was now past, or when it was still present? For then it could be long, when there was something that could be long; but as past, it was no more; therefore it could not be long, since it was not at all. So let us not say, "A past time was long" (for we shall not find what was long, since from the moment it is past, it is not), but let us say, "That present time was long," because, when it was present, it was long. For it had not yet passed away, so as not to be; and therefore there was something that could be long; but after it passed away, what ceased to be ceased at the same time to be long.

#### Whether present time can be long

**19**. Let us see, then, human soul, whether present time can be long: for it has been given to you to perceive intervals and to measure them. What will you answer me? Are a hundred present years a long time? See first whether a hundred years can be present. For if the first of them is being run, it is present, but the ninety-nine are future, and so they are not yet; but if the second year is being run, then one is already past, another present, the rest future. And so, whichever of the middle years of this number of a hundred we set as present, those before it will be past, those after it future. Therefore a hundred years cannot be present.

See at least whether the one year being run is itself present. For if its first month is being run, the others are future; if the second, then the first is already past and the rest are not yet. Therefore not even the year being run is wholly present; and if it is not wholly present, the year is not present. For a year is twelve months, of which whichever one month is being run, that one is present, the others either past or future.

Although not even the month being run is present, but a single day; if the first, with the rest future; if the last, with the rest past; if whichever of the middle ones, then between past and future.

**20**. Behold, present time, which alone we were finding could be called long, has scarcely been contracted to the space of a single day. But let us examine even this, because not even a single day is wholly present. For it is made up of twenty-four nightly and daily hours, of which the first has the rest as future, the last as past, and any of those in between has those before it as past, those after it as future. And one hour itself is run with fleeting particles: whatever of it has flown is past, whatever remains is future.

If anything of time is understood that cannot now be divided into even the smallest particles of moments, that alone is what is called present; and yet it flies so swiftly from future into past that it is not extended by even the slightest delay. For if it is extended, it is divided into past and future: but the present has no space.

Where, then, is the time which we may call long? Is it the future? Of this we do not say, "It is long," because what could be long is not yet, but we say, "It will be long." When, then, will it be? For if it will still then be future, it will not be long, because what could be long will not yet be; but if it will then be long, when, from being future which is not yet, it has begun now to be and has become present, so that there can be something that is long—already, by the earlier arguments, the present time cries out that it cannot be long.

### Chapter 16: We measure time as it passes

**21**. And yet, Lord, we perceive intervals of times and compare them with one another, and say some are longer, some shorter. We even measure how much longer or shorter this time is than that, and we answer that this is double or triple, that one is simple, or that this is as much as that. But we measure times as they pass when we measure them by perceiving; the past, which is no more, or the future, which is not yet—who can measure these, unless someone perhaps dares to say that what is not can be measured? While time passes, then, it can be perceived and measured; but when it has passed, since it is not, it cannot.

### Chapter 17: Future and past do exist, evidently

**22**. I ask, Father, I do not affirm: my God, preside over me and *rule me*[^77]. Who is it that will say to me that there are not three times, as we learned as children and have taught children—past, present, and future—but only the present, since those other two are not?

Or do they too exist, but proceeding from some hidden place, when from future it becomes present, and withdrawing into some hidden place, when from present it becomes past? For where did they see them, who sang of future things, if they are not yet? For what is not cannot be seen. And those who tell of past things would certainly not tell true things, if they did not discern them in mind; and if they were nothing, they could not be discerned at all. Therefore future and past things do exist.

### Chapter 18: Past and future, wherever they are, are present

**23**. Let me, *Lord*, search further, *my hope*[^78]; let not my attention be disturbed. For if future and past things do exist, I wish to know where they are. And if I cannot yet, I know nonetheless that, wherever they are, they are not there as future or past, but as present. For if they are future there too, they are not yet there; if they are past there too, they are no longer there. Wherever, then, they are, whatever they are, they are only as present.

Although, when past things are truly told, there are brought forth from memory not the things themselves that have passed, but words conceived from images of them which they have fixed in the mind, as it were traces, in passing through the senses. My childhood, indeed, which is no more, is in past time which is no more; but its image, when I call it to mind and tell it, I behold in present time, since it is still in my memory.

Whether the cause is similar even of foretelling future things—that images of things which are not yet, already existing, are perceived in advance—I confess, my God, I do not know. But this I do know: that we generally premeditate our future actions, and that this premeditation is present, while the action which we premeditate is not yet, because it is future; and when we have undertaken it and have begun to do what we premeditated, then that action will be, because then it will be not future but present.

#### On the hidden presensing of future things

**24**. In whatever way, then, the hidden presensing of future things stands, only what is can be seen. But what already is, is not future but present. When, then, future things are said to be seen, it is not they themselves, which are not yet—that is, which are future—but perhaps their causes or signs are seen, which already are; and so these are not future but present to those now seeing, from which future things are predicted as conceived in mind. These conceptions, again, already are, and those who predict future things behold them as present with themselves.

Let so great a multitude of things speak to me through one example. I look at the dawn: I announce that the sun is about to rise. What I look at is present; what I announce is future—not the sun, which is future, since it already is, but its rising, which is not yet. Yet unless I also imagined the rising itself in my mind, as I do now in speaking of it, I could not predict it. But neither is the dawn that I see in the sky the sun's rising, although it precedes it; nor is that imagination in my mind; these two are discerned as present, so that the future rising may be spoken of beforehand.

Future things, then, are not yet; and if they are not yet, they are not; and if they are not, they cannot be seen at all; but they can be predicted from present things, which already are and are seen.

### Chapter 19: How does God teach future things?

**25**. You, then, ruler of your creature, what is the way in which you teach souls the things that are to come? For you have taught your prophets. What is the way in which you teach future things, you to whom nothing is future? Or rather do you teach things present about future things? For what is not cannot be taught. This way is too far from my gaze; *it has grown strong beyond me; I shall not be able to reach it*[^79]; but I shall be able from you, when you give it, *sweet light* of my *hidden eyes*[^80].

### Chapter 20: Properly speaking, the times are not three

**26**. But what is now clear and plain is that neither future nor past things are; nor is it properly said that times are three—past, present, and future—but perhaps it would be properly said: times are three—a present of past things, a present of present things, a present of future things. For there are these three in the soul, I know not what; and elsewhere I do not see them: the present of past things is memory; the present of present things is direct attention; the present of future things is expectation. If we are permitted to speak so, I see three times, and I confess: they are three.

Let it also be said, "Times are three, past, present, and future," as custom abuses; let it be said. Behold, I do not care, nor resist, nor reprove, so long as what is said is understood—that neither what is future already is, nor what is past. For there are few things which we speak properly; there are more which we speak not properly, yet what we mean is recognized.

### Chapter 21: We measure time in some space

**27**. I said, then, a little before, that we measure passing times, that we may say that this stretch of time is double that simple one, or that this is as much as that, and whatever else of the parts of times we can report by measuring. And so, as I was saying, we measure passing times; and if anyone should say to me, "How do you know?" I should reply: "I know, because we measure them; and we cannot measure what is not, and past or future things are not." But present time—how do we measure it, when it has no space? We measure it as it passes, then; but when it has passed, it is not measured; for what could be measured will not be.

But from where, and through where, and where does it pass when it is measured? From where, but from the future? Through where, but through the present? Where, but into the past? From that, then, which is not yet; through that which lacks space; into that which is no more. But what do we measure, except time in some space? For we do not say "simple" and "double" and "triple" and "equal," and whatever else we say in this way about time, except spaces of times.

In what space, then, do we measure passing time? Is it in the future, from which it passes? But we do not measure what is not yet. Is it in the present, through which it passes? But what has no space, we do not measure. Is it in the past, into which it passes? But what is no more, we do not measure.

### Chapter 22: Let God unlock what Augustine longs to know

**28**. My mind has caught fire to know this most intricate riddle. Do not shut up, Lord my God, good Father—through Christ I beseech you—do not shut up from my longing these things, both familiar and hidden, lest it should not enter into them and they should grow bright as your mercy shines on them, Lord.

Whom shall I question about these things? And to whom shall I more fruitfully confess my ignorance than to you, to whom my zeal, vehemently burning in your Scriptures, is not burdensome? Give what I love: for I love, and this you have given. Give, Father, who truly know *how to give good gifts to your children*[^81]; give, since I have undertaken to know, and *labor is before me*[^82], until you open[^83].

Through Christ I beseech you—in his name, the holy of holies, let no one rage against me. *And I have believed, for which reason I also speak*[^84]. This is my hope; for it I live, *that I may contemplate the delight of the Lord*[^85]. *Behold, you have set my days old*[^86], and they pass away—and how, I do not know.

And we say "time" and "time," "times" and "times": "How long ago he said this"; "How long ago he did this"; and "For how long a time I have not seen that"; and "This syllable has double the time of that simple short one." We say these things and we hear them, and we are understood and we understand. They are most plain and most familiar, and yet they lie hidden too deeply, and finding them out is a new thing.

## Whether time is motion

### Chapter 23

**29**. I have heard from a certain learned man that the motions of the sun and moon and stars are themselves the times, and I did not assent. For why are the motions of all bodies not rather the times? Or, if the lights of heaven stopped and the potter's wheel kept moving, would there be no time by which we could measure those revolutions, and say either that they are made at equal intervals, or, if it moved at one time more slowly, at another more swiftly, that some are longer-lasting, others less?

Or when we said these things, would we not also be speaking in time? Or would there be in our words some long syllables and some short, unless because the former had sounded for a longer time, the latter for a shorter?

God, grant to human beings to see in a small thing common notions of things small and great. There are *stars and luminaries of heaven for signs and for seasons and for days and for years*[^87]. They are, indeed; but I would not say that one revolution of that little wooden wheel was a day, nor would the learned man say that, on that account, it was not time.

**30**. I desire to know the power and nature of time, by which we measure the motions of bodies and say, for example, that this motion is twice as long-lasting in time as that. For I ask, since a day is called not only the sun's tarrying above the earth—according to which day is one thing, night another—but also its whole circuit from east to east, according to which we say, "So many days have passed" (for so many days are spoken of with their nights, and the spaces of the nights are not reckoned apart)—since, then, a day is completed by the sun's motion and circuit from east to east, I ask whether the day is the motion itself, or the duration in which it is accomplished, or both.

For if the first were the day, then it would be a day even if the sun had run its course in as small a space of time as a single hour. If the second, then it would not be a day if from one rising of the sun to another there were as brief a stretch as one hour, but the sun would have to go round twenty-four times to complete a day. If both, neither would it be called a day if the sun ran its whole circuit in the space of an hour, nor that, if, with the sun stopped, as much time passed as the sun usually takes to run its whole circuit from morning to morning.

So now I will not ask what that is which is called a day, but what time is, by which, measuring the sun's circuit, we should say that it was completed in less than half the space of time it usually takes, if it were completed in as much space of time as twelve hours take, and, comparing the two times, we should say the one was simple and the other double—even if at one moment the sun were to go around from east to east in that simple time, at another in this double. Let no one, then, tell me that the motions of heavenly bodies are the times, because, when at one man's prayer the sun stood still so that he might finish a victorious battle, the sun stood still[^88]—but time went on. For that battle was waged and finished through its own space of time, sufficient for it.

I see, therefore, that time is a certain distention. But do I see, or do I only seem to myself to see? You will show me, Light, Truth[^89].

### Chapter 24: We measure by time how long bodies are in motion

**31**. Do you bid me approve if someone says that time is the motion of a body? You do not bid it. For I hear that no body is moved except in time: you say so. But that the motion of a body itself is time, I do not hear: you do not say so.

For when a body is moved, I measure by time how long it is moved, from the moment it begins to move until it stops. And if I did not see when it began, and it continues to move so that I do not see when it stops, I cannot measure it, except perhaps from the moment I begin to see until I cease.

And if I see it for a long time, I report only that there is a long time, but not how much, because, when we say how much, we say it by comparison, as: "This is as much as that," or "This is double that," and whatever else of this kind.

But if we are able to mark the spaces of places—from where and to where the body that is moved comes, or its parts, if it moves as on a lathe—we can say how much time it is, since from that place to that place the motion of the body or of its part was carried out.

Since, then, the motion of a body is one thing, and that by which we measure how long it lasts is another, who does not feel which of these is rather to be called time?

For if a body sometimes moves, sometimes is at rest, we measure by time not only its motion but also its rest, and we say: "It stood as long as it moved," or "It stood double or triple of what it moved," and whatever else our measurement either has grasped or estimated, as we say more or less. Time, therefore, is not the motion of a body.

### Chapter 25: The Lord is the light of the mind

**32**. *And I confess to you, Lord*[^90], that I still do not know what time is; and again *I confess to you, Lord*, that I know I say these things in time, and that I have already spoken long of time, and that this "long" is not long except by the delay of time.

How, then, do I know this, when I do not know what time is? Or do I perhaps not know how to say what I know? Woe is me, who do not even know what I do not know!

Behold, my God, *before you, that I do not lie*[^91]; as I speak, so is my heart. You will *light my lamp, Lord, my God; you will light my darkness*[^92].

## We measure time by time

### Chapter 26

**33**. Does not my soul confess to you in a truthful confession that I measure times? Is it so, then, my God, that I measure, and what I measure I do not know? I measure the motion of a body by time. Likewise, do I not measure time itself? Or should I measure the motion of a body—how long it lasts and how long it takes to go from here to there—unless I measured the time in which it is moved?

By what, then, do I measure time itself? Do we measure a longer time by a shorter, as the length of a beam by the length of a cubit? For so we seem to measure the space of a long syllable by the space of a short syllable, and to call it double.

So we measure the spaces of poems by the spaces of verses, and the spaces of verses by the spaces of feet, and the spaces of feet by the spaces of syllables, and the spaces of long syllables by the spaces of short—not on pages (for in that way we measure places, not times), but as voices pass by in being pronounced, and we say: "It is a long poem, for it is woven of so many verses; the verses are long, for they consist of so many feet; the feet are long, for they are extended by so many syllables; the syllable is long, for it is double to a short."

But not even thus is a sure measure of time grasped, since it can happen that a shorter verse sounds for a longer space of time, if it is pronounced more drawn out, than a longer one pronounced more curtly. So with a poem, so with a foot, so with a syllable.

From this it seemed to me that time is nothing else than a distention—but of what thing, I do not know; and it would be a wonder if it were not of the mind itself. For what do I measure, I ask, my God, when I say either indefinitely, "This time is longer than that," or even definitely, "This is double that"? I measure time, I know; but I do not measure the future, because it is not yet; I do not measure the present, because it is stretched in no space; I do not measure the past, because it is no more. What, then, do I measure? Times as they pass, not times past? For so I had said.

### Chapter 27: What do we measure when we measure times?

**34**. Press on, my mind, and attend with all your strength: *God is our helper*[^93]; *he made us, and not we ourselves*[^94]. Attend, where truth begins to dawn.

Suppose, for example, a bodily voice begins to sound, and sounds, and still sounds; and behold, it ceases, and now there is silence, and that voice is past and is no longer a voice. Before it sounded, it was future, and could not be measured, because it was not yet; and now it cannot be, because it is no more.

Then, then, it could be measured, when it was sounding, because then there was something that could be measured. But then too it was not standing still; it was going and passing. Was it then more able? For in passing, it was being extended into some space of time in which it could be measured, since the present has no space.

If, then, it could then, suppose: behold, another voice has begun to sound, and still sounds, in a continuous tone with no interruption. Let us measure it while it sounds; for when it has ceased to sound, it will already be past, and there will be nothing that can be measured. Let us plainly measure it, and say how long it is.

But it still sounds, and it cannot be measured except from its beginning, at which it began to sound, up to the end, at which it ceases. For the very interval we measure from some beginning to some end. Therefore the voice which is not yet finished cannot be measured, so as to say how long or how short it is, nor said either to be equal to some other, or simple or double or anything else compared to it. But when it has been finished, it will no longer be. How, then, can it be measured?

And yet we do measure times—not those that are not yet, nor those that are no more, nor those that are extended by no delay, nor those that have no boundaries. Therefore we measure neither future nor past nor present nor passing times—and yet we do measure times.

**35**. *Deus Creator omnium*: this verse of eight syllables alternates between short and long syllables; the four short ones—first, third, fifth, seventh—are simple to the four long ones—second, fourth, sixth, eighth. Each of the latter has double the time of each of the former; I pronounce and report, and so it is, as far as is perceived by plain sense.

So far as plain sense goes, I measure the long syllable by the short, and I perceive it to have twice as much. But when one sounds after another, the short first, the long after, how shall I hold the short, and how, in measuring, shall I apply it to the long, to find that it has twice as much—since the long does not begin to sound until the short has ceased to sound?

And do I measure the long itself as present, when I do not measure it unless it is finished? But its finishing is its passing. What, then, is it that I measure? Where is the short by which I measure? Where is the long that I measure?

Both have sounded, have flown away, have passed, are no more; and yet I measure, and I confidently reply, so far as a practiced sense can be trusted, that the one is simple, the other double—in the space, that is, of time. And I cannot do this except because they have passed and are finished. It is not, then, the syllables themselves, which are no more, but something in my memory that I measure, which remains fixed.

#### We measure times in our mind

**36**. In you, my mind, I measure times. Do not make a noise against me—that is, do not make a noise against yourself with the crowds of your affections. In you, I say, I measure times. The affection which passing things produce in you, and which remains when they have passed—that itself I measure as present, not the things that have passed in order to produce it; this itself I measure when I measure times. Therefore either these are the times, or I do not measure times.

What of when we measure silences and say that this silence held as much time as that voice held—do we not extend our thought to the measure of the voice, as if it were sounding, that we may be able to report something about the intervals of silences in the space of time? For even with voice and mouth at rest, we run through, by thinking, poems and verses and whatever speech, and any kind of measure of motions, and we report concerning the spaces of times—how much this is to that—in no other way than if we were saying them by sounding.

Suppose someone wishes to produce a somewhat long voice, and has decided by premeditation how long it will be: he has surely run through that space of time in silence, and, entrusting it to memory, has begun to produce that voice which sounds, until it is brought to the proposed end:

rather, it has sounded and it will sound; for what of it is already completed has surely sounded, but what remains will sound; and so it is completed, while the present attention transfers the future into the past, with the diminishing of the future and the increasing of the past, until, by the consumption of the future, the whole is past.

### Chapter 28: Expectation, attention, memory

**37**. But how is the future diminished or consumed, since it is not yet, or how does the past grow, since it is no more, unless because in the mind which does this there are three: namely, expectation, attention, and memory, so that what is expected passes through what is attended to into what is remembered?

Who, then, denies that future things are not yet? But yet there is already in the mind an expectation of future things.

And who denies that past things are no more? But yet there is still in the mind a memory of past things.

And who denies that present time has no space, since it passes in a point? But yet attention endures, through which what will be present moves on to be absent.

It is not, then, future time that is long, which is not; but a long future is a long expectation of the future; nor is it past time that is long, which is not; but a long past is a long memory of the past.

#### After the distention of life—oh, that we may flow together into God!

**38**. I am about to recite a song which I know: before I begin, my expectation is extended over the whole; but when I have begun, my memory is also extended over as much of it as I have plucked off into the past; and the life of this action of mine is distended into memory, for what I have said, and into expectation, for what I am about to say. Yet my attention is present, through which what was future is transferred to become past. As this is done more and more, the more is the expectation shortened and the memory lengthened, until the whole expectation is consumed, when the whole action, having been completed, has passed into memory.

And what happens with the whole song, happens with each of its parts and with each of its syllables; what happens with a longer action of which that song is perhaps a part; what happens with the whole life of a person, whose parts are all the actions of the person; what happens with the whole age *of the sons of men*[^95], whose parts are all the lives of all human beings.

## After the distention of life—oh, that we may flow together into God!

### Chapter 29

**39**. But since *your mercy is better than lives*[^96], behold, my life is a distention, and *your right hand has upheld me*[^97] in my Lord, the Mediator, the Son of Man, between you the one and us the many[^98], among many through many—that through him I may *lay hold*, by whom I have also been *laid hold*, and may be gathered up from my old days by following the One; *forgetting the things behind*, not distended into things future and to pass away, but *extended toward the things that are before*—not according to distention, but according to intention, *I follow on to the prize of the heavenly calling*[^99], where *I shall hear the voice of praise*[^100] and *contemplate your delight*[^101], neither coming nor passing away.

But now *my years are in groanings*[^102]; and you, my solace, Lord, are my eternal Father; but I have been split apart into times whose order I do not know, and my thoughts—the inmost entrails of my soul—are torn by tumultuous varieties, until I flow together into you, purged and made clean by the fire of your love.

### Chapter 30: God is before all times

**40**. And I shall stand[^103] and be made firm in you, in my form, your truth; nor shall I suffer the questions of men who, by a penal disease, thirst for more than they can grasp, and say: "What was God doing before he made *heaven and earth*[^104]?" or "What came into his mind, that he should make something, when before he had never made anything?"

Grant them, Lord, to think well[^105] about what they say, and to discover that "never" is not said where there is no time. He who is said never to have made anything—what else is said but that at no time did he make?

Let them see, then, that no time can be without a creature, and let them stop speaking this vanity[^106]. Let them too *be extended toward the things that are before*[^107], and understand that you, before all times, are the eternal Creator of all times; and that no times are coeternal with you, nor any creature, even if any creature is above times.

### Chapter 31: God knows all things in a wondrous way

**41**. Lord my God, what bosom is that of your deep secret? And how far from there have the consequences of my offenses cast me? Heal my eyes, and let me rejoice together in your light.

Surely, if there is a mind so powerful in knowledge and foreknowledge that all past and future things are known to it as one song most familiar is to me, that mind is too wonderful and to be marveled at with awe, since nothing of what is done and nothing of what remains of the ages would be hidden from it, just as nothing is hidden from me when I sing that song—what and how much of it has gone from the beginning, what and how much remains to the end.

But far be it that you, the founder of the universe, the founder of souls and bodies, far be it that you should so know all future and past things. Far, far more wondrously, and far more secretly.

For not as the feeling of one singing a song he knows, or hearing a song he knows, is varied by expectation of voices to come and memory of those past, and his sense is distended—so does anything happen to you, unchangeably eternal, that is, the truly eternal Creator of minds.

As, then, you knew *in the beginning heaven and earth*[^108] without any variety of your knowledge, so you made *in the beginning heaven and earth* without any distention of your action.

Let him who understands confess to you, and let him who does not understand confess to you. O how high you are[^109], and yet *the humble of heart*[^110] are your house! For you *lift up those who have been cast down*[^111], and they do not fall, whose loftiness you are.
