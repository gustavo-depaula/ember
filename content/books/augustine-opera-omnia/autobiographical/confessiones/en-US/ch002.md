# Book 1: Birth, Infancy, and Boyhood

## The Invocation of God

### Chapter 1: How shall we call upon God?

**1**. *Great are you, O Lord, and greatly to be praised; great is your power, and your wisdom is beyond all measure*[^1]. And man longs to praise you—man, who is but a small part of your creation; man, who carries his mortality about with him, carrying the evidence of his sin[^2] and the evidence that *you resist the proud*[^3]; and still he longs to praise you, this small part of your creation. You stir us to take delight in praising you, for you have made us for yourself, and our heart is restless until it rests in you.

Grant me, Lord, to know and understand[^4] whether to call upon you comes first or to praise you, and whether knowing you comes before calling upon you. But who can call upon you without knowing you? For someone who does not know you might call upon something else in your place.

Or should you rather be called upon so that you may be known? But *how are they to call upon him in whom they have not believed? And how are they to believe without a preacher?*[^5] And *those who seek the Lord shall praise him*[^6], for those who seek him find him[^7] and, finding him, will praise him.

I will seek you, Lord, by calling upon you, and call upon you by believing in you, for you have been preached to us. My faith calls upon you, Lord—the faith you gave me, which you breathed into me through the humanity of your Son, through the ministry of your preacher.

### Chapter 2: Why do we call upon God?

**2**. And how shall I call upon my God, my God and my Lord, since in calling upon him I am surely calling him into myself? What place is there in me for my God to come to—the God who *made heaven and earth*[^8]? Is there really anything in me, O Lord my God, that can contain you? Or can heaven and earth, which you made and in which you made me, contain you? Or is it that, since nothing that exists could exist without you, whatever exists therefore contains you?

Since I too exist, why do I ask that you come into me—I who would not exist unless you were in me? For I am not in hell now, and yet you are even there; for *even if I go down into hell, you are present*[^9]. So I would not exist, my God—I would not exist at all—unless you were in me.

Or rather, would I not fail to exist unless I existed in you, *from whom are all things, through whom are all things, in whom are all things*[^10]? Yes, Lord, even so, even so. To what place do I call you, since I am already in you? And from where would you come into me? For where could I go, beyond heaven and earth, that my God might come to me from there—the God who said, *I fill heaven and earth*[^11]?

### Chapter 3: God fills all things

**3**. Do heaven and earth, then, contain you, since you fill them? Or do you fill them and still have more to spare, since they cannot contain you?

And where do you pour back whatever is left over of you once heaven and earth are filled? Or have you no need to be contained by anything—you who contain all things—since what you fill, you fill by containing it?

For the vessels that are full of you do not give you stability, since even if they break you are not spilled out. And when you are poured out upon us[^12], you do not lie fallen but lift us up; you are not scattered but gather us together. But all that you fill, you fill with the whole of yourself.

Or is it that, since all things cannot contain the whole of you, they contain a part of you—and all of them contain the same part at once?

Or do individual things contain individual parts, the greater things more and the smaller things less? Then is one part of you greater and another smaller? Or are you wholly present everywhere, while no single thing contains the whole of you?

### Chapter 4: What is God?

**4**. What, then, are you, my God? What, I ask, but the Lord God? For *who is Lord but the Lord? Or who is God but our God?*[^13] Most high, most good, most mighty, most omnipotent; most merciful and most just; most hidden and most present; most beautiful and most strong; steadfast yet beyond our grasp; unchanging, yet changing all things; never new, never old, yet making all things new[^14] and *bringing the proud to decay without their knowing it*[^15]; ever acting, ever at rest; gathering in, yet wanting nothing; bearing and filling and protecting; creating and nourishing and bringing to perfection; seeking, though nothing is lacking to you. You love without being inflamed; you are jealous[^16] yet free of care; you repent[^17] without grieving; you are angry[^18] yet remain serene; you change your works without changing your plan; you take back what you find, though you never lost it; never in need, you rejoice in your gains; never grasping, yet you demand interest[^19].

More than the debt is paid to you[^20], so as to put you in debt—yet who has anything that is not yours? You repay debts, though you owe nothing to anyone; you remit debts, and lose nothing.

And what have we said, my God, my life, my holy sweetness? What does anyone say when he speaks of you? Yet woe to those who keep silent about you, for even the eloquent are left mute[^21].

### Chapter 5: The soul longs for God and calls upon him

**5**. Who will grant me to rest in you? Who will grant that you come into my heart and make it drunk, so that I forget my evils[^22] and embrace you, my one and only good? What are you to me? Have mercy, that I may speak. What am I to you, that you command me to love you, and are angry with me if I do not, and threaten me with enormous miseries[^23]? Is it, then, a small misery in itself not to love you? Alas for me! Tell me, in your mercies, O Lord my God, what you are to me. *Say to my soul, I am your salvation*[^24]. Say it so that I may hear. See, the ears of my heart are before you, Lord; open them, and *say to my soul, I am your salvation.* I will run after that voice and lay hold of you. Do not hide your face from me[^25]; let me die, lest I die, that I may see it.

**6**. The house of my soul is too narrow for you to come into it: let it be enlarged by you. It is in ruins: rebuild it. It holds things that would offend your eyes: I confess it, I know it. But who will cleanse it? To whom other than you shall I cry: *cleanse me from my hidden faults, O Lord, and spare your servant from the faults of others*[^26]? *I believe, and so I speak*[^27].

*Lord, you know*[^28]. Have I not declared to you *my sins against myself*, my God, *and have you not forgiven the wickedness of my heart*[^29]? I do not *go to law with you*[^30], you who are the truth[^31]; and I have no wish to deceive myself, lest my iniquity lie to itself[^32]. So I do not go to law with you, for *if you, Lord, should mark iniquities, Lord, who could stand?*[^33]

## Birth and Infancy

### Chapter 6: Man does not know where he comes from

**7**. Yet allow me to speak before your mercy—me, who am but *dust and ashes*[^34]. Allow me to speak, since it is your mercy I address, and not a man who would mock me. Perhaps you too laugh at me[^35], but you will turn and have mercy on me[^36]. For what is it I want to say, Lord, except that I do not know where I came from when I came into this—shall I call it a mortal life or a living death? I do not know.

And *the consolations of your mercies*[^37] took me up, as I have heard from the parents of my flesh, the one from whom and the one in whom you formed me in time; for I myself do not remember it. So the consolations of human milk received me, though neither my mother nor my nurses filled their own breasts; rather, through them you gave me an infant's nourishment, according to your ordinance and the riches you have laid down to the very depth of things.

You also gave me to want no more than you were giving, and gave those who nursed me the will to give me what you were giving them; for by a well-ordered affection they wanted to give me what they had in abundance from you. For the good I had from them was a good for them too—though it came not from them but through them, since indeed all good things are from you, O God, and from my God comes all my well-being[^38].

This I came to notice only later, as you cried out to me through these very gifts that you bestow within and without. For at that time I knew how to suck, to take contentment in what pleased me, and to cry at anything that hurt my flesh—nothing more.

#### What infants are like

**8**. Later I began to smile as well, first in my sleep, and then awake. This was told me about myself, and I believed it, since we see the same in other infants; for I do not remember it of my own self.

And bit by bit I began to perceive where I was, and I wanted to make my wishes known to those who could fulfill them, but I could not, for my wishes were within me while they were outside, and by no sense of theirs could they enter my soul. So I threw my limbs about and made noises, signs resembling my wishes—the few I could manage, such as I could manage—though they were not truly like them.

And when I was not obeyed—either because I was not understood or because it would have harmed me—I grew indignant that my elders would not submit to me, that free persons would not be my servants, and I avenged myself on them by crying.

That infants are like this I have learned from those I have been able to observe; and that I myself was such, my nurses—who did not know it—have shown me more surely than those who did.

#### God alone lives forever

**9**. And see, my infancy is long since dead, yet I am alive. But you, Lord, who live forever and in whom nothing dies—since before the first beginnings of the ages, and before all that can be called "before," you are, and you are God and Lord of all that you have created; with you stand the causes of all unstable things, with you abide the unchanging origins of all changeable things, and the everlasting accounts of all irrational and temporal things live on—tell me, your suppliant, O God; in your mercy tell me, your wretched one: did my infancy succeed some earlier age of mine that had already died? Was it the age I passed within my mother's womb? For of that too something has been told me, and I have myself seen women with child. And what was there before that, my sweetness, my God? Was I anywhere, was I anyone? I have no one to tell me this: neither my father nor my mother could, nor the experience of others, nor my own memory. Do you laugh at me for asking such things, and bid me praise you and confess to you for what I do know?

#### The first beginnings of life

**10**. *I confess to you, O Lord of heaven and earth*[^39], giving you praise for my origins and my infancy, which I do not remember. You have granted that a man infer these things about himself from others, and believe much about himself even on the authority of mere women. For I existed and was alive even then, and already, at the close of infancy, I was seeking signs by which to make my feelings known to others.

Where does such a living creature come from, if not from you, Lord? Is anyone the maker of his own self? Or is there some channel drawn from elsewhere through which being and life flow into us, other than your making us[^40], Lord—you for whom to be and to live are not two different things, since to be supremely and to live supremely are one and the same?

For you are supreme, and you do not change[^41]; and today does not run its course in you, and yet it does run its course in you, since all these things too are in you[^42]—for they would have no ways of passing by[^43] unless you held them. And since your years do not fail, your years are this present day; and however many of our days and our fathers' days have already passed through your today, from it they took their measures and somehow came to be, and others still will pass and take their measures and somehow come to be. *But you are the same*[^44], and all of tomorrow and beyond, and all of yesterday and before, you will make today, you have made today.

What is it to me if someone does not understand? Let him too rejoice and say, *What is this?*[^45] Let him rejoice even so, and choose to find you by not finding rather than, by finding, to fail to find you.

### Chapter 7: Not even an infant is free from sin

**11**. Hear me, O God. Woe to the sins of men![^46] A man says this, and you have mercy on him, since you made him but did not make the sin in him. Who reminds me of the sin of my infancy—for *no one is clean* of sin before you, not even an infant whose *life on earth is but a single day*[^47]? Who will remind me? Some tiny child now, perhaps, in whom I see what I do not remember of myself?

What sin, then, did I commit in those days? Was it that I bawled, gaping after the breast? For if I were now to gape in that way—not after the breast, of course, but after food suited to my years—I would be laughed at and quite rightly rebuked. So even then I was doing things that deserved rebuke; but since I could not understand anyone who rebuked me, neither custom nor reason allowed that I be rebuked. For as we grow up we root out and cast away such behavior.

And I have never seen anyone who, in clearing something away, knowingly throws out what is good. Or were these things good for that age—to demand by crying even what would do harm if given; to be fiercely indignant at free persons who would not submit, at one's elders, at the very ones who gave one life, and at many wiser besides who would not obey one's beck and call; and to try one's hardest to strike and hurt them, because one's commands were not obeyed—commands that it would have been ruinous to obey?

So it is the weakness of an infant's limbs that is innocent, not the infant's mind. I myself have seen and known a jealous little one: he could not yet speak, and he glared, pale and bitter-faced, at the child who shared his nurse. Who does not know this? Mothers and nurses say they expiate it by some remedy or other.

Unless, indeed, this too counts as innocence: that where the fountain of milk flows richly and to spare, one will not endure a sharer who is utterly destitute and dependent for life on that one food. Yet these things are indulgently tolerated—not because they are nothing or are slight, but because they will pass away with the years. You may grant as much from the fact that the very same behavior cannot be borne with composure when it is found in someone older.

**12**. You, therefore, O Lord my God, who gave the infant life and a body—which, as we see, you have furnished with senses, knit together with limbs, graced with a shape, and into which, for its wholeness and well-being, you have implanted all the impulses of a living creature—

you bid me praise you for these things, *to confess to you and sing praise to your name, O Most High*[^48], because you are God almighty and good, even had you made only these things—which no one else can make but you, the One, from whom comes all measure, most beautiful, who give form to all things and by your law set all things in order.

This age, then, Lord—which I do not remember living, about which I have believed others, and which I infer I passed through from watching other infants, however reliable that inference may be—I am reluctant to count as part of this life of mine that I live in this world. For so far as the darkness of my forgetfulness goes, it is on a level with the time I lived in my mother's womb. And if *I was conceived in iniquity, and in sins my mother nourished me in her womb*[^49], then where, I beg you, my God, where, Lord, was *I, your servant*[^50]—where or when was I innocent? But see, I pass over that time; for what have I now to do with it, when I can recall no trace of it?

## Augustine learns to speak

### Chapter 8: Boyhood

**13**. Did I not, advancing from infancy, arrive here at boyhood? Or rather, did boyhood not come into me and succeed my infancy? Yet my infancy did not depart—for where did it go? And still it was no longer there. For I was no longer an infant unable to speak, but already a boy who could talk. This I remember; and only later did I notice how I had learned to speak.

For it was not my elders who taught me, presenting me with words in some fixed order of instruction, as they did a little later with letters; rather, I myself, with the mind you gave me, my God, wanted by groans and various sounds and various movements of my limbs to express the feelings of my heart, so that my will might be obeyed—and yet I could neither express all that I wanted nor make it known to all I wished. I would seize on things with my memory: when people named some object, and at that sound moved their body toward it, I saw and grasped that the thing was called by them by the sound they made when they wanted to point it out. That this was what they meant was made plain by their bodily movements—the natural language, as it were, of all peoples, made with the face and the glance of the eyes, the action of the other limbs, and the tone of voice, which signals the mind's feelings in seeking, keeping, rejecting, or avoiding things.

And so, hearing words again and again in their places in various sentences, I gradually gathered what things they were signs of; and once my mouth had been trained to those signs, I would declare my wishes through them. Thus with those among whom I lived I shared the signs by which we express our wishes, and I entered more deeply into the stormy society of human life, depending on the authority of my parents and the beck and call of my elders.

## He is beaten at school and mocked by his elders

### Chapter 9

**14**. O God, my God, what miseries and mockeries I experienced there, when as a boy it was set before me as right living to obey those who warned me to flourish in this world and to excel in the arts of the tongue, which serve the honor of men and false riches. Then I was sent to school to learn letters, in which—wretch that I was—I had no idea what use there could be. And yet, if I was slow at learning, I was beaten. For this was praised by our elders, and many who had lived this life before us had laid out the toilsome paths we were forced to travel, with labor and sorrow multiplied for the sons of Adam[^51].

But we found people who prayed to you, Lord, and we learned from them, perceiving you, as best we could, to be someone great, who could—though not visible to our senses—hear us and come to our aid. For as a boy I began to pray to you, *my help and my refuge*[^52], and in calling upon you I broke the knots of my tongue; and small as I was, with no small feeling I begged you that I might not be beaten at school. And when you did not hear me—which *was not counted to me as folly*[^53]—my beatings were laughed at by my elders, and even by my parents, who wished no harm to befall me; and they were then my great and grievous evil.

**15**. Is there anyone, Lord, of so great a spirit, cleaving to you with so vast a love—is there, I ask, anyone (for a certain dull-wittedness does as much)—is there, then, one who, by cleaving devoutly to you, is so grandly affected that he reckons the rack and the iron claws and the various torments of that kind (to escape which people throughout all lands entreat you with great fear) as of so little account, loving those who dread them most bitterly, in just the way our parents laughed at the torments we boys suffered from our masters? For we feared them no less, nor did we beg you any less to escape them;

and yet we sinned, by writing or reading or thinking about our lessons less than was required of us. For I did not lack, Lord, memory or talent—which you willed us to have in sufficient measure for that age—but I delighted in play, and I was punished for it by those who were of course doing the very same themselves.

But the trifles of grown men are called business, while children's trifles, though of the same sort, are punished by grown men, and no one pities the children, or the grown men, or both alike. Unless, indeed, some good judge of things would approve of my being beaten because as a boy I played ball, and by that game was hindered from learning quickly the letters with which, as a grown man, I would play more shamefully still. Or was the very man who beat me doing anything different? When he was bested by a fellow teacher in some petty dispute, he was wracked with more bile and envy than I was when beaten by a playmate in a game of ball.

### Chapter 10: Through love of play he was disobedient

**16**. And yet I was sinning, O Lord God, you who order and create all natural things, but who only order—and do not create—sins; O Lord my God, I was sinning by acting against the precepts of my parents and those teachers. For I could afterward make good use of the letters they wanted me to learn, whatever their own purpose was. For I was disobedient not because I was choosing something better, but out of love of play, loving the proud victories of contests and the tickling of my ears with false tales, so that they itched all the more hotly, while that same curiosity flashed more and more from my eyes toward the shows and games of grown men—

and those who put on such shows are endowed with a standing so high that nearly everyone wishes the same for their own little ones—whom, nevertheless, they willingly allow to be beaten if those very shows distract them from the studies by which the parents desire them to attain to putting on such shows themselves.

*Look* upon these things, *Lord*[^54], in mercy, *and deliver us*[^55] who now call upon you; deliver also those who do not yet call upon you, that they may call upon you and you may deliver them.

## Gravely ill, he begged for baptism

### Chapter 11

**17**. For even as a boy I had heard of the eternal life promised to us through the humility of our Lord God, who came down to our pride; and I was already signed with the sign of his cross and seasoned with his salt from the time I came forth from my mother's womb—she who greatly hoped in you. You saw, Lord, when I was still a boy and one day was suddenly burning with a stomach complaint and near to death—you saw, my God, for already you were my guardian[^56], with what stirring of soul and with what faith I begged for the baptism of your Christ, my God and my Lord, from the devotion of my mother and of the mother of us all, your Church. And the mother of my flesh was thrown into distress—since with a chaste heart, in your faith, she was the more lovingly in labor for my eternal salvation—and in haste she would have seen to it that I was initiated into the saving sacraments and washed clean, confessing you, Lord Jesus, for the forgiveness of sins, had I not at once recovered.

And so my cleansing was put off, as though it were necessary that I be defiled still more[^57] if I should live, on the grounds that after that washing the guilt of sins committed would be greater and more dangerous. So already I believed, and so did she and the whole household, except my father alone—who nevertheless did not overcome in me the claim of my mother's devotion, so as to keep me from believing in Christ, as he himself did not yet believe.

For she was at pains that you, my God, should be my father rather than he; and in this you helped her to prevail over her husband, whom, though the better of the two, she served, because in this too she was of course serving you, who command it.

#### With what purpose his baptism was deferred

**18**. I ask you, my God—I would like to know, if you should so will, with what purpose my baptism was deferred, so that I was not baptized then: was it for my good that the reins of sinning were, as it were, loosened for me? Or were they not loosened? Why then, even now, does this saying about one person and another sound on every side in our ears: "Leave him alone, let him do as he likes; he is not yet baptized"? And yet, where the health of the body is concerned, we do not say, "Let him be wounded still more; he is not yet healed."

How much better, then, had I been healed quickly, and had the diligence of my own people and of myself been directed to this: that the salvation of my soul, once received[^58], should be kept safe under your protection, since you would have given it. Better, indeed.

But how many and how great were the waves of temptation that seemed to be looming after boyhood—my mother already knew them, and she chose to entrust to them the earth from which I would later be shaped, rather than the finished likeness itself.

### Chapter 12: He did not love the studies he was driven to

**19**. Yet in that very boyhood, about which less was feared for me than about my youth, I did not love letters, and I hated being driven to them; and I was driven to them all the same, and it was done well for me, though I did not do well; for I would not have learned unless compelled. But no one does well against his will, even if what he does is good. Nor did those who drove me do well; rather, it was done well for me by you, my God. For they had no regard for the use to which I would put what they compelled me to learn—beyond the satisfying of the insatiable desires of a wealthy poverty and a disgraceful glory.

But you, for whom *the very hairs of our head are numbered*[^59], used the error of all those who pressed me to learn for my benefit; and my own error, in being unwilling to learn, you used for my punishment—a punishment I did not deserve to escape, tiny boy and great sinner that I was. So out of those who did not do well you did well for me, and out of my own sinning self you justly repaid me[^60]. For you have ordained—and so it is—that every disordered soul is its own punishment.

## What he thinks of Greek and Latin letters

### Chapter 13

**20**. But what was the reason I hated Greek letters, in which I was steeped as a small boy? Even now it is not quite clear to me. For I had come to love Latin—not what the first teachers teach, but what those called grammarians teach. Those first lessons, where one learns to read and write and count, I found no less burdensome and punishing than all the Greek. Yet where did this too come from, if not from sin and the vanity of life, by which *I was flesh, and a wind that goes and does not return*[^61]?

For surely those first lessons were the better, because the more reliable—by which there came to be in me, and now is, and I possess, the power both to read whatever I find written and to write myself whatever I wish—than the ones in which I was made to hold in memory the wanderings of some Aeneas, forgetful of my own wanderings, and to weep over Dido dead, who killed herself for love, while all the while I bore with dry eyes, most wretchedly, my own dying away from you amid these things, O God, my life[^62].

**21**. For what is more wretched than a wretch who does not pity himself, who weeps over the death of Dido, which came about through loving Aeneas, but does not weep over his own death, which came about through not loving you, O God, light of my heart, and bread of the inward mouth of my soul[^63], and the power that weds my mind and the bosom of my thought?

I did not love you, and I committed fornication away from you[^64], and to me as I fornicated there sounded on every side, *Well done, well done!*[^65] For the friendship of this world is fornication *away from you*, and "Well done, well done!" is said so that a man may be ashamed if he is not like that.

And over this I did not weep, yet I wept over Dido, *who died and followed her end by the sword*[^66]—I myself following the lowest of your creatures, having forsaken you, earth going into earth. And if I were forbidden to read these things, I would have grieved that I could not read what made me grieve. Such madness is reckoned more honorable and richer learning than the lessons by which I learned to read and write.

#### He loved the fictions of poetry too much, and disdained the useful arts

**22**. But now let my God cry out in my soul, and let your truth tell me: It is not so, it is not so; that earlier teaching is by far the better. For see, I am readier to forget the wanderings of Aeneas and all things of that sort than how to read and write. True, curtains hang at the doorways of the grammar schools—but these signify less the honor of something hidden than the covering over of error. Let those I no longer fear not cry out against me, while I confess to you what my soul desires, my God, and find rest in the rebuke of my evil ways[^67], that I may love your good ways. Let neither the sellers nor the buyers of grammar cry out against me; for if I put the question to them, whether it is true, as the poet says, that Aeneas once came to Carthage, the less learned will answer that they do not know, and the more learned will even deny that it is true. But if I ask with what letters the name of Aeneas is spelled, all who have learned this will give me the true answer, according to that agreement and convention by which men have settled these signs among themselves. Again, if I ask which of these one could forget with the greater inconvenience to this life—how to read and write, or those poetic fictions—who does not see what anyone would answer who has not utterly forgotten himself?

So I sinned as a boy when I set those empty things, out of love, above these more useful ones—or rather, I hated the one and loved the other. By then "one and one are two, two and two are four" was a hateful chant to me, while the sweetest spectacle of vanity was the wooden horse full of armed men, the burning of Troy, *and the very ghost of Creusa*[^68].

### Chapter 14: Because of the difficulty of the foreign tongue, the sweetest Greek poets became bitter to him

**23**. Why, then, did I hate Greek literature too, which sings of such things? For Homer also was skilled at weaving such tales, and is most sweetly vain. Yet to me as a boy he was bitter. I believe Virgil is the same to Greek boys, when they are made to learn him as I was made to learn Homer. The difficulty, plainly—the sheer difficulty of mastering a foreign tongue—sprinkled as if with gall all the sweetness of the Greek storytelling. For I knew none of those words, and I was urgently pressed, with savage terrors and punishments, to learn them.

For there was a time, as an infant, when I knew no Latin either; and yet I learned it simply by paying attention, without any fear or torment, amid the caresses of my nurses, the jokes of those who smiled at me, and the delight of those who played with me. I learned it without the punishing pressure of taskmasters, since my own heart pressed me to bring its conceptions to birth—which it could not have done unless I had learned some words, not from teachers but from people speaking, into whose ears I too gave birth to whatever I felt.

From this it shines out clearly enough that free curiosity has greater power for learning these things than does fearful compulsion. Yet the free flow of the one is checked by the other, under your laws, O God—your laws, from the schoolmasters' canes to the trials of the martyrs—your laws that have power to mix in wholesome bitterness, calling us back to you from the pestilent pleasure by which we drew away from you.

### Chapter 15: May all things serve God

**24**. *Hear, O Lord, my supplication*[^69]; let my soul not faint[^70] under your discipline, and let me not faint in confessing to you your acts of mercy[^71], by which you drew me out of all my most evil ways[^72]—that you may grow sweet to me beyond all the seductions I once followed; that I may love you with all my strength and clasp your hand with all my heart; and that you may rescue me from every temptation *to the very end*[^73].

For see, you, Lord, *my king and my God*[^74]—let whatever useful thing I learned as a boy serve you; let my speaking and writing and reading and counting serve you. For while I was learning empty things, you were giving me discipline, and in those empty things you forgave me the sins of my delights[^75]. For I did learn many useful words in them—but these can be learned in things that are not empty, and that is the safe path on which children should walk.

### Chapter 16: Base poets badly form the young

**25**. But woe to you, river of human custom! *Who will resist you?*[^76] How long until you run dry? How long will you roll the children of Eve into that great and fearsome sea which even those who have boarded the wood can scarcely cross? Did I not read in you of Jupiter both thundering and committing adultery? Of course he could not do both at once; but it was contrived so that real adultery might have the authority to be imitated, with the false thunder playing the procurer.

But which of those cloaked masters listens with a sober ear to a man crying out from the same dust and saying, *Homer invented these things and transferred human matters to the gods; I would rather he had brought divine matters down to us*[^77]?

But it is said more truly that he did indeed invent these things, yet by attributing what is divine to depraved men, so that wicked deeds might not be thought wicked, and so that whoever did them might seem to be imitating not lost men but the gods of heaven.

**26**. And yet, O river of hell, into you the sons of men are flung, with fees paid, so that they may learn these things; and a great affair is made of it when this is done publicly in the forum, in the sight of laws that decree salaries over and above the fees. And you beat against your rocks and roar, saying, "Here words are learned; here is acquired the eloquence most necessary for persuading and for setting forth one's views." As though, indeed, we would not otherwise come to know these words—"shower" and "golden" and "lap" and "deceit" and "the temples of heaven" and the other words written in that passage—had not Terence brought on a worthless youth setting up Jupiter as a model for his own debauchery, as he gazes at a certain picture painted on a wall, where *there was this picture: how, they say, Jupiter once sent down into Danaë's lap a golden shower, a trick played upon the woman*[^78]. And see how he rouses himself to lust, as though under heavenly tutelage:

*And what a god!* he says. *One who shakes the temples of heaven with his mighty thunder. And I, a mere mortal, should not do the same? Indeed I did it, and gladly*[^79].

It is not at all the case that these words are learned more conveniently through such filth; rather, through these words that filth is committed more boldly. I do not accuse the words, which are like chosen and precious vessels[^80], but the wine of error that was poured out for us in them by drunken teachers; and unless we drank it we were beaten, with no leave to appeal to any sober judge. And yet I, my God, in whose sight my remembering is now untroubled, learned these things gladly, and—wretch that I was—took delight in them, and for this I was called a boy of good promise.

### Chapter 17: Augustine's outstanding talent was worn away on the ravings of the poets

**27**. Allow me, my God, to say something about my own talent, your gift, and on what ravings it was worn away. There was set before me a task quite disturbing to my soul, with the reward of praise and the fear of disgrace or of a beating: to speak the words of Juno, angry and grieving that she could not *turn the king of the Teucrians away from Italy*[^81]—words I had never heard that Juno said. But we were forced to follow, in our wandering, the tracks of the poets' fictions, and to say in prose something like what the poet had said in verse. And the one won more praise in whom, fittingly to the dignity of the character portrayed, a feeling of anger and grief stood out more lifelike, in words that suitably clothed the thoughts.

What did it profit me, O true life, my God[^82], that my recitation was applauded above many of my own age and fellow students? Is not all that smoke and wind? Was there really nothing else on which my talent and tongue might be exercised? Your praises, Lord, your praises through your Scriptures would have held up the tender shoot of my heart, and it would not have been swept away through the emptiness of trifles, a shameful prey to the birds of the air. For there is more than one way of sacrificing to the rebel angels.

### Chapter 18: While men observe the rules of grammar, they neglect the law of God

**28**. But what wonder that I was carried off into vanities in this way, and went outside, away from you, my God, when the men set before me for imitation were such that, if they reported some deed of theirs—not a bad one—with a barbarism or a solecism, they were rebuked and put to shame, but if they recounted their lusts in correct and properly ordered words, *richly and ornately*[^83], they were praised and gloried in it?

You see these things, Lord, and you keep silent, *long-suffering and full of mercy and truth*[^84]. Will you keep silent forever?[^85] Even now you draw up out of this monstrous deep the soul that seeks you and thirsts for your delights[^86], the soul whose heart says to you, *I have sought your face; your face, Lord, will I seek*[^87]—for I was far from your face in a darkened love.

For it is not on foot, or through distances of place, that one goes from you or returns to you. That younger son of yours[^88] did not look for horses or chariots or ships, did not fly off on visible wings or make his journey by the bending of a knee, in order to live in a far country and prodigally squander what you had given him as he set out—you, the kind father, kinder still to him when he came back in need. So it is in lustful love, that is, in darkened love; and that is what it is to be far from your face.

**29**. See, O Lord God—and patiently, as you do, see—how diligently the sons of men observe the conventions of letters and syllables received from earlier speakers, while they neglect the eternal covenants of everlasting salvation received from you; so that a man who holds or teaches those old conventions of sound, if contrary to grammatical rule he drops the aspirate and says *ominem* for *hominem* ("man"), gives more offense to men than if, contrary to your precepts, he hates a man, though he himself is a man.

As if any human enemy could be more harmful to him than the hatred itself by which he is provoked against the man; or as if anyone, by persecuting another, lays him waste more grievously than he lays waste his own heart by his enmity. And surely the knowledge of letters lies no deeper within than the conscience written there[^89]—that he is doing to another what he would not wish to suffer himself[^90].

How hidden you are, dwelling on high[^91] in silence, O God, alone great, by your tireless law scattering punishing blindnesses over unlawful desires—when a man chasing the fame of eloquence, standing before a human judge with a crowd of men around him, pursuing his enemy with the most monstrous hatred, takes the most vigilant care not to say, by a slip of the tongue, *inter omines* for *inter homines* ("among men"), yet takes no care lest, by the fury of his mind, he take a man away from among men.

## The sins of Augustine as a boy

### Chapter 19

**30**. As a boy I lay wretched on the threshold of such ways, and this was the wrestling-ground of that arena, where I feared more to commit a barbarism than I took care, if I did commit one, not to envy those who did not. I say this and confess it to you, my God—the things for which I was praised by those whom to please was then, in my eyes, to live honorably.

For I did not see the whirlpool of filth into which I had been *cast away from before your eyes*[^92]. For by then what was more foul than I, when I displeased even people like those, deceiving with countless lies my attendant, my teachers, and my parents—out of love of play, a craving to watch worthless shows, and a restless urge to imitate them?

I would even commit thefts from my parents' larder and table, either at the bidding of gluttony or so that I might have something to give the other boys, who would sell me their play—in which they too of course took equal delight—in exchange. In that play, too, I myself, overcome by an empty craving for preeminence, would often hunt for fraudulent victories.

Yet what did I so refuse to suffer, and ferociously denounce if I caught someone at it, as the very thing I was doing to others? And if I was caught and denounced, I preferred to rage rather than to give way. Is this the innocence of childhood? It is not, Lord, it is not—I beg you, my God.

For these are the very things that, as the greater ages succeed one another, pass over from tutors and teachers, from nuts and little balls and pet sparrows, to prefects and kings, to gold and estates and slaves—exactly these same things—just as greater punishments succeed the cane. It was, then, the symbol of humility in a child's small stature that you approved, our king, when you said, *to such belongs the kingdom of heaven*[^93].

## He gives thanks to God for his gifts

### Chapter 20

**31**. And yet, Lord, to you, the most excellent and best founder and ruler of the universe, *thanks be to our God*[^94], even had you willed me to be no more than a boy. For I existed even then, I was alive and had sensation, and I took care for my own well-being—a trace of that most hidden unity from which I came; by an inward sense I guarded the wholeness of my senses, and in my small thoughts about small things I took delight in truth. I did not want to be deceived, my memory was vigorous, I was being furnished with speech, I was soothed by friendship, I shunned pain, abjection, and ignorance.

> In such a living creature,  
> what is not wonderful and praiseworthy?  
> Yet all these things are gifts of my God.  
> I did not give them to myself:  
> they are good,  
> and all of them are I.  
> Good, then, is he who made me,  
> and he himself is my good,  
> and to him I exult[^95] for all the good things  
> by which, even as a boy, I was.  
> For this was my sin,  
> that not in him,  
> but in his creatures—myself and  
> the rest—I sought pleasures, heights,  
> and truths,  
> and so I fell headlong  
> into sorrows, confusions, and errors.  
> *Thanks* be to you, my sweetness  
> and my honor and my confidence,  
> my God; *thanks* be to you for your gifts;  
> but do you preserve them for me.  
> For so will you preserve me,  
> and the gifts you have given me will be increased and made perfect,  
> and I myself will be with you,  
> for even my existing  
> is your gift to me.
