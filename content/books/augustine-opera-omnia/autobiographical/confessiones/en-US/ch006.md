# Book 5: Leaving Carthage, He Seeks Rome, and from There Milan

## Preface

### Chapter 1: Let all creation praise God and confess his mercies

**1**. Receive the sacrifice of my confessions[^1] from the hand of my tongue[^2], which you formed and stirred up to confess to your name[^3]; and heal all my bones, and let them say: *Lord, who is like you?*[^4] For one who confesses to you does not teach you what is happening within him, since a closed heart does not shut out your eye, nor does the hardness of men repel your hand; but you melt it when you will, whether in mercy or in punishment, *and there is no one who can hide from your heat*[^5].

But *let my soul praise you*[^6], that it may love you, and let it confess to you your mercies[^7], that it may praise you. Your whole creation does not cease, nor is it silent, in your praises: not every spirit[^8], through a mouth turned toward you[^9]; not the animals and bodily things, through the mouth of those who consider them—

so that our soul may rise up to you from its weariness, leaning on the things you have made, and passing through to you, who made them wonderfully[^10]; and there is refreshment and true strength.

### Chapter 2: God is everywhere, who consoles the restless

**2**. Let the restless and the unjust go, and flee[^11] from you. You see them and distinguish the shadows, and behold, all things are beautiful with them, while they themselves are base. And what harm have they done you? Or in what have they dishonored your rule, just and whole from the heavens down to the last things? For where did they flee, when they fled from your face? Or where do you not find them?

But they fled so as not to see you seeing them, and, blinded, to stumble against you[^12] (since you forsake nothing of the things you have made[^13])—that the unjust might stumble against you and be justly afflicted, withdrawing themselves from your gentleness and stumbling against your uprightness and falling upon your roughness. Plainly they do not know that you are everywhere, whom no place circumscribes, and that you alone are present even to those who make themselves far from you.

> Let them turn, then, and seek you,  
> because you have not forsaken  
> your creature  
> as they have forsaken  
> their Creator[^14].  
> Let them turn,  
> and behold, there you are, in their heart—  
> in the heart of those who confess to you  
> and cast themselves upon you  
> and weep in your bosom  
> after their difficult ways[^15];  
> and you, gentle, wipe away their tears[^16],  
> and they weep the more  
> and rejoice in their weeping,  
> because you, Lord—  
> not some man, flesh and blood[^17],  
> but you, Lord, who made them—  
> remake and console them.

And where was I, when I sought you? You were before me, but I had departed even from myself, and could not find myself—much less you!

## The ignorance of the Manichees; Faustus, the Manichee bishop, a snare of the devil

### Chapter 3

**3**. I will speak out, in the sight of my God, of that twenty-ninth year of my age.

A certain bishop of the Manichees had already come to Carthage, Faustus by name, a great snare of the devil[^18], and many were entangled in it through the allurement of his smooth speech. This I now praised, indeed, yet I distinguished it from the truth of the things I was eager to learn; nor did I look at the kind of dish his speech was, but at what knowledge that Faustus, famous among them, would set before me to eat. For report had gone before him to me, that he was most skilled in all the honorable disciplines, and outstandingly learned in the liberal arts.

And since I had read much of the philosophers and held it committed to memory, I compared some of it with those long fables of the Manichees; and what those men said seemed to me the more probable—those who *had power enough to judge of this passing world, though they did not at all find its Lord*[^19].

For *you are great, Lord, and you look upon the lowly, but the lofty you know from afar*[^20]; nor do you draw near except to the contrite of heart[^21]; nor are you found by the proud, even if by their curious skill they number the stars and the sand and measure out the starry tracts and trace the paths of the heavenly bodies.

## Men have discovered and know many things, but of God's wisdom there is no measure

**4**. For they seek these things by their own mind and the talent you gave them, and they have found out much, and foretold many years in advance the eclipses of those lights, the sun and the moon—on what day, at what hour, to what extent they would occur—and their reckoning did not fail them. And it happened just as they foretold; and they wrote down the rules they had traced out, and these are read today, and from them it is foretold in what year, in what month of the year, on what day of the month, at what hour of the day, and to what portion of its light the moon or the sun will be eclipsed: and so it comes to pass, just as foretold.

And people who do not know these things marvel and are amazed, while those who know them exult and are exalted; and by an impious pride, withdrawing and falling away from your light, they foresee a coming eclipse of the sun so long before, but do not see their own present eclipse. For they do not religiously ask whence they have the talent by which they seek these things.

And finding that you made them, they do not give themselves to you, that you might keep what you have made; nor do they slay, as a sacrifice to you, such as they had made of themselves[^22]; nor do they slaughter their own exaltations like birds, and their curiosities like the fish of the sea, by which they roam the secret paths of the deep, and their wantonness like the beasts of the field[^23]—that you, O God, a devouring fire[^24], might consume their dead cares and remake them immortally.

**5**. But they did not know the way, your Word, through whom you made the things they number, and those who number, and the sense by which they perceive what they number, and the mind from which they number[^25]; and *of your wisdom there is no number*[^26].

But the Only-begotten himself *was made for us wisdom and justice and sanctification*[^27], and was numbered among us, and paid the tribute to Caesar[^28]. They did not know this way, by which they might descend from themselves to him, and through him ascend to him. They did not know this way, and they think themselves lofty with the stars and shining; and behold, they have fallen to the earth, and *their foolish heart was darkened*[^29].

And they say many true things about the creation, but the Truth, the maker of the creation, they do not seek devoutly, and therefore they do not find it; or if they find it, *knowing God, they do not honor him as God or give thanks*, but become vain *in their thoughts, and say that they are wise*[^30], attributing to themselves what is yours; and by this, with the most perverse blindness, they strive to attribute even to you what is theirs—laying their lies, that is, upon you, who are the Truth[^31], and *changing the glory of the incorruptible God into the likeness of the image of corruptible man, and of birds and four-footed beasts and serpents*; and they turn *your truth into a lie*, and worship and serve *the creature rather than the Creator*[^32].

#### Augustine was bidden to believe the ravings of Mani

**6**. Yet I retained many true things said by them out of the creation itself, and the reasoning held for me through numbers and the order of times and the visible attestations of the stars; and I compared it with the sayings of Mani, who wrote much on these matters, raving most copiously; and there occurred to me no account either of the solstices and equinoxes, or of the eclipses of the lights, or of anything of the kind such as I had learned in the books of secular wisdom. But there I was bidden to believe, and it did not square with those accounts verified by numbers and by my own eyes, but was far different.

### Chapter 4: The knowledge of God makes a man blessed

**7**. Surely, *O Lord God of truth*[^33], does whoever knows these things thereby please you? For unhappy is the man who knows all those things but does not know you; while blessed is the one who knows you, even if he does not know them. And whoever knows both you and them is not the more blessed on their account, but is blessed on account of you alone, if, knowing you, he glorifies you as God and gives thanks, and does not become vain in his thoughts[^34].

For just as a man who knows how to possess a tree, and gives you thanks for the use of it—though he does not know how many cubits high it is, or how wide its spread—is better than one who measures it and counts all its branches, but neither possesses it nor knows or loves its Creator; so the faithful man, *to whom the whole world of riches belongs*[^35], and who, *as having nothing, yet possesses all things*[^36] by cleaving to you, whom all things serve—though he does not so much as know the circlings of the Great Bear—it is foolish to doubt that he is surely better than one who measures the heaven and numbers the stars and weighs the elements, but neglects you, who *have ordered all things in measure and number and weight*[^37].

## How arrogant the Manichee was

### Chapter 5

**8**. But still, who asked that some Mani or other should write these things too, without skill in which piety could be learned? For you have said to man: *Behold, piety is wisdom*[^38]. Of this he could be ignorant, even if he knew those things perfectly; but these things, since he did not know them, yet most impudently dared to teach, he could in no way know that. For it is vanity to profess these worldly things even when they are known, but it is piety to confess to you.

Hence that wanderer spoke much on these matters, so that, convicted by those who had truly learned them, it might be plainly known what his understanding was in other, more hidden matters. For he did not wish to be esteemed of small account, but tried to persuade men that the Holy Spirit, the comforter and enricher of your faithful, was personally in him with plenary authority.

And so, when he was caught having said false things about the heaven and the stars and the movements of the sun and moon—though these do not pertain to the teaching of religion—still it would be sufficiently evident that his attempts had been sacrilegious, since he spoke of things not only unknown but even false, with so insane a vanity of pride that he strove to attribute them to himself as to a divine person.

**9**. For when I hear that some Christian brother or other is ignorant of these things, and holds one thing for another, I look patiently on the man as he opines, and I do not see it harm him—provided he believes nothing unworthy of you, Lord, Creator of all[^39]—if perchance he is ignorant of the position and disposition of bodily creation.

But it does harm him if he supposes this pertains to the very form of the teaching of piety, and dares too obstinately to affirm what he does not know. Yet even such weakness, in the cradle of faith, is borne up by love, the mother, until the new man rises up[^40] *into a perfect man*, and can no longer *be carried about by every wind of doctrine*[^41].

But in the case of one who—as teacher, author, leader, and chief of those to whom he urged these things—dared to act so that those who followed him should think they were following not just any man, but your Holy Spirit, who would not judge that such madness, once he was convicted of having said false things, ought to be detested and cast far away?

But still I had not yet clearly found out whether the alternations of longer and shorter days and nights, and of night and day themselves, and the eclipses of the lights, and whatever of the sort I had read in other books, could also be explained according to his words—so that, if perchance it could, it would remain uncertain to me whether the matter stood this way or that, but I would set his authority before my faith on account of his reputed holiness.

## Faustus, when he came to Carthage, disappointed Augustine's expectation and hope

### Chapter 6

**10**. And for nearly those same nine years, during which I listened to them with a wandering mind, I awaited the coming of this Faustus with too eager a longing.

For the rest of them, whom I happened to come upon, who failed at the questions I raised about such matters, kept promising me this man, by whose coming and conferred conversation these things—and any greater ones I might happen to ask—would most easily and most clearly be resolved for me.

So when he came, I found a man pleasant and agreeable in his words, and prattling much more sweetly the very things they are accustomed to say. But what was the most elegant cupbearer of precious cups to my thirst? My ears were already sated with such things, nor did they seem to me the better for being better said, nor the truer for being eloquent, nor the soul wise because the face was fitting and the speech comely. But those who promised him to me were no good judges of things, and so to them he seemed prudent and wise, because he delighted them as he spoke. Yet I sensed that there is another kind of men who hold even truth suspect, and will not rest content with it, if it is set forth in polished and copious speech.

But my God had already taught me by wonderful and hidden ways—and I believe that you taught me, because it is true, and there is no other teacher of the truth but you, wherever and from whatever source it shines forth. By now, then, I had learned from you that nothing should seem true because it is said eloquently, nor false because the signs of the lips sound clumsily; and again, nothing true because it is uttered without polish, nor false because the speech is splendid; but that wisdom and folly are like foods useful and useless, while words ornate and unadorned are like vessels city-made and country-made, and either kind of food can be served in either.

**11**. And so my eagerness, with which I had so long awaited that man, was indeed delighted by the movement and feeling of him as he disputed, and by his fitting words, ready at hand to clothe his thoughts. I was delighted, and along with many—or even more than many—I praised and extolled him; but I was vexed that, in the crowd of his hearers, I was not permitted to press my own questions upon him and share them with him, conferring familiarly and giving and receiving discourse. When I was able to do this, and began, with my friends, to engage his ears at a time when it was not unfitting to discuss things by turns, and I put forward some things that troubled me, I found the man, first of all, unversed in the liberal disciplines except grammar, and that only in the ordinary way. And because he had read some orations of Cicero, and a very few books of Seneca, and some of the poets, and whatever volumes of his own sect were written in Latin and in good order, and because he had daily practice in speaking, he had a supply of eloquence, which was made the more acceptable and seductive by the governance of his talent and a certain natural charm.

Is it not so, as I recall it, O Lord my God, judge of my conscience? Before you are my heart[^42] and my memory—you who were then guiding me by the hidden secret of your providence, and were already setting my dishonorable errors before my face[^43], that I might see them and hate them.

### Chapter 7: The modesty and shame of Faustus were pleasing to Augustine

**12**. For after it became plain enough to me that he was unskilled in the arts in which I had thought him to excel, I began to despair of his being able to open up and resolve for me the things that troubled me—of which, indeed, a man might be ignorant and still hold the truth of piety, but only if he were not a Manichee.

For their books are full of the longest fables about the heaven and the stars and the sun and the moon; and I no longer thought that he could explain to me with subtlety—which was just what I desired—whether, when the reckonings of numbers I had read elsewhere were compared, things were rather as the Manichees' books held, or whether at least an equal account could be given from them.

Yet when I brought these things forward to be considered and discussed, he, quite modestly, did not even dare to take up that burden. For he knew that he did not know these things, and he was not ashamed to confess it. He was not of the sort I had endured in many a talkative man, who tried to teach me these things and said nothing. This man had a *heart*, though *not right toward you*[^44], yet not too incautious toward himself. He was not altogether ignorant of his own ignorance, and would not, by rash disputing, hem himself into a corner from which there would be no way out and no easy return. And for this too he pleased me the more. For the restraint of a confessing mind is more beautiful than the things I desired to know. And such I found him in all the more difficult and subtle questions.

#### Augustine's zeal for the teaching of Mani was broken

**13**. And so, my zeal, which I had bent upon the writings of Mani, being broken, and despairing the more of their other teachers, since in the many things that troubled me that famous man had shown himself such as he did,

I began to spend time with him for the sake of his own zeal, with which he was aflame for the very literature that I, then already a rhetorician, was teaching to the young men of Carthage; and to read with him either what he desired to hear, or what I judged suited to such a talent.

But my whole effort, by which I had resolved to advance in that sect, utterly collapsed once I had come to know the man—not so that I separated from them altogether, but, as though finding nothing better than the thing into which I had somehow plunged, I resolved to be content with it for the time, unless perhaps something more to be chosen should shine forth.

So that Faustus, who to many had been a snare of death[^45], had now begun, neither willing nor knowing it, to loosen the snare in which I had been caught. For your hands, my God, in the hidden depths of your providence did not forsake my soul; and from the blood of my mother's heart, through her tears, sacrifice was offered to you for me day and night, and you dealt with me in wonderful ways[^46]. You did that, my God; for *a man's steps are directed by the Lord, and he will choose his way*[^47]. Or what provision of salvation is there, apart from your hand remaking what you have made?

## At Rome he approves the Academic philosophers, who doubt all things

### Chapter 8

**14**. You dealt with me, then, so that I was persuaded to go to Rome and to teach there, rather than at Carthage, what I was teaching. And how I was persuaded of this, I will not pass over confessing to you, because in these things too your most profound depths and your most present mercy toward us must be pondered and proclaimed. I did not want to go to Rome because greater gains and greater dignity were promised me by the friends who urged it (although these things too were drawing my mind at the time), but the chief and almost the only cause was that I heard that young men studied there more quietly and were kept in order by a more disciplined restraint, so that they would not rush riotously and shamelessly into the school of a master they do not attend, nor be admitted at all unless he permitted it.

At Carthage, on the contrary, the license of the students is foul and intemperate: they break in shamelessly and, with almost frenzied faces, disturb the order that each master has established for his pupils' progress.

They do many injurious things, with astonishing stupidity, deserving punishment by the laws, were not custom their patroness—showing them all the more wretched in that they do, as though it were now lawful, what by your eternal law will never be lawful; and they think they act with impunity, when they are punished by the very blindness of their acting, and suffer incomparably worse things than they do.

So the manners which, as a student, I had refused to make my own, I was forced, as a teacher, to endure in others; and therefore it pleased me to go where all who knew indicated that such things were not done.

But in truth *you, my hope and my portion in the land of the living*[^48], were applying goads at Carthage, that I might be torn from there, for the salvation of my soul[^49], and were setting before me at Rome allurements by which I might be drawn—through men who love a dead life, here doing insane things, there promising vain ones; and to set my steps straight[^50] you secretly used both their perversity and my own. For those who disturbed my quiet were blind with a foul madness, and those who invited me elsewhere savored of the earth[^51]; while I, who here detested true misery, there sought a false felicity.

## He deceitfully left his mother, who was holding him back by force

**15**. But why I was going from here and going there, you knew, O God, and you did not tell me, nor my mother, who wept bitterly at my departure and followed me down to the sea. But I deceived her as she held me back by force, that she might either call me back or come with me; and I pretended that I did not want to leave a friend until, the wind rising, he should sail.

And I lied to my mother—and to such a mother—and I escaped; for this too you mercifully forgave me, keeping me, full of execrable filth, from the waters of the sea, all the way to the water of your grace, by which, once I was washed, the rivers of my mother's eyes would be dried—the eyes with which, for me, she daily watered the ground beneath her face.

And yet, as she refused to return without me, I scarcely persuaded her to stay that night in a place near our ship, where there was a shrine in memory of blessed Cyprian. But that night I secretly set out, while she remained praying and weeping.

And what was she asking of you, my God, with so many tears, but that you would not let me sail? But you, in your deep counsel, hearing the hinge of her desire, did not care for what she then asked, that you might make me what she was always asking.

The wind blew and filled our sails and drew the shore from our sight; and there, in the morning, she was wild with grief, and filled your ears with complaints and groaning—you who despised these things, while you were both snatching me away by my desires, to put an end to those very desires, and beating her carnal desire with the just scourge of sorrows.

For she loved to have me present with her, as mothers do, but far more than many mothers; and she did not know what joys you were going to make for her out of my absence. She did not know, and so she wept and wailed, and by those torments she was shown to bear in herself the remnant of Eve, seeking with groaning what with groaning she had brought forth[^52]. And yet, after the accusation of my deceits and cruelty, she turned again to entreating you for me, and went back to her usual ways, and I to Rome.

## At Rome he fell into a sickness, by which he almost went down to hell

### Chapter 9

**16**. And behold, there I was received by the scourge of bodily sickness, and I was already going down to hell[^53], bearing all the evils I had committed against you, and against myself, and against others—many and grievous, over and above the bond of original sin, by which we all die in Adam[^54]. For you had forgiven me none of them in Christ, nor had he by his cross undone the enmities[^55] that I had contracted with you by my sins. For how could he undo them on the cross of a phantasm, which is what I had believed him to be? As false, then, as the death of his flesh seemed to me, so true was the death of my soul; and as true as the death of his flesh was, so false was the life of my soul, which did not believe it. And as the fevers grew worse, I was already going and perishing. For where would I have gone, if I had then departed hence, but into the fire[^56] and the torments worthy of my deeds, in the truth of your order?

And she did not know this, and yet she was praying for me, though absent. But you, present everywhere, heard her where she was, and had mercy on me where I was, so that I recovered the health of my body, though still mad in my sacrilegious heart. For not even in so great a peril did I desire your baptism; and I was better as a boy, when I begged for it from my mother's devotion, as I have already recalled and confessed[^57].

But I had grown up to my own disgrace, and, mad, I mocked the counsels of your medicine—you who did not let me, such as I was, die twice.

If my mother's heart had been struck with that wound, it would never have been healed. For I do not sufficiently express what feeling she had toward me, and with how much greater anguish she was in labor with me in the spirit than when she had borne me in the flesh[^58].

#### Monica's prayers

**17**. I do not see, then, how she would have been healed, if such a death of mine had pierced through the vitals of her love. And where would there have been those great and frequent prayers, without intermission[^59]? Nowhere but with you. But would you, *God of mercies*[^60], despise *the contrite and humbled heart*[^61] of a chaste and sober widow, generous in almsgiving, dutiful and serving your holy ones, letting no day pass without an offering at your altar, coming twice a day, morning and evening, to your church without any intermission—not for vain fables and old wives' chatter, but that she might hear you in your discourses, and you her in her prayers[^62]?

Could you despise and repel from your help the tears of such a one, with which she begged of you not gold and silver, nor any changeable or fleeting good, but the salvation of her son's soul[^63]—you, by whose gift she was such? By no means, Lord; rather, you were present and heard her, and did things in the order in which you had predestined them to be done. Far be it that you should have deceived her in those visions and answers of yours, which I have already recalled[^64], and others I have not recalled, which she held in her faithful heart, and, ever praying, urged upon you as though they were your own signed bonds[^65]. For you deign—*since your mercy endures forever*[^66]—to become a debtor by your promises even to those to whom you forgive all debts[^67].

### Chapter 10: Even then at Rome Augustine was joined to the Manichees

**18**. You restored me, then, from that sickness, and made whole the son of your handmaid[^68]—for the time, in body—that there might be one to whom you would give a better and surer health.

And even then, at Rome, I was joined to those false and deceiving "holy ones"—not only to their hearers, of whose number was also the man in whose house I had fallen sick and recovered, but even to those whom they call the "elect." For it still seemed to me that it is not we who sin, but some other nature in us that sins; and it delighted my pride to be beyond fault, and, when I had done some evil, not to confess that I had done it, so that you might heal my soul, since it had sinned against you[^69]; but I loved to excuse myself, and to accuse some other thing that was with me and yet was not I.

But in truth I was the whole of it, and my impiety had divided me against myself[^70]; and that sin was the more incurable by which I judged myself not to be a sinner; and it was an execrable iniquity to prefer that you, O God almighty[^71], should be overcome in me to my ruin, rather than that I should be overcome by you to my salvation.

You had not yet set *a guard over my mouth and a door of restraint about my lips, that my heart might not turn aside to evil words, to make excuses for sins with men who work iniquity*; and so I was still keeping company with their *elect*[^72]—yet by now despairing that I could make progress in that false doctrine, and the very things with which I had resolved to be content if I found nothing better, I now held more loosely and carelessly.

## He came at last to think the Academics wiser than the other philosophers

**19**. For there had also arisen in me the thought that those philosophers whom they call Academics had been wiser than the rest, because they had judged that everything is to be doubted, and had decreed that nothing of the truth can be comprehended by man[^73]. For so they clearly seemed to me to have held, as they are commonly taken, even though I did not yet understand their real intention. And I did not conceal my effort to check that host of mine from the excessive confidence I sensed he had in those fabulous matters of which the Manichees' books are full.

Yet I made use of their friendship more familiarly than that of other men who had not been in that heresy. Nor did I defend it any longer with my old animosity; but still their familiarity (for Rome hides many of them) made me the slower to seek something else—especially as I despaired of being able to find the truth in your Church, *O Lord of heaven and earth*[^74], Creator of all things visible and invisible[^75], from which they had turned me away. And it seemed to me very base to believe that you had the figure of human flesh, and were bounded by the bodily outlines of our members. And since, whenever I wished to think of my God, I knew no way to think except of masses of bodies (for nothing seemed to me to exist that was not such), this was the greatest and almost the sole cause of my unavoidable error.

#### He believed evil to be a kind of substance

**20**. For from this I also believed that there was some such substance of evil, having its own mass, foul and deformed—whether gross, which they called earth, or thin and subtle, like the body of air, which they imagine to be a malignant mind creeping through that earth. And because a piety of whatever kind compelled me to believe that a good God had created no evil nature, I set up two masses opposed to each other, each infinite, but the evil one narrower, the good one larger; and from this pestilent beginning the other sacrileges followed me. For when my mind tried to run back to the catholic faith, I was beaten back, because what I supposed to be the catholic faith was not.

And I seemed to myself more devout if I believed you, my God—to whom your mercies confess out of me[^76]—to be infinite on the other sides, though on the one side, where the mass of evil was set against you, I was forced to confess you finite, than if I supposed you bounded on all sides by the form of a human body. And it seemed to me better to believe that you had created no evil (which to me, in my ignorance, seemed not only some substance, but even a bodily one, since I did not know how to think of mind except as a subtle body diffused through spaces of place) than to believe that there came from you such a nature of evil as I supposed.

And our Savior himself, your Only-begotten, I thought of as, so to speak, stretched out from the mass of your most shining substance for our salvation, so that I believed nothing of him but what I could imagine in my vanity. Such a nature of his, then, I thought could not be born of the Virgin Mary without being mingled with the flesh. But how it could be mingled and not defiled, I did not see, given what I pictured to myself. I feared, therefore, to believe him born in the flesh, lest I be forced to believe him defiled by the flesh. Now your spiritual ones will smile at me gently and lovingly, if they read these confessions of mine; but such was I.

### Chapter 11: What the Manichees thought of the holy Scriptures, and what they found fault with in them

**21**. Next, I did not think that the things they had found fault with in your Scriptures could be defended; yet at times I sincerely desired to confer on each point with someone most learned in those books, and to test what he thought about them. For already the discourses of a certain Elpidius, who spoke and disputed face to face against those same Manichees, had begun to move me even at Carthage, when he brought forward from the Scriptures things not easily resisted.

And the answer of those men seemed to me feeble—an answer which, indeed, they did not readily produce in public, but only more secretly to us, when they said that the Scriptures of the New Testament had been falsified by some persons or other, who wished to graft the law of the Jews onto the Christian faith; yet they themselves brought forward no uncorrupted copies.

But it was chiefly those masses, as I thought of bodily things, that captured and, as it were, choked and oppressed me; gasping beneath them, I could not breathe the pure and simple air of your truth.

## The Roman students are not "wreckers," but cheats

### Chapter 12

**22**. So I began diligently to do what I had come for: to teach the art of rhetoric at Rome, and first to gather at home some by whom and through whom I was beginning to be known. And behold, I learn that other things are done at Rome which I had not suffered in Africa. For it was made plain to me that those disruptions by abandoned youths were indeed not done there: "but suddenly," they say, "so as not to pay the master his fee, many youths conspire and transfer themselves to another—deserters of good faith, and to whom justice is cheap for the love of money."

These too my heart hated, though not with *a perfect hatred*[^77]. For perhaps I hated them more for what I was going to suffer from them than for the unlawful things they did to anyone at all.

Yet surely such people are base, and *they commit fornication away from you*[^78] by loving the fleeting mockeries of the times and the muddy gain that defiles the hand when it is grasped, and by embracing the fleeing world and despising you who abide, and call back, and forgive the human soul, that harlot, when she returns to you.

And now I hate such crooked and distorted men, though I love them as needing to be corrected, so that they may prefer to money the very teaching they learn, and to that teaching, you, God, the truth and the abundance of a sure good and the chastest peace.

But then I was more unwilling to endure them as evil for my own sake than I was willing that they should become good for your sake.

## Augustine, sent to Milan, was received as a father by Bishop Ambrose

### Chapter 13

**23**. And so, after a request was sent from Milan to Rome, to the prefect of the city, that a teacher of rhetoric be provided for that city, with public transport even granted,

I myself made application, through those very men drunk with Manichean vanities (to be rid of whom I was going—though neither they nor I knew it), that, a set speech being delivered, the prefect Symmachus, finding me approved, might send me.

And I came to Milan, to Bishop Ambrose, known throughout the world among the best, your devout worshiper, whose eloquent words then strenuously dispensed to your people *the fatness of your wheat*[^79] and the gladness of oil[^80] and the sober intoxication of your wine[^81]. To him I was led by you, unknowing, that through him I might be led, knowing, to you.

That man of God[^82] received me in a fatherly way, and as a bishop should, was well pleased at my coming as a stranger. And I began to love him, not at first indeed as a teacher of the truth—which I utterly despaired of finding in your Church—but simply as a man kind to me. And I listened attentively as he disputed before the people, not with the intention I ought to have had, but as if testing his eloquence, whether it matched its fame, or flowed greater or less than was reported; and I hung upon his words intent, but stood by as one careless and contemptuous of the matter, and was delighted by the sweetness of his speech—

more learned, indeed, yet less cheering and soothing than that of Faustus, as far as the manner of speaking goes. But of the matters themselves there is no comparison; for the one was wandering away through Manichean deceits, while the other was teaching salvation most wholesomely. But *salvation is far from sinners*[^83], such as I then was; and yet I was drawing nearer, little by little and unknowing.

### Chapter 14: Ambrose expounded the Scriptures spiritually

**24**. For although I did not trouble to learn what he was saying, but only to hear how he said it (this empty care had remained with me, despairing that a way to you lay open to man), there came into my mind, together with the words I loved, the matters too that I was neglecting. For I could not separate them. And while I opened my heart to take in how eloquently he spoke, there entered equally how truly he spoke—by degrees, indeed.

For first these things too began to seem to me capable of being defended; and the catholic faith, on whose behalf I had thought nothing could be said against the assailing Manichees, I now judged could be asserted without shame—especially after I had heard one and another, and more than once, of the riddles of the old writings solved, where, when I took them according to the letter, I was slain[^84].

And so, many passages of those books being expounded spiritually, I now reproved that despair of mine—at least the one by which I had believed that the Law and the Prophets[^85] could in no way be withstood against those who detested and mocked them. Yet I did not therefore feel that the catholic way was to be held, because it too could have its learned defenders, who would copiously and not absurdly refute objections; nor that what I held was therefore to be condemned, because the cases of the two sides were made equal. For the catholic faith did not seem to me vanquished, yet did not yet appear victorious either.

#### Leaving the Manichees, Augustine resolved to be a catechumen in the catholic Church

**25**. Then indeed I strenuously bent my mind, to see whether by any means I could, by some certain proofs, convict the Manichees of falsity. If only I could have conceived a spiritual substance, at once all those contrivances would have been undone and cast out of my mind; but I could not. Nevertheless, concerning the very body of this world and all nature that the sense of the flesh could reach, the more I considered and compared, the more I judged that most of the philosophers had held far more probable views. And so, doubting of all things in the manner of the Academics, as they are supposed to do, and wavering among all things, I resolved that the Manichees were indeed to be left—not thinking that, in that very time of my doubting, I ought to remain in that sect, to which I now preferred some of the philosophers; to which philosophers, however, because they were without the saving name of Christ, I altogether refused to entrust the healing of my soul's sickness[^86]. I resolved, therefore, to be a catechumen in the catholic Church, commended to me by my parents, until something certain should shine forth by which I might direct my course.
