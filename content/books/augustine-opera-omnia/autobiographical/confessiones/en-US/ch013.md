# Book 12: A meditation on heaven and earth, which God is said to have made

### Chapter 1: We are needy, but God is for us

**1**. Much my heart bestirs itself, Lord, in this need of my life, struck by the words of your Holy Scripture; and therefore the want of human understanding is for the most part abundant in speech, because inquiry says more than discovery, and asking is longer than receiving, and the hand that knocks works harder than the hand that takes[^1].

We hold the promise: who will corrupt it? *If God is for us, who is against us?*[^2] *Ask, and you shall receive; seek, and you shall find; knock, and it shall be opened to you. For everyone who asks receives, and he who seeks shall find, and to him who knocks it shall be opened*[^3]. These are your promises, and who shall fear to be deceived, when the Truth promises[^4]?

## The "heaven of heaven," as it is called

### Chapter 2

**2**. The lowliness of my tongue confesses to your loftiness[^5], that you made heaven and earth—this heaven that I see, and this earth that I tread, from which is this earth that I carry; you made them. But where is the *heaven of heaven*, Lord, of which we have heard in the voice of the Psalm: *the heaven of heaven is the Lord's; but the earth he gave to the sons of men*[^6]?

Where is the heaven that we do not behold, to which this whole that we behold is earth? For this whole bodily realm did not wholly receive its beautiful form everywhere in the last things, of which our earth is the foundation; but in comparison with that *heaven of heaven*, even our earth's heaven is earth.

And both these great bodies are not absurdly called earth, in comparison with that "heaven," I know not of what kind, which is the Lord's, not the sons of men's.

## By the earth, by darkness and abyss, formless matter is signified

### Chapter 3

**3**. And surely this earth was *invisible and unformed*, and some I-know-not-what depth of the abyss, above which there was no light, because it had no form; for which reason you commanded that it be written, *darkness was over the abyss*[^7]—what else but the absence of light?

For where would light be, if it were, except above—by standing out and lighting up? Where, then, light was not yet, what was the presence of darkness but the absence of light?

Darkness, then, was above, because above light was absent—just as where sound is not, there is silence. And what is it for there to be silence there, but for there to be no sound there?

Have you not, Lord, taught this soul that confesses to you? Have you not, Lord, *taught me*[^8] that, before you formed and distinguished this unformed matter, there was not anything—not color, not shape, not body, not spirit? And yet not altogether nothing: there was a certain formlessness without any form.

### Chapter 4: By familiar words a certain obscure notion is hinted at

**4**. What, then, should it be called, that it might in some way be hinted to those slower of perception, but by some familiar word? But what in all the parts of the world can be found more akin to complete formlessness than earth and abyss? For they are less beautiful, by their lowest rank, than other higher things that are altogether clear and luminous. Why, then, should I not take the formlessness of matter—which you had made without form, to make from it the beautiful world—as so conveniently intimated to people that it be called *earth invisible and unformed*[^9]?

### Chapter 5: The mind wavers in doubt

**5**. So that when thought seeks in it what sense can reach, and says to itself, "It is not an intelligible form like life, like justice, because it is the matter of bodies; nor is it sensible, since in what is invisible and unformed there is nothing to be seen or perceived"—while human thought says these things to itself, let it try either to know by not-knowing, or not-know by knowing.

### Chapter 6: What Augustine formerly thought about matter

**6**. But I, Lord, if I should confess to you in full, with my mouth and my pen[^10], whatever you have taught me about this matter—whose name I formerly heard and did not understand, while those who told me of it did not understand it either—I used to think of it with innumerable and varied forms, and so I was not thinking of it at all;

my mind kept turning over foul and horrible forms in disordered ranks, yet still forms; and I called formless not what lacked form, but what had such a form that, if it appeared, my sense would turn away from it as unusual and incongruous, and the weakness of a man would be disturbed;

but what I was really thinking was formless not by the privation of every form, but by comparison with more beautiful forms, and true reason was persuading me to take away absolutely all remains of any form whatsoever, if I wished to think of what was utterly formless—and I could not;

for I judged that what was deprived of every form would more readily not be at all than that I should think something between form and nothing, neither formed nor nothing—a formless almost-nothing.

And my mind ceased to question my spirit on this point—full as it was of images of formed bodies and changing and varying them at will—and I turned my attention to the bodies themselves, and looked more deeply at their mutability, by which they cease to be what they had been and begin to be what they were not; and I suspected that the same transition from form to form takes place through some formless something, not through absolute nothing. But I wished to know, not to suspect.

And if my voice and my pen should confess to you in full whatever you have untangled for me on this question, who among readers will have the patience to grasp it? Yet for that reason my heart shall not cease to give you honor and a song of praise concerning the things it is not equal to setting down. For the mutability of mutable things is itself capable of all the forms into which mutable things are changed.

And what is this? Is it mind? Is it body? Is it a form of mind or body? If it could be said "a nothing-something" and "an is-is-not," I would call it this; and yet it already was in some way, that it might receive these visible and composed forms.

### Chapter 7: From nothing God made matter

**7**. And from where would it in any way be, unless it were from you, from whom are all things, in however much they are? But the more unlike you, the farther from you—and not in places.

And so you, Lord, who are not at one moment one thing and at another otherwise, but the Selfsame and the Selfsame and the Selfsame, *holy, holy, holy, Lord God almighty*[^11], in the Beginning that is from you, in your Wisdom, which is born of your substance, made something also out of nothing.

For you made *heaven and earth*[^12] not out of yourself: for it would be equal to your Only-begotten, and through this to you as well; and in no way would it be just that what was not out of you should be equal to you. And there was nothing else besides you from which to make them, God, one Trinity and triune Unity; and so you made *heaven and earth* out of nothing—something great and something small—since you are almighty and good for making all things good: a great heaven and a small earth.

You were, and there was nothing else, from which you made *heaven and earth*—two things: one near to you, the other near to nothing; one above which only you were, the other below which only nothing was.

### Chapter 8: From unformed matter God made all these things

**8**. But that *heaven of heaven* is yours, Lord; the earth, however, which you gave *to the sons of men*[^13] to behold and to touch, was not such as we now behold and touch it. For *it was invisible and unformed*, and it was an abyss above which there was no light, or *darkness was over the abyss*[^14]—that is, more than in the abyss.

For this abyss of now-visible waters has, even in its depths, its own form of light, perceptible in some way to fish and to living things creeping at its bottom; but that whole was almost nothing, since it was still wholly formless; and yet it already was something that could be formed. For you, Lord, made *the world from formless matter*[^15], which you made out of no thing—an almost-no thing—from which to make the great things which we sons of men marvel at.

For this bodily heaven is very wonderful, which on the second day, after the establishing of light, you said: *Let there be a firmament between water and water; and so it was done*[^16]. This firmament you called *heaven*[^17]—but the heaven of this earth and sea, which you made on the third day[^18] by giving visible form to the unformed matter which you had made before any day. For you had already made even heaven before any day—but the heaven of this heaven; since *in the beginning you made heaven and earth*[^19].

But the *earth* itself, which you had made, was unformed matter, because *it was invisible and unformed, and darkness was over the abyss*; out of which earth, invisible and unformed, out of which formlessness, out of which almost-nothing, you would make all these things of which this mutable world consists and does not consist, in which mutability itself appears, in which times can be perceived and numbered—because times come about through the changes of things, while forms are varied and turned about—forms whose matter is the aforesaid invisible earth.

## The heaven of heaven and unformed matter are outside time

### Chapter 9

**9**. And so the Spirit, the teacher of your servant, when he records that you made *in the beginning heaven and earth*[^20], is silent about times, silent about days. For surely the *heaven of heaven*[^21], which *in the beginning* you made, is some intellectual creature—although in no way coeternal with you, the Trinity—yet a partaker of your eternity, who greatly restrains its mutability through the sweetness of most happy contemplation of you; and, without any lapse from the time it was made, by cleaving to you it transcends every changing vicissitude of times.

But this formlessness, *the invisible and unformed earth*[^22], is not itself numbered among the days either. For where there is no form, no order, nothing comes or passes; and where this does not happen, surely there are no days nor any vicissitude of temporal spaces.

### Chapter 10: Return to the fountain of life

> **10**. O Truth, light of my heart,  
> let not my darkness speak to me!  
> I slipped down to these things and was darkened,  
> but from there, even from there, I came to love you.  
> *I strayed*[^23] *and remembered you*[^24].  
> *I heard your voice behind me*[^25],  
> that I should return,  
> and I scarcely heard it for the tumults of the unpeaceful.  
> And now, behold, I return,  
> burning and panting for your fountain.  
> Let no one keep me back:  
> this I will drink, and this I will live[^26].  
> Let me not be my own life:  
> I lived ill from myself,  
> I was death to myself;  
> in you I come back to life[^27].  
> You—speak to me, you—converse with me.  
> I have believed in your books,  
> and their *words* are deeply *arcane*[^28].

### Chapter 11: God is eternal and made all things

**11**. You have already said to me, Lord, with a strong voice in my inner ear, that you are eternal, *alone having immortality*[^29], since you are changed by no form or motion, nor is your will varied by times—for that will is not immortal which is now one thing and now another[^30]. This is clear to me *in your sight*[^31], and let it grow more and more clear, I pray, and let me persist in that manifestation soberly under your wings.

Again you have said to me, Lord, with a strong voice in my inner ear, that you have made all natures and substances that are not what you are and yet are; and only this is not from you—that which is not; and that the motion of the will away from you, who are, to that which is less, because such a motion is offense and sin; and that no one's sin either harms you or disturbs the order of your rule, either at the top or at the bottom. This is clear to me in your sight, and let it grow more and more clear, I pray, and let me persist in that manifestation soberly under your wings.

#### A pure mind suffers no change

**12**. Again you have said to me, with a strong voice in my inner ear, that not even that creature is coeternal with you, whose pleasure you alone are; and, drinking you in with most persevering chastity, it nowhere and never thrusts forth its mutability; and with you ever present to it, to whom it holds itself with its whole affection, having no future to expect, nor casting into the past what it would remember, it is varied by no change nor distended into any times.

O blessed, if such there is, in cleaving to your beatitude—blessed in you, its sempiternal indweller and enlightener! Nor do I find what I would more gladly call the *heaven of heaven of the Lord*[^32] than your house contemplating your delight[^33], without any defect of going out into something else—a pure mind most concordantly one, in the establishment of the peace of holy spirits, citizens of your city in the heavenly places above these heavenly things.

**13**. From this let the soul understand, whose pilgrimage has been made far[^34], if it now thirsts for you, if its *tears have now become its bread, while it is said to it daily: Where is your God?*[^35]—if it now asks *one thing of you and seeks this*, that it may dwell *in your house all the days of its life*[^36]? And what is its life but you? And what are your days but your eternity, as *your years*, which do not fail, because *you yourself are the same*[^37]? From this, then, let the soul that can understand, how far above all times you are eternal, since your house, which has not gone on pilgrimage, although not coeternal with you, yet, by cleaving to you without cease or failure, suffers no vicissitude of times. This is clear to me in your sight, and let it grow more and more clear, I pray, and let me persist in this manifestation soberly under your wings.

#### Unformed matter does not exhibit changes of time

**14**. Behold, some I-know-not-what formlessness is in these changes of the lowest and farthest things. And who shall tell me—unless one who wanders and turns about through the empty places of his heart with his own phantasms—who, unless such a one, will tell me that, with every form lessened and consumed, if formlessness alone remained, by which a thing was changed and turned from form to form, it could exhibit the changes of times? It cannot at all, because without variety of motions there are no times; and there is no variety where there is no form.

## Two creatures are without times

### Chapter 12

**15**. Having considered these things—so far as you grant, my God, so far as you stir me up to knock, and so far as you open to him who knocks[^38]—I find two things you made that are without times, though neither is coeternal with you: one, so formed that without any defect of contemplation, without any interval of change, although mutable, yet unchanged, it enjoys your eternity and unchangeableness; the other, so unformed that it had no form from which into another form of motion or of rest it would be changed, by which to be subjected to time.

But you did not leave this to be formless, since before any day, *in the beginning you made heaven and earth*[^39], these two of which I was speaking. *But the earth was invisible and unformed, and darkness was over the abyss*[^40]. By these words formlessness is hinted at, so that those may gradually be received who cannot think of an entire privation of form, yet not a passing into nothing—from which there should be made the other heaven, and the visible and composed earth, and beautiful water, and whatever else is recorded as made thereafter in the constitution of this world, and not without days[^41]; for they are such that in them the vicissitudes of times are carried out, by the ordered changes of motions and forms.

### Chapter 13: Intellectual heaven and unformed earth were made outside time

**16**. This is what I think for the present, my God, when I hear your Scripture speaking: *In the beginning God made heaven and earth; but the earth was invisible and unformed, and darkness was over the abyss*[^42]—and not stating on which day you made these.

So I think for the present, on account of that *heaven of heaven*[^43], the intellectual heaven, where it is given to the understanding to know together—not *in part*, not *in an enigma*, not *through a mirror*, but as a whole, in manifestation, *face to face*[^44]; not now this, now that, but, as has been said, to know together without any vicissitude of times—and on account of the invisible and unformed earth, without any vicissitude of times, which is wont to have now this and now that, because where there is no form, nowhere is there "this" and "that";

on account of these two—the one first formed, the other utterly unformed: the one heaven, but heaven of heaven; the other earth, but invisible and unformed earth—on account of these two, I think for the present, your Scripture says without mentioning days: *In the beginning God made heaven and earth*. For it at once added what earth it meant.

And the fact that on the second day the firmament is recorded as made and called heaven[^45] implies of which heaven the speech earlier spoke, without days.

## Diverse things can be understood in the words of Holy Scripture

### Chapter 14: Wondrous is the depth of God's utterances

**17**. Wondrous is the depth of your utterances, whose surface, behold, before us, is winning to little ones: but wondrous is the depth, my God, wondrous is the depth! It is a thrill to look into it, a thrill of awe, and a trembling of love.

I hate its enemies vehemently: oh, that you would slay them with a two-edged sword[^46], that they may no longer be its enemies! For I so love that they be slain to themselves, that they may live to you.

But behold, others are not reproachers but praisers of the book of *Genesis*: "No," they say, "this is not what the Spirit of God, who wrote these things through Moses his servant, willed to be understood in these words; he did not will what you say to be understood, but something else, which we say." To these, with you as judge, God of us all, I reply thus.

### Chapter 15: The Father, indeed: — That God is eternal;

**18**. Will you say that what the Truth tells me with a strong voice in my inner ear about the true eternity of the Creator is false—that his substance is in no way varied by times, nor is his will outside his substance?

Hence he does not will now this and now that, but at once and together and always wills all that he wills; not again and again, nor now these things now those, nor willing later what he did not will, or not willing what he willed before; for such a will is mutable, and every mutable thing is not eternal; *but our God is eternal*[^47].

Likewise, what he says to me in my inner ear: the expectation of things to come becomes direct attention when they have come, and the same direct attention becomes memory when they have passed; and every attention that is so varied is mutable, and every mutable thing is not eternal—but our God is eternal.

These things I gather and put together, and I find that my God, the eternal God, did not found a creature by some new will, nor does his knowledge undergo anything transitory.

#### that matter and the heaven of heaven are from him

**19**. What, then, will you say, you contradictors? Are these things false? "No," they say. What of this? Is it false that every formed nature, or formable matter, is not but from him who is supremely good, because he supremely is? "We do not deny this either," they say. What then? Do you deny this, that there is a certain sublime creature cleaving with so chaste a love to the true and truly eternal God, that, although it is not coeternal with him, it yet flows away from him into no variety and vicissitude of times, but rests in the most truthful contemplation of him alone?

Since you, God, to one who loves you as much as you command, show yourself to him and are sufficient for him[^48]—and therefore he does not turn aside from you nor to himself? This is the *house of God*[^49], not earthly, nor any heavenly bodily mass, but spiritual, and a partaker of your eternity, because without lapse for ever. For *you have set it for an age and for an age of ages; you have given a precept, and it shall not pass away*[^50]. And yet not coeternal with you, because not without beginning—for it was made.

**20**. For although we find no time before it (for *before all things wisdom was created*[^51])—and this is certainly not that Wisdom which is plainly coeternal and equal to you, our God, its Father, and through whom all things were created, and in which Beginning you made *heaven and earth*[^52]; but the Wisdom which was created—that is, an intellectual nature, which is light by contemplation of light

(for it too is called Wisdom, though created; but as great is the difference between the light that illumines and that which is illumined, so great is the difference between the Wisdom that creates and that which is created—just as between the justice that justifies and the justice that has been made by justification; for we too are called your justice; for a certain servant of yours says: *that we might be the justice of God in him*[^53])

—therefore, because *before all things a certain wisdom was created*—that wisdom which was created, the rational and intellectual mind of your chaste city, our mother, which *is above and is free*[^54] and *eternal in the heavens*[^55] (and what heavens, but those that praise you, the *heavens of heavens*[^56], because this too is the *heaven of heaven of the Lord*[^57]?)—although we find no time before it, because it precedes even the creature of time, being itself *created before all things*, yet before it is the eternity of the Creator himself, from whom it took its beginning when it was made—not the beginning of time, since there was as yet no time, but the beginning of its own coming-to-be.

**21**. So it is from you, our God, in such a way that it is plainly other than you, and not the Selfsame. Although we find no time, not only before it, but not even in it, because it is fit always to see your face[^58] and is nowhere turned away from it; whence it comes about that it is varied by no change. Yet there is in it that very mutability, from which it would grow dark and cold, did it not, cleaving to you with a great love, shine and burn from you as a perpetual noonday[^59].

O house of light and beauty, *I have loved your loveliness, and the place of the dwelling of the glory* of my Lord[^60], your builder and possessor! For you let my pilgrimage sigh, and I say to him who made you, that he may possess me too in you, since he made me as well. *I have strayed like a lost sheep*[^61], but on the shoulders of my shepherd[^62], your builder, I hope to be carried back to you.

#### that these two creatures are outside time

**22**. What do you say to me, contradictors whom I was addressing—you who yet believe Moses to be a devout servant of God, and his books oracles of the Holy Spirit? Is not this the house of God, not indeed coeternal with God, yet, according to its own mode, eternal in the heavens, where you seek the vicissitudes of times in vain, because you do not find them[^63]? For it transcends every distention and every revolving space of an age, for which *it is good always to cleave to God*[^64]. "It is," they say.

What, then, of what *my heart cried out to my God*[^65], when it heard inwardly the *voice of his praise*[^66]—what at last do you maintain to be false? Or is it because there was an unformed matter, where, since there was no form, there was no order? But where there was no order, there could be no vicissitude of times; and yet this almost-nothing, in so far as it was not altogether nothing, was surely from him from whom is whatever is, in however small a way it is[^67]. "This also," they say, "we do not deny."

## With what kind of people Augustine is speaking

### Chapter 16

**23**. For with these I wish to converse a little before you, my God, who concede that all these things, which your truth does not keep silent within my mind, are true. As for those who deny them, let them bark as much as they wish and make their own clamor; I shall try to persuade them to be quiet and to give a way for your word to reach them. But if they will not, and reject me, I beseech, *my God, do not keep silent from me*[^68].

Speak truly in my heart[^69]; for you alone speak so. And I will leave them outside, blowing on the dust and stirring up the earth into their own eyes; and I will enter into my chamber[^70], and sing to you a love-song, groaning with unutterable groanings[^71] on my pilgrimage, and remembering Jerusalem with my heart stretched up toward it[^72]—Jerusalem my homeland, Jerusalem my mother[^73]—and you above it as ruler, enlightener, father, guardian, husband, chaste and strong delights and solid joy and all ineffable goods—all together, because one supreme and true good:

and I will not turn away, until you gather the whole of what I am, from this dispersion and deformity, into the peace of that dearest mother, where are the first fruits of my spirit[^74]—from which these things are certain to me—and shape me anew and confirm me forever, *my God, my mercy*[^75].

But with those who do not call false all those things which are true—honoring your Holy Scripture, set forth through holy Moses, and setting it with us at the summit of authority to be followed, yet contradicting us in something—I speak thus. Be you, our God, the judge between my confessions and their contradictions.

### Chapter 17: The names "heaven and earth" signify: — the whole world;

**24**. For they say: "Although these things are true, yet Moses was not looking at those two when, with the Spirit revealing it, he said: *In the beginning God made heaven and earth*[^76]. By the name *heaven* he did not signify that spiritual or intellectual creature always contemplating the face of God[^77], nor by the name *earth* unformed matter."

What, then?

"What we say," they say, "that man perceived; that he expressed in these words."

What is that?

"By the name," they say, "of *heaven and earth*, he wished first, universally and briefly, to signify this whole visible world, so that afterward he might set out, by an enumeration of days, as it were point by point, all the things the Holy Spirit was pleased to announce in this way. For that rude and carnal people, to whom he spoke[^78], were such that he judged that to them he should commend only the visible works of God."

But that *the invisible and unformed earth* and the *darksome abyss*—from which, in consequence, it is shown that all these visible things known to all were made and arranged through those days—is not incongruously to be understood as that unformed matter, they agree.

#### unformed matter, and also invisible and visible nature;

**25**. What if another should say that the same formlessness and confusion of matter was first intimated by the name *heaven and earth*, because from it this visible world, with all the natures that most plainly appear in it, was founded and completed—the world which is often called by the name "heaven and earth"?

What if another should also say that *heaven and earth* were not unfittingly called the invisible and visible nature, and through this that the whole creation, which God made *in Wisdom—that is, in the Beginning*—is comprehended by these two words; but yet that, since not from the very substance of God but out of nothing all things were made[^79], since they are not the very thing that God is, and there is in all a certain mutability—whether they abide, like the eternal house of God, or are changed, like the soul and body of a human—

—that the common matter of all invisible and visible things, still unformed but certainly formable, from which heaven and earth would be made—that is, the invisible and the visible, now each a formed creature—was set forth by these names by which it would be called *invisible and unformed earth, and darkness over the abyss*[^80], with this distinction: that *invisible and unformed earth* should be understood as corporeal matter before the qualification of form, and *darkness over the abyss* as spiritual matter before the restraint of its, as it were, flowing immoderation, and before the illumination of Wisdom?

#### The beginning and the creatable matter of things

**26**. There is still what may be said, if anyone else wishes: namely, that not by the name *heaven and earth* are signified, in this passage, invisible and visible natures already completed and formed, when it is read, *In the beginning God made heaven and earth*—but the still-unformed beginning of things, formable and creatable matter, was called by these names; because in it were already those things in confusion, not yet distinguished by qualities and forms, which now, set out in their orders, are called heaven and earth—the former a spiritual, the latter a corporeal creature.

## One person can perceive one true thing in Holy Scriptures, another another

### Chapter 18

**27**. Having heard and considered all these things, I do not wish to *contend with words; for it is of no use except to the overthrow of the hearers*[^81]. But *for edification the law is good, if one uses it lawfully, since its end is love from a pure heart and a good conscience and unfeigned faith*[^82]; and I know on what two precepts our Master suspended the whole law and the prophets[^83]. To me ardently confessing these things, my God, *the light of my eyes*[^84] in secret—what does it hurt me that diverse things may be understood in these words, which are nevertheless true?

What, I say, does it hurt me if I should perceive something different from what another perceived him who wrote to have perceived? All of us who read strive to track down and grasp what the one we read willed; and when we believe him truthful, we do not dare to think he said anything we know or judge to be false. While each one, then, tries to perceive in Holy Scriptures what the one who wrote them perceived in them, what evil is there if he perceives that which you, light of all truthful minds, show to be true—even if the one he reads did not perceive this, when he too perceived what was true, although not this?

### Chapter 19: Certain things are surely true

**28**. For it is true, Lord, that you made *heaven and earth*[^85]. And it is true that the Beginning is your Wisdom, in which *you made all things*[^86].

Likewise it is true that this visible world has as its great parts heaven and earth, in a brief embrace of all natures made and founded.

And it is true that every mutable thing hints to our knowledge of a certain formlessness, by which it receives form or by which it is changed and turned.

It is true that what so cleaves to the unchangeable form that, although it is mutable, it is not changed, suffers no times.

It is true that formlessness, which is almost nothing, cannot have vicissitudes of times.

It is true that, that from which something comes to be can, by a certain kind of speech, already have the name of the thing that comes from it; from which any formlessness from which heaven and earth were made could be called "heaven and earth."

It is true that, of all formed things, nothing is nearer to the formless than earth and abyss.

It is true that not only what is created and formed, but also whatever is creatable and formable, you have made, *from whom are all things*[^87].

It is true that everything that is formed from the formless is first formless, then formed.

### Chapter 20: Some things rightly explained about the words "In the beginning"

**29**. From all these true things, about which those do not doubt to whose inner eye you have granted to see such things, and who unshakeably believe that Moses, your servant, spoke in the Spirit of truth[^88],

from all these, then, one takes one thing for himself who says: *In the beginning God made heaven and earth*[^89], that is, in his Word coeternal with him God made the intelligible and the sensible, or the spiritual and the corporeal, creation;

another, who says: *In the beginning God made heaven and earth*, that is, in his Word coeternal with him God made this whole mass of this corporeal world with all the manifest and known natures it contains;

another, who says: *In the beginning God made heaven and earth*, that is, in his Word coeternal with him he made the unformed matter of the spiritual and corporeal creation;

another, who says: *In the beginning God made heaven and earth*, that is, in his Word coeternal with him God made the unformed matter of the corporeal creation, in which heaven and earth were still in confusion—heaven and earth which now, already distinguished and formed in the mass of this world, we perceive;

another, who says: *In the beginning God made heaven and earth*, that is, in the very outset of his making and working, God made the unformed matter holding heaven and earth in confusion, from which, now formed, they stand out and appear with all that is in them.

### Chapter 21: Some things rightly explained about the unformed earth and the abyss

**30**. Likewise, as to the understanding of the words that follow, from all those true things one takes one thing for himself who says: *But the earth was invisible and unformed, and darkness was over the abyss*[^90], that is, that corporeal thing which God made was still the unformed matter of corporeal things, without order, without light;

another, who says: *But the earth was invisible and unformed, and darkness was over the abyss*, that is, this whole that was called heaven and earth was still unformed and darksome matter, from which the corporeal heaven and the corporeal earth would be made, with all that is in them, known to corporeal senses;

another, who says: *But the earth was invisible and unformed, and darkness was over the abyss*, that is, this whole that was called heaven and earth was still unformed and darksome matter, from which the intelligible heaven (elsewhere called *heaven of heaven*[^91]) and earth—namely, every corporeal nature, under which name even this corporeal heaven is understood—would be made; that is, from which every invisible and visible creature would be made;

another, who says: *But the earth was invisible and unformed, and darkness was over the abyss*, that the Scripture did not call that formlessness by the name of heaven and earth, but, he says, that very formlessness already was, which it named *invisible and unformed earth and darksome abyss*, from which it had said God made heaven and earth—namely, the spiritual and the corporeal creation;

another, who says: *But the earth was invisible and unformed, and darkness was over the abyss*, that is, a certain formlessness was already the matter from which Scripture had said God made heaven and earth—namely, the whole corporeal mass of the world distributed into the two greatest parts, upper and lower, with all the familiar and known creatures that are in them.

### Chapter 22: Scripture did not record many things made by God

**31**. For when someone tries to resist these two extreme opinions by saying: "If you do not wish this formlessness of matter to be seen as called by the name of heaven and earth, then there was something which God had not made, from which to make heaven and earth; for Scripture has not recorded that God made this matter, unless we understand it to be signified by the term *heaven and earth*, or of earth alone, when it was said: *In the beginning God made heaven and earth*[^92], so that what follows: *But the earth was invisible and unformed*[^93], although it pleased to call the unformed matter so, we understand nothing but that which God made in what was first written: *he made heaven and earth*,"

the assertors of those two opinions which we set as the extremes, of either, when they hear these things, will reply and say: "We do not deny that this unformed matter was made by God, by God from whom are *all things very good*[^94]; because, as we say that what is created and formed is more good, so we confess that what has been made creatable and formable is less good, but still good:

but that Scripture did not record that God made this formlessness, just as it did not record many other things, such as the Cherubim and Seraphim[^95], and those which the Apostle distinctly names: *thrones, dominations, principalities, powers*[^96]—and yet it is manifest that God made all these.

Or if, by what was said—*he made heaven and earth*—all things are included, what shall we say of the waters, *over which the Spirit of God was being borne*[^97]? For if, when the earth was named, they are also understood together, how is unformed matter now taken under the name of earth, when we see such beautiful waters?

Or if it is so taken, why is it written that from the same formlessness was made the firmament, and called heaven[^98], and not written that the waters were made? For they are not still unformed and invisible—those waters which we see flowing in so beautiful a form.

Or if they received that form then, when *God said: Let the water under the firmament be gathered together*[^99], so that the gathering is itself the formation, what will be answered about the *waters above the firmament*[^100]—since neither, unformed, would they have deserved to receive so honorable a place, nor is it written by what word they were formed?

Hence, if *Genesis* kept silence about something God made, which nevertheless neither sound faith nor sure understanding doubts that God made—and yet no sober doctrine will dare to say that those waters are coeternal with God, just because we hear them recorded in the book of *Genesis*, but do not find where they were made—why may we not also, with truth teaching, understand that this unformed matter, which this Scripture calls *invisible and unformed earth, and darksome abyss*, was made by God out of nothing, and therefore is not coeternal with him, even though this narrative has omitted to state where it was made?"

### Chapter 23: Two kinds of disagreement

**32**. Having heard and considered these things, then, according to the capacity of my weakness—which I confess to you, my God, who know it—I see that two kinds of disagreement can arise when something is set forth by truthful messengers through signs: one, if there is disagreement about the truth of the things; the other, if about the will of the one who sets it forth. For we inquire in one way about the condition of the creature, what is true; in another way about what in these words Moses, the outstanding member of your household of faith, willed the reader and hearer to understand.

In that first kind, let all depart from me who think they know what is false. In this second kind likewise, let all depart from me who think that Moses said what is false.

But let me be joined to those, Lord, in you, and let me delight with them in you[^101], who are fed with your truth in the breadth of love[^102]; and let us together approach the words of your book, and seek in them your will[^103] through the will of your servant, by whose pen you dispensed them.

### Chapter 24: What truth did Moses mean when writing these words?

**33**. But which of us, among so many true things that come to seekers in those words variously understood, has so found it that he may say as confidently that Moses perceived this and willed this to be understood in that narrative, as he confidently says that this is true—whether the writer perceived this or something else?

For behold, my God, *I your servant*, who have vowed to you a sacrifice of confession in these writings, and pray that, by your mercy, *I may render my vows to you*[^104]—behold, as confidently as I say that in your unchangeable Word you made all things, invisible and visible, do I say with equal confidence that Moses paid attention to nothing other than this when he wrote, *In the beginning God made heaven and earth*[^105]? For not as I see this certain in your truth do I see in his mind that he was thinking this when he wrote these things.

For he could have been thinking of the very outset of making when he said: *in the beginning*; he could also have wished by *heaven and earth* in this place not any already formed and perfect nature, spiritual or corporeal, to be understood, but both inchoate and as yet unformed.

For I see that any of these things could truly be said; but which of these he thought in these words, I do not so see—although, whether some of these or something else not mentioned by me, that great man beheld in his mind when he uttered these words, I do not doubt that he saw what was true and aptly set it forth.

### Chapter 25: The pride and rashness of some interpreters of Scripture

**34**. *Let no one henceforth trouble me*[^106], saying to me: "This is not what Moses perceived, what you say, but he perceived this, what I say."

For if he were to say to me: "How do you know that Moses perceived this, which you express about these words of his?"—I should bear it with an even mind, and reply, perhaps, what I replied above, or somewhat more fully, if he were more obstinate.

But when he says, "It is not this that he perceived, what you say, but what I say," and yet does not deny that what each of us says is true—O life of the poor, my God, in whose bosom there is no contradiction, rain softenings into my heart, that I may patiently bear such people, who do not say this to me because they are inspired and have seen in the heart of your servant what they say, but because they are proud and do not know Moses's meaning, but love their own, not because it is true, but because it is their own.

Otherwise they would love another true opinion equally, as I love what they say when they say what is true—not because it is theirs, but because it is true; and therefore now it is no longer theirs, because it is true. But if they love it because it is true, then it is both theirs and mine, since it is in common to all lovers of the truth.

But that they contend that Moses perceived not what I say but what they themselves say, this I do not wish, do not love; because, even if it is so, yet such rashness is not of knowledge but of audacity, and not vision but pride gave it birth.

And so, Lord, your judgments are to be feared, since your truth is neither mine nor his nor that other's, but ours, all of us whom you publicly call to its communion, terribly warning us that we should not wish to have it as our private possession, lest we be deprived of it[^107]. For whoever claims for himself as his own what you propose to all for their enjoyment, and wants what belongs to all to be his, is driven from what is common to his own—that is, from truth to a lie. For he *who speaks a lie speaks of his own*[^108].

#### That love must not be offended

**35**. *Attend*[^109], best judge, God, Truth itself[^110], *attend*, what I shall say to this contradictor, *attend*; for before you I speak, and before my brethren who use the law lawfully unto the end which is love[^111]; *attend and see*[^112], what I shall say to him, if it please you.

For I direct to him this fraternal and peaceable voice: if we both see that what you say is true, and we both see that what I say is true, where, I ask, do we see this? Surely not I in you, nor you in me, but both of us in that very unchangeable truth which is above our minds. Since, then, we do not contend about the light itself of our Lord God, why do we contend about the thought of our neighbor, which we cannot see as the unchangeable truth is seen—since, even if Moses himself had appeared to us and said, "This I thought," we would not see it even so, but believe it?

Let no one, then, *beyond what is written, be inflated for one against another*[^113]. Let us love *the Lord our God with our whole heart, with our whole soul, with our whole mind, and our neighbor as ourselves*[^114]. For unless we believe that Moses, in whatever he perceived in those books, perceived in accordance with these two precepts of love, we shall *make the Lord a liar*[^115], when we opine concerning the mind of a fellow servant otherwise than he taught.

Now see how foolish it is, in so great an abundance of most true opinions which can be drawn out from those words, rashly to affirm which of them Moses chiefly perceived, and by ruinous contentions to offend that very love for whose sake he said everything, whose sayings we are trying to expound.

## What Augustine would have wished, if he had then been what Moses was

### Chapter 26

**36**. And yet I, my God, loftiness of my lowliness and rest of my labor, who hear my confessions and *forgive my sins*[^116]—since you command me to love my neighbor as myself[^117]—I cannot believe less concerning Moses, your most faithful servant, than I should wish and long that you would give me as a gift, had I been born in that time when he was, and had you set me in that place, so that through the service of my heart and tongue those writings might be dispensed, which so long after were to profit all the nations, and which throughout the whole world, by so high a summit of authority, were to surpass the words of all false and proud teachings.

I should wish, if I were then Moses (for we all come *from the same lump*[^118]; and *what is man, but that you are mindful of him?*[^119])—I should wish, then, if I were then what he was, and the book of *Genesis* were assigned to me by you to write, that such a faculty of speaking should be given me, and such a manner of weaving discourse, that those who cannot yet understand how God creates would not refuse the words as exceeding their powers, and those who already can, into whatever true opinion they had come by thinking, would find it not passed over in your servant's few words,

and if another had seen another in the light of truth, that also should not be missing for understanding in the same words.

## The weakness of little ones and saving faith

### Chapter 27

**37**. For as a fountain in a small place is more abundant, and supplies a flow into broader spaces by many streams, than any one of those streams which, through many places, is led from the same fountain—so the narrative of your dispenser[^120], destined to be of use to many who will discourse, gushes from its small measure of speech with streams of clear truth,

from which each may draw to himself the truth—what he can about these matters, this one this, that one that—through the longer windings of speech.

For some, when they read or hear these words, think of God as if a human, or as some power endowed with an immense mass, who by a certain new and sudden resolve, outside itself as in places distant from it, made heaven and earth—two great bodies above and below, in which all things would be contained. And when they hear, *God said: Let that be, and that was done*[^121], they think of words begun and ended, sounding in time and passing, after whose passing there at once springs into being what was commanded to spring into being; and if anything else they opine in this manner from their familiarity with flesh.

In these creatures, still little ones, while their weakness is borne by this most lowly kind of words as in a mother's bosom, faith is wholesomely built up, by which they may hold and have it sure that God made all the natures which their sense beholds round about in wonderful variety.

And if any of them, having spurned, as it were, the meanness of the words, should stretch himself with proud weakness beyond his nourishing cradle, alas, he will fall—wretched man—and, Lord God, have mercy, lest those who pass by the way trample the unfledged chick[^122]; and *send your angel*[^123] to put him back in his nest, that he may live until he flies.

### Chapter 28: The learning and joy of the learned

**38**. But others, to whom these words are no longer a nest but shadowy thickets, see in them hidden fruits, and they flit about rejoicing, and chatter as they search, and pluck them.

For they see, when they read or hear these words of yours, eternal God, that by your stable abiding all past and future times are surpassed; and yet that there is nothing of temporal creation which you did not make—whose will, since it is what you are, has in no way changed, nor was it that which previously was not,

by a will arising, that you made all things—not from yourself, your likeness, the form of all, but from nothing, an unformed unlikeness, which would be formed through your likeness, returning to you, the One, according to its ordained capacity, as much as is given to each thing in its own kind; and that *all things might be made very good*[^124], whether they abide near you, or, by a gradually more remote distance, through times and places, make or undergo beautiful variations.

They see these things and rejoice in the light of your truth, so far as here they are able.

#### Some other things explained about the words "In the beginning"

**39**. And one of them turns his attention to what is said: *In the beginning God made*[^125], and looks at Wisdom as the Beginning, because she also speaks to us[^126]. Likewise another turns his attention to the same words and understands "the beginning" as the outset of the things founded, and so takes *in the beginning he made* as if it had been said, "first he made."

And among those who understand *in the Beginning*—that it is in Wisdom you made *heaven and earth*[^127]—one of them believes that the very heaven and earth, the creatable matter of heaven and earth, is so named; another that they are already formed and distinguished natures; another that one is formed (the spiritual) under the name of heaven, and the other is unformed (of corporeal matter) under the name of earth.

But those who understand in the names "heaven and earth" the still-unformed matter from which heaven and earth would be formed do not themselves understand this in one way: but one understands it as that from which the intelligible and the sensible creature would be brought to completion; another only as that from which this sensible mass of body, holding in its great bosom plain and ready natures.

Nor do those understand in one way who believe that already arranged and disposed creatures are in this place called "heaven and earth": but one means the invisible and the visible, another only the visible, in which we look up at the luminous heaven and the misty earth and whatever is in them.

### Chapter 29: Matter first made

**40**. But the one who takes *in the beginning he made* in no other sense than if it had been said, "first he made," has no way truly to understand *heaven and earth*[^128], unless he understands the matter of heaven and earth—namely, the universal matter, that is, of the intelligible and corporeal creature.

For if he should wish the universe already formed, it can rightly be asked of him: if God made this first, what did he make next? And after the universe he will find nothing, and thus, unwillingly, he will hear: "How was that first, if nothing came after?"

But when he says, first unformed, then formed, he is not absurd, provided only that he is competent to distinguish what precedes in eternity, what in time, what in choice, what in origin;

in eternity, as God all things; in time, as flower fruit; in choice, as fruit flower; in origin, as sound song.

Of these four, the first and the last which I mentioned are most difficult to understand; the two middle ones, most easily.

For it is a rare and exceedingly difficult sight, Lord, to behold your eternity unchangeably making mutable things, and thereby being prior.

Then who is so keen of mind as without great labor to distinguish how sound is prior to song—namely, because song is formed sound, and something can indeed be unformed, but what is not cannot be formed? So matter is prior to that which is made from it—not prior because it makes, since rather it is made; nor prior by an interval of time. For we do not in an earlier time produce unformed sounds without song, and then in a later time fit them into the form of a song, or shape them, as one does wood from which a chest is made, or silver from which a vessel is wrought; for such materials precede in time also the forms of things made from them.

But in song it is not so. For when it is sung, its sound is heard; it does not first sound unformed and then is formed into song. For what at first sounds in any way passes away, nor will you find any of it that you can take up again and compose by art; and so song turns in its own sound, which sound is its matter. For that very sound is formed, that it may be song.

And therefore, as I was saying, the matter of sounding is prior to the form of singing: not prior by the power of making, for sound is not the maker of singing, but lies subject to the singing soul, from the body, out of which it makes the song; nor prior in time, for it is produced together with the song; nor prior in choice, for the sound is not preferable to the song, since song is not only sound, but beautiful sound.

But it is prior in origin, because the song is not formed in order that it may be sound, but the sound is formed in order that it may be song.

By this example let him who can understand that the matter of things was made first and called "heaven and earth," because from it heaven and earth were made; not first made in time, because the forms of things bring forth times, but it was unformed, and yet, simultaneously with them, is observed in time;

and yet nothing can be told of it unless as if it were prior in time, although it is reckoned the lowest, since indeed formed things are better than the unformed, and it is preceded by the eternity of the Creator, that it might be from nothing, from which something might come to be.

## Consequence: in the diversity of opinions let there be concord of minds and love of the truth

### Chapter 30

**41**. In this diversity of true opinions let the truth itself give birth to concord; and let our God have mercy on us, that we may use the law lawfully, at the end which is the precept—pure love[^129]. And accordingly, if anyone asks me which of these your servant Moses perceived, these are not the speeches of my confessions, unless I confess to you: "I do not know." And yet I know that those opinions are true, except for the carnal ones, of which I have spoken as much as I judged. Yet these words of your book do not terrify the little ones of good hope—words lofty in humility, and few in abundance—

but let all whom I admit to behold and say true things in these words love one another, and equally love you, our God[^130], the fountain of truth, if we thirst not for vain things but for it; and let us so honor that same servant of yours, the dispenser of this Scripture, full of your Spirit, that we believe that he, with you revealing it, paid attention, when he wrote these things, to that which in them excels most both by the light of truth and by the fruit of usefulness.

### Chapter 31: Moses perceived many things

**42**. And so, when one says, "He perceived this, what I do," and another, "No, that, what I do," I judge it more religious to say: Why not rather both, if both are true? And if anyone sees a third, or a fourth, or anything else true in these words, why should he not be believed to have seen all those, through whom the one God so tempered the sacred Letters to the senses of many who would see true and diverse things?

I, certainly—what I declare boldly out of my heart—if I were to write anything at the summit of authority, would rather write so that whatever true thing each could grasp about these matters, my words might echo, than so that I should set down one true opinion more plainly, in order to exclude the others, whose falsity could not offend me.

I do not wish, then, my God, to be so headstrong as not to believe that that man earned this from you. He surely perceived, in these words, and thought, when he wrote them, whatever of truth we have been able to find here, and whatever we have not been able—or have not yet been able—and yet can be found in them.

### Chapter 32: May God feed us, and not error delude us

**43**. Finally, Lord, you who are God and not *flesh and blood*[^131]—if a person should have seen less, could anything that you yourself were going to reveal in these words to later readers be hidden from your good Spirit, who *will lead me into the right land*[^132], even if he through whom they were said perhaps thought of only one of many true opinions? And if it is so, then let that one which he thought be more lofty than the rest. But to us, Lord, either show that very one, or whatever other true one pleases you, so that, whether you reveal to us what you did to that man of yours, or something else from the occasion of those same words, you may yet feed us, and error not delude us.

Behold, Lord my God, how many things—I beg you—have we written about a few words! What strength of ours, what times, will be sufficient for all your books in this manner?

Allow me, then, more briefly to confess to you in them, and to choose some one thing which you have inspired as true, certain, and good—even if many things should occur where many can occur—with the faith of my confession that, if I say what your minister perceived, it will be rightly and best (for that is what I ought to try);

but if I do not attain this, that I may yet say what your truth willed to say to me through his words—which to him also said what it willed.
