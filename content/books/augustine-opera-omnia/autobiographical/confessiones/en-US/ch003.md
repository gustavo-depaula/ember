# Book 2: The Sixteenth Year of His Life

## A restless youth

### Chapter 1: Why he recalls what is most painful to him

**1**. I want to recall my past foulnesses and the carnal corruptions of my soul—not because I love them, but that I may love you, my God. It is for love of your love that I do this, going back over my most wicked ways in the bitterness of my remembering, that you may grow sweet to me—a sweetness that does not deceive, a sweetness happy and secure—and that you may gather me up from the scattering in which I was torn to pieces, while, turned away from you, the One, I wasted myself among the many. For there was a time in my youth when I burned to take my fill of things of hell, and I dared to run wild in a tangle of shadowy loves; my comeliness wasted away, and I rotted[^1] before your eyes, pleasing myself and longing to please the eyes of men[^2].

### Chapter 2: His feeble age seethed with the unbridled impulses of the soul

**2**. And what was it that delighted me but to love and to be loved? But the measure was not kept that runs from mind to mind, the bright boundary of friendship; rather, mists steamed up from the muddy concupiscence of the flesh and from the bubbling spring of puberty, and they clouded and darkened my heart, so that the clear weather of love could not be told apart from the fog of lust. The two of them boiled together in confusion and swept my feeble age over the cliffs of desire and plunged it into a whirlpool of disgraceful deeds. Your wrath had grown strong over me, and I did not know it. I had grown deaf from the clanking of the chain of my mortality, the penalty of my soul's pride; and I went farther from you, and you let me be; and I was tossed about and spilled out and ran to waste and boiled over in my fornications, and you kept silent[^3].

O my joy, so slow in coming! You kept silent then, and I went on, farther and farther from you, into more and more barren seeds of sorrows, in proud dejection and restless weariness.

**3**. Who could have set my misery in measure, and turned to good use the fleeting beauties of these newest things, and fixed limits to their sweetness, so that the tides of my youth might have spent themselves on the shore of marriage—if calm could not be had in them, content with the end of begetting children, as your law prescribes[^4], O Lord, you who shape even the offspring of our mortality, able to lay a gentle hand to soften the thorns shut out from your paradise[^5]? For your omnipotence is not far from us, even when we are far from you.

Or at least I should have paid more watchful heed to the sound of your clouds: *Such will have trouble in the flesh; but I would spare you*[^6]; and, *It is good for a man not to touch a woman*[^7]; and, *The unmarried man is concerned about the things of the Lord, how to please God; but the married man is concerned about the things of the world, how to please his wife*[^8].

These voices, then, I would have heard more watchfully, and, made a eunuch *for the kingdom of heaven*[^9], I would have waited more happily for your embraces.

**4**. But I boiled over, wretch that I was, following the rush of my own current, having forsaken you; I overstepped all your lawful bounds, and did not escape your scourges—for what mortal does? You were always present, mercifully raging, sprinkling the most bitter disappointments over all my unlawful pleasures, that I might seek a pleasure free of disappointment; and where I might find this, I could find nothing but you, Lord, nothing but you, *who fashion pain into a precept*[^10], and who strike that you may heal, and kill us lest we die away from you[^11]. Where was I, and how far was I exiled from the delights of your house[^12], in that sixteenth year of the age of my flesh, when the madness of unbridled lust—disgraceful in the sight of men, unlawful by your laws—took up its scepter over me, and I gave it my hands entirely? My family took no care to save me from my fall by marriage; their one care was that I should learn to make the finest speech and to persuade by my words.

## His studies interrupted

### Chapter 3

**5**. That year my studies had been interrupted: after I was brought back from Madauros, a neighboring town to which I had already begun to travel for the sake of acquiring literature and oratory, the funds for a longer journey to Carthage were being prepared—more by my father's high spirit than by his means, for he was a citizen of Thagaste of quite slender resources. To whom am I telling this? Not to you, my God; but in your presence I tell it to my own kind, to the human race, however small the portion of it that may chance upon these writings of mine. And to what end? So that I, and whoever reads this, may consider out of what depths we must cry to you[^13]. And what is nearer to your ears than a confessing heart and a life lived from faith[^14]?

For who did not then extol with praises that man, my father, because beyond the means of his estate he would lay out whatever was needed even for a son traveling far away for the sake of his studies? For many citizens far wealthier took no such trouble for their children; while all the while that same father took no care what sort of person I was growing into for you, or how chaste I was—provided only that I was cultivated in speech, or rather left uncultivated in your tilling, O God, you who are the one true and good Lord of your field[^15], which is my heart.

#### On holiday from school, he plunged into pleasures

**6**. But when, in that sixteenth year, with leisure forced on me by domestic necessity, I began to live with my parents, idle from all school, the brambles of lust shot up over my head, and there was no hand to root them out.

On the contrary, when that father of mine saw me at the baths, now growing toward manhood and clothed in restless adolescence, he was as delighted as if he were already longing for grandchildren, and gladly reported it to my mother—glad in that intoxication in which this world has forgotten you, its Creator, and has loved your creature in place of you[^16], drunk on the invisible wine of a will perverse and bent down toward the depths.

But in my mother's heart you had already begun your temple and the beginning of your holy dwelling[^17]; while he was as yet only a catechumen, and that but recently. And so she started up in pious trepidation and trembling[^18], and, though I was not yet one of the faithful, she nonetheless feared the twisted ways in which those walk who turn *their back to you and not their face*[^19].

## His mother's warnings and anxieties

**7**. Woe is me! And do I dare to say that you kept silent, my God, while I went farther from you? Were you really silent to me then? And whose were those words but yours, which through my mother, your faithful one, you sang in my ears? Yet nothing of them went down into my heart to make me act on it. For she wanted—and I remember privately how she warned me with enormous anxiety—that I should not fornicate, and above all that I should not commit adultery with anyone's wife. These seemed to me womanish warnings, which I would have blushed to obey.

But they were yours, and I did not know it; I thought you were silent and that she was the one speaking, whereas through her you were not silent to me[^20]; and in her you were despised by me—by me, her son, the son of *your handmaid*[^21], your servant.

But I did not know it, and I rushed on headlong in such blindness that among my own age-mates I was ashamed to be less disgraceful, since I heard them boasting of their shameful deeds and glorying the more, the more debased they were; and I took pleasure in doing the same, not only for the pleasure of the deed but also for the praise. What is worthy of blame but vice? But I, so as not to be blamed, made myself more vicious; and where I had no deed by which, once it was committed, I might be the equal of the most abandoned, I pretended I had done what I had not, lest I seem the more contemptible the more innocent I was, and be held the cheaper the more chaste I was.

**8**. See with what companions I walked the streets of Babylon, and wallowed in its mire as though in cinnamon and precious ointments[^22]. And to make me stick the more tightly to its very navel, the invisible enemy trod me down[^23] and seduced me, because I was one who could be seduced.

For not even she who had by now *fled out of the midst of Babylon*[^24]—though she went more slowly in the rest of it, the mother of my flesh—did, though she warned me to chastity, take it to heart so as to confine within the bounds of married affection what she had heard about me from her husband, and now felt to be pestilent and dangerous for the future, if it could not be cut back to the quick. She did not take it to heart, because there was a fear that my hope might be hampered by the shackle of a wife—not that hope which my mother had in you for the world to come, but the hope of letters,

which both my parents were over-eager that I should know: he, because he thought next to nothing about you, and only vain things about me; she, because she reckoned that the customary course of learning would be not only no hindrance but even some help toward attaining you. So I conjecture, recalling as best I can the characters of my parents.

The reins were also slackened for me, beyond the restraint of any severity, to play amid the dissolution of various affections; and over all of it there was a fog cutting me off, my God, from the clear weather of your truth, and *my iniquity came forth as it were from fatness*[^25].

## That theft of the pears

### Chapter 4: With his companions he stole a huge load of pears

**9**. Theft, surely, your law punishes, Lord—and so does the law written in the hearts of men[^26], which not even iniquity itself blots out; for what thief tolerates another thief with composure? Not even a rich one tolerates a thief driven by want. And I chose to commit theft, and did so, compelled by no need unless it were a poverty of justice and a disgust for it, and a glut of iniquity. For I stole something I had in plenty, and far better; nor did I want to enjoy the thing I sought by stealing, but the theft itself and the sin.

There was a pear tree near our vineyard, loaded with fruit that was tempting in neither look nor taste. To shake it down and carry off its fruit, a band of us worthless youths set out late at night—having prolonged our games out of doors, as our pestilent custom was—and we took huge loads from it, not for our own eating, but rather to throw to the pigs; and even if we ate some of it, what we relished was the doing of what was not allowed.

Behold my heart, O God, behold my heart, on which you had mercy in the depths of the abyss. Let my heart now tell you what it was looking for there, that I should be gratuitously evil, with no cause for my malice but malice itself.

It was foul, and I loved it; I loved my own undoing, I loved my falling away—not the thing toward which I was falling, but my falling away itself I loved—a base soul, leaping away from your firm support *into ruin*[^27], seeking nothing through the disgrace but the disgrace itself.

## Sins are committed through an immoderate inclination toward lower goods

### Chapter 5

**10**. Indeed there is beauty in lovely bodies, and in gold and silver and all such things; in the touch of flesh a fitting harmony counts for much; and each of the other senses has its own suited adaptation of bodily things. Temporal honor too, and the power to command and to prevail, have their own splendor, from which also arises the craving for revenge; and yet, in pursuing all these, we must not depart from you, Lord, nor stray from your law. The life, too, that we live here has its own allurement, because of a certain measure of comeliness of its own and its fittingness with all these lowest beauties.

Human friendship, too, is sweet, with its dear bond, because of the unity it makes out of many souls. On account of all these things and their like, sin is committed, when through an immoderate inclination toward these—which are the lowest goods—the better and highest goods are forsaken: you, O Lord our God, and your truth and your law[^28]. For these lowest things do have their delights, but not like my God, who made all things; for in him the just man delights, and he is the delight of the upright in heart[^29].

**11**. And so, when it is asked about a crime why it was done, it is not usually believed unless it appears that there could have been a desire of attaining one of those goods we called the lowest, or a fear of losing it.

For they are beautiful and comely, although, set beside the higher and beatifying goods, they are abject and lowly.

A man has committed murder. Why did he do it? He loved the man's wife, or his estate; or he wanted to rob him for a living; or he was afraid of losing some such thing to him; or, having been injured, he burned to avenge himself. Would he commit murder with no cause, taking delight in the murder itself? Who would believe it?

For even of that senseless and exceedingly cruel man of whom it was said that *he was evil and cruel gratuitously*, a cause was nonetheless given beforehand: *lest*, he says, *through idleness his hand or his mind should grow sluggish*[^30].

Ask that too: why so? So that, of course, by that exercise in crime, once the city was seized, he might attain honors, commands, and riches, and be free from the fear of the laws and from the difficulties he faced through the poverty of his estate and the consciousness of his crimes[^31].

So not even Catiline himself loved his own crimes, but surely something else, for the sake of which he was doing them.

### Chapter 6: All sins are in truth a perverse imitation of God's loftiness

**12**. What was it that I, wretched, loved in you, O theft of mine, O that nocturnal deed of mine in the sixteenth year of my age? For you were not beautiful, since you were a theft. Or are you anything at all, that I should speak to you?

Beautiful were the pears we stole, since they were your creation, O most beautiful of all, Creator of all, O good God[^32], God the highest good and my true good; beautiful were those pears, but it was not them that my wretched soul desired. For I had an abundance of better ones, and these I plucked only that I might steal. For once plucked I threw them away, having feasted there on iniquity alone, in which I rejoiced and took my pleasure. For even if some of those pears entered my mouth, what gave it savor was the crime.

And now, O Lord my God, I ask what it was that delighted me in the theft; and behold, it has no beauty—not such as is in justice and prudence, nor such as is in the mind of man, and in memory and the senses and the life that gives growth; nor such as the stars are, beautiful and lovely in their places, or the earth and sea, full of offspring that succeed by being born to those that pass away; not even such as is in some defective and shadowy beauty of the deceiving vices.

**13**. For pride too imitates loftiness, while you are the one *God most high* above all things[^33].

And what does ambition seek but honors and glory, while you are to be honored above all, the one who alone is glorious forever?

And the cruelty of the powerful wishes to be feared: but who is to be feared except the one God? From his power what can be snatched away or withdrawn—when, or where, or whither, or by whom?

And the caresses of the wanton wish to be loved: but nothing is more caressing than your love, nor is anything loved more wholesomely than your truth, beautiful and shining above all things.

And curiosity seems to court a zeal for knowledge, while you supremely know all things. Ignorance, too, and folly themselves are cloaked under the name of simplicity and innocence, because nothing is found simpler than you. And what is more innocent than you, since it is their own deeds that are the enemies of the wicked?

And sloth seems to long for rest: but what sure rest is there besides the Lord?

Self-indulgence likes to be called fullness and abundance: but you are fullness and the unfailing store of an incorruptible sweetness.

Prodigality casts the shadow of generosity: but you are the most lavish giver of all good things.

Avarice wants to possess much: and you possess all things.

Envy quarrels over excellence: what is more excellent than you?

Anger seeks revenge: who takes revenge more justly than you?

Fear shudders at the unaccustomed and the sudden—at things that threaten what is loved—as it takes precautions for its security: but to you what is unaccustomed? What is sudden? Or who separates from you what you love? Or where is there firm security except with you?

Sadness wastes away over things lost in which desire took its delight, because it would wish nothing to be taken from it, just as nothing can be taken from you.

**14**. So the soul commits fornication when it is turned away from you and seeks outside you the things it does not find pure and clear until it returns to you. All who put themselves far from you and lift themselves up against you imitate you perversely. But even by thus imitating you they show that you are the Creator of all nature, and that there is therefore nowhere one may withdraw from you altogether.

What, then, did I love in that theft, and in what did I imitate my Lord, even if only in a faulty and perverse way? Did it please me to act against the law, at least by deceit, since I could not by power—so that, a captive, I might mimic a maimed liberty by doing with impunity what was not allowed, a shadowy likeness of omnipotence? See, here is that servant fleeing his master and chasing a shadow[^34]. O rottenness, O monstrousness of life and abyss of death! Could what was not allowed be pleasing for no other reason than that it was not allowed?

### Chapter 7: As God had mercy, so let men have mercy on Augustine

**15**. *What shall I render to the Lord*[^35], that my memory recalls these things and my soul is not afraid of it? I will love you, Lord, and give you thanks, and confess to your name[^36], because you have forgiven me such great evils and wicked deeds. To your grace I ascribe it, and to your mercy, that you have melted my sins away like ice[^37]. To your grace I ascribe also whatever evils I did not do; for what was I not capable of doing, I who loved even a gratuitous crime? And I confess that all have been forgiven me—both the evils I did of my own accord and those that, with you for my guide, I did not do.

Who is there among men who, considering his own weakness, dares to credit his chastity and innocence to his own powers, so as to love you the less, as though he had had less need of your mercy, by which you forgive the sins of those who turn to you[^38]?

For let anyone who, called by you, has followed your voice and shunned the things he reads here as I recall and confess them of myself—let him not laugh at me for being healed, when sick, by the same physician through whose help it was granted to him not to fall sick, or rather to fall less sick; and for that reason let him love you just as much, indeed all the more, because through the one by whom he sees me delivered from such great languors of sin, he sees himself never entangled in such great languors of sin.

### Chapter 8: What he loved in the theft

**16**. *What fruit* did I, wretched, once have *in those things*[^39] that I now blush to recall—above all in that theft, in which I loved the theft itself and nothing else, though it too was nothing, and I the more wretched for it? And yet, alone, I would not have done it (such is my memory of my mind at the time)—alone I would by no means have done it. So there I loved also the fellowship of those with whom I did it. Then did I love nothing other than the theft? Yes, truly, nothing other—for that fellowship too is nothing.

What is the truth of it? Who will teach me, but the one who enlightens my heart[^40] and discerns its shadows? What is it that has come into my mind to ask and discuss and consider? For if I had at that time loved the pears I stole and desired to enjoy them, I could even alone have committed that iniquity—had it been enough—to arrive at my pleasure, and I would not have needed to inflame the itch of my desire by the rubbing together of guilty minds. But since the pleasure for me was not in those pears, it was in the crime itself, which the fellowship of fellow sinners produced.

### Chapter 9: The very fellowship of fellow sinners is pleasing to the sinner

**17**. What was that feeling of the soul? Surely it was utterly base, and woe to me[^41] who had it. But still, what was it? *Who understands his transgressions?*[^42] It was a laughter, as though the heart were tickled, that we were deceiving those who did not think we were doing these things and who vehemently did not want us to. Why, then, did it delight me that I did not do it alone? Is it because no one easily laughs alone? No one easily, indeed; yet all the same, laughter sometimes overcomes even men alone and by themselves, when no one else is present, if something exceedingly ridiculous meets their senses or their mind.

But that deed I would not have done alone—I would not have done it at all alone. Behold, before you, my God, is the living memory of my soul. Alone I would not have committed that theft, in which what pleased me was not what I stole but the fact that I stole; and to do it alone would have given me no pleasure at all, nor would I have done it.

O friendship too unfriendly, unfathomable seduction of the mind, greed to do harm out of sport and jest, an appetite for another's loss with no desire of gain for myself and no lust for revenge—but when it is said, "Let's go, let's do it," one is ashamed not to be shameless.

## He longs for innocence and rest in God

### Chapter 10

**18**. Who can unravel this most twisted and tangled knot? It is foul; I do not want to look at it, I do not want to see it. I want you, O justice and innocence, beautiful and comely to honorable eyes, and a satisfaction that never sates. With you there is rest indeed, and a life that cannot be troubled. Whoever enters into you enters *into the joy of his Lord*[^43], and will not fear, and will be most well in the Best. I flowed away from you and *went astray*[^44], my God, too far wandering from your stability in my youth, and I became to myself a land of want[^45].
