# Book 4: For Nine Years a Teacher at Thagaste and Carthage

## A follower among the initiates

### Chapter 1: They were oppressed by pride, superstition, and vanity

**1**. Through that same span of nine years, from my nineteenth year of age to my twenty-eighth, we were seduced and we seduced others, deceived and deceiving, amid various desires—openly through the teachings they call liberal, and secretly under the false name of religion: here proud, there superstitious, everywhere vain. On the one side pursuing the emptiness of popular glory, even to the applause of the theaters and contests in verse and the struggle for crowns of straw and the follies of the shows and the intemperance of lusts; on the other side seeking to be cleansed of these defilements by carrying food to those who were called the "elect" and "holy," from which, in the workshop of their belly, they might fashion for us angels and gods, through whom we should be set free. And these things I pursued and did, together with my friends, deceived through me and with me.

Let the arrogant laugh at me, those not yet wholesomely struck down and broken by you, my God; but let me confess to you my disgraces *to your praise*[^1]. Allow me, I beg, and grant me to range, with present memory, over the past windings of my error, and to offer you a sacrifice of jubilation[^2]. For what am I to myself without you, but a guide over a precipice? Or what am I, when all is well with me, but one suckling your milk[^3], or enjoying you, the food that does not perish[^4]? And what is any man, any man at all, seeing that he is but a man? Let the strong and the mighty laugh at us; but we, the weak and the needy[^5], let us confess to you.

## He publicly taught the art of rhetoric, and kept an unlawful wife at home

### Chapter 2

**2**. In those years I taught the art of rhetoric, and, overcome by greed, I sold a victorious loquacity. Yet I preferred, *Lord, you know*[^6], to have good pupils, as they are called good; and without guile I taught them their wiles—not the wiles they would use against the life of the innocent, but sometimes for the life of the guilty.

And, O God, from afar you saw me stumbling on slippery ground, and amid much smoke flickering[^7] a spark of the good faith which I showed, in that teaching, to those who loved vanity and sought a lie[^8]—I, their companion.

In those years I had one woman, known to me not in what is called lawful marriage, but one whom my wandering ardor, devoid of prudence, had tracked down—yet one only, keeping faith with her bed as well. With her I learned by my own example the difference between the measure of the marriage covenant, made for the sake of begetting, and the bargain of a lustful love, where offspring is born even against one's wish—though, once born, it compels itself to be loved.

#### He detested the art of the soothsayers

**3**. I recall, too, that when I had decided to enter a contest of theatrical verse, some soothsayer sent to ask me what I would pay him to win; but I, detesting and abhorring those foul rites, answered that not even if the crown were of immortal gold would I allow a fly to be killed for my victory. For he was going to kill living creatures in his sacrifices, and by those honors to invite the demons, as it seemed, to vote for me.

But this evil too I rejected not out of chastity toward you, *O God of my heart*[^9]. For I did not know how to love you, I who did not know how to conceive of anything but bodily splendors. And does not a soul, sighing after such figments, *commit fornication away from you*[^10], and *trust in falsehoods and feed the winds*[^11]? Yet I would not, of course, have sacrifices offered to demons for me—while by that very superstition I was sacrificing myself to them. For what is it to *feed the winds* but to feed the demons themselves, that is, by going astray to become their pleasure and their mockery?

## The astrologers stubbornly consulted

### Chapter 3

**4**. And so I did not cease openly to consult those impostors whom they call astrologers, on the grounds that they used, as it were, no sacrifice and directed no prayers to any spirit for the sake of divination. Yet true Christian piety consistently rejects and condemns this.

For *it is good* to confess to you, Lord[^12], and to say, *Have mercy on me; heal my soul, for I have sinned against you*[^13], and not to abuse your indulgence as a license to sin, but to remember the Lord's words: *Behold, you have been made whole; sin no more, lest something worse befall you*[^14]. All this wholesome teaching they try to kill when they say, "The cause of your sinning is fixed for you from heaven," and, "Venus did this, or Saturn, or Mars"—so that man, *flesh and blood*[^15] and proud rottenness, may be without blame, while the blame falls instead on the Creator and orderer of heaven and the stars. And who is this but our God, the sweetness and source of justice, who *will render to each according to his works*[^16], and who *do not despise a contrite and humbled heart*[^17]?

#### Vindicianus and Nebridius try in vain to dissuade him from consulting astrologers

**5**. There was at that time a shrewd man, most skilled in the art of medicine and most renowned in it, who as proconsul had with his own hand placed that contest-crown on my unsound head—but not as a physician. For of that disease the healer is you, who *resist the proud, but give grace to the humble*[^18].

And yet, even through that old man, did you fail me, or cease to heal my soul? For when I had become more familiar with him, and clung steadily and fixedly to his conversations (for they were pleasant and weighty, lively in their thoughts though without ornament of words), and he learned from my talk that I was devoted to the books of the horoscope-casters,

he warned me, kindly and as a father, to throw them away, and not to waste on that vanity the care and effort needed for useful things—saying that he himself had so learned that art that in his early years he had wanted to make its profession the means by which he would earn his living; and if he had understood Hippocrates, he could surely have understood those writings too;

and yet, he said, he had afterward abandoned them and taken up medicine for no other reason than that he had found them utterly false, and, being a serious man, was unwilling to seek his living by deceiving people. "But you," he said, "who have rhetoric to support yourself among men, pursue this deception by free choice, not from any need of household means. All the more, then, ought you to believe me about it, who labored to learn it as perfectly as I wished to live by it alone."

When I asked him what, then, was the reason why many true things were foretold from it, he answered, as best he could, that the force of chance, diffused everywhere throughout the nature of things, brought this about.

For if, when someone happens to consult the pages of some poet who is singing and intending something far different, a verse often comes out wonderfully in tune with the matter at hand, it was no wonder, he said, if from the human soul, by some higher instinct not knowing what was happening within it, there should sound—not by art but by chance—something that chimed with the affairs and deeds of the inquirer.

**6**. And this, indeed, you procured for me from him, or through him, and you sketched in my memory what I should afterward search out for myself. But at that time neither he, nor my dearest Nebridius—a young man very good and very chaste, who mocked that whole kind of divination—could persuade me to throw these things away, since the authority of their authors moved me the more, and I had not yet found any certain proof, such as I sought, by which it might appear to me without ambiguity that the true things said by those consulted were said by luck or chance, and not by the art of inspecting the stars.

## The death of a most dear friend

### Chapter 4: He had won for himself a too-dear friend

**7**. In those years, when I first began to teach in the town where I was born, I had won a friend, too dear to me through a shared zeal for studies—my own age, and like me in the flower of youth. As a boy he had grown up with me, and we had gone to school together and played together. But he was not yet a friend in this way—nor even then in the way true friendship is—for friendship is not true unless you bind it fast between those who cleave to you by the love poured out *in our hearts through the Holy Spirit, who is given to us*[^19].

Yet it was sweet to us, ripened in the fervor of like studies. For I had bent him away from the true faith—which, as a youth, he did not hold genuinely and deeply—toward those superstitious and ruinous fables on account of which my mother wept for me. Already that man was wandering in mind with me, and my soul could not be without him.

And behold, you, pressing close on the back of your fugitives, *God of vengeance*[^20] and fountain of mercies at once, who turn us to yourself in wondrous ways[^21]—behold, you took that man out of this life when he had scarcely completed a year in my friendship, a friendship sweeter to me than all the sweetnesses of that life of mine.

#### The friend's death

**8**. Who can recount your praises[^22] for what he has experienced in his own single self? What did you do then, my God, and how unsearchable is the abyss of your judgments[^23]?

For when he was struggling with fevers, he lay a long time senseless in a deathly sweat; and when he was despaired of, he was baptized without knowing it—while I cared nothing, presuming that his soul would retain rather what it had received from me than what was done in his unconscious body.

But it was far otherwise. For he recovered and was made well; and at once, as soon as I could speak with him (and I could as soon as he could, since I did not leave his side, and we hung too much on each other), I tried to joke with him, as though he too would join me in mocking the baptism he had received while utterly absent in mind and sense. But he had by now learned that he had received it. And he shuddered at me as at an enemy, and with a wonderful and sudden freedom warned me that, if I wished to be his friend, I should stop saying such things to him.

But I, dumbfounded and disturbed, put off all my feelings until he should first grow strong and be fit in health and vigor, so that I might deal with him as I wished. But he was snatched away from my madness, to be kept safe with you for my consolation: a few days later, in my absence, the fevers returned, and he died.

#### Augustine was consumed with grief

**9**. By that grief my heart was darkened[^24], and whatever I looked upon was death. My homeland was a torment to me, and my father's house a strange unhappiness; and whatever I had shared with him had turned, without him, into a monstrous torture. My eyes sought him everywhere, and he was not given to them; and I hated all things, because they did not hold him, nor could they now say to me, "Look, he is coming," as they did when he was alive and absent. I had become a great riddle to myself, and I questioned my soul, why it was sad and why it troubled me so greatly, and it had nothing to answer me. And if I said, "Hope in God,"[^25] it rightly did not obey, because the man it had lost, most dear, was truer and better than the phantasm in which it was bidden to hope. Weeping alone was sweet to me, and it succeeded my friend in the delights of my soul[^26].

### Chapter 5: His grief was wondrously soothed by tears

**10**. And now, Lord, those things have passed, and with time my wound has been soothed. May I hear from you, who are the truth[^27], and put the ear of my heart to your mouth, that you may tell me why weeping is sweet to the wretched? Have you, though present everywhere, cast our misery far from you—do you abide in yourself, while we are tossed about in trials[^28]? And yet, unless we wept into your ears, nothing would remain of our hope.

Whence, then, is the sweet fruit gathered from the bitterness of life—to groan and weep and sigh and complain? Is this what is sweet in it, that we hope you hear us? This is rightly so in prayers, for they have a longing to reach you. But was it so in the grief over a thing lost, and the mourning in which I was then wrapped? For I did not hope he would come back to life, nor did I beg this with my tears; I only grieved and wept. For I was wretched, and I had lost my joy. Or is weeping itself a bitter thing, and does it please us only out of a distaste for the things we once enjoyed, when we now shrink from them?

### Chapter 6: Weariness of life and the fear of death

**11**. But why do I speak of these things? For now is not the time to ask questions, but to confess to you. I was wretched; and wretched is every soul bound by friendship for mortal things, and torn apart when it loses them, and then it feels the misery by which it is wretched even before it loses them. Such was I at that time, and I wept most bitterly and found rest in bitterness[^29].

So wretched was I that I held that wretched life itself dearer than my friend. For though I wished to change it, I would yet have been less willing to lose it than to lose him; and I do not know whether I would even have been willing to lose it for him, as is told—if it is not invented—of Orestes and Pylades, who wished to die for each other, or both together, since for them not to live together was worse than death.

But in me there had arisen some feeling quite contrary to this, and there was in me a most grievous weariness of living and a fear of dying. I believe that the more I loved him, the more I hated and feared, as a most cruel enemy, the death that had taken him from me; and I thought it would suddenly consume all men, since it had been able to take him. Such, I remember, was my whole condition.

Behold my heart, my God, behold within it; see, for I remember, *my hope*[^30], you who cleanse me from the uncleanness of such affections, directing my eyes to you and plucking *my feet out of the snare*[^31].

For I wondered that other mortals lived, since he, whom I had loved as though he would never die, was dead; and I wondered the more that I, who was a second self to him, lived while he was dead. Well did someone say of his friend that he was "the half of his soul"[^32]. For I felt that my soul and his soul had been one soul in two bodies; and therefore life was a horror to me, because I did not want to live as a half; and perhaps it was for this that I feared to die—lest he whom I had loved so dearly should wholly die.

### Chapter 7: Seeking solace, he came from the town of Thagaste to Carthage

**12**. O madness that knows not how to love human beings as human beings! O foolish man, then bearing the human lot without measure! Such was I then. And so I seethed, I sighed, I wept, I was troubled; there was no rest, no counsel. For I bore about a soul cut to pieces and bloodied, impatient of being borne by me, and I found nowhere to lay it down. Not in pleasant groves, not in games and songs, nor in sweet-smelling places, nor in lavish banquets, nor in the pleasure of couch and bed, nor, finally, in books and poems did it find rest. All things were a horror, even the light itself; and whatever was not what he was, was hateful and odious, except groaning and tears—for in these alone was there a little rest.

But when my soul was drawn away from these, it loaded me with a great burden of misery. To you, Lord, it should have been lifted up[^33] to be healed, I knew; but I was neither willing nor able, all the more because, when I thought of you, you were to me nothing solid and firm.

For it was not you, but an empty phantasm, and my own error was my god. If I tried to set my soul down there, to rest, it slipped through the void and fell back again upon me; and I remained to myself an unhappy place, where I could neither stay nor depart. For where could my heart flee from my heart? Where could I flee from myself? Where would I not follow myself?

And yet I fled from my homeland[^34]. For my eyes sought him less where they were not used to seeing him; and from the town of Thagaste I came to Carthage.

## What followed at Carthage; the consolations of other friends restored him

### Chapter 8

**13**. Times are not empty, nor do they roll idly through our senses: they work wondrous works in the mind. Behold, they came and went *from day to day*[^35], and by coming and going they sowed in me other hopes and other memories, and little by little they patched me up with former kinds of delights, before which that grief of mine gave way; yet there succeeded it not indeed other griefs, but the causes of other griefs. For whence had that grief penetrated me so easily and so deeply, except that I had poured out my soul upon the sand[^36] by loving a man bound to die as though he would not die? What restored and revived me most were the consolations of other friends, with whom I loved what I loved in place of you; and this was a vast fable and a long lie, by whose adulterous rubbing our mind, itching in its ears[^37], was corrupted. But that fable did not die in me, if one of my friends died.

There were other things in them that more fully captured my mind: to converse and laugh together and do kindnesses for one another in turn; to read sweet-spoken books together; to jest together and to show one another respect; to disagree at times without ill will, as a man does with himself, and by that rarest of disagreements to season our many agreements;

to teach one another something or learn from one another; to long for the absent with vexation, and to welcome the coming with joy. By these and like signs, proceeding from the heart of those who love and are loved in return, through the mouth, the tongue, the eyes, and a thousand most pleasing gestures, our minds were fused as if by kindling, and out of many were made one.

### Chapter 9: Blessed is he who loves God

**14**. This is what is loved in friends, and so loved that a man's conscience holds itself guilty if he does not love one who loves him, or does not love in return one who loves him, seeking nothing from his body beyond the signs of goodwill.

Hence that mourning if anyone dies, and the darkness of sorrows, and the heart drenched, its sweetness turned to bitterness, and, from the lost life of the dying, the death of the living.

Blessed is he who loves you, and his friend in you, and his enemy for your sake[^38]. For he alone loses no one dear, to whom all are dear in the One who is not lost. And who is this but our God, *the God who made heaven and earth*[^39] and fills them[^40]—for by filling them he made them?

No one loses you, except one who forsakes you; and because he forsakes you, where does he go, or where does he flee[^41], but from you the appeased to you the angered? For where does he not find your law in his own punishment? And *your law is truth*[^42], and *you are the Truth*[^43].

## All human things are fleeting

### Chapter 10

**15**. *God of hosts, turn us, and show us your face, and we shall be saved*[^44]. For wherever the soul of man turns, it is fixed upon sorrows anywhere but in you, even though it is fixed on beautiful things outside you and outside itself. Yet these would be nothing, were they not from you. They rise and set, and by rising they begin, as it were, to be, and they grow that they may reach perfection, and once perfected they grow old and perish: and not all grow old, but all perish.

So when they rise and tend toward being, the more quickly they grow toward being, the more they hasten toward not being. Such is their measure. So much have you given them, because they are parts of things that do not all exist at once, but by passing away and succeeding they all enact the whole of which they are parts. So too is our speech accomplished, through sounding signs. For the speech would not be whole unless one word passed away, once it had sounded its parts, so that another might succeed it.

> Let my soul praise you for these things[^45],  
> *O God, Creator of all*[^46],  
> but let it not be stuck fast in them  
> by the glue of love,  
> through the senses of the body.  
> For they go where they were going[^47],  
> that they may be no more,  
> and they tear it apart  
> with pestilent desires,  
> since the soul wishes to be  
> and loves to rest in the things it loves.  
> But in them there is no place to rest,  
> because they do not stand still;  
> they flee,  
> and who can follow them with the sense of the flesh?  
> Or who can grasp them,  
> even when they are at hand?  
> For the sense of the flesh is slow,  
> because it is the sense of the flesh:  
> that is its measure.  
> It suffices for another thing,  
> for which it was made,  
> but for this it does not suffice:  
> to hold the things that run past  
> from their due beginning  
> to their due end.  
> For in your Word,  
> through which they are created,  
> they hear:  
> "From here, and to here."[^48]

### Chapter 11: God alone abides forever

**16**. Do not be vain, my soul, and do not let the ear of your heart grow deaf with the din of your vanity. Hear, you also: the Word himself cries out to you to return, and there is the place of untroubled rest, where love is not forsaken, if it does not itself forsake. Behold, those things depart, that others may succeed them, and the lowest universe is made up of all its parts.

"Do I depart anywhere?" says the Word of God. There fix your dwelling[^49]; there entrust whatever you have from there, my soul, weary at least of deceits. Entrust to the Truth whatever you have from the Truth, and you will lose nothing; and your rotting parts will flower again, and all your sicknesses will be healed, and your fleeting things will be reshaped and renewed[^50] and bound fast to you, and they will not set you down where they descend, but will stand with you and abide before God, who stands and abides forever[^51].

**17**. Why do you walk perversely after your flesh? Let it rather follow you, now that you are converted. Whatever you perceive through it is in part, and you do not know the whole of which these are parts, and yet they delight you. But if the sense of your flesh were fit to grasp the whole, and had not itself, as part of your punishment, received a just measure within a part of the universe, you would wish that whatever exists in the present should pass away, so that the whole might please you more. For what we say, too, you hear through the same sense of the flesh, and you certainly do not want the syllables to stand still, but to fly past, so that others may come and you may hear the whole.

So always, with all things of which some one thing is composed (and the things of which it is composed do not all exist at once), the whole gives more delight than the single parts, if all can be perceived. But far better than these is he who made all things, and *he is our God*[^52], and he does not depart, because nothing succeeds him.

### Chapter 12: Whoever seeks the happy life, let him draw near to God

**18**. If bodies please you, praise God for them[^53], and turn your love back upon their craftsman, lest in the things that please you, you yourself displease.

If souls please you, let them be loved in God; for they too are changeable, and only when fixed in him are they made stable—otherwise they would go and perish. In him, then, let them be loved, and carry off to him with you whatever souls you can, and say to them:

> "Let us love him, let us love him: he made these things[^54], and he is not far off"[^55]. For he did not make them and depart, but they are from him and in him. Behold where he is: where truth is savored. He is most inward to the heart, but the heart has wandered from him[^56].

> *Return, you transgressors, to your heart*[^57], and cleave to him who made you. Stand with him, and you will stand; rest in him, and you will be at rest. Where do you go[^58], into rough places? Where do you go? The good that you love is from him; but only insofar as it is referred to him is it good and sweet. Rightly will it turn bitter, because whatever is from him is unjustly loved if he is forsaken. Why, again and again, do you walk hard and toilsome ways[^59]? There is no rest where you seek it. Seek what you seek, but it is not there where you seek it. You seek a happy life in the region of death[^60]; it is not there. For how can there be a happy life where there is not even life?

> **19**. And our very Life[^61] came down here, and bore our death, and slew it[^62] out of the abundance of his own life; and he thundered, crying out that we should return from here to him, into that secret place from which he came forth to us—into that first virginal womb, where the human creature was wedded to him, mortal flesh, that it might not be forever mortal; and from there, *like a bridegroom coming forth from his chamber, he rejoiced like a giant to run his course*[^63].

> For he did not delay, but ran, crying out by words, deeds, death, life, descent, ascent[^64]—crying out that we should return to him. And he departed from our eyes[^65], that we might return to our heart and find him.

> For he departed, and *behold, he is here*[^66]. He would not be with us long, and he did not leave us. For he departed to that place from which he never withdrew, because *the world was made through him, and he was in this world*[^67], and *he came into this world to save sinners*[^68]. To him my soul confesses, and he heals it, because it has sinned against him[^69]. *O sons of men, how long will you be heavy of heart?*[^70] Even after Life has come down, do you not wish to ascend and live? But where will you ascend, when you are on high and have set your mouth against heaven[^71]? Come down, that you may ascend, and ascend to God. For you fell by ascending against God.

Tell them these things, that they may weep *in the valley of weeping*[^72], and so carry them off with you to God, because you say these things to them by his Spirit, if you say them burning with the fire of love.

## On the beautiful and the fitting; he wrote some books on the beautiful and the fitting

### Chapter 13

**20**. These things I did not then know, and I loved the lower beauties, and I was going down into the depths[^73], and I would say to my friends: "Do we love anything but the beautiful? What, then, is the beautiful? And what is beauty? What is it that allures us and binds us to the things we love? For unless there were in them a comeliness and a form, they would in no way move us toward themselves."

And I noticed and saw that in bodies themselves there is one thing that is, as it were, a whole, and therefore beautiful, and another that is becoming because it is aptly fitted to something else, as a part of the body to its whole, or a shoe to the foot, and the like. And this consideration welled up in my mind from the depths of my heart, and I wrote books *On the Beautiful and the Fitting*—two, I think, or three; you know, O God[^74], for it has slipped from me. For we do not have them; they have strayed from us, I know not how.

### Chapter 14: He wrote the books on the beautiful and the fitting to the orator Hierius

**21**. But what was it that moved me, O Lord my God, to write those books to Hierius, an orator of the city of Rome? Him I did not know by face, but I had come to love the man for the fame of his learning, which was bright; and I had heard some words of his, and they had pleased me.

But he pleased me more because he pleased others, and they extolled him with praises, astonished that from a Syrian, first taught in Greek eloquence, there had afterward come forth a wonderful speaker in Latin too, and one most knowledgeable in matters pertaining to the study of wisdom.

A man is praised and loved while absent. Does that love enter the heart of the hearer from the mouth of the one who praises? Far from it; rather, one lover is kindled by another.

For from this is one loved who is praised, when he is believed to be extolled by the heart of the praiser without deceit—that is, when one who loves him praises him.

**22**. For so I then loved men by the judgment of men, not by yours, my God, in whom no one is deceived.

Yet why was it not as one loves a famous charioteer, or a hunter renowned in popular favor, but far otherwise, and gravely, and just as I would wish to be praised myself?

For I would not wish to be praised and loved as actors are—though I myself praised and loved them—but I would choose to be hidden rather than so known, and even to be held in hatred rather than so loved.

Where are these weights of various and diverse loves distributed in one soul? What is it that I love in another, which, again, unless I hated it, I would not detest and repel from myself, since both of us are men? For it is not as a good horse is loved by one who would not want to be a horse, even if he could—the same cannot be said of an actor, who shares our nature. Do I, then, love in a man what I hate to be, since I am a man? Man himself is a great deep, whose very hairs you, Lord, have numbered[^75], and they are not lessened in you; and yet his hairs are more easily numbered than his affections and the movements of his heart.

**23**. But that rhetorician was of the kind I so loved that I wished to be such myself; and I went astray through conceit and was carried about by every wind[^76], and was steered by you most secretly. And how do I know, and how do I confess to you with certainty, that I had loved him more in the love of those who praised him than in the very things for which he was praised?

Because if those same people, instead of praising him, were to disparage him, and were to tell of those very same things while disparaging and scorning them, I would not have been kindled and roused over him—and surely the things would be no different, nor the man himself other, but only the feeling of those who told of them. See where the weak soul lies, not yet cleaving to the solidity of truth. Just as the breezes of tongues blow from the breasts of those who opine, so it is carried and turned, twisted and twisted back; and the light is clouded over for it, and the truth is not discerned. And behold, it is before us.

And it was a great thing to me if my discourse and my studies should become known to that man; for if he approved them, I would blaze the more, but if he disapproved, my empty heart, void of your solidity, would be wounded. And yet that "beautiful and fitting," about which I had written to him, I gladly turned over in my mind before the face of my contemplation, and marveled at it with no one to praise it with me.

### Chapter 15: How Augustine then judged the beautiful and the fitting

**24**. But I did not yet see the hinge of so great a matter in your art, O almighty, *who alone do wondrous things*[^77]; and my mind moved among bodily forms, and I defined and distinguished "the beautiful" as that which is so in itself, and "the fitting" as that which is becoming through being accommodated to something else, and I built this up with bodily examples. And I turned to the nature of the soul, but the false opinion I held about spiritual things would not let me discern the truth.

And the very force of the truth struck my eyes, yet I turned my quivering mind away from the incorporeal thing toward lines and colors and swelling magnitudes; and because I could not see these in the soul, I thought I could not see the soul.

And whereas in virtue I loved peace, and in viciousness I hated discord, in the one I noted a unity, in the other a kind of division; and in that unity the rational mind seemed to me to consist, and the nature of truth and of the highest good; while in that division I, wretched, supposed there was some substance of irrational life and the nature of the highest evil—which would not only be a substance, but would altogether be life, and yet would not be from you, my God, *from whom are all things*[^78].

And the former I called a *monad*, as a mind without any sex, and the latter a *dyad*—anger in crimes, lust in disgraceful deeds—not knowing what I was saying. For I had neither known nor learned that evil is no substance, nor that our mind itself is not the highest and unchangeable good.

#### Vanity, error, and the weight of pride

**25**. For just as crimes occur if that movement of the soul in which there is impulse is vicious, and flings itself about insolently and turbulently; and disgraceful deeds occur if that affection of the soul by which carnal pleasures are drained is immoderate—so do errors and false opinions contaminate life, if the rational mind itself is vicious. Such was it in me then, when I did not know that it must be lit by another light to be a partaker of truth, since it is not itself the nature of truth: *for you will light my lamp, O Lord; my God, you will lighten my darkness*[^79], and *of your fullness we have all received*[^80]. For you are *the true light that enlightens every man coming into this world*[^81], because in you *there is no change, nor shadow of alteration*[^82].

**26**. But I strove toward you, and was repelled by you[^83], that I might taste death[^84], because *you resist the proud*[^85]. And what is prouder than that I should assert, with astonishing madness, that I was by nature what you are? For whereas I was changeable—and this was plain to me, since I desired to be wise precisely in order to become better from worse—I yet preferred to think you also changeable, rather than that I was not what you are.

And so I was repelled, and you resisted my windy neck, and I imagined bodily forms; and, flesh myself, I accused the flesh; and, *a wind that goes and does not yet return*[^86], I did not return to you, but in my going I walked among things that are not—neither in you, nor in me, nor in body—things not created for me by your truth, but feigned by my vanity out of body. And I would say to your little faithful ones, my fellow citizens, from whom I was unknowingly an exile, I would say to them, chattering and foolish: "Why, then, does the soul err, which God made?"

But I was unwilling to be told: "Why, then, does God err?" And I contended rather that your unchangeable substance erred under compulsion, than confessed that my own changeable substance had gone astray of its own accord, and erred as its punishment.

**27**. And I was perhaps twenty-six or twenty-seven years of age when I wrote those volumes, turning over within me bodily figments that clamored in the ears of my heart—which I was straining, O sweet truth, toward your inner melody, as I thought about the beautiful and the fitting, and longed to stand and hear you and *rejoice with joy at the bridegroom's voice*[^87]; and I could not, because I was dragged outside by the voices of my error, and by the weight of my pride I fell into the depths. For you did not give *to my hearing joy and gladness*, nor did *the bones exult* that had not been *humbled*[^88].

## He read Aristotle's Ten Categories on his own

### Chapter 16

**28**. And what did it profit me that, at about the age of twenty, when there came into my hands a certain work of Aristotle's which they call the *Ten Categories* (at whose very name the Carthaginian rhetorician, my teacher, and others who were held to be learned, would puff out their cheeks with conceit, so that I hung agape as if upon some great and divine thing), I read it on my own and understood it?

When I compared it with those who said that they had scarcely understood it even with the most learned masters—not merely speaking, but drawing many things in the dust—they could tell me nothing about it other than what I had come to know by reading it on my own;

and the categories seemed to me to speak clearly enough about substances, such as a man is, and about what is in them—such as the figure of a man, what sort it is, and his stature, how many feet tall he is; and kinship, whose brother he is; or where he is placed, or when he was born; whether he stands or sits; whether he is shod or armed; or whether he is doing something or having something done to him—and all the countless things that are found in these nine classes, of which I have given some examples, or in the class of substance itself.

**29**. What did this profit me, when it even did me harm—when, thinking that whatever exists at all was comprehended under those ten predicaments, I tried to understand you also, my God, wonderfully simple and unchangeable, as if you too were a subject of your greatness or beauty, so that these were in you as in a subject, as in a body—whereas your greatness and your beauty are you yourself, while a body is not great and beautiful by the fact that it is a body, since, even if it were less great and less beautiful, it would nonetheless be a body?

For it was falsehood that I thought about you, not truth—figments of my misery, not the firm supports of your blessedness. For you had commanded, and so it was done in me, that the earth should bring forth thorns and thistles for me, and that with toil I should come to my bread[^89].

#### He read all the books of the liberal arts by himself

**30**. And what did it profit me that I—then the most worthless slave of evil desires—read by myself and understood all the books of the arts they call liberal, whatever I was able to read? And I took joy in them, and did not know whence came whatever was true and certain in them. For I had my back to the light, and my face toward the things that are lit; and so my face itself, by which I saw the things lit, was not lit. Whatever I understood, without great difficulty and with no man to teach me, of the art of speaking and reasoning, of the dimensions of figures, of music, and of numbers—*you know, O Lord my God*[^90]—for both quickness of understanding and keenness of discernment are your gift.

But from them I offered you no sacrifice[^91]. And so they availed me not for use but rather for ruin, since I was at pains to have so good a part of my substance in my own power, and did not keep my strength for you[^92], but set out away from you into a far country, to squander it on harlot desires[^93]. For what did a good thing profit me, when I did not use it well? For I did not perceive that those arts are understood with great difficulty even by the studious and able, until I tried to expound them to such people, and the most excellent among them was reckoned the one who could follow me, as I expounded, no more slowly than the rest.

#### Talent and learning profit nothing when we have withdrawn far from God

**31**. But what did this profit me, when I thought that you, Lord God, the Truth, were a luminous and immense body, and I a fragment of that body? Excessive perversity! But such was I; nor do I blush, my God, to confess to you your mercies upon me[^94], and to call upon you—I who did not blush then to profess my blasphemies to men, and to bark against you[^95].

What, then, did it profit me at that time that my mind, nimble through those disciplines, unraveled so many most knotty books with no aid of human teaching, while in the teaching of piety I was erring with a base and sacrilegious foulness?

Or how did it harm your little ones to have a far slower mind, since they did not withdraw far from you, so that in the nest of your Church they might safely grow their feathers and nourish the wings of love with the food of a sound faith[^96]?

> O Lord our God,  
> let us hope *under the covering of your wings*,  
> and *protect us*[^97], and carry us.  
> You will carry us, both the little ones  
> and even to gray hairs  
> you will carry us[^98];  
> for when our firmness is you,  
> then it is firmness,  
> but when it is our own,  
> it is infirmity.  
> Our good lives with you always,  
> and because we have turned away from it,  
> we have become perverse.

> Let us return now, Lord,  
> that we may not be overturned,  
> for our good lives with you without any defect—  
> the good that is you yourself[^99]—  
> and we do not fear that there will be no place to return to,  
> because we ourselves fell from it;  
> but in our absence our home does not fall:  
> your eternity.
