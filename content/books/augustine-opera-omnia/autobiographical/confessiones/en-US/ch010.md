# Book 9: Baptism and the Return to Africa

## In the Retreat at Cassiciacum

### Chapter 1: Thanks and praises to God

**1**. *O Lord, I am your servant; I am your servant and the son of your handmaid. You have burst my bonds; I will sacrifice to you a sacrifice of praise*[^1]. Let my heart and my tongue praise you[^2], and let *all my bones* say: *Lord, who is like you?*[^3] Let them say, and answer me, *and say to my soul: I am your salvation*[^4].

Who was I, and what kind of man? What evil was there not, either in my deeds, or, if not in deeds, in my words, or, if not in words, in my will? But you, Lord, good and merciful[^5], your right hand looking upon the depth of my death and draining out from the bottom of my heart the abyss of corruption. And this was the whole of it: to be unwilling for what I willed, and to will what you willed[^6]. But where, in all that long time, and from what deep and hidden recess, was my free choice called forth in a moment, by which I should bow my neck to your gentle yoke and my shoulders to your light burden[^7], *Christ Jesus, my helper and my redeemer*[^8]?

How sweet it suddenly became to me to be without the sweetnesses of those trifles—and what I had feared to lose, it was now joy to dismiss. For you cast them out from me, you, the true and supreme sweetness; you cast them out, and entered in their place, sweeter than every pleasure, but not to *flesh and blood*[^9]; brighter than every light, but more inward than every secret; loftier than every honor, but not to those who are lofty in their own eyes. Now my mind was free from the gnawing cares of ambition and acquiring and wallowing and scratching the itch of lusts; and I prattled to you, my brightness and my riches and my salvation, the Lord my God.

## The waiting for the vacation

### Chapter 2

**2**. *And it pleased me in your sight*[^10], not to tear away the service of my tongue tumultuously from the marketplace of loquacity, but to withdraw it gently, so that the boys—*meditating* not on *your law*[^11], not on your peace, but on lying follies[^12] and the wars of the forum—should no longer buy from my mouth weapons for their fury. And conveniently, very few days now remained before the vintage vacation, and I resolved to bear with them, that I might depart in due form, and, redeemed by you, no longer return for sale.

Our plan, then, was before you; but before men, except our own, it was not; and it had been agreed among us that it should not be poured out to anyone at large—though you had given us, as we ascended from the valley of weeping[^13] and sang the song of degrees[^14], *sharp arrows and devastating coals against the deceitful tongue*[^15], which, as if counseling, contradicts, and, as it is wont with food, by loving consumes.

**3**. You had shot our heart with your love[^16], and we carried your words fixed in our vitals; and the examples of your servants, whom you had made shining from black and living from dead, heaped together in the bosom of our thought, burned and consumed our heavy torpor, lest we sink to the depths; and they kindled us so strongly that every blast of contradiction *from the deceitful tongue*[^17] could but inflame us the more sharply, not extinguish us.

Yet, because for the sake of your name, which you have sanctified throughout the lands[^18], our vow and purpose would surely have its praisers too, it seemed like boastfulness not to wait for the vacation time, now so near, but to depart beforehand from a public profession set before the eyes of all—so that, with the eyes of all who looked turning to my deed, they would say much about how I had wished to anticipate the vintage day so near at hand, as if I had sought to seem great.

And what was it to me that men should think and dispute about my mind, and that *our good should be blasphemed*[^19]?

#### Augustine was then afflicted with a pain in the chest

**4**. Moreover, that very summer my lungs had begun to give way under the excessive labor of literary work, and to draw breath with difficulty, and by pains in the chest to testify that they were injured, and to refuse a clearer or more sustained voice. This had at first disturbed me, because it almost compelled me, of necessity, to lay down the burden of that teaching, or, if I could be cured and recover, at least to interrupt it.

But when a full will arose in me and was strengthened, to be at leisure and to see *that you are the Lord*[^20] (you know it, my God), I even began to rejoice that there was at hand this excuse too, no lying one, which might temper the offense of men who, for their children's sake, never wished me to be free.

Full, therefore, of such joy, I bore that interval of time until it should run out (I do not know whether there were even twenty days); yet they were borne bravely, because the greed that used to bear with me the heavy business had departed, and I would have remained to be crushed, had not patience taken its place.

Perhaps some servant of yours, my brothers, will say that I sinned in this, that, with a now full heart for your service, I allowed myself to sit even one hour in the chair of lying[^21]. But I do not contend. Yet you, most merciful Lord, did you not forgive and remit this sin too, along with the rest, horrible and deadly, in the holy water?

### Chapter 3: The anxiety of Verecundus

**5**. Verecundus was worn with anxiety over this good of ours, because, on account of his own bonds, by which he was most tightly held, he saw himself deserted by our fellowship. Not yet a Christian, with a faithful wife, he was yet held back by her—that very tie tighter than the rest—from the journey we had undertaken; and he said he did not wish to be a Christian in any other way than that in which he could not be. Kindly, however, he offered that, as long as we were there, we should be on his estate. You will repay him, Lord, *in the resurrection of the just*[^22], since you have already repaid him with the lot itself[^23]. For although, in our absence, when we were now at Rome, he was seized with bodily sickness, and in it was made a Christian and one of the faithful, and departed from this life.

So you had mercy not only on him but also on us, lest, thinking of our friend's outstanding kindness toward us and not numbering him in your flock, we should be tortured with intolerable grief. *Thanks be to you, our God*[^24]! We are yours. Your exhortations and consolations declare it:

faithful promiser, you will repay Verecundus, for that estate of his at Cassiciacum, where we rested from the fever of the world in you, with the loveliness of your eternally green paradise, since you forgave him his sins upon the earth[^25], on the curded mountain, your mountain, the fertile mountain[^26].

#### Nebridius too, a little later, becomes one of the faithful

**6**. So at that time he himself was in anguish, but Nebridius rejoiced with us. For although he too, not yet a Christian, had fallen into that pit of most pernicious error[^27], so as to believe the flesh of your Son, the Truth[^28], a phantasm—yet, emerging from it, so he was: not yet steeped in any sacraments of your Church, but a most ardent inquirer after truth.

Not long after our conversion and regeneration through your baptism, him too—now a faithful catholic, serving you in perfect chastity and continence in Africa among his own, when his whole household had been made Christian through him—you loosed from the flesh. And now he lives in the bosom of Abraham[^29]. Whatever that is which is signified by that bosom, there my Nebridius lives, my sweet friend, but your son, Lord, adopted from a freedman: there he lives.

For what other place is there for such a soul? There he lives, about which he used to ask me many things—me, an inexperienced little man. Now he no longer puts his ear to my mouth, but his spiritual mouth to your fountain, and drinks, as much as he can, wisdom according to his eagerness, happy without end. Nor do I think he is so intoxicated by it as to forget me, since you, Lord, whom he drinks, are mindful of us[^30].

So, then, we were—consoling Verecundus, sad (with friendship safe) over such a conversion of ours, and exhorting him to faith of his own degree, namely, of married life; but awaiting Nebridius, when he would follow. And this he could do from so near, and was now all but about to do, when, behold, those days at last rolled away. For they had seemed long and many, before the love of free leisure, to sing from all my marrow: *To you my heart has said, I have sought your face; your face, Lord, will I seek*[^31].

## What Augustine did in his writings while staying at the country estate of Cassiciacum

### Chapter 4

**7**. And the day came when I was in fact released from the profession of rhetoric, from which in thought I was already released. And so it was done: you rescued my tongue, from which you had already rescued my heart, and I blessed you, rejoicing as I set out for the country estate with all my own.

What I did there in my writings—now indeed serving you, yet still, as if in a pause, panting from the school of pride—is attested by the books of the discussions held with those present and with myself alone before you; and what passed with Nebridius in his absence is attested by my letters.

And when would there be time enough to recall all your great benefits toward us in that time, especially as I hasten on to greater matters? For my memory calls me back, and it becomes sweet to me, Lord, to confess to you by what inward goads you subdued me, and how you leveled me low—the mountains and hills of my thoughts—and made my crooked ways straight and my rough ways smooth[^32]; and in what way you also brought Alypius himself, the brother of my heart, into subjection to the name of your Only-begotten, *our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ*[^33], which at first he disdained to have inserted in our writings. For he wanted them rather to be redolent of the cedars of the schools, which the Lord has now broken[^34], than of the wholesome herbs of the Church, effective against serpents.

## He reads the Psalms of David

**8**. What cries I uttered to you, my God, when I read the Psalms of David, those songs of faith, those sounds of piety that shut out the swollen spirit—I, untrained in your genuine love, a catechumen on holiday at the country estate with the catechumen Alypius, my mother clinging to us in a woman's dress but with a man's faith, an old woman's serenity, a mother's love, a Christian's devotion! What cries I uttered to you in those Psalms, and how I was set aflame for you by them, and burned to recite them, if I could, throughout the whole world against the pride of the human race! And yet they are sung throughout the whole world, *and there is none who can hide himself from your heat*[^35].

With what vehement and bitter pain I was indignant at the Manicheans, and again pitied them, because they did not know those sacraments, those medicines, and were raving against the antidote by which they might have become well! I could wish they had been somewhere nearby then, and—without my knowing they were there—had watched my face and heard my cries when I read the fourth Psalm in that leisure, and what that Psalm made of me: *When I called upon you, you heard me, O God of my righteousness; in tribulation you gave me room. Have mercy on me, Lord, and hear my prayer*[^36]—let them hear, without my knowing whether they heard, lest they should think I said those things for their sake which I said amid these words,

since in truth I would neither say those things, nor say them in that way, if I felt myself to be heard and seen by them; nor, if I did say them, would they take them as I spoke them with myself and to myself before you, out of the intimate feeling of my soul.

#### A meditation on the fourth Psalm

**9**. I shuddered with fear, and in the same place grew warm with hope and exulting *in your mercy*[^37], Father. And all this went out through my eyes and my voice, when, turned toward us, *your good Spirit*[^38] said to us: *Sons of men, how long will you be heavy of heart? Why do you love vanity and seek a lie?*[^39] For I had loved vanity and sought a lie. And you, Lord, had already glorified your holy one[^40], *raising him from the dead and setting him at your right hand*[^41], that from on high he might send his promise[^42], *the Paraclete, the Spirit of Truth*[^43]. And he had already sent him[^44], but I did not know it. He had sent him, because he had already been glorified, *rising from the dead*[^45] and ascending into heaven[^46]. But before, *the Spirit had not yet been given, because Jesus had not yet been glorified*[^47].

And the prophecy cries out: *How long will you be heavy of heart? Why do you love vanity and seek a lie? And know that the Lord has glorified his holy one*[^48]. It cries *how long*, it cries *know*; and I, so long not knowing, had loved vanity and sought a lie, and therefore I heard and trembled[^49], because these words are spoken to such as I remembered myself to have been. For in the phantasms which I had held for the truth there was vanity and a lie. And I cried out many things, heavily and forcefully, in the pain of my remembrance. Would that they had heard them, those who even now love vanity and seek a lie; perhaps they would have been troubled and would have vomited it out, and you would have heard them when they cried to you[^50], since by a true death of the flesh he died for us, *who intercedes for us*[^51].

**10**. I read: *Be angry, and sin not*[^52], and how I was moved, my God, who had now learned to be angry with myself for things past, that thereafter I might not sin—and rightly angry, because it was not some other nature of a race of darkness that sinned in me, as those say who are not angry with themselves and store up for themselves *wrath in the day of wrath and of the revelation of your just judgment*[^53]!

And now my good things were not outside, nor were they sought with eyes of flesh in this sun of ours. For those who wish to rejoice outwardly easily grow empty and are poured out upon the things that are seen and are temporal[^54], and with famished thought they lick at their images. Oh, if only they would grow weary with hunger and say: *Who will show us good things?*[^55] And let us say, and let them hear: *The light of your countenance is sealed upon us, Lord*[^56].

For we are not the light *that enlightens every man*[^57], but we are enlightened by you, so that we who were *once darkness* may be *light*[^58] in you. Oh, if only they could see the inward eternal! Because I had tasted it[^59], I gnashed my teeth, since I could not show it to them if they brought their heart to me in their eyes, away from you, and said: *Who will show us good things?*

For there, where I was angry with myself, within, in my chamber, where I was pricked with compunction[^60], where I had offered sacrifice, slaying my old self[^61], and, having begun the meditation of my renewal, hoping in you—there you had begun to grow sweet to me and had given *gladness in my heart*[^62].

And I cried out as I read these things outwardly and recognized them inwardly, and I did not wish to be multiplied with earthly goods, devouring times and devoured by times, since in eternal simplicity I had another *grain and wine and oil*[^63].

**11**. And I cried out at the verse that follows, with the loud cry of my heart: O in peace! O in the Selfsame! Oh, what did he say: *I will sleep and take my rest*[^64]? For who shall stand against us, when the word that is written shall come to pass: *death is swallowed up in victory*[^65]? And you are supremely the Selfsame, you who do not change[^66], and in you is the rest that forgets all toils[^67], since there is no other with you, nor any need to gain those many other things which are not what you are; but *you, Lord, have established me singularly in hope*[^68].

I read and I burned, and I found nothing I could do for the deaf and dead, among whom I had been—a pest, a bitter and blind barker against the writings that are honeyed with the honey of heaven and luminous with your light[^69]—and I wasted away *over the enemies* of this Scripture[^70].

#### Augustine is wondrously freed from a toothache

**12**. When shall I recall all the things of those days of holiday? Yet I have neither forgotten nor will I be silent about the sharpness of your scourge and the wonderful swiftness of your mercy. With a toothache you tormented me then, and when it grew so heavy that I could not speak, it *came into* my *heart*[^71] to urge all my own who were present to pray to you for me, the God of every kind of salvation[^72]. And I wrote this on wax and gave it to be read to them.

As soon as we bent our knees with suppliant feeling, that pain fled. But what pain? Or how did it flee? I was terrified, I confess, *my Lord, my God*[^73]. For I had experienced nothing like it from my earliest years. And your nods were intimated to me in the depths, and rejoicing in faith I praised your name[^74]; and that faith did not let me be secure about my past sins, which had not yet been forgiven me through your baptism.

### Chapter 5: After the vintage holidays, he resigns the public profession of rhetoric

**13**. When the vintage holidays were over, I resigned, so that the Milanese might provide their students with another seller of words, because I had chosen to serve you, and also because I was no longer adequate to that profession through the difficulty of breathing and the pain in my chest.

And I made known by letter to your bishop, the holy man Ambrose, my former errors and my present resolve, that he might advise what among your books I ought especially to read, so that I might become more ready and fit to receive so great a grace. But he ordered Isaiah the prophet—because, I believe, he is, more than the others, an opener-announcer of the Gospel and of the calling of the nations. Yet I, not understanding the first reading of him and judging the whole to be of the same kind, put it off to be taken up again when I was more practiced in the Lord's speech.

## Augustine is baptized at Milan with Alypius and Adeodatus

### Chapter 6

**14**. From there, when the time came at which I had to give in my name, we left the country and returned to Milan. It pleased Alypius too to be reborn in you with me, already clothed with the humility suited to your sacraments, and a most steadfast tamer of his body, so far as to wear down even the icy Italian ground with bare foot, with unwonted daring.

We joined to us also the boy Adeodatus, born to me carnally of my sin. You had made him well. He was about fifteen years old, and in talent he surpassed many serious and learned men.

I confess your gifts to you, Lord my God, Creator of all things[^75] and mighty to form anew our deformities; for in that boy I had no part but the sin. That he was being reared by us in your discipline—you had inspired this in us, no other: I confess your gifts to you.

There is a book of ours entitled *The Teacher*: it is he who speaks there with me. *You know*[^76] that all the thoughts there put in the mouth of my interlocutor are his, when he was sixteen years old. Many other still more wonderful things of his I came to know. That genius was a thing of awe to me: and who but you is the maker of such marvels?

Soon you took his life from the earth, and I remember him with the greater security, fearing nothing for his childhood or his youth or for that man at all. We joined him to us, our coeval, in your grace, to be brought up in your discipline:

and we were baptized, and the anxiety of our past life fled from us. Nor could I be sated in those days with the wonderful sweetness of considering the depth of your counsel concerning the salvation of the human race.

How I wept at your *hymns and canticles*[^77], keenly moved by the voices of your sweetly singing Church! Those voices flowed into my ears, and the truth was distilled into my heart, and from it the feeling of devotion boiled up, and my tears ran down, and it was well with me amid them.

## The custom of singing hymns in the Church of Milan

### Chapter 7

**15**. Not long before, the Church of Milan had begun to practice this kind of consolation and exhortation, with the brethren singing together with great zeal, with their voices and their hearts. It was about a year, or not much more, since Justina, the mother of the boy-king Valentinian, had been persecuting your man Ambrose for the sake of her heresy, into which she had been seduced by the Arians. The devout people kept watch in the church, ready to die with their bishop, your servant.

There my mother, your handmaid, holding first place in anxiety and in vigils, lived for her prayers. We, still cold from the heat of your Spirit, were nonetheless stirred by the city, astonished and troubled.

Then it was established that hymns and psalms should be sung after the manner of the Eastern regions, lest the people should waste away in the weariness of their grief; and from that time it has been kept to this day, with many—indeed almost all—of your flocks imitating it, throughout the rest of the world as well.

## The bodies of the martyrs Protasius and Gervasius are unearthed and translated to the Ambrosian Basilica

**16**. Then you revealed in a vision to your bishop, of whom I have spoken, the place where the bodies of the martyrs Protasius and Gervasius lay hidden, which you had kept incorrupt for so many years in the treasury of your secret, to bring them forth at the opportune moment to curb a fury that was a woman's, but a queen's.

For when, brought to light and unearthed, they were being translated with due honor to the Ambrosian basilica, not only were those whom unclean spirits vexed healed, those very demons confessing it[^78], but also a certain citizen, blind for many years and very well known to the city, when he had asked and heard the cause of the people's tumultuous joy, leapt up and begged his guide to lead him there.

Led there, he obtained leave to be admitted, that he might touch with his handkerchief the bier of your saints, *whose death is precious in your sight*[^79]. When he had done this and brought it to his eyes, at once they were opened.

From there the report ran abroad; from there your praises grew fervent and shining; from there the mind of that enemy, though not brought to the soundness of believing, was nonetheless checked from the fury of persecuting. *Thanks be to you, my God*[^80]!

From where, and to what, have you led my remembrance, that I should confess to you these things too, great as they are, which I had forgotten and passed over? And yet then, when the fragrance of *your ointments* was so strong, we were not running *after you*[^81]; and so I wept the more amid the singing of your hymns—having long sighed for you, and at last breathing freely, as far as the air finds its way into a house of hay.

## The death of Monica at Ostia on the Tiber

### Chapter 8: Monica's upbringing

**17**. You who *make those of one mind to dwell in a house*[^82] joined to us also Evodius, a young man from our own town. While serving in the imperial service as an agent, he had been converted to you before us[^83] and baptized, and, leaving the worldly service, had girded himself for yours. We were together, meaning to dwell together by a holy resolve. We were seeking what place might hold us most usefully as we served you; together we were on our way back to Africa. And when we were at Ostia on the Tiber, my mother died.

I pass over many things, because I am in great haste. Receive my confessions and thanksgivings, my God, for things beyond number, even in silence. But I will not pass over whatever my soul brings to birth concerning that handmaid of yours, who brought me to birth—both in the flesh, that I might be born into this temporal light, and in heart, that I might be born into the eternal. Not her gifts, but yours in her, will I tell. For she had not made herself, nor brought herself up: you created her, and neither her father nor her mother knew what she would become from them. And the rod of your Christ[^84], the rule of your Only-begotten, brought her up *in your fear*[^85], in a faithful household, a good member of your Church.

And she used to praise, for the diligence shown toward her upbringing, not so much her mother as a certain decrepit servant woman, who had carried her father as an infant, as little ones are commonly carried on the backs of older girls. For this reason, and because of her old age and excellent character, she was held in considerable honor by the masters in that Christian household. Hence too she carried out diligently the care of the daughters of the house committed to her, and was vehement, with a holy severity, in restraining them when need required, and sober and prudent in teaching them.

For except at those hours when they were fed most moderately at their parents' table, she would not let them drink even water, however they burned with thirst, guarding against a bad habit and adding a *sound word*[^86]: "Now you drink water, because you have no wine in your power; but when you come to husbands and are made mistresses of storerooms and cellars, water will seem mean, but the habit of drinking will prevail."

By this method of instruction and authority of command she bridled the greediness of the tenderer age and shaped the very thirst of the girls to an honorable measure, so that now they took no pleasure in what was not seemly.

#### How Monica was healed of a fondness for wine

**18**. And yet, as your handmaid told me, her son, a fondness for wine had crept upon her. For when, as a sober girl, she was bidden by her parents to draw wine from the cask, holding the cup beneath the opening at the top, before she poured the wine into the flagon she would sip a little with the tips of her lips, since she could take no more, her taste refusing it. She did this from no drunken craving, but from certain overflowing excesses of that age, which bubble up in playful impulses and in childish minds are usually pressed down by the weight of their elders. And so, adding daily a little to that little—since *he who despises little things falls little by little*[^87]—she had slipped into such a habit that she would greedily drain little cups now nearly full of wine.

Where then was the shrewd old woman and that vehement prohibition? Could anything avail against the hidden disease, unless your medicine, Lord, kept watch over us? With father and mother and guardians absent, you were present—you who created her, who call, who through the men set over us also work some good for the salvation of souls. What did you do then, my God? How did you cure her? How did you heal her?

Did you not bring forth, from another soul, a hard and sharp reproach, like a surgeon's iron, out of your hidden provisions, and with one stroke cut away that rottenness? For a maidservant, with whom she used to go to the cask, quarreling with her young mistress, as happens, alone with her alone, threw this fault in her face with the bitterest insult, calling her a wine-bibber. Struck by that goad, she looked upon her own foulness, and at once condemned and put it off.

As flattering friends pervert, so quarreling enemies for the most part set right. Yet you repay them not for what you accomplish through them, but for what they themselves intended. For she, in her anger, wished to provoke her younger mistress, not to heal her, and therefore spoke secretly—either because the place and time of the quarrel had so found them, or lest she herself should perhaps be in danger for having disclosed it so late.

But you, Lord, ruler of things heavenly and earthly, who turn the deeps of the torrent to your own uses and order the turbulent flux of the ages, healed one soul even by the madness of another, lest anyone who marks this should ascribe it to his own power if, by his word, another is set right whom he wishes to be set right.

### Chapter 9: Monica softened her fierce husband by her gentleness

**19**. Brought up thus, chastely and soberly, and made subject by you to her parents rather than by her parents to you, when, in her full years, she was of marriageable age[^88], she was given to a husband and served him as a lord[^89], and busied herself to win him for you[^90], speaking of you to him by her character, by which you made her beautiful and reverently lovable and admirable to her husband.

And she so bore the wrongs of his bed that she never had any quarrel with her husband over this matter. For she awaited your mercy[^91] upon him, that, believing in you, he might be made chaste. And he was, besides, as outstanding in kindness, so hot in anger. But she knew not to resist her husband when he was angry, not in deed only, but not even in word. And when she had seen him grown calm and quiet at an opportune moment, she would render the account of her conduct, if perhaps he had been stirred more rashly than he should.

In short, when many matrons, whose husbands were gentler, bore the marks of blows even on their disfigured faces, and in friendly conversation found fault with the lives of their husbands, she would find fault with their tongues—admonishing them gravely, as if in jest—that from the time they had heard those tablets called marriage contracts read out, they ought to have reckoned them as legal instruments by which they had been made servants; and so, mindful of their condition, they ought not to be proud against their lords.

And when those who knew what a fierce husband she endured marveled that it had never been heard, nor shown by any sign, that Patricius had beaten his wife, or that they had quarreled with each other in domestic strife for even a single day, and asked the reason in a friendly way, she would teach them her rule, which I have mentioned above. Those who observed it, having tried it, gave thanks; those who did not observe it, kept in subjection, were tormented.

#### Monica lived with her mother-in-law in wonderful concord

**20**. Her mother-in-law too, at first set against her by the whisperings of wicked maidservants, she so overcame by her attentiveness, persevering in patience and gentleness, that the older woman of her own accord disclosed to her son the meddling tongues of the maids, by which the domestic peace between herself and her daughter-in-law was disturbed, and demanded that they be punished.

And so, after he—obeying his mother, caring for the discipline of the household, and looking to the concord of his own—had punished with beatings the maids who were betrayed, at the discretion of her who betrayed them, she promised that any maid who, to please her, should speak anything bad of her daughter-in-law ought to expect like rewards from her; and, with none now daring to do so, they lived together in a memorable sweetness of goodwill.

#### Monica showed herself a peacemaker among those at variance

**21**. On that good bondservant of yours too, in whose womb you created me, *my God, my mercy*[^92], you had bestowed a great gift: that among any souls at variance and in discord, where she could, she showed herself so much a peacemaker that, although she heard from both sides many most bitter things about each other—such as swelling and undigested discord is wont to belch up, when to a friend who is present the rawness of hatreds is breathed out, through sour conversations, about an enemy who is absent—she yet disclosed nothing of the one to the other except what would avail to reconcile them.

This would seem a small good to me, did I not, in sorrow, experience countless crowds—through some horrible plague of sins ranging far and wide—not only betraying to angry enemies the words of angry enemies, but even adding what was never said; whereas, on the contrary, it ought to be too little for a humane person merely not to stir up or increase enmities among people by speaking evil, unless he also strove to extinguish them by speaking well. Such was she, with you teaching her, the inmost Master, in the school of her heart.

#### Monica, a servant of the servants of God

**22**. At last she won even her husband for you[^93], now at the very end of his temporal life; and in him, now one of the faithful, she did not bewail what she had endured in him while he was not yet of the faithful. She was also a servant of your servants. Whoever of them knew her praised much in her, and honored, and loved you, because he felt your presence in her heart, with the fruits of her holy way of life[^94] bearing witness.

For she had been *the wife of one man*, had *repaid the debt to her parents, had governed her house* piously, *had the testimony of good works*. She had *brought up children*[^95], bringing them to birth as often[^96] as she saw them straying from you. Finally, for all of us, Lord—since by your gift you allow your servants to speak—who before her falling asleep were already living joined together in you, having received the grace of your baptism, she took such care as if she had borne us all, and so served us as if she had been borne by us all.

## Mother and son in conversation at Ostia on the Tiber

### Chapter 10

**23**. Now as the day drew near on which she was to depart from this life—the day which you knew, though we did not—it had come about, as I believe, by your secret arranging, that she and I stood alone, leaning at a certain window, from which the garden within the house that held us was looked upon, there at Ostia on the Tiber, where, far from the crowds, after the labor of a long journey, we were restoring ourselves for the voyage.

We were conversing, then, alone, very sweetly, and, *forgetting the things that are behind* and *stretching forward to those that are ahead*[^97], we were inquiring between ourselves, in the presence of the Truth, which you are[^98], what the eternal life of the saints would be like, *which neither eye has seen nor ear heard, nor has it entered into the heart of man*[^99]. But we panted with the mouth of our heart for the supernal streams of your fountain, the *fountain of life, which is with you*[^100], that, sprinkled from it according to our capacity, we might in some way think upon so great a matter.

**24**. And when the conversation was brought to this point—that the delight of the carnal senses, however great, in whatever bodily light, seemed, beside the sweetness of that life, not worthy of comparison, nor even of mention—raising ourselves up with a more ardent affection toward *the Selfsame*[^101], we passed step by step through all bodily things, and through heaven itself, from which sun and moon and stars shine upon the earth. And still we ascended inwardly, by thinking and speaking and marveling at your works, and we came into our own minds and transcended them, that we might reach the region of unfailing abundance[^102], where you *feed Israel*[^103] forever with the food of truth; and there life is the wisdom through which all these things come to be, both those that have been and those that shall be—and it itself does not come to be, but is as it was, and so shall be forever.

Rather, to have been and to be going to be is not in it, but only to be, since it is eternal; for to have been and to be going to be is not eternal. And while we spoke and panted for it, we touched it slightly with the whole stroke of our heart; and we sighed, and left bound there the *first fruits of the Spirit*[^104], and returned to the noise of our mouth, where a word both begins and ends. And what is like your Word, our Lord, who abides in himself without growing old and yet makes all things new[^105]?

> **25**. We were saying, then:  
> "If for anyone the tumult of the flesh fell silent,  
> silent the phantasms  
> of earth and waters and air,  
> silent too the poles,  
> and the soul itself fell silent to itself  
> and passed beyond itself  
> by not thinking of itself,  
> silent its dreams  
> and imagined revelations,  
> every tongue and every sign  
> and whatever comes to be by passing away—  
> if for anyone these fell wholly silent  
> (since, if one listens, they all say:  
> *We did not make ourselves,  
> but he made us*[^106] who *abides forever*[^107])—  
> if, these things said, they should now fall silent,  
> since they have raised their ear  
> to him who made them,

> and he alone should speak,  
> not through them, but through himself,  
> that we might hear his word,  
> not through the tongue of flesh,  
> nor through the voice of an angel,  
> nor through the sound of a cloud[^108],  
> nor through the riddle[^109] of a likeness,  
> but himself,  
> whom in these things we love,  
> himself we should hear without these—  
> just as now we reach out and with swift thought  
> touch the eternal Wisdom  
> that abides above all things—  
> if this could be prolonged,  
> and other visions of a far inferior kind  
> be withdrawn,  
> and this one alone ravish and absorb  
> and hide away in inward joys  
> the one who beholds it,  
> so that everlasting life might be such  
> as was this moment of understanding  
> for which we sighed,  
> is this not: *Enter into the joy of your Lord*[^110]?  
> And when shall this be?  
> When *we shall all rise again,  
> but shall not all be changed*[^111]?"

**26**. I was saying such things, though not in this way and in these words; yet, *Lord, you know*[^112] that on that day, while we were speaking such things, and this world, with all its delights, was growing worthless to us amid our words, then she said: "Son, for my part, I now take delight in nothing in this life. What I should still do here, and why I am here, I do not know, now that my hope in this age is spent. There was one thing for which I desired to linger a little while in this life: that I might see you a catholic Christian before I died. My God has granted me this more abundantly, that I should see you, with earthly happiness despised, his servant. What am I doing here?"

## After an illness of eight days Monica died

### Chapter 11

**27**. What I answered her to this I do not well remember, since meanwhile, within scarcely five days or not much more, she took to her bed with fever. And while she was ill, one day she suffered a fainting of soul and was for a little while withdrawn from those present. We ran to her, but she was soon restored to her senses, and, looking at me and my brother standing by, she said to us, like one inquiring: "Where was I?" Then, gazing at us, stunned with grief: "Here," she said, "you will bury your mother." I kept silent and restrained my weeping. But my brother said something by which he wished for her, as if more happily, to die not abroad but in her homeland.

Hearing this, she, with an anxious face, struck him back with her eyes for relishing such things, and then, gazing at me: "See," she said, "what he says." And soon to us both: "Lay this body anywhere," she said; "let no care of it trouble you. This only I ask of you, that wherever you are, you remember me at the altar of the Lord." And when she had explained this thought in such words as she could, she fell silent, and was tried by her worsening illness.

**28**. But I, thinking of your gifts, *invisible God*[^113], which you send into the hearts of your faithful, from which wonderful fruits spring up, rejoiced and gave you thanks[^114], recalling what I had known: with how great a care she had always burned over the tomb she had provided and prepared for herself beside the body of her husband. For because they had lived in great concord, she also wished this—for the human mind is less capable of divine things—to be added to that happiness, and to be remembered by people: that it had been granted her, after her journey across the sea, that the earth of both spouses should be covered by a common earth.

But when this emptiness had begun, through the fullness of your goodness[^115], to be no longer in her heart, I did not know; and I rejoiced, marveling that she had so shown herself to me—although even in that conversation of ours at the window, when she said, "What am I now doing here?" she did not appear to desire to die in her homeland. I heard afterward, too, that already, while we were at Ostia, she had one day, with a mother's confidence, conversed with certain friends of mine—when I myself was not present—about the contempt of this life and the good of death; and when they were astonished at the woman's courage (since you had given it to her) and asked whether she did not dread to leave her body so far from her own city: "Nothing," she said, "is far from God, nor is it to be feared that he should not know, at the end of the world, from where to raise me up."

And so, on the ninth day of her illness, in the fifty-sixth year of her age and the thirty-third of mine, that religious and devout soul was released from the body.

## That death not to be wept over

### Chapter 12

**29**. I closed her eyes, and an immense sadness flowed together into my heart and was overflowing into tears, while at the same time my eyes, under the violent command of my mind, drank back their own fountain to dryness; and in such a struggle it went very ill with me. Then indeed, when she breathed her last, the boy Adeodatus cried out in lamentation, and, checked by all of us, fell silent. In this way too something childish in me, which was slipping into weeping, was checked and silenced by the voice of my youth, the voice of my heart. For we did not think it fitting to mark that death with tearful complaints and groans, since by these a certain misery of the dying, or as it were their utter extinction, is usually bewailed. But she was neither dying miserably nor altogether dying. This we held both from the testimony of her character and from her *unfeigned faith*[^116] and from sure reasons.

#### Augustine is anguished and tormented over his mother's death

**30**. What was it, then, that grieved me heavily within, but a fresh wound from the sweetest and dearest habit of living together, suddenly torn apart? I rejoiced indeed in her testimony, that in that very last illness, caressing me in return for my attentions, she called me dutiful, and recalled, with a great feeling of love, that she had never heard from my mouth a harsh or insulting word hurled at her.

But yet what such thing, my God, who made us[^117]—what comparable thing had the honor I paid her, and her service to me? Since, then, I was being bereft of so great a solace of hers, my soul was wounded, and the life that had been made one out of mine and hers was, as it were, torn to pieces.

**31**. When the boy had been kept from weeping, then, Evodius took up the psalter and began to sing a psalm. And the whole household answered him: *Mercy and judgment I will sing to you, Lord*[^118]. When it was heard what was going on, many brethren and religious women came together; and while those whose office it was took care of the funeral, after the custom, I, in a part of the house where I fittingly could, discussed with those who judged that I should not be left alone what was suited to the time, and with that poultice of truth I soothed a torment known to you, while they, not knowing it, listened intently and thought that I was without any feeling of grief.

But I, in your ears, where none of them was listening, rebuked the softness of my feeling and restrained the flow of my grief; and it yielded to me a little, then again was borne on by its own force—not to the bursting forth of tears, nor to a change of countenance; but I knew what I was pressing down in my heart. And because it greatly displeased me that these human things had such power over me, things which by the due order and the lot of our condition must necessarily happen, I grieved over my grief with another grief, and was wasted by a double sadness.

#### Monica's funeral

**32**. And behold, the body was carried out; we went, we returned, without tears. For not even in those prayers which we poured out to you, when the sacrifice of our ransom was offered for her, the corpse now placed beside the tomb before it was laid in it, as is the custom there—not even in those prayers, then, did I weep; but all that day I was heavily sad in secret, and with a troubled mind I asked you, as I could, to heal my grief; and you did not, intimating to my memory, I believe, by this one lesson, the bond of all habit even against a mind that no longer feeds on a deceitful word.

It also seemed good to me to go and bathe, because I had heard that the baths took their name from this—since the Greeks said *balaneion*—because it drives anxiety from the mind. And behold, this too I confess to your mercy, *Father of orphans*[^119]: I bathed, and I was just as I had been before I bathed. For the bitterness of grief did not sweat out of my heart. Then I slept, and woke, and found my grief soothed in no small part; and, as I lay alone on my bed, I remembered the truthful verses of your Ambrose. For you are:

> *God, Creator of all things  
> and ruler of the heavens, who clothe  
> the day with seemly light,  
> the night with gracious sleep,  
> that rest may give relaxed limbs  
> back to the use of labor,  
> and relieve weary minds  
> and free the anxious from grief*.[^120]

#### Augustine at last wept for his dead mother

**33**. And from there I gradually brought back into my former mind your handmaid, and her way of life—devout toward you, and toward us holily tender and obliging—of which I was suddenly bereft; and it pleased me to weep *in your sight*[^121] over her and for her, over myself and for myself.

And I let go the tears I had been holding back, that they might flow out as much as they would, spreading them beneath my heart; and it rested in them, because there were your ears, not those of any man proudly interpreting my weeping.

And now, Lord, I confess to you in writing. Let whoever wishes read and interpret as he wishes; and if he finds it a sin that I wept for my mother for a small part of an hour—the mother now dead to my eyes, who had wept over me for many years that I might live to your eyes—let him not mock, but rather, if he is of great love, let him himself weep to you for my sins, to you, the Father of all the brethren of your Christ.

### Chapter 13: A great hope of our God's forgiveness holds us

**34**. But I, my heart now healed of that wound in which a carnal affection could be reproved, pour out to you, our God, for that handmaid of yours, a far different kind of tears, which flows from a spirit shaken by the consideration of the perils of every soul that dies in Adam.

Although she, made alive in Christ[^122], even before she was released from the flesh, so lived that your name is praised in her faith and character, yet I do not dare to say that, from the time you regenerated her through baptism[^123], no word came from her mouth against your commandment. And it was said by the Truth, your Son[^124]: *whoever says to his brother, You fool, shall be liable to the fire of Gehenna*[^125]; and woe even to the praiseworthy life of human beings, if you should examine it with mercy set aside!

But because you do not search out our faults severely, we confidently hope for some place with you. Yet whoever counts up to you his true merits, what does he count up to you but your gifts? Oh, if only people would recognize themselves to be people, and *let him who glories glory in the Lord*[^126]!

#### A plea to God for his mother's sins

**35**. And so I, *my praise*[^127] and my life, *God of my heart*[^128], setting aside for a little while her good deeds, for which I joyfully *give you thanks*[^129], now plead with you for my mother's sins. *Hear me*[^130] through the medicine of our wounds, which hung upon the wood[^131], and, sitting *at your right hand, intercedes for us*[^132]. I know that she acted mercifully and from her heart forgave the debts of her debtors; forgive her also her debts[^133], if any she contracted through so many years after the water of salvation.

Forgive, Lord, *forgive, I beseech you*[^134]; *do not enter into judgment* with her[^135]. Let *mercy exult over judgment*[^136], since your words are true, and you have promised mercy to the merciful[^137]. That they should be merciful, you gave it to them—you who *will have mercy on whom you will have mercy, and will show compassion to whom you will be compassionate*[^138].

**36**. And, I believe, you have already done what I ask of you; but *approve the freewill offerings of my mouth, Lord*[^139]. For she, as the day of her dissolution drew near[^140], did not think of having her body covered sumptuously or embalmed with spices, nor did she covet a chosen monument, nor care for her ancestral tomb; she did not charge us with these things, but desired only that her memory be made at your altar, which she had served without the omission of a single day, knowing that from it is dispensed the holy victim by which *the record of debt that stood against us was blotted out*, by which the enemy was triumphed over[^141]—who reckons up our offenses and seeks something to lay to our charge, and finds nothing in him[^142] in whom we conquer. Who will pay him back the innocent blood? Who will restore to him the price by which he bought us[^143], to take us away from him?

To the sacrament of this our ransom your handmaid bound her soul with the bond of faith. Let no one tear her away from your protection. Let neither the lion nor the dragon[^144] interpose itself, by force or by guile; for she will not answer that she owes nothing, lest she be convicted and seized by the cunning accuser, but she will answer that her debts have been forgiven by him to whom no one can repay what he, owing nothing, repaid for us.

## Let all who read this remember Monica and Patricius at the altar of God

**37**. Let her, then, be in peace with her husband, before whom she was married to none, and after whom to none[^145], whom she served, bringing forth *fruit to you with patience*[^146], that she might win him too for you[^147]. And inspire, *my Lord, my God*[^148], inspire your servants, my brethren, your sons, my masters, whom I serve with heart and voice and writing, that as many as read this may remember at your altar Monica, your handmaid, with Patricius, once her husband, through whose flesh you brought me into this life, in what way I know not.

Let them remember, with devout affection, my parents in this transitory light, and my brethren under you, our Father, in our mother the Catholic Church, and my fellow citizens in the eternal Jerusalem, for which your people sigh on their pilgrimage from their setting out until their return; so that what she asked of me at the last may be more abundantly granted to her, through the prayers of many called forth by these confessions, than by my prayers alone.
