# Letter 54

To the most beloved son Januarius, Augustine sends greeting in the Lord

**1. 1.** To the things which you have asked me, I would prefer first to know what you yourself, being asked, would answer; for thus, by either approving or amending your answers, I could answer much more briefly, and most easily either confirm or correct you. This indeed, as I said, I would prefer. But yet, that I might answer you now, I have preferred to make a longer discourse rather than a longer delay. First, then, I want you to hold what is the head of this disputation: that our Lord Jesus Christ, as he himself says in the Gospel, has subjected us to his easy yoke and light burden[^1]: whence by sacraments very few in number, very easy in observance, most excellent in signification, he has bound together the fellowship of the new people, such as is baptism consecrated in the name of the Trinity, the communion of his body and blood, and whatever else is commended in the canonical Scriptures, except those things which burdened the servitude of the old people, in accordance with the fitness of their heart and of the prophetic time, which are read also in the five books of Moses. But those things which we keep not written but handed down, which indeed are observed throughout the whole world of lands, are given to be understood as commended and appointed to be retained either by the Apostles themselves, or by plenary councils, whose authority in the Church is most wholesome: such as that the Lord's passion and resurrection and ascension into heaven, and the coming of the Holy Spirit from heaven, are celebrated by an anniversary solemnity, and whatever else of the kind occurs which is observed by the universal Church, wherever it spreads itself.

**2. 2.** But other things which vary through the places and regions of lands—such as that some fast on the Sabbath, others do not; some communicate daily in the body and blood of the Lord, others receive on certain days; in one place no day is passed over on which it is not offered, in another only on the Sabbath and the Lord's day, in another only on the Lord's day: and whatever else of this kind can be observed—this whole class of matters has free observances; nor is there any better discipline in these for a grave and prudent Christian than to act in the way in which he sees the Church to which he perhaps comes to act. For what is proved to be neither against the faith nor against good morals is to be held indifferent, and is to be observed for the sake of the fellowship of those among whom one lives.

**2. 3.** I believe you have at some time heard from me, but yet even now I recall it. My mother, having followed me to Milan, found the Church not fasting on the Sabbath; she began to be troubled and to waver as to what she should do: at that time I did not care about such things, but on her account I consulted Ambrose, a man of most blessed memory, about this matter; he answered me that he could teach me nothing except what he himself did, because, if he knew anything better, he would rather observe that. And when I had thought that, no reason being given, he wished to admonish us by his authority alone not to fast on the Sabbath, he went on and said to me: When I come to Rome, I fast on the Sabbath; when I am here, I do not fast. So you too, to whatever Church you come, observe its custom, if you do not wish to be a scandal to anyone, nor anyone to you. When I had reported this to my mother, she gladly embraced it. But I, thinking again and again about this opinion, always held it as though I had received it from a heavenly oracle. For I have often felt, grieving and groaning, that many disturbances of the weak come about, through the contentious obstinacy or superstitious timidity of certain brothers, who, in matters of this kind, which can come to no certain determination by the authority of holy Scripture, nor by the tradition of the universal Church, nor by the usefulness of correcting life (only because there underlies some reasoning of one thinking, or because in his own homeland he himself was so accustomed, or because he saw it otherwise elsewhere, and on his travels thinks himself the more learned the more remote he was made from his own people); stir up such litigious questions that they esteem nothing right except what they themselves do.

**3. 4.** Someone will say that the Eucharist ought not to be received daily: you will ask why. Because, he says, days are to be chosen on which a man lives more purely and more continently, so that he may approach so great a sacrament worthily: *For he who eats unworthily, eats and drinks judgment to himself*[^2]. Another, on the contrary: Nay, he says, if the wound of sin and the force of the disease is so great that such medicines are to be deferred, each one ought, by the authority of the prelate, to be removed from the altar to do penance, and by the same authority to be reconciled. For this is to receive unworthily, if one receive at that time when one ought to do penance; not that, by one's own judgment, when one pleases, one either withdraw oneself from communion or restore oneself. But for the rest, if sins are not so great that one is judged to be excommunicated, one ought not to separate oneself from the daily medicine of the Lord's body. Perhaps someone settles the quarrel between them more rightly who admonishes that they especially remain in the peace of Christ: but let each one do what, according to his faith, he piously believes ought to be done. For neither of them dishonors the body and blood of the Lord, but they vie in striving to honor the most wholesome sacrament. For Zacchaeus and that Centurion did not quarrel with one another, nor did either of them set himself before the other, when the one of them received the Lord into his house rejoicing[^3], and the other said: *I am not worthy that you should enter under my roof*[^4]: both honoring the Savior in a diverse and as it were contrary manner; both wretched in sins, both having obtained mercy. To this likeness it also avails that, in the first people, the manna tasted in each one's mouth according to his own will[^5], so in the heart of each Christian that sacrament by which the world has been subjugated. For the one, by honoring it, does not dare to take it daily, and the other, by honoring it, does not dare to pass over any day. This food alone refuses contempt, as the manna refused disgust. For thence too the Apostle says that it was received unworthily by those who did not distinguish it from other foods by the veneration singularly due: for immediately when he had said: *He eats and drinks judgment to himself*, he added so as to say: *not discerning the body of the Lord*[^6]; which sufficiently appears in that whole passage in the first Epistle to the Corinthians, if it is diligently attended to.

**4. 5.** Suppose someone is a stranger in some place, perhaps, where, persevering in the observance of Lent, they do not bathe even on the fifth day of the week, nor relax the fast: I will not fast today, he says. The cause is asked: Because it is not done, he says, in my homeland. What else does he do but try to prefer his own custom to another's custom? For he will not recite this to me from the Book of God, nor will he contend, with the full voice of the whole Church wherever it spreads, nor will he show that the other acts against the faith, but he according to the faith and the best morals, nor will he convict the other of violating, or himself of keeping. They do indeed violate their own quiet and peace by quarreling over a superfluous question. Yet I would prefer, in matters of this kind, that both the one in this man's, and this one in the other's homeland, should not shrink from what the rest do. But if, even in a foreign homeland, when he was traveling, where the people of God is greater and more frequent and more fervent, he saw, for example, that on the fifth day of the week of the last week of Lent there was an offering twice, both in the morning and toward evening, and, coming into his own homeland, where the custom is to offer at the end of the day, should contend that it is done ill and unlawfully, because he himself saw it otherwise elsewhere, this is a childish sense, to be guarded against in ourselves, to be tolerated and corrected in our own people.

**5. 6.** Therefore your first inquiry, which you set in your memorandum, attend to of which of these three kinds it is. For you ask in these words: "What ought to be done on the fifth feria of the last week of Lent? Is it to be offered in the morning, and again after supper, on account of that which was said, *Likewise after they had supped*[^7]? Or is one to fast, and to offer only after supper? Or is one even to fast, and, after the oblation, as we are wont to do, to sup?" To these, then, I answer thus: that, as to which of these is to be done, if the authority of divine Scripture prescribes, it is not to be doubted that we ought to do so as we read, so that we now dispute not how it is to be done, but how the sacrament is to be understood. Similarly too, if the whole Church throughout the world frequents any of these: for here too it is the height of insolent madness to dispute that it is to be so done. But neither this nor that is in what you ask. It remains, then, that it be of that third kind, which varies through places and regions. Let each one, then, do what he finds in the Church to which he comes. For none of those things is done against the faith, or against morals better on this side or that. For by these causes, that is, either on account of the faith, or on account of morals, either what was being done amiss ought to be amended, or what was not being done ought to be instituted. For the very change of custom, even that which helps by usefulness, disturbs by novelty. Wherefore that which is not useful, by an unfruitful disturbance, is consequently harmful.

**5. 7.** Nor ought it to be thought that it was instituted in many places that on that day, after a meal, it should be offered, because it is written: *Likewise also the cup, after they had supped, saying*, etc. For he could have called that the supper at which they had now received the body, so that afterward they might receive the cup. For the Apostle elsewhere says: *When you come together into one, it is not to eat the Lord's supper*[^8], calling this very reception of the Eucharist the Lord's supper. **6.** This could rather have moved men, whether, already refreshed on that day, they should either offer or take the Eucharist, because in the Gospel it is said: *But while they were eating, Jesus took bread and blessed it*[^9]; when he had also said above: *When it was evening, he reclined with the twelve, and while they were eating he said: One of you will betray me*[^10]. For afterward he gave the sacrament. And it clearly appears that, when the disciples first received the body and blood of the Lord, they did not receive it fasting.

**6. 8.** Yet is the universal Church on that account to be calumniated, because it is always received by those fasting? For from this it pleased the Holy Spirit that, in honor of so great a sacrament, the Lord's body should enter the mouth of the Christian before other foods: for therefore throughout the whole world this custom is observed. For not because the Lord gave it after food, ought brothers on that account, having lunched or supped, to come together to receive that sacrament, or, as those did whom the Apostle reproves and amends, to mingle these things with their own tables. For the Savior, in order the more vehemently to commend the loftiness of that mystery, willed to fix this last in the hearts and memory of the disciples, from whom he was about to depart to his passion. And therefore he did not prescribe in what order it should thereafter be taken, that he might reserve this place for the Apostles, through whom he was about to arrange the Churches. For if he had admonished this, that it should always be received after other foods, I believe that no one would have varied that custom. But when the Apostle says, speaking of this sacrament: *Wherefore, brothers, when you come together to eat, wait for one another. If anyone is hungry, let him eat at home, that you may not come together unto judgment*[^11]; he immediately subjoined: *But the rest I will set in order when I come*[^12]. Whence it is given to be understood (because it was too much that in a letter he should intimate to them the whole order of acting which the universal Church throughout the world observes) that it was set in order by him which is varied by no diversity of morals.

**7. 9.** But a certain probable reasoning has delighted some, that on one certain day in the year, on which the Lord himself gave that supper, as for a more notable commemoration, it is permitted that the body and blood of the Lord be offered and received after food. But I judge it more becoming that it be done at that hour, so that one who has even fasted may, after the refreshment which takes place at the ninth hour, be able to come to the oblation. Wherefore we compel no one to lunch before that Lord's supper, but neither do we dare to contradict anyone. Yet I think this was not instituted except because the more, and nearly all, in most places, were accustomed to bathe on that day. And because some also keep the fast, in the morning it is offered on account of those who lunch, because they cannot endure fasts and baths at the same time; but toward evening on account of those who fast.

**7. 10.** But if you ask why the custom of bathing too arose: nothing more probable occurs to me thinking about this matter, except that the bodies of those to be baptized, soiled through the observance of Lent, would come to the font with an offense to the sense, unless they were washed on some day. But this day was rather chosen for this, on which the Lord's supper is celebrated annually. And because this was granted to those about to receive baptism, many wished to bathe with them, and to relax the fast. These things having been disputed as I could, I admonish that you keep, as far as you can, the things I have prefaced, as becomes a prudent and peaceful son of the Church. The other things you have asked, if the Lord wills, I will unfold at another time.
