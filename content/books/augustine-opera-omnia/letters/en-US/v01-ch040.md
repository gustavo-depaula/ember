# Letter 40

To the most beloved lord, and a brother and fellow presbyter to be observed and embraced with the most sincere devotion of love, Jerome, from Augustine

**1. 1.** I am thankful that, for a subscribed greeting, you returned me a full letter, but a much briefer one than I would wish to receive from you—from such a man, from whom, however much time he occupies, no discourse is long. Although, therefore, we are surrounded by huge cares of others' affairs, and those secular ones; yet I would not easily pardon the brevity of your letter, did I not consider with how much fewer words of mine it would be repaid. Wherefore, undertake, I beg, this literary conversation with us, lest bodily absence avail much to disjoin us: although in the Lord we are joined by *the unity of the spirit*[^1], even if we rest from the pen and are silent. And the books, indeed, which you have wrought from the Lord's granary almost exhibit you wholly to us. For if we do not know you on this account, that we have not seen the face of your body: in this way neither do you know yourself; for you too do not see it. But if you are known to yourself for no other reason than that you know your own mind; we too know it in no small measure in your letters, in which we bless the Lord that he has given you, such as you are, to you, and to us, and to all the brothers who read your works.

**2. 2.** A certain book of yours, among others, came not long ago into our hands: what its title is, we do not yet know; for the codex itself did not, as is usual, set it forth on the opening page. Yet the brother with whom it was found said it was called *Epitaphium*; which name we would believe had pleased you to be given to it, if we had there read only the lives or writings of those who were already dead. But since the works of many living both at the time when it was written, and even now, are recalled there; we wonder why you either imposed this title on it, or are believed to have imposed it. We sufficiently approve that the same book was usefully written by you.

**3. 3.** In your exposition too of the Epistle of the apostle Paul to the Galatians, we find something that greatly moves us. For if into the holy Scriptures officious lies, as it were, are admitted, what authority will remain in them? what opinion, finally, will be brought forth from those Scriptures, by whose weight the wickedness of contentious falsehood may be crushed? For as soon as you have brought it forth, if one who strives against it savors otherwise, he will say that the very thing brought forth was lied by the writer out of some honorable duty. For where will this not be possible, if it was possible in that narrative which the Apostle, beginning, says: *But the things which I write to you, behold, before God, I do not lie*[^2], for it to be believed and affirmed that he lied in that place where he said of Peter and Barnabas: *When I saw that they were not walking rightly toward the truth of the Gospel*[^3]? For if they were walking rightly, he lied; but if he lied there, where did he speak the truth? Or will he be thought to have spoken the truth there where he said that which the reader savors; but when something occurs against the reader's sense, it will be reckoned an officious lie? For there will be no lack of causes why it may be thought that he not only could, but even ought to have lied, if place is granted to this rule. There is no need to plead this cause with many words, especially with you, to whom, wisely providing, enough is said. By no means would I arrogate to myself that I should strive to enrich your talent, golden by a divine gift, with my coins; nor is there anyone more fit than you to correct that work.

**4. 4.** For you need not be taught by me how that is to be understood which the same Apostle says: *I became to the Jews as a Jew, that I might gain the Jews*[^4], and the other things which are said there, out of the compassion of mercy, not the pretense of deceit. For it is done as a healthy man who ministers to a sick man; not when he lies that he himself has a fever, but when, with the mind of one suffering along, he thinks how he himself would wish to be served, if he were ill. For he was surely a Jew; but, made a Christian, he had not abandoned the Jewish sacraments, which that people had received fittingly and lawfully at the time when it was right. And so he undertook to celebrate them, though now an Apostle of Christ; but in order to teach that they are not ruinous to those who wished to keep them, as they had received them from their parents through the Law, even when they had believed in Christ, provided they did not now place in them the hope of salvation; since through the Lord Jesus salvation itself, which was signified by those sacraments, had arrived. And therefore he judged that they ought in no way to be imposed on the gentiles, since they would call back from the faith men unaccustomed to them by a heavy and unnecessary burden.

**4. 5.** Wherefore he did not correct Peter for this, that he observed the ancestral traditions: which, if he wished to do, he would do neither mendaciously nor unfittingly; for although now superfluous, yet, being customary, they would not harm: but because he was compelling the gentiles to live like Jews[^5], which he could in no way do, except by so acting as though they were still, even after the Lord's coming, necessary to salvation; which the truth, through the apostolate of Paul, vehemently dissuaded. Nor was the apostle Peter ignorant of this; but he did it, *fearing those who were of the circumcision*[^6]. Thus both he himself was truly corrected, and Paul narrated true things, lest the holy Scripture, which was published for the faith of posterity, with the authority of a lie admitted, should waver and fluctuate, wholly doubtful. For it can neither be, nor ought to be, explained in writing how great and how inexplicable evils follow, if we grant this. But it could be demonstrated opportunely and less dangerously, if we conversed between ourselves face to face.

**4. 6.** This, then, of the Jews Paul had abandoned, which they had as evil; and first of all that, *being ignorant of the righteousness of God, and wishing to establish their own righteousness, they were not subject to the righteousness of God*[^7]. Then, that after the passion and resurrection of Christ, the sacrament of grace *according to the order of Melchizedek*[^8] being given and made manifest, they still thought the old sacraments must be celebrated, not from the custom of solemnity, but from the necessity of salvation: which, however, if they had never been necessary, the Maccabees would have become martyrs for them fruitlessly and emptily[^9]. Finally, this, that the Jews persecuted the preachers of grace, the Christians, as enemies of the Law. These and such errors and vices he says he *reckoned loss and dung, that he might gain Christ*[^10]; not the observances of the Law, if they were celebrated after the ancestral manner, as they were celebrated even by him without any necessity of salvation—not as the Jews thought they must be celebrated, or by a deceitful pretense, which he had reproved in Peter. For if he celebrated those sacraments for the reason that he pretended to be a Jew that he might gain them; why did he not also sacrifice with the gentiles, since to those who were without the Law he became as one without the Law, that he might gain them too[^11]—unless because the one thing he did as a Jew by nature, and all this he said, not that he might deceitfully feign himself to be what he was not, but that he might feel that they must be mercifully helped in such a way as if he himself labored in the same error; not, of course, by the cunning of one lying, but by the affection of one suffering along? Just as in that very place he generally inferred: *I became weak to the weak, that I might gain the weak*; so that the following conclusion: *I became all things to all, that I might gain all*[^12], may be understood as referring to this, that he may appear to have pitied each one's weakness as though in himself. For neither, when he said: *Who is weak, and I am not weak?*[^13], did he wish it understood that he had simulated rather than suffered along with another's weakness.

**4. 7.** Wherefore seize, I beseech you, an ingenuous and truly Christian severity, with love, to correct and emend that work, and, as it is said, sing a palinode. For incomparably more beautiful is the truth of the Christians than the Helen of the Greeks. For our martyrs fought more bravely for that truth, against this Sodom, than those heroes for her, against Troy. Nor do I say this in order that you may receive the eyes of the heart; which far be it that you have lost: but that you may turn them—which, though you have them sound and watchful, you have, by I know not what dissimulation, turned away, so as not to attend to what adversities follow, if it should once be believed that a writer of the divine Books can honorably and piously lie in some part of his work.

**5. 8.** I had already once written you a letter on this matter, which was not delivered, because the one to whom I had given it to deliver did not set out: from which this was suggested to me, while I was dictating this one, which in this one too I ought not to omit: that, if your opinion is otherwise, and the same is better, you would gladly pardon my fear. For if you think otherwise, and you think the truth (for unless it be true, it cannot be better), then—not to say it is no fault—surely it is no great fault that my error favors the truth, if rightly in anyone the truth can favor a lie.

**6. 9.** But as to what you deigned to write back about Origen, I already knew that you approve and praise the right and true things which we find not only in ecclesiastical writings, but in all; but disapprove and reprove the false and depraved. But this I desired, and still desire, from your prudence and learning: that you make known to us those very errors of his, by which that so great a man is convicted of having departed from the faith of the truth. In the book too in which you recalled all the ecclesiastical writers, of whom you could be mindful, and their writings, it would be done more conveniently, as I judge, if, having named those whom you know to be heresiarchs—since you were unwilling to pass over even them—you subjoined also in what respects they were to be guarded against: although you have also passed over some; which I would desire to know with what counsel it was done. Or if perhaps you were unwilling to burden that volume, so that, the heretics being recalled, you would not add in what respects catholic authority condemned them; I ask that it not be grievous to your literary labor—by which, through the grace of our Lord God, you have in no small measure both kindled and helped the studies of the holy in the Latin tongue—that which, through my lowliness, brotherly love indicates to you: that, if your occupations allow, you publish, briefly digested in one little book, the perverse dogmas of all the heretics who, up to this time, have tried, whether by impudence or by obstinacy, to deprave the rectitude of the Christian faith, for the knowledge of those who either have not leisure on account of other business, or are not able on account of a foreign tongue, to read and learn so many things. I would ask you long, were this not wont to be a sign of one presuming too little upon love. Meanwhile I much commend to your kindness this brother of ours Paul in Christ, to whose good repute in our regions we bear good testimony before God.
