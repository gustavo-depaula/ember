# Letter 9\*

A MEMORANDUM FOR THE HOLY BROTHER ALYPIUS, FROM AUGUSTINE

**1.** I received the memorandum of Your Holiness on the seventh day before the Kalends of September, and wrote back on the sixth; I had already seen the priest Commodianus and through him had written—not indeed anything about the case itself, because I had not wished to see him for that reason that I might do so, but, anxious lest perhaps something had been done with the man which could not be defended by reason, I wished to know how the things were done about which he complained. After he reported to me, I saw that nothing was to be done, if it comes to your judgment, except that what was done be shown, as far as concerns that same priest's case. For about the assault that was inflicted, which Pope Caelestinus wished alone to be punished, he could tell me nothing, since he answered that he did not know. Hence only this scruple remained for me about this matter, because it seems to me difficult that those who found him with that woman—whom, a professed nun, he had brought from his homeland for the mockery of debauchery—abstained from bodily injury to her.

**2.** For you know how this question is wont to wear us down—both how these evils are left unpunished with the governance of the Church preserved, or how they ought to be vindicated by the Church, since by the public laws they cannot be. What, then, is a bishop or the clergy to do in such—not just any sins, but—crimes of men? This, accordingly, ought first to be inquired of those who think that a bodily punishment is by no means to be inflicted on anyone, especially on account of such people as care nothing at all for ecclesiastical excommunication, whether because they are not Christians or Catholics, or because they so live as to be practically not so. But if the honors move us which each one bears or has borne in the world, neither is it lawful for us to diminish or take away these from anyone in such sins, so that by punishment of this kind the license of doing evil be repressed in those whom you cannot bind or beat. Although, if, while they have the honor of either the curia or the forum—which the one of whom this matter treats seems to have—they wished to dance in the church, I do not see how those could spare them who, for this very thing, even carry in their hands the means by which they impose the discipline of stripes; and surely it is much graver to subject the profession of holiness to one's lust than to dare to dance within the walls of the Church.

**3.** What, then, is to be done in such cases and with such persons, caught in the guilt of those cases, those ought first to inquire and define who wish, when these things are judged, to bring a right sentence; otherwise, if we are moved by their complaints when they receive bodily discipline, and are not moved by their deeds when they do God a most impious injury and, with most restless wickedness, disturb the honor of the honesty and holiness of the Church and its quiet, or if we are so moved by the very misdeeds of these most worthless men, which they perpetrate in the Church by wicked daring, that we punish as small the things they suffer for discipline's sake, but the great things they commit against discipline we judge are either to be left unpunished by us, or by no means can find how they are to be punished—I do not at all see what account of our judgments we are to render to our Lord[^1]. First, then, an ordered punishment for the restless and the lost is to be sought, found, established, then at last is it to be punished, if any disordered or immoderate vengeance can be proved to have been inflicted on them; which, until it be done, what sentence is to be brought against the servants of God who, for the house of their Lord, do something incomparably less wicked than is prepared by the public laws—so that there be at any rate something for those to fear who do not fear that these same public laws can be moved against them by bishops or clergy—I altogether do not see.

**4.** Yet I think—nay rather, I do not doubt—that, if that son of ours to be mourned had set down in his petition what he himself committed, the venerable Pope would by no means judge that he should be vindicated when complaining that he was beaten by clerics, unless perhaps the one who did this to him exceeded the measure of Christian moderation. But I estimate that he, in the judgment of Your Holiness, cannot deny so manifest a thing, that is, his own deed which he kept silent in his petition. But now, in ecclesiastical judgments, if not even that justice is preserved which the public laws most providently established—lest anyone be easily and inequitably impleaded by an imperial rescript, so that he lose the benefit, and that it not be unpunished for whoever, in the petitions given to the emperor, has suppressed something which is established to pertain to the case—and this man who did this, in a petition handed to so holy a See, seems not only not to be punished by the bishops, but even to be vindicated, I do not know what to say.

[^1]: See Rom 14:12
