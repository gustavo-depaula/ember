# Letter 181

Innocent, to Aurelius and all the holy bishops who were present at the council of the Church of Carthage, his most beloved brothers, greetings in the Lord.

**1.** In inquiring into divine matters, which it befits priests to treat with all solicitude, and especially a true, just, and catholic council, keeping the examples of ancient tradition, and mindful of ecclesiastical discipline, you have, by true reasoning, confirmed the vigor of our religion no less now in consulting than before when you pronounced, you who approved that it should be referred to our judgment, knowing what is owed to the apostolic See, since all of us placed in this position desire to follow that very Apostle from whom the episcopate itself and the whole authority of this name emerged. Following whom, we know how both to condemn evils and to approve things laudable; just as that which you, keeping by your priestly office the institutions of the fathers, judge ought not to be trampled, since they decreed it not by human, but by divine sentence: that whatever might be transacted even in disjoined and remote provinces, they would not reckon it to be finished, unless it came to the notice of this See; that by the whole authority of this See whatever just pronouncement there was might be confirmed, and from there the other Churches (just as all waters proceed from their native fount, and through the diverse regions of the whole world the pure streams of an incorrupt head flow forth) might take what they should command, whom they should wash, whom the water worthy of clean bodies should avoid as soiled with filth that cannot be cleansed.

**2.** I rejoice, then, dearest brothers, that through our brother and fellow bishop Julius you sent letters to us, and that, along with those Churches over which you preside, you show your solicitude for the usefulness of all, and demand that there be decreed, for all through all the Churches of the whole world, what may profit them in common: so that the Church, established by its rules, and confirmed by this decree of just pronouncement, by which it may beware of those men, may not be able to dread such persons as, instructed—or rather destroyed—by perverse subtleties of words, disputing under the image of catholic faith, breathing out as it were a pestiferous poison, that they may corrupt the hearts of right-thinking men toward the worse part, seek to overturn the whole discipline of true doctrine.

**3.** It must, then, be healed more quickly, lest the disease, to be execrated, creep further into minds: just as a physician, when he has seen that there is some weakness of this earthly body, esteems it a great proof of his art, if anyone given up for lost quickly escapes by his intervention; or when he has looked upon a festering wound, applies poultices or other things by which that which had arisen as a wound may be able to be closed; and if, remaining, it cannot be healed, lest it corrupt the rest of the body by its rot, he amputates with the knife what was harming, so as to keep the rest whole and untouched. That, then, must be cut off, which crept as a wound upon a body too pure and sound, lest, when it is wiped away too slowly, the bilge-water of this evil, not to be drained out, settle in the very inmost parts.

**4.** For what shall we hereafter rightly think in our minds about these people, who think they owe to themselves that they are good, and do not consider him whose grace they daily obtain? But now these people, who are such, obtain no grace of God, who trust that they can attain by themselves as much, without him, as scarcely those who ask of him deserve to receive. For what can be so iniquitous, so barbarous, so ignorant of all religion, so hostile to Christian minds, as for you to deny that you owe to him whatever you obtain in daily grace, to whom you yourself confess you owe that you were born? Will you, then, be more excellent for yourself in providing than he can be in you, who made you that you might be! And when you think you owe him that you live, how do you not think you owe him that, by obtaining his daily grace, you so live? And you who deny that we need divine help, as if we were perfected wholly from our own possibility, how do we not provoke his help upon us, when we can be such even from ourselves?

**5.** For one who denies the help of God, I would like to ask what he says: that we do not deserve it, or that he cannot grant it? or that there is nothing on account of which each should ask for it? That God can do this, the very works testify; and that we need daily help, we cannot deny. For whether we live well, we provoke it, that we may live better and more holily; or, thinking perversely, we are turned from good things, that we may return to the right way, we need his help more. For what could seem so deadly, so headlong to a fall, so exposed to all dangers, as if, thinking that this alone can suffice us, that we received free choice when we were born, we should henceforth seek nothing further from the Lord—that is, forgetful of our author, should abjure his power, that we may show ourselves free, as though he no longer had anything he could give, who made you free in your origin? not knowing that, unless the grace of God, implored by great prayers, descend upon us, we should in no way strive to conquer the errors of earthly defilement and the worldly body, since not free choice, but only the help of God, can make us equal to resisting.

**6.** For if that man cries out that he has need of divine help, who would not worthily seek it, if free choice profited anyone more—since indeed a blessed man and already chosen by the Lord needed nothing; yet so beseeches God, asking: *Be my helper; do not forsake me, nor despise me, O God of my salvation*[^1]—do we ask free choice for ourselves; he asks God as helper for himself! What we are born with, we say can suffice us; he begs God that he be not forsaken! Do we not, I ask, manifestly learn what we should pray for, when that man, so blessed, as we said above, longs not to be despised? For those must necessarily argue these things, who confirm those things. For let David be accused as ignorant of prayer, and ignorant of his own nature, who, although he knows that so much is in his nature, longs, prone in prayers, for God as helper, and as constant helper—nor does a constant one suffice him, but that he never despise him—and through the whole body of the Psalter both preaches and cries out this! If, then, that man so great confessed this both to be said constantly, and to be so necessary that he taught it; how do Pelagius and Celestius, all the answer of the Psalms set aside, and such doctrine renounced, trust that they will persuade some that we ought neither to seek the help of God, nor need it; when all the saints testify that they can do nothing without it?

**7.** For that man, having once endured free choice, while he used his own goods too inconsiderately, falling, was sunk into the depths of transgression; and found nothing by which he could thence rise; and, deceived in his liberty forever, would have lain crushed by this ruin, had not the coming of Christ afterward relieved him by his grace: who, through the purification of the new regeneration, purged every past fault by the washing of his baptism, and, confirming his state so that he might proceed more rightly and stably, nevertheless did not deny his grace for the future. For although he had redeemed man from past sins, yet, knowing that he could sin again, he reserved many things for his repair, by which he might be able to correct him even after these: he furnishes daily remedies, on which unless we lean and trust, we shall in no way be able to conquer human errors. For it is necessary that, by whom helping we conquer, by him again not helping we be conquered. But I could say more, were it not established that you have said all.

**8.** Whoever, then, seems to assent to this opinion, by which he says that we have no need of divine help, professes himself an enemy to the catholic faith, and ungrateful for the benefits of God. For they are not worthy even of our communion, which by such preaching they have polluted. For they themselves, of their own accord, while they follow what they say, have fled further from true religion. For since this whole consists in our profession, and in our daily prayers we do nothing except how we may obtain the mercy of God; how can we bear those boasting these things? What, I ask, so great an error blinds their breasts, that, if they themselves perceive no grace of God, because they are neither worthy nor deserving, they do not even consider concerning others what divine grace daily bestows on individuals? These indeed are most worthy of all blindness, who have not even left themselves this: that they should believe themselves able to be called back from errors by divine help. For by denying the help, they have utterly taken it away, not from others, but from themselves; who must be torn further away, and removed far from the bowels of the Church, lest, occupying many for a long time, the incurable error grow afterward. For if they shall have been long engaged under this impunity, they must necessarily lead many into this depravity of their mind, and deceive innocents, or rather the imprudent, who follow the catholic faith. For they will think those think rightly, whom they shall have seen still continuing in the Church.

**9.** Let the insane wound, then, be separated from the sound body, and, the breath of the raging disease removed, let those things which are sincere endure more cautiously, and let the purer flock be cleansed from the contagion of this evil cattle. Let there be the unimpaired perfection of the whole body, which we have learned that you follow and hold by this pronouncement against them, and which we, with one and a like assent, keep along with you. But if they shall provoke in themselves some help of God, which hitherto they have denied, and shall acknowledge that they have need of his aid, that they may be freed from this defilement into which by the bending of their heart they had fallen, and, as if drawn into the light from foul darkness, all things removed and renounced by which their whole gaze was soiled and darkened, lest they look upon the truth, condemn these things which hitherto they held, and at some time lending their mind to right disputations, somewhat corrected from this defilement, give and submit themselves to be healed by true counsels. And if they do this, it will be in the power of the pontiffs to come somewhat to their aid, and to grant to such wounds some care, which the Church is wont not to deny to the lapsed when they have come to their senses: so that, called back from their precipices, they may be brought back within the fold of the Lord; lest, placed outside, and excluded from so great a guard of the fortification of faith, they be exposed to all dangers, to be devoured by the teeth of wolves and harassed, which they cannot resist by this perversity of doctrine by which they had irritated them against themselves. But it is sufficiently shown that an answer has been made to your admonitions, so abounding with examples of our law; nor have we thought there remained anything to say, since it is established that nothing was passed over by you, nothing suppressed, by which they may be refuted and utterly acknowledged to be convicted. And therefore no testimonies are set down by us, both because your report is full of them, and it is sufficiently established that so many most learned priests have said all, nor does it befit to believe that you passed over anything that could profit the cause. (And in another hand:) Fare well, brothers.

[^1]: Ps 27:9 (26:9)
