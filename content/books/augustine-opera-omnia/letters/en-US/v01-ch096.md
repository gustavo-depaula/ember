# Letter 95

TO HIS MOST BELOVED AND MOST SINCERE LORDS, HOLY AND DESIRABLE AND VENERABLE BROTHERS, FELLOW-DISCIPLES UNDER THE MASTER, THE LORD JESUS, PAULINUS AND THERASIA, AUGUSTINE SENDS GREETING IN THE LORD.

**1.** When our brothers, most closely joined to us—whom, longed-for, you are wont to long for with us, and, greeted, to greet again—see you assiduously, our good things are not so much increased as our evil things consoled. For we try, as much as we can, to avoid the very cases and necessities, and yet, I know not how—I believe for our merits—they cannot be lacking: but when they come to us, and see us, there happens what is written: *According to the multitude of my sorrows in my heart, your exhortations have gladdened my soul*[^1]. Accordingly, as to that joy with which brother Possidius is with you, when you hear from him what a sad cause compelled him, you will know that I say this most truly: and yet, if any of us were to go across the sea for this alone, to enjoy your presence, what could be found more just than this cause, what more worthy? But this our bonds would not bear, by which we are bound to serve the sicknesses of the weak, and not to leave them by bodily presence, except when this is compelled the more imperiously, the more dangerously they are ill. Whether we are exercised by these things, or rather chastised, I do not know, except that he does not deal with us according to our sins, nor repay us according to our iniquities[^2], who mixes so great consolations with our sorrows, and acts by a marvelous medicine, lest we love the world, lest we fail in the world.

**2.** I asked of you in my earlier letter what the future eternal life of the saints seems to you to be like; but you answered me well, that consultation must surely still be had also about the state of the present life: except that you wished me to consult about what either you do not know with me, or know with me, or perhaps know more than I; since you said most truly that we must first die the evangelical death, before we anticipate the carnal dissolution by a voluntary departure, withdrawing from the life of this age not by death, but by resolve. This action is simple, and fluctuates with no surge of doubt—that we judge we ought so to live in this mortal life that we be in some way fitted to the immortal life. But the whole question which disturbs men, such as I am, in acting and inquiring, is this: in what way one must live, either among or for the sake of those who do not yet know how to live by dying—not by the dissolution of the body, but by turning themselves, by a certain affection of the mind, from bodily allurements. For for the most part it seems to us that, unless we conform a little to them in those very things from which we desire them to be drawn out, we will be able to do nothing wholesome with them. And when we do this, the delight of such things creeps over us too, so that often it even delights us to speak vain things, and to lend an ear to those speaking, and not only to smile, but even to be overcome and dissolved by laughter: thus, burdening our souls with certain dusty—or even muddy—affections, we lift them more laboriously and sluggishly to God, that we may live the evangelical life, by dying the evangelical death. But if at some time we succeed, immediately there is suggested, Bravo, Bravo; not by men—for no man perceives such a recognition of the mind in another—but in a certain inner silence, from I know not where it is cried, Bravo, Bravo. On account of this kind of temptation, so great an Apostle confesses that he was buffeted by an angel[^3]. Behold, whence human life upon the earth is wholly a temptation; when even there a man is tempted, where, as much as he can, he is fitted to the likeness of the heavenly life.

**3.** What shall I say about avenging, or not avenging? since we wish this whole thing to profit the salvation of those against whom we judge it must be avenged or not avenged. What too the measure of avenging may be—not only according to the quality or quantity of the faults, but also according to certain strengths of souls, what each may bear, what refuse, lest he not only not profit, but even fail—how deep and full of hiding-places it is! Of those too fearing the impending vengeance which is feared from men, I do not know whether more have been corrected than have gone to the worse. What, when it often happens that, if you have avenged against someone, he himself perishes; if you have left it unavenged, another perishes? I confess that I daily sin in these things, and do not know when, or in what way, to keep that which is written: *Reprove those who sin before all, that the rest may have fear*[^4]; and what is written: *Rebuke him between you and him alone*[^5]; and what is written: *Do not judge before the time*[^6]; and what is written: *Do not judge, that we be not judged*[^7]—for here he did not add "before the time"; and what is written: *Who are you to judge another's servant? To his own master he stands or falls: but he will stand; for God is able to make him stand*[^8]; whence he confirms that he speaks of those who are within: and again he bids them be judged, when he says: *For what is it to me to judge those who are outside? Do you not judge those who are within? Remove the evil from among yourselves*[^9]. And when this too seems must be done, to what extent it should be done, of how great care and fear it is? lest perhaps there befall what is understood, of that very man, to be warned against in the second Epistle to the same, *lest such a one be swallowed up by greater sorrow*[^10]. And lest anyone think this not much to be cared for, there he says: *That we be not taken possession of by Satan: for we are not ignorant of his minds*[^11]. What trembling, my Paulinus, holy man of God, is in all these things! What trembling, what darkness! Do we not think it was said of these things: *Fear and trembling have come upon me, and darkness has covered me; and I said: Who will give me wings like a dove, and I will fly away and be at rest? Behold, I have gone far off, fleeing, and I have remained in the wilderness*[^12]. Yet even in the wilderness itself, perhaps, he experienced what he adds: *I awaited him who would save me from faintheartedness and the storm*[^13]. Surely, then, human life upon the earth is a temptation[^14].

**4.** What of this: are not the divine words themselves rather groped at than handled by us, while in very many more things we seek rather what should be thought than think something defined and fixed? And though that caution be full of solicitude, it is yet much better than the rashness of affirming. Is it not, in many things, if a man does not savor according to the flesh—which the Apostle says is death—a great scandal to him who still savors according to the flesh[^15]? where both to say what you think is most dangerous, and not to say it most laborious, and to say something other than you think most pernicious. What? when, the things which we do not approve in the speech or writings of those who are within, thinking it pertains to the liberty of brotherly love, we do not hide our judgment, and this we are believed to do not from benevolence, but from envy—how much is sinned against us! and when likewise we suspect those who reprove our opinions of wishing rather to wound than to correct, how much do we sin against others! Certainly hence enmities arise for the most part even among the dearest and most familiar persons, while, beyond what is written, one is puffed up for one against another[^16], and while they bite and devour one another, it is to be feared lest they be consumed by one another[^17]. *Who,* then, *will give me wings like a dove, and I will fly away, and be at rest*[^18]? For whether because the perils in which each is engaged are graver than the unexperienced, or because in truth it is so, any faintheartedness and storm of the wilderness whatever seems to me less troublesome than those things which we either suffer, or fear, in crowds.

**5.** Accordingly, I much approve your opinion, that we must deal with the state of this life, or rather the course rather than the state. I add another thing, that this must first be inquired into and held than that—of what sort that will be to which this course bears. Thence, therefore, I asked what you think, as though, the right rule of this life retained and preserved, we were now secure, while in so many things, and especially in those which, briefly, as I could, I recalled, I feel that I labor most dangerously. But because all this ignorance and difficulty seems to me to arise hence, that, in the great variety of morals, and of souls having most hidden wills and weaknesses, we conduct the affair of a people—not earthly and Roman, but of the heavenly Jerusalem—it pleased me rather to speak with you out of that which we shall be, than out of this which we are. For there, even if we do not know what good things are to come, yet we are certain about no small matter: that these evils will not be there.

**6.** Concerning, then, the conducting of this temporal life, in the way by which one must come to the eternal, I know that carnal desires are to be bridled; and that one must yield to the delights of the bodily senses only as much as is enough for sustaining and conducting a life of this kind, and that all temporal troubles, for the truth of God, and for our eternal salvation and our neighbor's, are to be patiently and bravely tolerated. I know too that one's neighbor must be consulted, with all the zeal of love, to this end: that he conduct this life rightly for the sake of the eternal. That spiritual things, too, are to be preferred by us to carnal, the unchangeable to the changeable; and that a man can do all these things so much the more or the less, the more or the less he is helped by the grace of God, through Jesus Christ our Lord. But why one is helped thus, and another thus, or is not helped, I do not know: yet I know that God does this with the highest equity, known to himself. But concerning those things which I recalled above—how one must live with men—if anything is clearly explored by you, teach me, I beg. But if these things move you also as they do me, confer them with some gentle physician of the heart, whether you find one there where you dwell, or when you go to Rome every year; and what the Lord shall have opened to you through him speaking, or to you conversing together, write to me.

**7.** But concerning the resurrection of bodies, and the future functions of the members in that incorruption and immortality—since you in turn asked me what I think—hear briefly what, if it is not enough, can, if the Lord helps, be disputed more broadly. It is most firmly to be held—whence there is a truthful and clear sentence of the holy Scriptures—that these visible and earthly bodies, which are now called animal, will be spiritual in the resurrection of the faithful and the just. Further, the quality of a spiritual body, being unexperienced by us, how it can be either grasped or insinuated, I do not know. There will certainly be no corruption there, and through this they will not then need this corruptible food which they now need; nor yet will they be unable to take it, and truly consume it, by power, not by necessity. Otherwise neither would the Lord have taken it after the resurrection, who furnished us the example of bodily resurrection in such a way that the Apostle says hence: *If the dead do not rise, neither has Christ risen*[^19]. He, when he appeared with all his members, and used their functions, also showed the places of his wounds. Which I have always taken to be scars, not the wounds themselves, and those very things by power, not by necessity. The ease of which power he then most showed, when he either displayed himself in another form[^20], or, with the doors shut, appeared as true to the disciples established in the house[^21].

**8.** Hence arises the question about the Angels, whether they have bodies fit for their functions and runnings-about, or are only spirits? For if we say that they have them, there occurs to us: *Who makes his Angels spirits*[^22]. But if we say that they do not have them, it has more of a scruple, how it is written that they were presented to the bodily senses of men without a body, received into hospitality, their feet washed, ministry done to them eating and drinking[^23]. For it can more easily seem that the Angels were called spirits in such a way as men are called souls—as it is written that with Jacob so many souls went down into Egypt[^24] (for they did not lack bodies)—than that all those things be believed done without bodies. Then a certain definite stature of an Angel is defined in the Apocalypse[^25], in a measure which can only be of bodies, so that what appeared to men may be referred not to falsehood, but to that power and ease of spiritual bodies. But whether the Angels have bodies, or whether anyone can show how, not having bodies, they could do all those things, yet in that city of the saints—where those too, redeemed through Christ from this generation, will be joined forever to the thousands of Angels—bodily voices will indicate minds that are not hidden; because in that divine society nothing of thought can be hidden from a neighbor, but there will be a concord, in agreement in the praise of God, expressed not only by the spirit, but also by the spiritual body: this seems to me.

**9.** Meanwhile, if you either already hold something more fitting to the truth, or could hear it from teachers, I most studiously await knowing it through you. Review, indeed, my letter, to which—since you alleged the deacon's haste as the reason you answered me most hastily—I therefore do not complain, but rather recall it, so that what was then omitted may now be rendered. For both what you think about Christian leisure for receiving or discoursing Christian wisdom, and about the leisure which I thought was yours—whose incredible occupations have been reported to me—seek out and see what I asked to know from you. (*And in another hand.*) Mindful of us, live happily, our great joys and consolations, holy ones of God.
