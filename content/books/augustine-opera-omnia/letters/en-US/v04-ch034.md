# Letter 242

TO ELPIDIUS, A DISTINGUISHED AND DESERVEDLY HONORABLE AND DESIRABLE LORD, AUGUSTINE.

**1.** Which of us errs in the faith or in the knowledge of the Trinity is another question. I am indeed grateful that you, although you did not know me by face, yet because you believed me to err, tried to recall me from error. May God repay you the reward of this goodwill, and make you know what you think you know: for the matter is, as I judge, an arduous one. And I beg you not to take it in any sense of contumely, that I have wished for you the gift of so great a knowledge. For I fear lest the prejudice of supposed knowledge should repel from your ears, if not true precepts, which I would by no means arrogate to myself toward you, yet at least our good wishes, which it is permitted me, even unlearned, to have for you (for they are to be shown not skillfully, but in a friendly way), and lest you should rather be angry at me that I did not congratulate you as already wise, than give thanks that I prayed wisdom for you. Nevertheless, if I, bearing the burden of the episcopal name, most gladly embrace your goodwill, that you deigned to send me even across the sea to Bonosus and Jason, most learned men, as you write, to carry back rich fruits from their disputations; that, finally, to wipe away the mists of all error, you took care, with anxious kindness, to have brought to me a little book elaborated by the genius and powers of a certain bishop of yours: how much more just is it that you take with a good spirit that I wished those things, which can be given by no human genius and powers, to be granted to you by the Lord God? For the Apostle says: *We have not received the spirit of this world, but the Spirit which is from God; that we may know the things that have been given to us by God: which things we also speak, not in the learned words of human wisdom, but taught by the Spirit, comparing spiritual things with spiritual. But the natural man does not perceive the things that are of the Spirit of God: for they are foolishness to him*[^1].

**2.** I would rather, then, with you, if it could be, inquire how far the natural man is to be so called, so that, if we have now transcended him, we may rightly perhaps rejoice that we have in some part touched those things which abide unchangeably above the human mind and intelligence. For care must be taken lest it seem foolish, when we hear that the Son is equal to the Father[^2], because we still act as the natural man, of whom it was said that the things which are of the Spirit of God are foolishness to him[^3]. Although indeed that majesty, sublime above all things, can be thought of by the spiritual, but can be uttered by no one; yet it is, as I judge, easy to see that he is not made through whom all things were made, and without whom nothing was made. For if he was made through himself, he was before he was made, that he could be made through himself: which is surely said the more absurdly the more vainly it is thought. But if he was not made through himself, he was not made at all; since whatever was made was made through him: *For all things were made through him, and without him was made nothing*[^4].

**3.** I wonder that too little attention is paid to the things the Evangelist wished so expressly to intimate, that he permitted no one to dissemble: for it was not enough to say, *All things were made through him,* unless he added, *and without him was made nothing.* But I, although slow, and bearing, the mist not yet wiped away, a wounded eye of the mind toward beholding the incomparable and ineffable preeminence of the Father and the Son; yet embrace with all ease this, which was for this reason sown beforehand for us in the Gospel, not that we should hence comprehend that divinity, but that we should hence be admonished that we ought not to boast of a rash comprehension. For if all things were made through him; whatever was not made through him was not made. But he himself was not made through himself: therefore he was not made. And we are compelled by the Evangelist to believe that all things were made through him: by the same one, therefore, we are compelled not to believe that he was made. Likewise, if without him was made nothing, then he himself is nothing, because he was made without himself. But if it is sacrilegious to suppose this; it remains that we confess that he was either not made without himself, or not made. But that he was made without himself, we cannot say. For if he made himself, he already was before he was made: but if he furnished help to another by whom he was made, to make himself; nonetheless, that he himself should be made with himself helping, he already was before he was made. It remains, therefore, that he was made without himself. But whatever was made without him is nothing. Either, then, he is nothing, or he was not made: but he is not nothing, therefore he was not made. But if he was not made, and yet is the Son, without doubt he was born.

**4.** "How," you say, "could a Son be born of the Father alone equal to him from whom he was born?" This now I cannot narrate, and I yield to the prophet saying: *His generation who shall declare?*[^5] But if you think it should be taken of the human generation by which he was born through the Virgin; examine yourself, and ask your soul whether, if it failed in the human generation, it would dare to narrate the divine. "Do not, then," you say, "call him equal." Why should I not say what the Apostle said? *He did not consider it robbery,* he says, *to be equal to God*[^6]. For although he did not unfold that equality to the human mind not yet purged, he nevertheless set it down in the Word, that it can be found in the purged reality. Let us, then, give effort to cleansing our heart, that thence may arise the keenness by which we may be able to see these things: *For blessed,* he says, *are the pure in heart, for they shall see God*[^7]. So, leaving behind the misty images of the natural man, we shall come to that serenity and sincerity by which we may be able to see what we see cannot be said.

**5.** For as to the little book you deigned to send, if I have leisure, and the means are granted to answer each point, I think you will come to know that each one is the less clothed with the light of truth the more he seems to himself to bring forth naked truth. For who would bear—to omit other things, and mention for now only this one which I groaned at most, since the apostle Paul says: *We see now through a mirror in an enigma; but then face to face*[^8]—that this man now says "with every covering removed, I bring forth truth naked"? Who, if he said, "We see naked truth," there would be nothing more blind than that arrogance of vision: but he did not say, "We see," but, "we bring forth"; so that now not only truth seems to lie open to the discovery of the mind, but even to lie subject to the power of the tongue. There are many things that may be said about the ineffability of the Trinity; not that it itself may be said—otherwise it is not ineffable—but that by those things said it may be understood that it cannot be said. But now, as I judge, my letter has exceeded its measure, since you admonished me through yours to write briefly: but because you deigned to excuse yourself by the practice of the ancients, I shall not seem absurd to you, if you would not be reluctant to recall the measure of certain letters of Cicero, since you made mention of him too in your letter.
