# Letter 8\*

A MEMORANDUM FOR THE HOLY BROTHER VICTOR, FROM AUGUSTINE

**1.** You will deign to consider how dear to me are both the life and the reputation of Your Holiness; the things which the Jew Licinius lamented before me, if they are true, much sadden me. He proved to me indeed, by the documents he carried, that he had bought certain small farms from those to whom his mother had sold them, and that a certain part had been conferred on his wife by donation, when he married her; but what he added in his complaint is very incredible: that Your Holiness bought everything from that same little old woman, his mother, and excluded him who possessed by the best right, and that, when he complained to you about you yourself, you answered him: "I bought it; [if] your mother sold it to me wrongly, dispute with her! Seek nothing from me, because I will give you nothing." But if he lied to me, deign to write back; but if, through ignorance of the law, this seemed to you the thing to answer, let your Charity know this: that one possessing could be excluded by no law, nor did she rightly sell what her own son possessed as his own, even if perhaps something was due to her. For first she ought to have defeated him, and then sold what, with him defeated, she could have obtained. For against him an action in rem lies; but with his mother he is by no means known to dispute, but with him who seized his property—in which deed I would not have your Fraternity found. It is very invidious and alien from your morals. If, then, he told me true things, deign to return his property to him and receive the price from his mother, if it was given. Which price, if perhaps she is unwilling to repay to you, neither will he be able to lose his property; for it is necessary that he receive it, justice intervening and the laws crying out. I ask you to consider what the Apostle says: *Be without offense to Jews and Greeks and the Church of God*[^1]. But it is better that, admonished by your most dear brother, you do what is just, than that this case come to episcopal judgment.

**2.** Indeed, when I had asked him whether perhaps he had done some injury to his mother, and in this way you had wished to vindicate her, with no desire of possessing his property, doing this rather to terrify him, he answered that his mother had indeed complained about an injury done by his wife and her maidservant, but that he himself had done none. Hence I ask Your Holiness, if the matter is so, that you give him too the discipline of stripes in the presence of his mother herself, if you learn that he is injurious to his mother, because he said he would gladly bear this; or to his wife, if perhaps she is at fault, because she too can receive, in the presence of her mother-in-law, by the judgment of Your Reverence, from her own husband a fitting discipline of stripes. For about the maidservant the case is easy, because over her his mother can much more easily be vindicated. Which he says he did not do for this reason, that he was ignorant of what injuries he had done to his mother; for he says that his mother complained afterward, the property having already been sold.

[^1]: See 1 Cor 10:32
