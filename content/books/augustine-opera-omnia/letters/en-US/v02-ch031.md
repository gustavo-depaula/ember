# Letter 130

Augustine, bishop, servant of Christ and of the servants of Christ, to the religious handmaid of God Proba, greetings in the Lord of lords.

**1. 1.** Recalling both that you asked and that I promised that I would write you something about praying to God, now that—by the grant of him whom we pray—time and opportunity have been granted, it was fitting that I should now at last pay my debt, and serve your devout zeal in the love of Christ. But how your very petition gladdened me—in which I learned how great a care you bear for so great a matter—I cannot unfold in words. For what greater business ought there to be of your widowhood than to persist in prayer night and day, according to the admonition of the Apostle? For he says, *But she who is truly a widow and desolate has hoped in the Lord, and persists in prayer night and day*[^1]. Whence it may seem a wonder, since you are, according to this world, noble, rich, and the mother of so great a family, and in it, though a widow, yet not desolate, how the care of praying has occupied your heart and especially claimed it for itself; unless because you prudently understand that in this world and in this life no soul can be secure.

**1. 2.** Accordingly, he who gave you that thought surely does what he did for his Disciples, saddened not for themselves but for the human race, and despairing that anyone could be saved, after they heard from him that it is easier for a camel to enter through the eye of a needle than for a rich man into the kingdom of heaven; he answered with a marvelous and most merciful promise: that to God is easy what to men is impossible. To him, then, to whom it is easy that even a rich man enter the kingdom of heaven, he inspired in you the devout solicitude about which you thought I should be consulted, in what way you ought to pray. For he, when he was still here in the flesh, sent the rich Zacchaeus into the kingdom of heaven, and, glorified by resurrection and ascension, afterward made many rich men, the Holy Spirit being imparted, despisers of this world, and richer by the ending of the desire of riches. For how would you so be zealous to pray to God, unless you hoped in him? But how would you hope in him, if you hoped in the uncertainty of riches, and despised that most wholesome precept by which the Apostle says, *Command the rich of this world not to be proudly wise, nor to hope in the uncertainty of riches, but in the living God, who furnishes us all things abundantly to enjoy; that they be rich in good works, give easily, share, lay up for themselves a good foundation for the future, that they may lay hold of the true life*[^2]?

**2. 3.** You ought, therefore, for the love of this true life, to think yourself desolate even in this world, in however great a happiness of it you are engaged. For just as there is that true life, in comparison with which surely this life, which is much loved, however pleasant and prolonged it be, is not even to be called life: so there is also a true solace, which the Lord through the prophet promises, saying, *I will give him true solace, peace upon peace*[^3]; without which solace, whatever earthly solaces there are, in them is found rather desolation than consolation. For riches and the summits of dignities, and other such things by which mortals devoid of that true happiness think themselves happy, what consolation do they bring, when it is more excellent not to be in need of them than to be eminent in them; things which torture more, once obtained, by the fear of losing them than, once desired, by the ardor of obtaining them? By such goods men are not made good, but, made good from elsewhere, by using them well they bring it about that these are goods. There are not, then, true solaces in them, but rather there where the true life is. For thence it is necessary that a man become blessed, whence he becomes good.

**2. 4.** But good men seem to afford no small solaces even in this life. For if poverty pinches, if mourning saddens, if pain of body disquiets, if exile grieves, if any other calamity vexes, let good men be present who know not only to rejoice with the rejoicing, but even to weep with the weeping, and know how to address and converse wholesomely; very much those hard things are softened, the heavy relieved, the adverse overcome. But he does this in them and through them, who by his Spirit made them good. On the contrary, if riches flow around, no bereavement befall, health of the flesh be present, one dwell unharmed in one's fatherland, and bad men dwell together with one—in whom there is no one to whom faith can be given, from whom deceit, fraud, angers, discords, ambushes are not feared and endured—do not all those things become bitter and hard, nor is there anything glad or sweet in them? Thus in any human affairs nothing is friendly to a man without a friendly man. But how rarely is such a one found, of whose mind and character there is in this life sure security? For just as no one is known to another as he is to himself; and yet not even is anyone so known to himself as to be secure about his own conduct tomorrow. Accordingly, although many are known from their fruits, and some by living well gladden their neighbors, others by living badly sadden them; yet, on account of the unknown and uncertain things of human minds, the Apostle most rightly admonishes that we judge nothing before the time, until the Lord come, and *illuminate the hidden things of darkness, and manifest the thoughts of the heart, and then there will be praise to each one from God*[^4].

**2. 5.** In these darknesses of this life, then, in which we journey away from the Lord, as long as *we walk by faith, not by sight*[^5], the Christian soul ought to reckon itself desolate, lest it cease to pray: and let it learn to fix the eye of faith on the word of the divine and holy Scriptures as on a lamp set in a dark place, until the day dawn and the daystar arise in our hearts. For of this lamp a certain ineffable fount is that light which so shines in the darkness that it is not comprehended by the darkness, for seeing which hearts are to be cleansed by faith: *For blessed are the pure in heart, for they will see God*[^6]; and, *We know that, when he appears, we will be like him, for we will see him as he is*[^7]. Then will be the true life after death, and the true solace after desolation: that life will rescue our soul from death, and that solace our eyes from tears; and since there will then be no temptation, therefore it follows in the same psalm, *my feet from falling*[^8]. Moreover, if there is no temptation, then there is no prayer: for there will then no longer be the expectation of the promised good, but the contemplation of the rendered good; whence, *I will please the Lord,* he says, *in the land of the living*[^9], where we shall then be, not in the desert of the dead, where we now are. For *you have died,* says the Apostle, *and your life is hidden with Christ in God: but when Christ, your life, shall appear, then you too will appear with him in glory*[^10]. For this is the true life, which the rich are commanded by good works to lay hold of: and there is the true solace; with which solace now the desolate widow—even she who has sons and grandsons and devoutly governs her house, dealing with all her own that they place their hope in God—yet says in prayer, *My soul has thirsted for you; how manifoldly my flesh too for you, in a land deserted, and without way, and without water*[^11]; which is this dying life, frequented by whatever mortal solaces, accompanied by whatever fellow travelers, heaped up with whatever abundance of things. For you know well how uncertain all these are: and in comparison with that promised happiness, what would they be, even if they were not uncertain?

**2. 6.** I have said these things because you, a rich and noble widow, and the mother of so great a family, asked my discourse about prayer, so that even with your own remaining and attending you in this life, you may feel yourself desolate, not yet having laid hold of that life where is the true and sure solace, where will be fulfilled what was said in prophecy: *We have been satisfied in the morning with your mercy; and we have exulted and been gladdened in all our days. We have been gladdened for the days in which you humbled us, the years in which we saw evils*[^12].

**3. 7.** Before, then, that consolation comes, in however great a happiness of temporal goods you flow around, remember that, in order to persist in prayers day and night, you are desolate. For the Apostle did not grant this office to just any widow: *But she who is truly a widow,* he says, *and desolate, has hoped in the Lord, and persists in prayers day and night*[^13]. But what follows, beware most vigilantly: *But she who lives in pleasures is dead while living*[^14]: for a man lives in those things which he loves, which he seeks as a great thing, by which he believes himself blessed. Wherefore what Scripture said about riches, *If riches abound, do not set your heart*[^15], this too I say to you about pleasures: if pleasures abound, do not set your heart. Do not therefore esteem yourself greatly because they are not lacking, because they supply abundantly, because, as from a most lavish fount of earthly happiness, they flow. Altogether despise and condemn these in yourself, nor seek anything in them except the entire health of the body. For this is not to be despised, on account of the necessary uses of life, before this mortal puts on immortality—that is, true and perfect and perpetual health, which is not, failing by earthly weakness, refreshed by corruptible pleasure, but, persisting in heavenly firmness, is enlivened by eternal incorruption. For the Apostle himself too says, *Do not make provision for the flesh in its concupiscences*[^16]; because we bear the care of the flesh, but for the necessity of health. For *no one ever hated his own flesh*[^17], as likewise he himself says. Hence it is that he admonishes Timothy too—too much, as it appears, a chastiser of the body—to use a little wine, on account of his stomach and his frequent infirmities.

**3. 8.** These pleasures, then—in which if a widow lives, that is, if by the delight of the heart she clings and dwells, she is dead while living—many holy men and women, guarding against in every way, have cast away by scattering to the poor riches, the very mothers, as it were, of pleasures, and in such a way have more safely stored them up in heavenly treasures. But if you, bound by some office of piety, do not do this, you know what account of them you render to God. For no one knows what is done in a man, except the spirit of the man which is in him. We ought not to judge anything before the time, until the Lord come, who will both illuminate the hidden things of darkness, and manifest the thoughts of the heart, and then there will be praise to each from God. Yet it pertains to your widowed care that, if pleasures abound, you not set your heart, lest by rotting in them there die what ought to be on high that it may live. Reckon yourself in the number of those of whom it is written, *Their hearts will live forever and ever*[^18].

**4. 9.** You have heard what sort you should be in praying; now hear also what you should pray, about which you thought I should especially be consulted, since it moves you that the Apostle says, *For what we should pray for as we ought, we do not know*[^19]; and you feared lest it harm you more not to pray as you ought than not to pray. What, then, can be said briefly: pray for the blessed life; for this all men wish to have; for even those who live most basely and lostly would in no way so live, unless in the same way they thought themselves to be, or to be able to be made, blessed. What else, then, ought you to pray, except that which both bad and good desire, but to which none but the good attain?

**5. 10.** Here perhaps you would now ask what the blessed life itself is. On which question the talents and leisures of many philosophers have been worn down, who yet could find it the less the less they honored its fount, and gave it no thanks. Attend first, then, whether assent is to be given to those who say that he is blessed who lives according to his own will. But far be it that we should believe this to be true: for what if he should will to live wickedly? Is he not convicted of being the more wretched the more easily his bad will is fulfilled? Deservedly even those who philosophized without the worship of God rejected this opinion. For a certain most eloquent man among them says: "But behold, others, not philosophers indeed, yet ready to dispute, say that all are blessed who live as they themselves will. That indeed is false: for to will what is not fitting is itself most wretched; nor is it so wretched not to obtain what you will as to wish to obtain what you ought not." What seems to you? Were not these words said by truth itself through some man? We can, then, here say what the Apostle says about a certain Cretan prophet, when his opinion pleased him: *This testimony is true*[^20].

**5. 11.** He, then, is blessed who has all that he wills, and wills nothing that is not fitting. But if this is so, see now what things men may not unfittingly will. One wishes to be married, another, widowed of marriage, thereafter chooses to live continently, another to experience no intercourse even in marriage itself. And if one of these is found better than another, yet none of these can we say wills anything unfittingly: so too, to wish for children—the fruit, that is, of marriage—and for those who shall be received life and health, with which wish even widowed continence is for the most part occupied; for even if, marriage being scorned, they no longer wish even to beget children, yet they wish those whom they have begotten to live unharmed. From all this care virginal integrity is immune. Yet all have dear ones, male and female, for whom they not unfittingly wish even temporal health. But when men have obtained this health in themselves and in those they love, can we say that they are now blessed? For they have something that it does not misbefit to will; but if they do not have other things, greater and better and fuller of usefulness and comeliness, they are still far from the blessed life.

**6. 12.** Does it please you, then, that over and above this temporal health they should wish for themselves and their own honors and powers? Surely, if it be so that through this they may take thought for those who live under them—not on account of these things themselves, but on account of some other good that comes of it—it befits to will them: but if on account of the empty pomp of elation, and the superfluous, or even harmful, display of vanity, it does not befit. Wherefore, if they wish for themselves and their own even a sufficiency of necessary things, of which the Apostle thus speaks: *But godliness with sufficiency is great gain. For we brought nothing into this world, nor can we take anything out of it: having food and covering, let us be content with these. For those who wish to become rich fall into temptation and a snare and many foolish and harmful desires, which sink men into ruin and perdition. For the root of all evils is avarice; which some, coveting, have erred from the faith, and have entangled themselves in many sorrows*[^21]: this sufficiency, then, whoever wishes it not unfittingly wills, nor wishes more; otherwise he does not will it itself, and therefore does not will even fittingly. This he wished, and for this he prayed, who said, *Give me neither riches nor poverty; but appoint for me sufficiently the things that are necessary, lest, being sated, I become a liar, and say, Who sees me? or, being made poor, I steal, and forswear the name of my God*[^22]. You certainly see that this sufficiency too is not sought for its own sake, but for the health of the body, and for a fitting habit of a man's person, by which habit he may not be unfitting to those with whom he must live honorably and dutifully.

**6. 13.** In all these things, then, the safety and friendship of a man are sought for their own sake; but the sufficiency of necessary things is wont to be sought not for its own sake, but for the two former, when it is fittingly sought. Safety, moreover, is established in life itself, and health, and the integrity of mind and body. Likewise friendship is not to be bounded by narrow limits: for it embraces all to whom love and affection are owed, although it inclines more readily toward some, more hesitantly toward others; but it reaches even to enemies, for whom too we are commanded to pray. Thus there is no one in the human race to whom love is not owed, even if not for mutual charity, yet for the fellowship of a common nature.  
But those greatly and justly delight us by whom we are in turn holily and chastely loved. These, when they are had, we must pray that they be kept; but when they are not had, we must pray that they be had.

**7. 14.** Is this the whole, and are these all the things by which the sum of the blessed life is gathered? Or does truth teach something else which is to be preferred to all these? For both that sufficiency, and safety itself, whether one's own or one's friends', so long as it is temporal, is to be cast away for the obtaining of eternal life: although perhaps the body, but the mind in no way is to be esteemed sound, which does not prefer eternal things to temporal; for one does not live usefully in time, except to acquire merit by which one may live in eternity. To that one life, then, by which one lives with God and from God, the other things which are usefully and fittingly wished are without doubt to be referred. For in this we love ourselves, if we love God: and by the other precept we so truly love our neighbors as ourselves, if we lead them, as far as is in us, to a like love of God. Let us love God, then, for his own sake, and ourselves and our neighbors for his sake: nor, when we so live, let us esteem ourselves already established in the blessed life itself, as though there were nothing more for which to pray. For how do we already live blessedly, when that still is lacking for whose sake alone we live well?

**8. 15.** Why, then, are we dispersed through many things, and seek what we should pray, fearing lest perhaps we pray as we ought not, and do not rather say with the Psalm, *One thing I have asked of the Lord, this will I seek; that I may dwell in the house of the Lord all the days of my life; that I may contemplate the delight of the Lord, and visit his temple*[^23]? For there all days are made all, not by coming and passing, nor is the beginning of one the end of another; all are together without end, where neither does that life itself have an end, of which they are the days. For obtaining this blessed life, the true blessed Life itself taught us to pray, not in much speaking, as though we are heard for being more talkative, when we pray him who knows, as the Lord himself says, what is necessary for us before we ask of him. Whence it may seem a wonder—although he forbade much speaking—why he so exhorted us to pray, who knows what is necessary for us before we ask of him, as to say, *It is necessary always to pray, and not to fail*[^24]; setting forth the example of a certain widow who, desiring to be avenged of her adversary, by often importuning an unjust judge bent him to hearing, moved not by justice or mercy, but overcome by weariness: that hence we might be admonished how much more certainly the merciful and just Lord God hears those praying without intermission, when she could not be despised even by an unjust and impious judge by her assiduous importuning; and how willingly and appeased he fulfills the good desires of those by whom he knows the sins of others are forgiven, if she who wished to be avenged came to what she wished. He too, to whom a friend had come from a journey, and who had nothing to set before him, desiring three loaves to be lent him by a friend—by which perhaps the very Trinity of one substance is figured—roused by most insistent and most troublesome petitioning the friend now sleeping with his servants, that he might give him as many as he wished, rather avoiding weariness himself than thinking of benevolence: that hence we might understand that, if one who is compelled to give while he sleeps is roused unwilling by one petitioning, how much more benignly does he give who knows not how to sleep, and rouses us sleeping that we may ask.

**8. 16.** Hence is also that: *Ask, and you will receive; seek, and you will find; knock, and it will be opened to you. For everyone who asks receives, and the one seeking finds, and to the one knocking it will be opened. Or what man is there among you, from whom his son asks bread—will he hand him a stone? or if he asks a fish, will he hand him a serpent? or if he asks an egg, will he hand him a scorpion? If, then, you, since you are evil, know how to give good gifts to your children, how much more will your heavenly Father give good things to those who ask him?*[^25] Since, then, according to those three things which the Apostle commends, faith is signified in the fish—either on account of the water of Baptism, or because in the floods of this world it is whole; to which that serpent is contrary, who, by venomous fraud, persuaded that God should not be believed: hope in the egg, because the life of the chick is not yet, but is to be, and is not yet seen, but is still hoped for; for *hope that is seen is not hope*[^26]; to which the scorpion is contrary, since he who hopes for eternal life forgets the things that are behind, and stretches himself to the things before; to whom it is harmful to look back; but the scorpion is to be guarded against on that part which, venomous and stinging, it has behind: love in the bread; for *the greater of these is love*[^27], and among foods surely the usefulness of bread surpasses the rest; to which the stone is contrary, since hard hearts reject love—whether these things signify something else more fitting, yet he who knows how to give good gifts to his children compels us to ask and to seek and to knock.

**8. 17.** Why he does this, who knows what is necessary for us before we ask of him, can move the mind, unless we understand that the Lord our God wishes, not that our will become known to him—which he cannot be ignorant of—but that our desire be exercised in prayers, by which we may be able to grasp what he prepares to give. For that is very great, but we are small and narrow for grasping it. Therefore it is said to us, *Be enlarged; do not be drawing the yoke with unbelievers*[^28]. For that which is very great—*which the eye has not seen*, because it is not a color; *nor the ear heard*, because it is not a sound; *nor has it ascended into the heart of man*[^29], because the heart of man ought to ascend thither—we shall receive the more capaciously the more faithfully we believe it, and the more firmly we hope, and the more ardently we desire it.

**9. 18.** In faith, then, and hope and love, by continued desire, we always pray. But for this reason, at fixed intervals of hours and times, we also ask God with words, so that by those signs of things we may admonish ourselves, and make known to ourselves how much we have progressed in this desire, and rouse ourselves more sharply to increase it. For a worthier effect will follow which a more fervent affection precedes. And through this, what the Apostle says, *Pray without intermission*[^30], what else is it than, "Without intermission desire the blessed life, which is none but the eternal, from him who alone can give it"? Let us, then, always desire this from the Lord God, and pray always. But for this reason, from other cares and businesses, by which that very desire in a manner grows lukewarm, at certain hours we recall the mind to the business of praying, by the words of prayer admonishing ourselves to attend to that which we desire, lest what had begun to grow lukewarm cool altogether and be utterly extinguished, unless it be more frequently inflamed. Whence also that which the same apostle says, *Let your requests become known before God*[^31], is not to be taken as though they become known to God, who knew them surely even before they existed, but as becoming known to ourselves before God by endurance, not before men by boasting. Or perhaps they even become known to the Angels who are before God, that in a manner they may offer them to God, and consult about them, and what they shall have learned, at his command, ought to be fulfilled—as they shall there have learned it ought to be—they may bring to us, whether evidently or hiddenly: for an angel said to a man, *And now, when you and Sarah prayed, I offered your prayer in the sight of the brightness of God*[^32].

**10. 19.** Since these things are so, even when there is leisure to pray long—that is, when the offices of other good and necessary actions are not hindered—although in these too, as I said, one must always pray by that desire, it is neither blameworthy nor useless. For it is not, as some suppose, to pray in much speaking, if one prays longer. Much speech is one thing, long-lasting affection another. For of the Lord himself too it is written that he passed the night in praying, and that he prayed more at length: where what else but an example was he affording us, in time an opportune supplicant, with the Father an eternal hearer?

**10. 20.** The brothers in Egypt are said to have frequent prayers, but yet most brief, and in a manner hurled rapidly, lest that intention, vigilantly erect, which is most necessary for one praying, should, through more prolonged delays, vanish and grow dull. And by this they themselves too show well enough that this intention, just as it is not to be blunted if it cannot endure, so, if it shall have endured, is not to be quickly broken off. For far be much speaking from prayer, but let not much praying be lacking, if a fervent intention persevere. For to speak much is to do a necessary thing in praying with superfluous words. But to pray much is to knock at him whom we pray with a long-lasting and devout rousing of the heart. For this business is for the most part carried on more by groans than by speeches, more by weeping than by speaking. But he sets our tears in his sight, and our groan is not hidden from him who made all things through the Word, and does not seek human words.

**11. 21.** To us, then, words are necessary, by which we may be reminded and look at what we ask, not by which we believe the Lord is to be either taught or bent. When, then, we say, *Hallowed be your name*, we admonish ourselves to desire that his name—which is always holy—be held holy also among men, that is, not despised; which profits not God, but men. And in that we say, *Your kingdom come*, which—whether we will or will not—will surely come, we rouse our desire to that kingdom, that it may come to us, and that we may deserve to reign in it. When we say, *Your will be done as in heaven so on earth*, we pray of him for ourselves that very obedience, that his will may so be done by us as it is done in the heavenly ones by his Angels. When we say, *Give us this day our daily bread*, by that which is said "this day" is signified "in this time," where we ask either that sufficiency—by the part which excels, that is, signifying the whole by the name of bread—or the Sacrament of the faithful, which is necessary in this time, yet not for attaining the happiness of this time, but that eternal one. When we say, *Forgive us our debts, as we also forgive our debtors*, we admonish ourselves both what we ask, and what we do, that we may deserve to receive. When we say, *Lead us not into temptation*, we admonish ourselves to ask this, lest, deserted by his help, we either consent to some temptation, deceived, or yield to it, afflicted. When we say, *Deliver us from evil*, we admonish ourselves to consider that we are not yet in that good where we shall suffer no evil. And this last indeed, which is set in the Lord's prayer, extends so widely that a Christian man, set in any tribulation, in it utters his groans, in it pours forth his tears, from it begins, in it lingers, at it ends his prayer[^33]. For by these words it behooved that the things themselves be commended to our memory.

**12. 22.** For whatever other words we say—which the affection of one praying either forms by going before, that it may shine, or attends by following, that it may grow—we say nothing else than what is set in that Lord's prayer, if we pray rightly and fittingly. But whoever says that which cannot pertain to that evangelical prayer, even if he does not pray unlawfully, prays carnally: which I do not know how it is not said to be unlawful, since it befits those reborn in the spirit to pray only spiritually. For he who says, for example, *Be glorified in all nations, as you have been glorified in us*[^34]; and, *Let your prophets be found faithful*[^35]—what else does he say than, "Hallowed be your name"? He who says, *God of powers, turn us, and show your face, and we will be saved*[^36]—what else does he say than, "Your kingdom come"? He who says, *Direct my ways according to your word, and let no iniquity have dominion over me*[^37]—what else does he say than, "Your will be done as in heaven so on earth"? He who says, *Give me neither poverty nor riches*[^38]—what else does he say than, "Give us this day our daily bread"? He who says, *Remember, O Lord, David, and all his meekness*[^39]; or, *O Lord, if I have done this, if there is iniquity in my hands, if I have repaid those who recompensed me with evils*[^40]—what else does he say than, "Forgive us our debts, as we also forgive our debtors"? He who says, *Take from me the concupiscences of the belly, and let not the desire of lust seize me*[^41]—what else does he say than, "Lead us not into temptation"? He who says, *Rescue me from my enemies, O God, and free me from those who rise up over me*[^42]—what else does he say than, "Deliver us from evil"? And if you run through all the words of the holy prayers, you will, I think, find nothing that is not contained and enclosed in that Lord's prayer. Whence it is free to say the same things, in praying, by other and other words; but it ought not to be free to say other things.

**12. 23.** These things both for ourselves, and for our own, and for strangers, and even for our very enemies, are to be prayed without the wavering of doubt: although in the heart of one praying there may arise or stand out an affection, one for this person, one for that, as the nearnesses or farnesses of relationships are. But whoever says in prayer, for example, "Lord, multiply my riches"; or, "Give me as much as you gave to this man or that"; or, "Increase my honors, make me preeminent and illustrious in this world," or anything of the sort; and says these things, having a concupiscence of them, not attending to this, that from them he may profit men according to God: I think he does not find in the Lord's prayer how he can fit these vows. Wherefore let him at least be ashamed to ask what he is not ashamed to covet; or, if he is ashamed of this too, but covetousness conquers, how much better is it asked that he be freed even from this evil of covetousness, to whom we say, "Deliver us from evil"!

**13. 24.** You have, I think, not only what sort you should pray, but even what you should pray; not by my teaching, but by his who deigned to teach us all. The blessed life is to be sought, it is to be asked of the Lord God. What it is to be blessed has been disputed by many in many ways; but why do we go to the many and to many things? Briefly and truly it has been said in the Scripture of God: *Blessed is the people whose God is the Lord himself*[^43]. That we may be in that people, and may be able to come to contemplating him and living with him without end, *the end of the precept is love from a pure heart, and a good conscience, and unfeigned faith*[^44]. In those same three, for a good conscience hope is set. Faith, then, and hope and love lead to God the one praying—that is, the one believing, hoping, desiring, and considering what he should ask of the Lord in the Lord's prayer. Fastings, and refraining from other pleasures of carnal concupiscence (without neglect of health), and most of all almsgiving, greatly help prayer, that we may be able to say, *In the day of my tribulation I sought out God, with my hands, in the night, before him; and I was not deceived*[^45]. For how is God, incorporeal and impalpable, sought out with hands, unless he be sought out by works?

**14. 25.** There is still perhaps something you would ask: why the Apostle said, *For what we should pray for as we ought, we do not know*[^46]: for it is in no way to be believed that either he, or those to whom he said these things, did not know the Lord's prayer. Why, then, do we think he said this—which he could neither rashly nor mendaciously say—except because temporal troubles and tribulations for the most part profit, either to heal the swelling of pride, or to prove and exercise patience, for which, once proved and exercised, a brighter and richer reward is reserved, or to scourge and abolish any sins; yet we, not knowing what these things profit, wish to be freed from all tribulation? From this ignorance the Apostle showed that not even he himself was foreign, unless perhaps he knew what to pray as he ought, when, in the magnitude of revelations, lest he be exalted, there was given him a thorn of the flesh, an angel of Satan, to buffet him; on account of which he thrice asked the Lord to take it from him, surely not knowing what he prayed as he ought. Finally he heard God's answer, why what so great a man prayed for was not done, and why it was not expedient that it be done: *My grace is sufficient for you; for power is perfected in weakness*[^47].

**14. 26.** In these tribulations, then, which can both profit and harm, what we should pray as we ought, we do not know: and yet, because they are hard, because troublesome, because against the sense of our weakness, by the universal human will we pray that these be taken from us. But we owe this devotion to the Lord our God, that if he does not take them away, we do not on that account esteem ourselves neglected by him, but rather, by devout patience of evils, hope for greater goods: for thus power is perfected in weakness. For to certain impatient ones the Lord God granted in anger what they asked, just as he denied to the Apostle, propitious, what he sought. For we read what and how the Israelites asked and received; but, the concupiscence being fulfilled, the impatience was gravely chastised. He gave a king too to those asking, according to their heart, as it is written, not according to his own. He gave even what the devil demanded, that his servant, to be proved, might be tempted. He heard even the unclean spirits asking, that a legion of demons might be sent into many swine. These things are written, lest perhaps anyone esteem himself greatly if he be heard, when he asks something impatiently, which not to obtain would profit more; or cast himself down, and despair of the divine mercy toward himself, if he be not heard, when perhaps he asks something by receiving which he would be more atrociously afflicted, or, corrupted by prosperity, utterly overthrown. In such things, then, what we should pray as we ought, we do not know. Whence, if something contrary to what we pray befalls us, by bearing it patiently, and in all things giving thanks, we ought least of all to doubt that this rather behooved, which God's will, not ours, held. For an example of this kind too that Mediator afforded us, who, when he had said, *Father, if it can be, let this cup pass from me*, transforming in himself the human will from the assumption of man, at once subjoined, *Yet not what I will, but what you will, Father*[^48]. Whence not without merit, *through the obedience of one, many are made just*[^49].

**14. 27.** But whoever asks that one thing of the Lord, and seeks it, asks sure and secure, nor fears lest perhaps it harm him when he has received it, without which whatever else he shall have received in praying, as he ought, profits nothing. For that itself is the one true and sole blessed life: that we contemplate the delight of the Lord forever, immortal and incorruptible in body and spirit. For the sake of this one thing the rest are sought, and not unfittingly asked. Whoever shall have it will have all that he wills, nor will he be able to wish to have anything there which it will not befit. For there is the fount of life, which it now behooves us to thirst for in prayer, as long as we live in hope, and what we hope for we do not yet see, under the protection of his wings, before whom is all our desire, that we may be inebriated by the abundance of his house, and given drink of the torrent of his pleasure; since with him is the fount of life, and in his light we shall see light, when our desire shall be satisfied in good things, and there will be nothing further that we seek by groaning, but which we hold by rejoicing. Nevertheless, because it is the peace which surpasses all understanding, even in asking it in prayer, what we should pray as we ought, we do not know. For what we cannot think of as it is, surely we do not know; but whatever occurs to us thinking, we cast away, spit out, disapprove; we know that this is not what we seek, although we do not yet know of what sort that is.

**15. 28.** There is, then, in us a certain, so to say, learned ignorance, but learned by the Spirit of God who helps our weakness. For when the Apostle had said, *But if we hope for what we do not see, by patience we wait*, there he subjoined, *Likewise the Spirit too helps our weakness: for what we should pray as we ought, we do not know; but the Spirit himself intercedes for us with unutterable groans. But he who searches the hearts knows what the Spirit relishes, because according to God he intercedes for the saints*[^50]. Which is not so to be understood that we should esteem the Holy Spirit of God—who in the Trinity is the unchangeable God, and with the Father and the Son one God—to intercede for the saints as one who is not what God is: for it was said, "he intercedes for the saints," because he makes the saints intercede; just as it is said, *The Lord your God tempts you, that he may know whether you love him*[^51]—that is, that he may make you know. And so he makes the saints intercede with unutterable groans, inspiring in them the desire of so great a thing still unknown, which we await by patience. For how is that narrated, when it is desired, which is unknown? For surely if it were utterly unknown, it would not be desired; and again, if it were seen, it would not be desired, nor sought with groans.

**16. 29.** Considering all these things, and whatever else about this matter the Lord may have intimated to you—which has either not occurred to me, or would have been long for me to say—strive in prayer to conquer this world; pray in hope, pray faithfully and lovingly, pray instantly and patiently, pray as a widow of Christ. For although praying pertains to all his members—that is, to all who believe in him and are associated to his body, as he taught—yet specially a more diligent care of prayers is found enjoined upon widows in his Scriptures. For there were two Annas honorably named: one married, who bore the holy Samuel; the other a widow, who recognized the Holy of holies when he was still an infant. The married one too prayed in grief of mind and affliction of heart, because she did not have children: then she obtained that very Samuel, and rendered him, once received, to God, because, when she asked, she vowed. But how her prayer pertains to that Lord's prayer is not easily found, except that in that which is set there, "Deliver us from evil," it seemed no small evil both to be wedded and to lack the fruit of marriage, since the procreation of children alone excuses marriage. But of that widow Anna see what is written: *That she did not depart from the temple, serving with fastings and supplications night and day*[^52]. And from no other source the Apostle, which I recalled above too: *But she who is truly a widow,* he says, *and desolate, has hoped in the Lord, and persists in prayers night and day*[^53]: and the Lord, when he exhorted us always to pray, and not to fail, recalled a widow, who, though an unjust and impious judge, and a despiser of God and men, she turned to hearing her cause by assiduous importuning. How much, then, beyond the rest, widows ought to be at leisure for prayers, can be sufficiently understood from this, that to all, for undertaking the zeal of praying, an example of exhortation is set forth from widows.

**16. 30.** But what in this kind of work was chosen in widows, except destitution and desolation? Wherefore, if every soul understands itself to be in this world destitute and desolate, as long as it journeys away from the Lord, it surely commends a certain widowhood of its own to God its defender by assiduous and most lavish prayer. Pray, then, as a widow of Christ, not yet having the sight of him whose help you entreat. And though you be most rich, pray as a poor woman: for you do not yet have the true riches of the age to come, where you may dread no losses. Though you have sons and grandsons, and a numerous family—which was said above too—pray as one desolate: for all temporal things are uncertain, even those that are to remain to the end of this life for our consolation. But you, if you seek and relish the things that are above, you desire eternal and certain things, which, as long as you do not yet have them, even with all your own safe and attending you, you ought to reckon yourself as desolate. And if you, surely also by your example your most religious daughter-in-law, and other holy widows and virgins established more securely under your care: for the more devoutly you govern your house, the more lavishly you ought to be intent on prayers, not occupied with the businesses of present things, except those that the cause of piety demands.

**16. 31.** Surely remember to pray for us too, not negligently. For we do not wish you so to defer to us the honor which we bear in danger, that you take away the help which we know is necessary. By the family of Christ it was prayed for Peter, it was prayed for Paul; and we rejoice that you are in his family, and we incomparably more than Peter and Paul need the helps of brotherly prayers. Pray in rivalry, by a concordant and holy contest: for you do not contend against one another, but against the devil, the enemy of all the saints. In fastings and vigils, and all chastisement of the body, prayer is very much helped. Let each of you do what she can: what one is less able to do, she does in the one who can, if in the other she loves what, because she herself cannot, she does not do; accordingly, let her who is less able not hinder the one more able, and let her who is more able not press the one less able. For you owe your conscience to God; but owe nothing to any of you, except to love one another. May the Lord hear you, who is able to do above what we ask or understand.
