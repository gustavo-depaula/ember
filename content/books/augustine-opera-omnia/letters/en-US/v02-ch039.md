# Letter 138

To the most excellent lord, and deservedly distinguished, most dear and most longed-for son Marcellinus, Augustine sends greetings in the Lord.

**1. 1.** To the illustrious and most eloquent man, and most beloved by us, Volusianus, no more had to be answered than to that which he thought should be inquired: but to those things which you yourself sent to be handled and solved by me in your letter—whether they be suggested or pressed by him, or even by others—they ought rather to be sent to you, handled and solved according to our powers; not in the manner in which these things must be dealt with by the business of books, but in the manner in which it can suffice for epistolary colloquy, so that, if it shall seem good to you—who experience their stirrings in daily disputations—this letter may be read to them too. But if for their little ears, less cultivated by the piety of faith, this discourse does not suffice, let first that be carried out between us which you think suffices for them, then at last let what has been carried out be conveyed to them. For there are many things from which, if their sense still shrinks back and recoils, they can perhaps sometimes be persuaded, either by a richer or a subtler reasoning, or at any rate by an authority which they think it unworthy to resist.

**1. 2.** In your letter, then, you set down that certain men are moved "why this God, who is also affirmed to be the God of the Old Testament, despising the old sacrifices, was delighted with new ones. For they assert that nothing can be corrected except what before was proved not rightly done; or that what was once rightly done ought in no way to be changed. For they say that things rightly done cannot be changed except unjustly." These words I have transferred from your letter into this mine. To which, if I should wish to answer copiously, time would fail me sooner than examples by which the very nature of things and human works, by a fixed reason, are changed according to the opportunity of times, nor yet is the same reason changeable by which these things are changed. Whence let me recall a few, so that from these, attention, in a manner roused, may now run vigilant through many like ones. Does not summer succeed to winter, heat being gradually added? Are not nocturnal times turned to diurnal? How often are our ages varied! Boyhood yields, not to return, to adolescence; youth, not to abide, succeeds to adolescence; old age, ending youth, is ended by death. All these things are changed, nor is the reason of divine providence changed, by which it comes about that these things are changed. But, I think, when the farmer commands something else in summer than he had commanded in winter, the reason of agriculture is not changed. And when he who rested by night rises in the morning, he has not changed the counsel of his life. The master imposed on the adolescent something else than he was wont upon the boy. The teaching, then, constant, the precept being changed, changed not its instruction, unchanged.

**1. 3.** For that great physician of our times, Vindicianus, consulted by a certain man, ordered to be applied to his pain what seemed to suit at the time; it being applied, health followed. Then, after some years, the same affliction of the body being again stirred, he thought this same thing should be applied; it being applied, it turned to the worse. Marveling, he runs back to the physician, indicates what was done: but he, being most keen, answered thus: "Therefore you were ill-affected, because I did not order it"; so that all who heard, and knew the man too little, supposed that he trusted not in the medical art, but in I know not what unlawful power. Whence, when he was afterward asked by certain men astonished, he disclosed what they had not understood, namely that to that age he would now not have ordered this. So much, then, does it avail, the reasoning and arts not being changed, what according to them is to be changed for the variety of times.

**1. 4.** It is not, then, true what is said, that what was once rightly done is in no way to be changed. For the cause of the time being changed, true reason for the most part demands that what had before been rightly done be so changed that, when they themselves say it is not rightly done if it be changed, on the contrary truth cries out that it is not rightly done unless it be changed; because both will then be right, if it will be diverse according to the variety of times. For what can happen in a diversity of persons at one time—that it be lawful for this man to do something with impunity, not lawful for that, not because the thing is dissimilar, but because he who does it is—so, by one and the same person at diverse times, it behooves something to be done then, not to be done then; not because he who does it is dissimilar to himself, but the time when he does it.

**1. 5.** How widely this question extends, surely whoever can—and does not neglect to gaze upon—the distance between the beautiful and the fitting, scattered in a manner throughout the universe of things, sees. For the beautiful is considered and praised through itself, to which the base and deformed is contrary. But the fitting, to which the unfitting is opposite, hangs, as it were bound, from something else, and is judged not from itself, but from that to which it is connected: surely the becoming and the unbecoming too is either this same thing, or is held the same. Come now, refer the things we have said to that of which we treat. The sacrifice which God had commanded was fitting in the first times, but now it is not so. For he commanded another which would be fitting to this time, who knows far more than man what is suitably applied to each time; what he should impart, add, take away, withdraw, increase, or diminish at any time, the unchangeable orderer of changeable things, as creator, so moderator, until the beauty of the whole age—of which those things that are fitting to their several times are the little parts—run out like a great poem of a certain ineffable composer, and thence those who duly worship God may pass into the eternal contemplation of his form, even when it is the time of faith.

**1. 6.** But they are deceived who think that God commands these things for the cause of his own benefit or pleasure: and they are deservedly moved why God changed these things, as though, by a changeable delight, commanding one thing to be offered to himself at that earlier time, another at this. But it is not so. God commands nothing that profits himself, but the one to whom he commands. Therefore he is the true Lord, who has no need of a servant, and whom the servant needs. For in that Scripture which is called the Old Testament, and at that time when those sacrifices were still being offered which at this time are not offered, it was said: *I said to the Lord, You are my God, for you have no need of my goods*[^1]. God, then, had no need of those sacrifices, nor does he ever need anything of anyone, but they are certain signs of things divinely imparted, either for imbuing the mind with virtues, or for attaining eternal salvation, by whose celebration and performance the offices of piety, useful not to God but to us, are exercised.

**1. 7.** But it is too long to dispute fittingly about the variety of signs, which, when they pertain to divine things, are called Sacraments. But just as a man is not therefore changeable because one thing in the morning, another in the evening; this in this month, that in another; not this in this year which in that: so God is not therefore changeable because in the earlier volume of the whole age he commanded one thing to be offered to himself, in the later another, by which he might fittingly dispose, through changeable times without any change of himself, the significations pertaining to the most wholesome teaching of religion. For that those whom these things move may know that this was already in the divine reason, and that, when these new things were established, the earlier did not suddenly displease as by a changeable will, but that this was already fixed and settled in the very wisdom of God—to whom about the greater changes of things too the same Scripture says, *You will change them, and they will be changed: but you are the same self*[^2]—it must be intimated to them that this change of the sacraments of the Old Testament and the New was even foretold by prophetic voices. For thus they will see, if they can, that which in time is new is not new with him who founded the times, and without time has all things which he distributes to their several times according to their variety. For in that psalm too, from which I recalled something, by which I might show that God has no need of our sacrifices, to whom it is said, *I said to the Lord, You are my God, for you have no need of my goods*[^3], a little after is read, from the person of Christ: *I will not gather their assemblies from bloods*[^4], that is, from victims of cattle, by which earlier the assemblies of the Jews were gathered: and elsewhere he says, *I will not take calves from your hand, nor he-goats from your flocks*[^5]: and another prophet says, *Behold, the days will come, says the Lord, and I will confirm over the house of Jacob a new testament, not like the testament which I disposed for their fathers, when I led them out of the land of Egypt*[^6]. And there are many other testimonies about this matter, by which it was foretold that God would do this, which it is now long to recall.

**1. 8.** Here, if it has now been sufficiently established that what was rightly established at one time can rightly be changed at another time—by the work of the one changing, not by a changed disposition, which disposition the intelligible reason contains, where, without time, those things are together which in times cannot be done together, because times do not run together—someone perhaps will await to receive from us the causes of the change itself, which you yourself know how prolix a business it is. Nevertheless it can be said briefly, what perhaps would suffice for an acute man, that Christ had to be foretold by some sacraments when he was about to come, and announced by others when he had come; just as now, we ourselves speaking this very thing, the diversity of matters compelled us even to change the words. For it is one thing to be foretold, another to be announced; one thing when he was about to come, another when he had come.

**2. 9.** Now next let us see what sort of thing it is that follows in your letter. For you added that they say "that the preaching and teaching of Christ in no part agrees with the customs of the republic, since it is agreed to be his precept that we ought to repay evil to no one for evil, and to offer the other cheek to one striking, and to give a cloak to him who persists to take away a tunic, and to go a doubled space of the journey with one who shall wish to compel us." All which are asserted to be contrary to the customs of the republic: "For who," they say, "would suffer something to be taken from him by an enemy, or would not wish to repay evils, by the law of war, to plunderers of a Roman province?" These and such words of detractors—or of those who speak such things not by detracting, but by inquiring—I would perhaps more laboriously refute, unless these debates were held with men liberally instructed. Whence what need is there that we labor longer, and not rather question them how they could govern and increase the republic—which they made from small and needy great and opulent—who, an injury received, preferred to forgive rather than to pursue? How did Cicero, extolling the character of Caesar, an administrator surely of the republic, say that he was wont to forget nothing except injuries? For so great a praiser, or so great a flatterer, said this: but if a praiser, he knew Caesar to be such; but if a flatterer, he showed that a prince of the city ought to be such as he fallaciously proclaimed him. But what is it not to repay evil for evil, except to shrink from the lust of avenging? Which is, an injury received, to prefer to forgive rather than to pursue, and to forget nothing except injuries.

**2. 10.** When these things are read in their authors, there is an outcry and applause; the characters seem to be described and proclaimed of which it would be worthy that a city should arise to command so many nations—because, an injury received, they preferred to forgive rather than to pursue. But when it is read, the divine authority commanding, that evil is not to be repaid for evil; when this so wholesome admonition sounds from a higher place to the congregations of peoples, as in public schools of both sexes and all ages and dignities, religion is accused as an enemy of the republic! Which, if it were heard as is worthy, would far better than Romulus, Numa, Brutus, and the rest of those illustrious men of the Roman nation, found, consecrate, firm up, and increase the republic. For what is a republic, except the affair of a people? An affair, then, common, the affair surely of a city. But what is a city, except a multitude of men reduced into a certain bond of concord? For among them it is thus read: "Thus in a short time the dispersed and wandering multitude, by concord, had become a city." But what precepts of concord did they ever judge should be read in their temples? Since the wretches were compelled to seek how, without offense of any of them, they could worship their gods, discordant among themselves: whom, if they should wish to imitate by discord, the bond of concord being broken, the city would fall; which afterward, little by little, the characters being discolored and corrupted, began to be done through civil wars.

**2. 11.** But how great precepts of concord—not sought out by human disputations, but written by divine authority—are habitually read in the churches of Christ, who, even foreign to that religion, is so deaf as not to know? For to this pertain even those things which they prefer to harass rather than to learn: that the other cheek is to be offered to one striking, that to one wishing to take away a tunic a cloak too is to be given, that with one compelling the journey is to be doubled. For this is done that the bad man be overcome by good—nay, that in the bad man evil be overcome by good, and the man be freed from the evil, not exterior and foreign, but inmost and his own, by which he is more gravely and more perniciously laid waste than by the savagery of any enemy from without. He, then, who overcomes evil by good, patiently loses temporal advantages, that he may teach how, for faith and justice, those things are to be despised by loving which too much he becomes bad; and so the injurious man may learn, from him to whom he did injury, of what sort the things are for which he did injury, and, repenting, may be won over into concord, than which nothing is more useful to a city—conquered not by the forces of one raging, but by the benevolence of one enduring. For it is then rightly done, when it seems it will profit the one for whose sake it is done, for working in him correction and concord. This surely must be done with this mind, even if another outcome follow, and he be unwilling to be corrected and pacified, for whose correction and pacification—as for curing and healing—this medicine was applied.

**2. 12.** Otherwise, if we attend to the words, and think we must serve their literal sense, the right cheek is not to be offered if the left has been struck. For *If anyone,* he says, *strikes you on the right cheek, offer him the left too*[^7]: but rather the left is struck, because the blow on the right is easier for the striker. But it is wont to be understood thus, as if it were said: If anyone has assailed your better things, offer him your lower too, lest, studying vengeance rather than patience, you despise eternal things for temporal, when rather temporal things are to be despised for eternal, as the left for the right. This was always the intention of the holy martyrs: for the final vengeance is justly demanded when no place of correction now remains, namely at the last and highest judgment. But now it is to be guarded against, lest by the desire of avenging there be lost—to say nothing else—patience itself, which is to be held of more worth than all that an enemy can take away even from one unwilling. For another evangelist, in the same saying, made no mention of the right, but named only the cheek and the other, so that it might be understood somewhat more distinctly in the one, while he himself simply commended the same patience. The just and devout man ought, therefore, to be prepared patiently to endure the malice of those whom he seeks to make good, so that the number of the good may rather grow, not that, by an equal malice, he add himself too to the number of the bad.

**2. 13.** Finally, that these precepts pertain rather to the preparation of the heart which is within, than to the work which is done openly—so that patience with benevolence be held in the secret of the mind, but in the manifest that be done which seems it can profit those to whom we ought to wish well—is clearly shown from this, that the Lord Jesus himself, a singular example of patience, when he was struck in the face, answered: *If I have spoken evil, reproach me concerning the evil; but if well, why do you strike me?*[^8] In no way, then, did he fulfill his own precept, if we look at the words. For he did not offer the other part to the striker, but rather forbade lest he who had done injury increase it; and yet he had come prepared not only to be struck in the face, but even to be crucified and killed for those very ones from whom he suffered these things, for whom, hanging on the cross, he says: *Father, forgive them, for they do not know what they do*[^9]. Nor does the apostle Paul seem to have fulfilled the precept of his Lord and master, where he too, struck in the face, said to the prince of the priests: *God will strike you, you whitewashed wall. You sit judging me according to the Law, and against the Law you order me to be struck!* And when it was said by those standing by, *You do injury to the prince of the priests*, he wished mockingly to admonish them what he had said, so that those who were wise might understand that the whitewashed wall was now to be destroyed by the coming of Christ—that is, the hypocrisy of the priesthood of the Jews: for he says, *I did not know, brethren, that he is the prince; for it is written, You shall not curse the prince of your people*[^10]; when without doubt he who had grown up among that same people, and had there been instructed in the Law, could not be ignorant of that prince of the priests, nor in any way could it deceive those to whom he was so known that he was ignorant.

**2. 14.** These precepts of patience, then, are always to be retained in the preparation of the heart, and benevolence itself, that evil not be repaid for evil, is always to be fulfilled in the will. But many things are to be done, even by chastising the unwilling with a certain kindly harshness, whose benefit rather than will is to be taken thought for; which in a prince of the city their writings most brilliantly praised. For in correcting a son, however harshly, surely the paternal love is never lost. Yet what he would not, and grieves at, is done, who, even unwilling, seems to be healed by grief. And through this, if that earthly republic keeps the Christian precepts, even its very wars will not be waged without benevolence, so that the conquered may be more easily taken thought for, toward the pacified fellowship of piety and justice. For he from whom the license of iniquity is wrested is usefully conquered; since nothing is more unhappy than the happiness of sinners, by which a penal impunity is nourished, and the bad will, like an interior enemy, is strengthened. But the perverse and averted hearts of mortals think human affairs happy when the splendor of roofs is attended to, and the ruin of souls is not attended to; when the masses of theaters are built up, and the foundations of virtues are dug out; when the madness of squandering is glorious, and the works of mercy are derided; when from those things which abound to the rich, the actors luxuriate, and the poor scarcely have necessities; when God, who by the public voices of his teaching cries out against this public evil, is blasphemed by impious peoples, and such gods are sought, in whose honor those very theatrical things, the disgraces of bodies and souls, are celebrated. If God permits these things to prevail, then he is more gravely indignant; if he dismisses these things unpunished, then he punishes more hostilely. But when he overturns the support of vices, and renders the copious lusts needy, he opposes mercifully. For mercifully, if it could be, even wars would be waged by the good, so that, the licentious desires being tamed, these vices might be destroyed, which a just empire ought either to root out or to repress.

**2. 15.** For if Christian discipline blamed all wars, this counsel of salvation would rather be given in the Gospel to the soldiers asking for it, that they cast away their arms and withdraw themselves altogether from soldiering. But it was said to them: *Do violence to no one, accuse no one falsely; let your wages suffice for you*[^11]. Whom he commanded that their own wages should suffice, he surely did not forbid to soldier. Accordingly, those who say that the teaching of Christ is adverse to the republic, let them give such an army as the teaching of Christ ordered the soldiers to be; let them give such provincials, such husbands, such wives, such parents, such sons, such masters, such servants, such kings, such judges, such, finally, payers and exactors of the debts of the very treasury, as Christian teaching commanded them to be, and let them dare to say that it is adverse to the republic; nay rather, let them not hesitate to confess it to be, if it be obeyed, the great welfare of the republic.

**3. 16.** But why should I answer that which they say, that through certain Christian emperors many evils befell the Roman empire? This general complaint is calumnious. For if they would recall, more openly at least, some things about past emperors, I too could recall similar, or perhaps even graver, things about non-Christian emperors, so that they might understand that either these are the vices of men, not of doctrine; or not of emperors, but of others without whom emperors can do nothing. From what time the Roman republic began to go to ruin is clear enough; their own writings speak it: long before the name of Christ shone forth in the lands, it was said, *O city for sale, and soon to perish, if it finds a buyer!* In the book too of the Catilinarian war, surely before the coming of Christ, the same most noble historian of theirs does not keep silent when the army of the Roman people first grew accustomed to make love, to drink, to admire statues, painted tablets, embossed vessels, to seize them privately and publicly, to spoil shrines, to pollute all things sacred and profane. When, then, neither men, nor even those whom they thought gods, were spared by the avarice and rapacity of corrupted and lost characters, then began to perish that praiseworthy honor and welfare of the republic. What successes the worst vices then had, and with how great an evil of human affairs that iniquity was made to prosper, it is now long to say. Let them hear their own Satirist, telling true things by chattering: *Humble fortune once kept the Latin women chaste;  
labor, brief sleep, hands roughened and worn by Tuscan wool,  
Hannibal close to the city, and husbands standing guard  
on the Colline tower kept vice from touching their humble homes.  
Now we suffer the evils of a long peace; luxury, fiercer than arms,  
has fallen upon us and avenges the conquered world.  
No crime or lustful outrage has been absent since  
Roman poverty perished.* Why, then, do you await that I should exaggerate how great evils that iniquity, raised up by prosperous success, brought in; since even they themselves who attended somewhat more prudently saw that it was more to be grieved that Roman poverty, than opulence, had perished? For in the former the integrity of character was kept; but through the latter, not the walls of the city, but the very minds of the city, a dire wickedness, worse than every enemy, burst in upon.

**3. 17.** Thanks to the Lord our God, who against these evils sent us a singular help. For whom would not that river of the dreadful wickedness of the human race carry off, whom not enwrap, into what depth not plunge, unless the cross of Christ, in so great as it were a mass of authority, were more eminently and more firmly fixed; by grasping whose strength we might be stable, lest, snatched away in so vast a whirlpool of this world by those who persuade evil, or impel into evils, we be swallowed up? For in that filth of the worst characters and the old lost discipline, most of all the heavenly authority ought to come and succor, which would persuade voluntary poverty, continence, benevolence, justice, and concord, and true piety, and the other luminous and strong virtues of life; not only for conducting this life most honorably, nor only for the most concordant fellowship of the earthly city; but even for attaining everlasting salvation, and the heavenly and divine republic of a certain everlasting people, into which faith, hope, love enroll us as citizens: so that, as long as we journey thence, we may bear—if we cannot correct them—those who wish the republic to stand by unpunished vices, the republic which the first Romans founded and increased by virtues, even though not having true piety toward the true God (which could lead them too, by a wholesome religion, into the eternal city); yet keeping a certain probity of their own kind, which could suffice for founding, increasing, and conserving an earthly city. For God thus showed, in the most opulent and illustrious empire of the Romans, how much civil virtues could avail even without true religion, so that it might be understood that, this being added, men are made citizens of another city, whose king is truth, whose law is love, whose measure is eternity.

**4. 18.** But who would not think it even worthy of laughter, that they try to compare Apollonius and Apuleius, and the other men most expert in magic arts, with Christ, or even to prefer them? Although it is to be borne more tolerably when they compare these men with him rather than their own gods: for, what must be confessed, Apollonius was much better than the author and perpetrator of so many defilements whom they name Jove. "These things," they say, "are fabulous." Let them then still praise the luxurious, licentious, and plainly sacrilegious happiness of the republic, which fashioned these reproaches of the gods, which set them not only to be heard in fables, but even proposed them to be watched in theaters; where the crimes were more than the divinities, which the gods themselves were content to have exhibited to them, who ought to have avenged upon their worshipers that they even watched them patiently. "But it is not they themselves," they say, "who are celebrated by the lie of such fables." Who, then, are these, who are appeased by the celebration of such basenesses? Because Christian doctrine has disclosed the perversity and deceit of these demons—through whom even the magic arts deceive human minds—because it has laid it open to the whole world, because it has distinguished the holy Angels from their malignity, because it has admonished that they are rather to be guarded against, and in what way to be guarded against, it is said to be an enemy of the republic! As though, if temporal happiness were altogether to be obtained through these, any unhappiness rather were not to be chosen. But neither here did God wish there to be doubt, who honored the first people—worshiping the one true God, but despising the most false gods—with so great a happiness of earthly things, as long as it behooved the Old Testament, where there is the veil of the New, to be overshadowed, that anyone might understand that even it is not in the power of demons, but in that of the one to whom the Angels are subject, before whom the demons tremble.

**4. 19.** For Apuleius—to speak especially of him, who, an African, is better known to us Africans—could not, with all his magic arts, attain, I do not say to a kingdom, but not even to any judiciary power of the republic, though born in an honorable place of his country, and liberally educated, and endowed with great eloquence. Or did he perhaps, as a philosopher, contemn these things by will, who, a priest of the province, held it a great thing to put on games and clothe huntsmen, and, over a statue to be set up for him among the people of Oea—from which city he had a wife—litigated against the contradiction of certain citizens? Which, lest it lie hidden from posterity, he committed to memory the written oration of that lawsuit. As far, then, as pertains to this earthly happiness, that magician was what he could be. Whence it appears that he was nothing more, not because he was unwilling, but because he could not. Although even against certain men, who had brought against him the charge of magic arts, he defended himself most eloquently. Whence I marvel that his praisers, who proclaim that he did I know not what miracles by those arts, try to be witnesses against his defense. But let them see whether they themselves bear true testimony, and he made a false defense. Let those, if they are wise, attend to this, who seek out magic arts surely only for earthly happiness or damnable curiosity, or, innocent of them, yet with a dangerous admiration praise them, and let them see our David, without any such arts, from a shepherd of sheep attain to royal dignity; whose both sins and merits the faithful Scripture did not keep silent, that we might know both by what ways God is not offended, and by what ways, offended, he is appeased.

**4. 20.** But as far as pertains to miracles, which are shown to human senses as things to be astonished at, they greatly err who compare magicians with the holy Prophets, eminent in the renown of great miracles; how much more if they compare them with Christ, whom those Prophets—with whom any magicians are in no way to be compared—foretold would come, both according to the flesh which he took from the Virgin, and according to the divinity by which he is never separated from the Father! I see that I have made a most prolix letter, nor yet have said about Christ all the things that could somehow suffice either for those who cannot attain divine things by talent, or for those whom, though they are acutely moved, a contentious zeal and a preoccupation of long-lasting error hinders from understanding. Nevertheless learn what moves them against it, and write back, so that, whether by letters or by books, if God shall help, we may take care to answer all things. Be happy in the Lord, by his grace and mercy, most excellent lord, and deservedly distinguished, most dear and most longed-for son.
