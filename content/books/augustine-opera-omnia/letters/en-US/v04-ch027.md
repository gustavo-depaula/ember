# Letter 235

AUGUSTINE, TO LONGINIANUS.

**1.** I have taken the fruit of my writing, namely the reply of your Benevolence. Hence I now see arisen and begun between us, as it were, the sowing of a great disputation about this great matter: this is what I wished first, then what I still wish, God will help. And it is this: that this thing begun be closed with a due and wholesome end. Accordingly, that you thought nothing about Christ was rashly to be either denied or affirmed by you, this temperateness in the mind of a pagan I would not unwillingly accept. But that you also desire to be taught by my writings about this matter, I will by no means refuse, nor will I cease to serve this so good will of yours, most dear to me. But first it is necessary somehow to clarify, and to take a clear sense of, your opinion about the ancient rites. For when you had said that the better way to God is that "by which a good man—proved by devout, just, pure, chaste, true words and deeds, without any catching at the boasting of changed times, and walled about by the company of the gods, having at any rate earned merit by the powers of God; that is, fulfilled with the powers of him who is one, and universal, and incomprehensible, and untiring, and ineffable Creator, whom, as is true, you call angels, or whatever else there is after God, or from God, or with God, or into God—hastens to go by the intention of mind and soul" (which words you recognize from your letter); then you added and said: "There is a way, I say, by which those purified hasten, refined by the devout precepts and most pure expiations of the ancient rites, and by abstemious observances, steadfast in soul and body."

**2.** In these words I perceive, unless I am mistaken, that it seems to you not to suffice, for the way by which one goes to God, that a good man should, by devout, just, pure, chaste, true words and deeds, earn the favor of the gods, by whose company walled about he may hasten to go to that highest God, the creator of all, unless he be also purified by the devout precepts and expiations of the ancient rites. Wherefore I would wish to know what you think is to be purified by the rites in him who, by living devoutly, justly, purely, and truthfully, earns the favor of the gods, and through them that one God of gods. For if he is still to be purified by rites, surely he is not clean: and if he is not clean, he does not live devoutly, justly, purely, and chastely. For if he already so lives, he is already clean: further, what need is there that one already clean and pure should be purified by being expiated through rites? And so here is the knot of our disputation, which loosed, we shall see the consequences: whether a man lives well, that he may be purified by the rites; or is purified by the rites, that he may live well; or whether any measure whatever of living well in a man is not yet fit for the blessed life, which is received from God, unless the aids of the rites be added; or whether to receive the rites too is a kind of portion, as it were, of living well, namely so that it is not one thing to live well and another to live sacredly, but the sacred life too is enclosed within the bounds of living well. Of these four which I have proposed, which you most approve, I ask you not to be reluctant to make clear by letter. For it makes a very great difference toward carrying through what we have undertaken in conversing between us, lest, while I strive to refute many unnecessary things—as though you thought what perhaps you do not think—the necessary time be consumed on superfluous matters. So I did not wish to burden you with a letter, that, by your writing back quickly, we may weave the rest together.
