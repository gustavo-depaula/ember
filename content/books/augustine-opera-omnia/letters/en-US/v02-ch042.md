# Letter 141

Silvanus the elder, Valentinus, Aurelius, Innocentius, Maximinus, Optatus, Augustine, Donatus, and the rest of the bishops of the Council of Zerta, to the Donatists.

**1.** Since the report grew frequent in our ears that your bishops say to you that the examiner was corrupted by a bribe, so that the sentence was brought forth against them, and that you easily believe these things, and on this account many of you still refuse to assent to the truth; it pleased us, the love of the Lord compelling, to send these writings to you from our council, by which you might first be admonished that those men, conquered and convicted, fling these lies: who even in their own Mandate, which they made for that conference, and confirmed with their names and subscriptions, saying that we there are traitors and their persecutors, were detected and convicted in falsehood and most manifest lying; so that, wishing to glory in the multitude of their fellow bishops, among the names of some absent ones they inserted even the name of a dead man; and when it was asked where he was, blinded by sudden disturbance, they themselves confessed that he had died on the journey. And when they were asked how he could subscribe at Carthage, who had already died on the journey, more vehemently disturbed, they bound themselves by another lie, answering that he had died returning from Carthage: from which lie they could in no way escape. Behold those whom you believe either about the ancient treachery, or about the corruption of the examiner, who could not write their Mandate, where they objected to us the crime of treachery, without the crime of falsehood. Accordingly, the things we believed most necessary we have inserted in this letter, gathered as it were into a summary, lest perhaps you either cannot easily come to the great volumes of the records, or think it laborious to read them.

**2.** We came to Carthage, both we and your bishops, and what before they were unwilling and said was unworthy, we came together into one. There were chosen from us and from them seven on this side, and seven on that, who should speak for the cause of all. There were chosen another seven on this side, and seven on that, with whom, where it was needful, they might deliberate counsel. There were chosen four on this side, and four on that, who should be guardians for the records to be written, lest anything falsified be said by anyone. There were given too by us and by them four notaries on this side, and four on that, that two with the recorders of the judge might alternate, lest any of us should claim he had said something which had not been recorded. To this so great diligence this too was added, that both we and they, just as the judge himself, should subscribe to our words, lest anyone say that in those records something was afterward corrupted. For since, while those who subscribed were still living, the same records became known in all places in which it behooves them to become known; so the truth, confirmed, will endure even to posterity. Do not, then, be ungrateful to so great a mercy of God, which through this diligence has been ministered to you. No excuse now remains; too hard, too diabolical are the hearts of men which still resist so great a manifestation of truth.

**3.** Behold, the bishops of your party, whom all chose to speak for all, tried, as much as they could, that the very cause should not be dealt with at all, on account of which so great a number of bishops of both parties had come to Carthage from all Africa, and from such far-off places. And when every soul, in suspense, awaited in so great a gathering what should be done, they vehemently insisted that nothing should be done. Why this, except because they knew their cause to be bad, and could not doubt that they could most easily be conquered, if it were dealt with? Their very mind, then, by which they feared lest the cause should be dealt with, showed them already conquered. For if they had extorted what they wished, that the conference itself should not now take place, nor the truth appear by our disputations: returning from Carthage, what would they answer you? What would they demonstrate? I believe they would bring forth the records, and say to you: We insisted that the cause should not be dealt with, they insisted that it should. You await to see what we did: behold, read where we conquered them, that we should do nothing. Perhaps you too would answer, if you had a heart: If you were going to do nothing, why to them? or rather, you who did nothing, why did you return?

**4.** Finally, after they could not effect what they tried—that is, that the cause should not be dealt with—the very action showed what they had feared, when they were conquered in all things. For they confessed that against the Catholic Church, which is spread through the whole world, they had nothing to say; because they were overwhelmed by the divine testimonies of the holy Scriptures, by which the Church is designated as beginning from Jerusalem, having grown through the places in which the Apostles preached, and having written the names of those same places in their Letters and Acts, and as being spread thence through the rest of the nations. Against this Church they professed with a manifest voice that they had no cause, where is our most evident victory in the name of God. For when they confirm the Church—with which it is manifest that we communicate, but they do not communicate—they attest that they have long been conquered; and to you, if you are wise, they most plainly indicate what you ought to forsake, and what to hold, not by that falsehood by which they do not cease still even now to lie to you, but by that truth which, conquered, they were compelled to confess.

**5.** Whoever, then, has been separated from this Catholic Church, however praiseworthily he may esteem himself to live, by this crime alone, that he is disjoined from the unity of Christ, will not have life; but the wrath of God remains over him. But whoever has lived well in this Church, the sins of others prejudice him in nothing; because each one in it will bear his own load, as the Apostle says. And whoever in it eats the body of Christ unworthily, eats and drinks judgment to himself: for this too the Apostle himself wrote. But when he says, "eats judgment to himself," he sufficiently shows that he eats judgment not to another, but to himself. This we maintained, and showed, and obtained, that the communion of the bad does not stain anyone by the participation of the Sacraments, but by the consent of deeds. For if in evil deeds anyone does not consent to them; the bad man bears his own cause and his own person, nor prejudices another whom he does not have as an associate of crime in the consent of an evil work.

**6.** This too they themselves were compelled to confess with a most open voice; not then when we were saying these things, but afterward when another matter was being dealt with. For when it had come to the cause of Caecilian too, which we distinguished from the cause of the Church—so that, if perhaps he were found bad, we would anathematize him, yet not on his account forsake the Church of Christ, to which he could not by his bad cause prejudice: when, then, it had come even to that cause of Caecilian, and they had read the council of Carthage, where they recited the sentences of seventy or so bishops brought forth against the absent Caecilian; we answered them that that council of bishops did not harm the absent Caecilian, in the same way as the council of very many bishops of the party of Donatus did not harm the absent Primian, when, in the cause of Maximian, about a hundred bishops had condemned him. Then they, the cause of Maximian being named—whence they know that they received even those whom they had condemned with honor intact, and that they confirmed and did not destroy the baptism given in the sacrilegious schism of Maximian; and in their Bagai sentence, by which they condemned them, gave a delay to certain who had been in that schism, and said that the seedlings of the sacrilegious shoot of Maximian did not pollute them: this cause, then, after it struck their ears, terrified and disturbed, and having forgotten whence above they were contending against us, at once said: Neither does cause prejudice cause, nor person person: and they confirmed by their own words what we earlier said about the Church, that not only to the Catholic Church overseas, against which they confessed they had nothing to say, but even to the Catholic Church of Africa, which is joined to it by the communion of unity, the cause and person of Caecilian, whatever it had been, could not prejudice; if Maximian, who with the rest of his associates condemned Primian, if even Felician, who together condemned Primian, and was afterward condemned in the cause of Primian by the party of Donatus, does not prejudice the party of Donatus, to which now, received as a bishop, as he had been before, he is joined; if even to his own associates Maximian does not prejudice, to whom these men gave a delay, saying that they were not polluted by him with whom they were, because neither does cause prejudice cause, nor person person.

**7.** What more, then, do you seek? They indeed loaded the records with many superfluous words; and because they could not effect that the cause should not be dealt with, they did this by speaking much, that what was done might be read with difficulty. But these few words of theirs too ought to suffice you, lest, on account of I know not what crimes of I know not what men, you hate the unity of the Catholic Church; because, as they themselves said, reread, subscribed: Neither does cause prejudice cause, nor person person. For even in the very cause of Caecilian, which, though not pertaining to the cause of the Church, we yet undertook to defend, so that even there their calumnies might be manifested, they were most openly conquered, and could prove nothing of the things they intended against Caecilian. Moreover, about the crimes of treachery, we brought forth episcopal records, whence we recited that some of those bishops who had spoken sentences against the absent Caecilian were most manifestly traitors. Against those very records they, because they had nothing to say, said they were false; but in no way could they prove it.

**8.** Moreover they confessed—or rather, professed as a great glory—that Caecilian was accused before the emperor Constantine by their predecessors. And they added a lie, that, those men accusing, he was condemned by the Emperor. Behold, here too they were conquered, whence they are wont to spread mists of error over you, stirring up odium against us, and bringing us into your hatred, because we deal with the cause of the Church before the Emperors. Behold, their forefathers, of whose names they glory, dealt with the cause of the Church before the Emperor; they persecuted, by accusing Caecilian before the Emperor, and said he was condemned. Now let them not seduce you with most empty and most lying words: return to your heart, fear the Lord, think on the truth, forsake falsehood. For whatever you have now suffered by the imperial laws—which you suffer not for justice but for iniquity—you will not be able to say that we are therefore unjust, because it ought not to have been so dealt with you, that the Emperor should restrain you from this iniquity. For your bishops confessed that their forefathers so dealt with Caecilian as you do not wish to be dealt with. And yet that they persecuted Caecilian before the Emperor, even by their own confessing and professing, it was sufficiently established: but that Caecilian was condemned by the Emperor, it was in no way established; nay rather, it was even established that he, their forefathers accusing and persecuting, was twice in number first by the bishops, and afterward by the Emperor himself, cleared. This too they themselves confirmed, by afterward bringing forth such things, as it were for their cause, that they were rather found to be against themselves, and even those things which they brought forth were recited for the cause of Caecilian. Whomever, then, they wished to accuse, they could prove by no certain documents: but whatever we said, both for the cause of the Church and for the cause of Caecilian, they themselves too confirmed both by their own words and by the readings brought forth by them.

**9.** For they brought forth first the codex of Optatus, whence they might as it were prove Caecilian condemned by the Emperor: which codex, when it was read against them, and rather showed Caecilian to have been cleared, they were laughed at by all. But because the laughter itself could not be recorded by the recorders, they attested in their own words, in the records, that they were laughed at. Again they recited and brought forth the petition given to the emperor Constantine by their forefathers, where they complained that the said emperor gravely persecuted them; and so by that same petition they manifested that they had been overcome before the Emperor by Caecilian, and that it was false what they had said, that he was condemned by the Emperor. Thirdly they brought forth a letter of the same Constantine given to the vicar Verinus, where he gravely detests them, and on this account says they are to be released from exile and dismissed to their own fury, because God had already begun to avenge upon them: and so by these letters too of the Emperor they confirmed that they had said falsely that Caecilian was condemned by the Emperor; since rather the Emperor showed that they themselves were conquered by Caecilian, when, vehemently execrating them, he therefore ordered that they be dismissed from exile, that, with God as judge, as they had already begun, they might be punished.

**10.** Afterward they brought into the midst the cause of Felix of Aptunga, the ordainer of Caecilian, saying that the same Felix was a traitor, by whom Caecilian had been ordained: and they brought forth a letter of the same emperor Constantine, for Caecilian against themselves, where he wrote to the proconsul that Ingentius should be sent to his Court. But this Ingentius had confessed, in the examination of the proconsul Aelianus, that he had committed a forgery against Felix the ordainer of Caecilian. They said, then, that the Emperor had not without cause wished Ingentius to be sent to him, except because the cause of Caecilian was still pending: and they tried to insinuate a most empty suspicion, that after Ingentius was sent to the Court, then the Emperor could judge against Caecilian, and rescind that judgment of his which we had recited, where, having examined the parties, he had cleared Caecilian, by a later sentence. But it was said to them that they should rather read this; and they brought forth nothing at all. But those letters of the Emperor, by which he ordered Ingentius to be sent to him, which they recited against themselves for Caecilian, contain this: that the proconsul Aelianus had afforded a competent hearing in the cause of Felix, and that it had been established that the same Felix was innocent of the crime of treachery: but that he ordered Ingentius to be sent to his Court for this reason, that it might appear and be intimated to those who were there, and did not cease to importune him daily, that they had wished in vain to procure odium against Caecilian, and to rise up violently against him.

**11.** Who would believe that they recited these things against themselves for us, unless it had been done by the will of omnipotent God, that not only their words should be held in the records, but even the hands of those subscribing should be read? For also the order of the consuls and days, which is expressed in the records, if anyone now diligently notices, will find that first Caecilian was cleared by episcopal judgment. Then, not long after, the cause of Felix of Aptunga was examined by the proconsul Aelianus, where it was established that he was innocent: in which cause Ingentius was ordered to be sent to the Court. And long afterward the Emperor himself examined and ended the cause between the parties: in which examination he judged Caecilian innocent, but them most calumnious. In which order of consuls and days it is sufficiently shown that they said fallaciously and calumniously that the Emperor, Ingentius being sent to the Court, changed his judgment, and afterward condemned Caecilian whom he had before cleared. But not only could they read nothing about this matter, and read so much against themselves; but even by the order of the consuls they are more evidently convicted that, after the cause of Felix was concluded by proconsular judgment—in which it was ordered that Ingentius be sent to the Court—and not at a small interval, but long afterward, the judgment of the said emperor being held between the parties, Caecilian was cleared.

**12.** Let them not, then, tell you that we corrupted the judge by a bribe. For what else are conquered men wont to say? Or, if we gave anything to the judge, that he might pronounce against them for us; what did we give to them, that against themselves they should for us not only say but even recite so much? Or do they perhaps wish that we should give them thanks before you, because, while they say the judge was corrupted by us with a bribe, they themselves freely furnished us with so many things, which they said and recited for us against themselves? Or surely, if they say that they conquered us on this account, that they dealt with the cause of Caecilian better than we; this indeed believe them. For we thought two readings sufficient for him: but they brought forth four.

**13.** But why do we load the letter with more? If you wish to believe us, believe; and let us alike hold the unity which God commands and loves. But if you do not wish to believe us, read the records themselves, or allow them to be read to you, and prove for yourselves whether the things we have written to you are true. But if you wish to do none of these things, and still desire to follow the falsehood of the party of Donatus, convicted by most manifest truth, we are clean from your penalty, when you will too late afterward repent. But if you shall not despise that which God has furnished you; and, after a cause so diligently dealt with, and so diligently manifested, forsaking your perverse custom, you shall consent to the peace and unity of Christ, we will rejoice over your correction: and the Sacraments of Christ, which in the sacrilege of schism you have unto judgment, will be useful and wholesome to you when in Catholic peace you shall have Christ as head, where love covers a multitude of sins. These things we have written to you on the fourteenth of June, in the ninth consulship of the most devout Honorius Augustus, that this letter might reach each of you when it could.
