# Letter 179

To the most blessed lord, and deservedly venerable brother and fellow bishop John, from Augustine, greetings in the Lord.

**1.** That I did not deserve writings from your holiness, I dare in no way to be angry: for I believe rather that a bearer was lacking, than I suspect that I was despised by your veneration, most blessed lord, and deservedly venerable brother. But now, since I have learned that the servant of God Lucas, through whom I have directed these things, would quickly return, I will give to the Lord, and to your kindness, abundant thanks, if you shall have deigned to visit me with a letter. But to Pelagius our brother, your son, whom I hear you love much, I suggest that you show him this love: that the men who have known him and diligently heard him may not suppose that your holiness is deceived by him.

**2.** For certain of his disciples, young men most honorably born, and instructed in liberal studies, left the hope they had in the world by his exhortation, and betook themselves to the service of God. In whom, however, when there had appeared certain things adverse to sound doctrine, which is contained in the Gospel of the Savior and declared by apostolic discourses—that is, when they were found to dispute against the grace of God, on account of which we are Christians, and in which by the spirit, from faith, we await the hope of righteousness—and they began to be amended by our admonitions, they gave me a book which they said was that same Pelagius's, asking that I rather answer it: which, after I saw I ought to do, so that in that way the wicked error itself might be more perfectly taken from their hearts, I read and answered.

**3.** In this book he calls the grace of God nothing but the nature by which we were created with free choice. But that grace which holy Scripture commends by innumerable testimonies, teaching that by it we are justified, that is, made righteous, and in every good work, whether in doing or in perfecting, are helped by the mercy of God—which even the prayers of the saints most plainly show, by which those things are asked from the Lord which are commanded by the Lord—this grace, then, he not only passes over in silence, but speaks many things contrary to it. For he affirms, and vehemently contends, that human nature can suffice itself, through free choice alone, for working righteousness and keeping all the commandments of God. Whence who would not see, when he has read this same book, how the grace of God is assailed, of which the Apostle says: *Wretched man that I am! Who will free me from the body of this death? The grace of God through our Lord Jesus Christ*[^1]; and that no place is left for divine help, on account of which, praying, we ought to say, *Lead us not into temptation*; and the Lord too seems to have said in vain to the apostle Peter, *I have prayed for you, that your faith fail not*[^2], if this whole is fulfilled in us with no help of God, but by the power of the will?

**4.** By these perverse and impious disputations, then, not only is there contradiction to our prayers, by which we ask of the Lord whatever we read and hold that the saints asked; but there is also resistance to our blessings, when over the people we say, wishing for them and asking from the Lord, that he may *make them abound in love toward one another, and toward all*[^3], and *grant them, according to the riches of his glory, to be strengthened with power through his Spirit*[^4]; and *fill them with all joy and peace in believing, that they may abound in hope, and in the power of the Holy Spirit*[^5]. Why do we ask these things for them, which we know the Apostle asked from the Lord for the peoples, if now our nature, created with free choice, can grant all these to itself by its own will? Why too does the same Apostle say: *For as many as are led by the Spirit of God, these are sons of God*[^6], if we are led by the spirit of our nature, that we may be made sons of God? Why does he say likewise: *The Spirit helps our weakness*[^7], if our nature is so created that it does not need to be helped by the Spirit to the works of righteousness? Why is it written: *But God is faithful, who will not permit you to be tempted beyond what you are able, but will make with the temptation also a way out, that you may be able to endure it*[^8], if we are now so constituted that by the powers of free choice we can, by enduring, overcome all temptations?

**5.** Why should I deal at greater length with your holiness? Since I feel myself burdensome; especially because you hear my letter through an interpreter. If you love Pelagius, let him too love you, or rather himself; and let him not deceive you. For when you hear him confess the grace of God and the help of God, you think he says this which you too, who are wise by the catholic rule, mean, since you do not know what he has written in his book. On account of this, I have sent the book itself, and mine by which I answered it; whence your venerability may perceive what grace or help of God he means, when it is charged against him that he contradicts the grace and help of God. Accordingly, show him, by teaching, and exhorting, and by praying for his salvation, which ought to be in Christ, that he confess that grace of God which the holy ones of God are proved to have confessed, when they asked from the Lord those very things which he commanded them to do: since they would neither be commanded, except that our will might be shown; nor would they be asked for, except that the weakness of the will might be helped by him who had commanded.

**6.** Let him be openly asked whether it pleases him that we must pray to the Lord that we not sin. And if this displeases him, let the Apostle be read in his ears, saying: *But we pray to God that you do no evil*[^9]: but if it pleases him, let him openly preach the grace by which we are helped, lest he himself do much evil. For by this grace of God, through Jesus Christ our Lord, all are freed, whoever are freed: since no one, apart from it, can be freed in any other way. On account of this it is written: *As in Adam all die, so also in Christ all will be made alive*[^10]: not because no one will be damned, but because no one will be freed otherwise. Because, as none but through Adam are sons of man, so none but through Christ are sons of God. All sons of man, therefore, can come to be only through Adam, and all of them sons of God only through Christ. Let him, then, openly express here too what he means: whether it pleases him that even little ones, who cannot yet will or refuse righteousness, are nevertheless, on account of the one man, through whom sin entered into the world, and through sin death, and so it passed to all men, in whom all sinned, freed through the grace of Christ; whether he believes that even for them the blood of Christ was poured out on account of original sin, which surely was poured out for the remission of sins. About these things especially we wish to know from him what he believes, what he holds, what he certainly confesses and preaches. But in the other things which are charged against him, even if he is convicted of erring, yet, until he is corrected, he is more tolerably borne with.

**7.** I ask too that you deign to transmit to us the ecclesiastical proceedings by which he is reported to have been cleared. This I ask from the desire of many bishops, whom, with me, an uncertain report about this matter disturbs: but I have written this alone for this reason, because I was unwilling to let pass the occasion of the bearer hastening from us, whom I heard could quickly return to us. In place of these proceedings he has now sent us, not indeed any part of the proceedings, but a certain defense as it were written by himself, by which he said he had answered the objections of the Gauls. In which, to omit other things, when he answered that which was charged against him, that he had said a man can be without sin, and keep the commandments of God if he wills: "We said," he says, "this; for God gave him this possibility. We did not say that anyone is found, from infancy to old age, who has never sinned; but that, converted from sins by his own labor, and helped by the grace of God, he can be without sin, nor on this account will he be in the future unable to be turned."

**8.** In this answer of Pelagius your reverence sees that he has confessed this: that the earlier life of a man, which is from infancy, is not without sin, but that he can be converted to a life which is without sin, by his own labor and helped by the grace of God. Why, then, in this book to which I answered, does he say that someone lived here so that he sinned nothing at all? For these are his words about this matter: "This," he says, "can rightly be said of those of whose good or evil the Scripture makes no mention: but of those whose righteousness it recalls, it would without doubt also have recalled their sins, if it had perceived that they had sinned in anything. But suppose," he says, "that at other times, on account of the great number of the crowd, it dissembled to weave together the sins of all; in the very first beginning of the world, where there were only four men, what," he says, "do we say, why it would not wish to recall the deeds of all? Was it on account of an enormous multitude, which did not yet exist? or because it recalled only those who had committed something, but of him who had committed nothing it could not have recalled? Certainly," he says, "at the first time Adam and Eve, from whom Cain and Abel were born, are reported to have been only four men. Eve sinned; the Scripture revealed this: Adam too transgressed; the same Scripture did not keep silent: but that Cain also sinned the Scripture itself too testified. Of whom it indicates not only the sins, but even the quality of the sins. But if Abel too had sinned," he says, "this too the Scripture would without doubt have said: if it did not say it, then he too did not sin."

**9.** These words I have plucked from his book, which your holiness will be able to find in that same volume too; so that you may understand how you ought to believe him denying the rest. Unless perhaps he should say that Abel himself sinned nothing; but that for this reason he was not without sin, and therefore cannot be compared to the Lord, who alone in mortal flesh was without sin, because there was in Abel original sin, which he had drawn from Adam, not committed of himself in himself: would that he might say at least this, so that we might meanwhile hold his certain opinion about the baptism of little ones! Or if perhaps, because he said from infancy to old age, he therefore says that Abel did not sin, because he is not shown to have grown old. His words do not indicate this: he said the earlier life from the beginning was sinful; but the later can be without sin. For he says that he did not say "that anyone is found, from infancy to old age, who has not sinned; but that, converted from sins by his own labor, and helped by the grace of God, he can be without sin." For when he says "converted from sins," he shows that the earlier life is led in sins. Let him confess, then, that Abel sinned, whose first life was in the world, which he confesses does not lack sins, and let him look at his own book, where it is established that he said what he says in this defense: "We did not say."

**10.** But if he denies that even this book, or this place in this book, is his; I indeed have suitable witnesses, honorable and faithful men, and without doubt his lovers, by whose attestation I can clear myself, that they themselves gave me this same book, and that this is read there, and that they said it was Pelagius's—so that at least this may suffice for me, that he not say it was either written or falsified by me. Now among those let each choose whom to believe; it is not mine to dispute longer about this matter. We ask that you at least transmit to him, if he denies that he holds these things which are charged against him as hostile to the grace of Christ. For his defense is so covered that, if it deceives your holy prudence, who do not know his other writings, by no ambiguity of words, we will rejoice with great joy; not much caring whether he never held those perverse and impious things, or has at some time corrected himself from them.
