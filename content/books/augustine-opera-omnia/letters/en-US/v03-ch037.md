# Letter 188

To the lady, to be honored with the offices due in Christ, and deservedly illustrious daughter Juliana, from Alypius and Augustine, greetings in the Lord.

**1. 1.** It happened to us quite gratefully and pleasantly that the letter of your reverence found us set together at Hippo, so that we might equally render this reply, rejoicing at the news of your well-being, and in turn announcing ours—which we trust is dear to you—by mutual love, lady to be honored with the offices due in Christ, and deservedly illustrious daughter. But we know quite well how great an affection of religion we owe you, and how great care of you there is to us both before God and among men. For although our littleness first by letters, then also by bodily presence, came to know you, pious and catholic, that is, true members of Christ; yet even through our ministry, when you had received the word of the hearing of God, as the Apostle says, you received it not as the word of men, but, as it truly is, the word of God. Of which our ministry, with the grace and mercy of the Savior helping, so great a fruit arose in your house, that, human nuptials being already prepared, holy Demetrias preferred the spiritual embrace of that Bridegroom, to whom, beautiful beyond the sons of men, virgins are wedded, to have the more abundant fecundity of the spirit, and not to lose the integrity of the flesh. But we would not know how that exhortation of ours was then received by the faithful and noble virgin, had we not learned, by the most joyful news and truthful testimony of your letter, that, after we had set out, when after a little while she had professed virginal holiness, this enormous gift of God—which through his servants he plants and waters, but through himself gives the increase—came to pass through us as workers.

**1. 2.** Since these things are so, no one will call us improper, if, in admonishing you to avoid the dogmas contrary to the grace of God, we are anxious with a more propense intimacy. For although the Apostle commands us to be insistent in preaching the word not only in season, but even out of season; yet we do not have you in the number of those men to whom our discourse or page would seem out of season, when we address you that you may vigilantly avoid that which does not pertain to sound doctrine. Hence, then, it is that you received our admonition with so grateful a mind, that in your letter, to which we now write back, you said: "Indeed, as to what your reverence exhorts me, that I not lend my ears to those men who often corrupt the venerable faith by depraved treatises, I give abundant thanks for so pious an admonition."

**1. 3.** What, then, you add and say: "But let your priesthood know that I and my little house are far separated from persons of this kind: and our whole family so follows the catholic faith, that it has at no time deviated into any heresy, nor ever lapsed; I do not say into those sects which are scarcely expiated, but not even into those which are seen to have small errors"; this is what compels us more and more not to keep silence with you about those who try to vitiate even the things that are sound. For we reckon your house no small Church of Christ. Nor indeed is the error of those small who think that we have from ourselves whatever of righteousness, continence, piety, chastity is in us, on the ground that God so created us, that beyond—besides the knowledge which he reveals to us—he helps us in nothing to do, by loving too, the things which we know by learning are to be done; defining, namely, that nature and doctrine alone are the grace of God and help, that we may live rightly and justly. But for having a good will—where this very thing is, that we live justly, and the very love which so excels among all the gifts of God that it is even called God, by which alone is fulfilled in us whatever of the divine law and admonition we fulfill—they are unwilling that we be helped divinely, but say that we ourselves suffice for ourselves to these things by our own choice. Let not this error seem to you slight: to profess oneself willing to be a Christian, and to be unwilling to hear the Apostle of Christ, who, when he had said: *The love of God is poured out in our hearts*; lest anyone think that he has it only through his own choice, immediately added, *by the Holy Spirit who has been given to us*[^1]. That this is the great grace of the Savior, who, ascending on high, led captivity captive and gave gifts to men—whoever does not yet confess it, you understand how much and how destructively he errs.

**2. 4.** How, then, could we dissimulate about admonishing you, to whom we owe so great a love, that you beware of such things, when we had read a book—whom anyone wrote to holy Demetrias, or whether it reached you, we wish rather to know from your reply—in which book, if it is lawful, let the virgin of Christ read whence she may believe that her virginal holiness, and all her spiritual riches, are from herself alone; and so, before she be most fully blessed, let her learn to be—which God forbid—ungrateful to God. For the words written to her in that same book are these: "You have, then, even here," he says, "things by which you may deservedly be set before others; nay rather, hence the more: for bodily nobility and opulence will be understood to be of your people, not yours; but spiritual riches no one but you yourself can confer on you. In these, then, you are rightly to be praised, in these deservedly to be preferred to the rest, which cannot be except from you and in you."

**2. 5.** You see, surely, how great a ruin is to be guarded against in these words. For that which was said: "These goods cannot be except in you," was said most excellently and most truly; this plainly is food: but what he says, "except from you," this is altogether poison. Far be it that the virgin of Christ hear these things gladly, who piously understands the proper poverty of the human heart, and therefore there knows not how to be adorned except by the gifts of her Bridegroom. Let her hear rather the Apostle saying: *I betrothed you to one husband, to present you a chaste virgin to Christ. But I fear lest, as the serpent seduced Eve by his cunning, so also your minds be corrupted from the chastity which is in Christ*[^2]: and therefore about these spiritual riches too, let her hear, not him who says, "No one can confer them on you but you, and they cannot be except from you and in you"; but him who says, *We have this treasure in earthen vessels, that the eminence of power may be God's, and not from us*[^3].

**2. 6.** About that holy virginal continence too—that it is not to her from herself, but is a gift of God, although bestowed on one believing and willing—let her hear that same truthful and pious teacher, who, when he dealt with this, says: *I would wish all to be as myself; but each one has his own gift from God, one in this way, another in that*[^4]. Let her hear too him who is not only her own, but the unique Bridegroom of the whole Church, saying about such chastity and integrity: *Not all grasp this word, but those to whom it is given*[^5]; that she may understand, from the fact that she has so great and splendid a good, that she ought rather to give thanks to God and our Lord, than to hear the words of anyone—that she has it as it were from herself—words, not to say of a flattering sycophant, lest we seem rashly to judge of the hidden things of men, but certainly of an erring praiser. For *every best gift, and every perfect gift*, as the apostle James too says, *is from above, descending from the Father of lights*[^6]: hence, then, holy virginity too, by which the daughter, willing and rejoicing, conquered you—later in birth, prior in deed, by lineage from you, in honor before you, in age following, in holiness preceding; in which there began to be yours too what could not be in you. For she did not wed carnally, that she might be increased spiritually not only for herself, but even for you beyond yourself: since you too are less than she by this compensation, that you so wedded that she might be born. These are gifts of God, and they are indeed yours, but not from you: for you have this treasure in earthly bodies, and as yet fragile, as in earthen vessels, that the eminence of power may be God's, and not from you. Nor wonder that we say it is both yours and not from you: for we call the daily bread ours too, but yet add: Give us, lest it be thought to be "from us."

**2. 7.** Accordingly, as it is written: *Pray without ceasing, in all things give thanks*[^7]: for you pray as those having perseveringly and advancingly; you give thanks, because you have it not from yourselves. For who distinguishes you from that mass of death and perdition from Adam? Is it not he who came to seek and to save what had perished? Or, when a man hears the Apostle saying: *For who distinguishes you?* is he going to answer, My good will, my faith, my righteousness, and not immediately to hear what follows: *For what have you that you did not receive? But if you have received it, why do you boast as if you had not received it?*[^8] We do not wish, then, that the holy virgin, when she hears or reads: "Spiritual riches no one can confer on you but you: in these you are rightly to be praised, in these deservedly to be preferred to the rest, which cannot be except from you and in you"; we do not at all wish her so to boast as though she had not received. Let her say indeed: *In me, O God, are your vows, which I will render as praises to you*[^9]: but because in her, not also from her, let her remember to say too: *Lord, in your will you bestowed strength upon my beauty*[^10]: because, even if it be also from her on account of her own choice, without which we do not work good, yet not, as that man said, except from her. For one's own choice, unless it be helped by the grace of God, cannot itself even be a good will in name. *For it is God*, the Apostle says, *who works in you both to will and to work, for his good will*[^11]: not, as those people think, only by revealing knowledge, that we may know what we ought to do; but also by inspiring love, that what we know by learning, we may also do by loving.

**2. 8.** For surely he knew how great a good continence was, who said: *And when I knew that no one can be continent unless God grant it*. Not only, then, did he know how great this good was, and how desirably it was to be longed for, but also that, unless God gave it, it could not be: for wisdom had taught him; for he says this: *And this very thing was wisdom, to know whose gift it was*. Nor yet did knowledge suffice him; but he says: *I went to the Lord, and besought him*[^12]. Not only, therefore, in this does God help us, that we may know what is to be done, but also that, by loving, we may do what we already know by learning. No one, then, can be not only knowing, but even continent, unless God grant it. Whence, when he already had knowledge, he prayed that he might have continence too, that there might be in him too what he knew, because it would not be from him: or, if on account of free choice, something also somewhat his own and from him, yet not except from him; because no one can be continent unless God grant it. But that man, about spiritual riches—among which surely is also that very luminous and beautiful continence—does not say: "They can be in you and from you"; but says, "they cannot be except from you and in you"; that, just as they are to her nowhere except in her, so they are believed to be able to be to her from nowhere except from her, and on account of this (which the merciful Lord turn away from her heart!) she so boast as though she had not received.

**3. 9.** And we indeed, about the holy virgin's discipline and Christian humility, in which she was nourished and brought up, think this: that when she read those words—if indeed she read them—she groaned, and humbly beat her breast, and perhaps even wept, and confidently prayed to the Lord to whom she is dedicated and by whom she is sanctified, that, just as those are not the words of herself, but of another, so neither may her faith be such as to believe that she has anything about which she may boast in herself, not in the Lord. For her glory is indeed in herself, not in the words of another, as the Apostle says: *But let each one prove his own work, and then he will have glory in himself, and not in another*[^13]. But far be it that her glory be herself, and not he to whom it is said, *My glory, and the lifter up of my head!*[^14] For so, surely, is her glory in her wholesomely, when God who is in her is himself her glory, from whom she has all the goods by which she is good; and will have all by which she will be better, insofar as in this life she can be better; and by which she will be perfect, when she shall have been perfected by divine grace, not by human praise. For *in the Lord her soul will be praised*[^15], who *satisfied her desire with good things*[^16]; because even this desire he himself inspired, that his virgin might not so boast in any good as though she had not received it.

**3. 10.** About this affection of hers, then, whether we are not deceived, make us rather more certain by writing back. For that we know quite well: that you, with all yours, are and have been worshipers of the undivided Trinity. But not from this alone does human error creep in, that something be thought otherwise about the undivided Trinity. For there are also other things in which one errs most perniciously, as is this, about which we have perhaps spoken in this letter longer than would be enough for your faithful and chaste prudence. Although whoever denies that the good which is from God is from God, we do not know to whom he does injury except to God, and through this surely to that Trinity: which evil be far from you, as we believe it is far! Far be it altogether that this book should have done any such thing in the mind—we do not say of yours, or of your daughter the holy virgin, but in that of any servant or handmaid of yours of the lowest merit—from which we thought certain words, which could be more easily understood, should be set down.

**3. 11.** But if you attend more diligently even to those things which there he seems to say as it were for the grace or help of God, you will find them so ambiguous that they can be referred either to nature, or to doctrine, or to the remission of sins. For even as to what they are compelled to confess—that we ought to pray that we enter not into temptation—they can refer it to this: that they answer that we are helped to this only so far as that, to us praying and knocking, the understanding of the truth is opened, where we may learn what we ought to do, not that our will may receive strength by which we may do what we learn. And as to what they say to us, about the grace or help of God, that the Lord Christ is a set example of living well, they recall it to that same doctrine; because, namely, in his example we learn how we ought to live: but they do not wish us to be helped to this, that what we know by learning, we may also do by loving.

**3. 12.** Or surely, find in that same book something, if you can, where—nature being excepted, that being excepted which pertains to that same nature, the choice of the will, the remission of sins and the revelation of doctrine being excepted—he confesses such a help of God as he confesses who said: *When I knew that no one can be continent unless God grant it, and this very thing was wisdom, to know whose gift it was, I went to the Lord and besought him*[^17]. For that man neither wished by praying to receive the nature in which he had been created; nor was busying himself about the natural choice of the will, with which he had been created; nor was desiring the remission of sins, who rather longed for continence, that he might not sin; nor was wishing to know what was to be done, since he already confessed that he knew whose gift it was: but he surely wished to receive such great strength of will, such great ardor of love, by the Spirit of wisdom, as would suffice to fulfill the greatness of continence. If, then, you can find any such thing there, we will give most abundant thanks, if you deign to intimate it by writing back.

**3. 13.** For it cannot be said enough how much we desire that, in the writings of those men who, on account of their acuteness and eloquence, are read by very many, there be found an open confession of that grace which the Apostle vehemently commends, who says that God divided to each one even the measure of faith itself (without which it is impossible to please God, from which the just lives, which works through love, before which and without which the good works of anyone are to be esteemed altogether nothing, since *everything that is not from faith is sin*[^18]): and that we are helped divinely not by the revelation of knowledge alone, that we may live piously and justly, which without love puffs up; but also by the inspiration of that very love, which is the fullness of the law, and which builds up our heart, so that knowledge may not puff up. But as yet nowhere have we been able to find any such thing in their writings.

**3. 14.** But we would most wish this to be in that book, from which we set down those copied words, where, praising the virgin of Christ as though no one but herself could confer spiritual riches on her, and as though they could not be except from her, he does not wish her to boast in the Lord, but to boast as though she had not received. In which book, although he expressed neither his own name nor that of your reverence, yet he recalls that it was asked of him by the virgin's mother that he write to her. But in a certain letter of his, the same Pelagius, where he both sets down his own name most plainly, and does not keep silent the name of the holy virgin, says that he wrote to her, and strives to prove, by the testimony of his own work, that he most plainly confesses the grace of God which he is asserted either to keep silent or to deny. But whether it is the book in which he set down those words about spiritual riches, or whether it has reached your holiness, we ask that you deign to make us more certain by your reply.
