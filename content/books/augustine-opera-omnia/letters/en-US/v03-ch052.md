# Letter 202/A

TO OPTATUS, MOST BLESSED AND SINCERELY MOST DEAR AND MOST LONGED-FOR LORD, BROTHER AND FELLOW BISHOP, AUGUSTINE SENDS GREETING IN THE LORD.

**1. 1.** Through the religious presbyter Saturninus I received the letter of your Veneration, demanding of me with great zeal what I do not yet have. But you opened to me the reason why you did this: namely, that you believe an answer has already been given to me consulting about this matter. Would that it were so! Far be it from me that I should defraud you—whose most eager expectation I know—of the sharing of this gift. But if you believe anything, dearest brother, behold, nearly five years have rolled by since I sent to the East a book not of my presumption but of my consultation; and I have not yet merited a reply by which this question might be unraveled for me—the question in which you desire me to bring you a sure verdict. I would, then, have sent both, if I had both.

**1. 2.** But this which I have, without the other which I do not yet have, it does not seem to me that I now owe to send or publish to anyone; lest he who is perhaps going to answer me, as I desire, rightly be angry that my inquiry, elaborated with most laborious discussion, should be spread through men's hands and notice without his answer, which is not yet to be despaired of; and lest he judge that I have done this more boastfully than usefully, as though I could ask what he could not unravel, when perhaps he can, and while he does it, it must be awaited. For I know rather that he is occupied with other things which must by no means be deferred, and must be weighed at a higher price.

**1. 3.** That your Holiness too may know this, attend a little to what he wrote to me in another year, by the bearer through whom I had written, as he was returning; for I have transferred this from his letter into this one: "There fell out," he says, "a most difficult time, when it was better for me to be silent than to speak; so that our studies ceased, lest, according to Appius, a doglike eloquence be exercised. And so to your two little books, which you dedicated to my name, most learned and shining with all the splendor of eloquence, I could not for a time reply—not because I think there is anything in them to be reprehended, but because, according to the blessed Apostle, *Each one abounds in his own sense; one indeed thus, another thus*[^1]. Certainly whatever could be said, and drawn by a lofty genius from the founts of the holy Scriptures, has been set down and discussed by you. But I beg your Reverence, suffer a little that I praise your genius. For we discourse among ourselves for the sake of erudition. But rivals, and especially heretics, if they see diverse opinions between us, will cavil that they descend from rancor of mind. But for me it is decreed to love, receive, cultivate, admire you, and to defend your sayings as my own. Certainly even in the dialogue which I lately published I made mention of your Blessedness, as was fitting: and let us rather give our effort that this most pernicious heresy be removed from the Churches, which always feigns penitence that it may have the faculty of teaching in the Churches; lest, if it betrays itself in the open light, expelled outdoors, it die."

**2. 4.** You see, then, venerable brother, that these words of my dearest one, given in reply to my inquiry, did not deny that answer, but excused it on account of the time, because he was compelled to spend his care on other more urgent things. You see also how benevolent a mind he bears toward me, and what he admonishes; namely, lest what we do among ourselves for the sake of erudition—with charity and the sincerity of friendship surely preserved—rivals, and especially heretics, should cavil descends from rancor of mind. Accordingly, if men read both our works, both where I have inquired and where he has answered the things inquired about—because it also behooves that, if the same question has been sufficiently explained according to his opinion, I should give thanks that I have been instructed—the fruit will be not small, when this comes into the notice of very many: so that those after us may not only know what they ought to think about this matter, which has been discussed between us by diligent debate, but may also learn, by our example, by God's mercy and favor, how, among dearest brethren, the disputation of mutual inquiry need not be lacking, yet love remain inviolate.

**2. 5.** But if my writing, where only a most obscure matter is read as inquired, without his reply, where perhaps the finding will appear, should go abroad, let it proceed more widely, let it reach even those who, *comparing*, as the Apostle says, *themselves with themselves*[^2], do not understand with what mind something is done by us, which they themselves do not know how to do with that mind; and they will set forth my goodwill toward a most beloved friend, to be honored for his immense merits, not as they see it (since they do not even see it), but as it pleases them, and as, at the dictation of their hatred, they surmise: which surely, as far as is in us, we ought to guard against.

**2. 6.** But if perhaps what we do not wish to become known through us should, even against our will, become known to those to whom we do not wish it: what will remain, except to bear the will of the Lord with an equal mind? For I ought not to write to anyone what I had always wished to lie hidden. For if—which God forbid—by some chance or necessity he should never write back, doubtless our consultation, which we sent to him, will at some time be made manifest. Nor will it be useless to readers: because, even if they do not find those things they seek, they will surely find how they are to be inquired into and not rashly affirmed, the things they do not know; and, according to what they read there, they too will take care, with studious charity, not discordant contention, to consult those whom they can; until either they find what they want, or by the very inquiry exercise the edge of the mind, so that they recognize that there is no need to inquire further. Now, however, so long as the answer of the friend already consulted is not yet to be despaired of, that our consultation must not be published—as far, at least, as is in us—I think I have persuaded your Charity: although you yourself did not ask for it alone, but desired that there be sent to you, joined to it, also the answer of the one I consulted; which I would surely do, if I had it. But if, to use the words of your Holiness which you set down in your letter—"a lucid demonstration of my wisdom, which the Author of light has attributed to me for the merit, as you write, of my life"—you do not mean by this my very consultation and inquiry, but think that the finding of that thing which I sought has already come to me, and ask rather for it, that I should send it: I would do it, if it were as you think. For I, I confess, have not yet found how the soul draws sin from Adam (whence it is not lawful to doubt), and is not itself drawn from Adam; which I must more diligently inquire into than more inconsiderately assert.

**3. 7.** Your letter has: "I know not how many old men, and men instructed by learned priests, whom you could not recall to the understanding of your modesty and to an assertion most full of truth"; nor yet do you express what is "your assertion most full of truth, to which you could not recall the old men, and men instructed by learned priests." For if these old men held, or hold, what they received from learned priests, how had the rustic and less instructed crowd of clerics generated annoyances for you in those matters in which they had been "instructed by learned priests"? But if those old men, or the crowd of clerics, by their own depravity deviated from what they had received from learned priests, they ought rather to have been corrected by those priests' authority, and restrained from a most contentious tumult. But again, when you say that, "being a new and untrained teacher, you feared to corrupt the traditions of so great and such bishops, and to convert men to the better part, and dreaded the injury of the deceased," what do you give to understand, except that those whom you desired to correct, by being unwilling to forsake the traditions of learned and great bishops now deceased, refused to acquiesce in a "new and untrained teacher"? In which matter I am for now silent about them; but I vehemently desire to know your "assertion," which you say is "most full of truth"—I do not mean the opinion itself, but its assertion.

**4. 8.** For that you disapprove of those who affirm that all the souls of men are propagated and transmitted from that one which was given to the first-formed man, through the succession of generations, you have indeed sufficiently brought to our notice; but by what reasoning, or by what testimonies of the divine Scriptures, you have shown this to be false—because your letter does not contain it—we do not know. Next, what you yourself hold in place of that which you disapprove, does not evidently appear to me as I read your letter—both the one which you formerly sent to the brethren of Caesarea, and the one which you most recently directed to me—except that I see you believe, as you write, that "God made men, and makes them, and will make them; and that there is nothing in heaven or on earth which has not stood and does not stand by him as author." This indeed is so true that no one ought to doubt it. But it still behooves you to express whence God makes the souls which you deny are made from propagation: whether from somewhere else, and, if so, what that is; or altogether from nothing. For that view of Origen and Priscillian, or if there are any others who think anything of the kind—that earthly and mortal bodies are given against the merits of a prior life—far be it that you should think it: for to this opinion apostolic authority is utterly opposed, saying that Esau and Jacob, before they were born, had done nothing good or evil. Therefore not wholly, but in part, your opinion on this matter is known to us; but its assertion—that is, whence it is taught to be true what you think—is wholly hidden from us.

**4. 9.** For this reason I had asked in my earlier letter that you deign to send me the Booklet of Faith which you recall you wrote, and to which you complained that some presbyter deceitfully subscribed; which even now I ask, and what testimonies of the divine writings you were able to apply to unlocking this question. For you say in the letter to the Caesareans that it pleased you that, for the full proof of the truth, even secular judges should take cognizance; with whom presiding, at the common entreaty, and examining all things for the faith, the Divinity, as you write, by the infusion of his mercy bestowed this: that they should bring forth a greater affirmation and assertion for their views than your modesty against them was maintaining with the authorities of immense testimonies. These authorities of immense testimonies, then, I desire with immense zeal to know.

**4. 10.** For you seem to have followed one single cause, by which to refute your contradictors: namely, that they denied that our souls are the work of God. But if they think this, deservedly is their opinion judged to be condemned. For if they said this of the very bodies, they would doubtless have to be amended or detested. For what Christian would deny that the bodies of each one being born are works of God? Nor yet do we therefore deny that they are begotten by parents, because we confess that they are divinely fashioned. When, then, it is said that, likewise, there are certain incorporeal seeds of our souls, of their own kind, and that they are both drawn from parents and yet that from them the souls are made by the work of God—to refute this, not human conjecture but divine Scripture must be brought as witness. For from the holy Books of canonical authority there could be supplied to us a supply of testimonies by which it is proved that God makes souls: but by such testimonies those are refuted who deny that each single soul in men being born is a work of God; not those who confess this, and yet contend that they, like bodies, are indeed formed by God working, but from the propagation of parents. To refute these you must seek sure divine testimonies; or, if you have already found them, they must, in mutual charity, be sent to us who have not yet found them, although we inquire most earnestly, as far as we can.

**5. 11.** For your consultation, brief and last, in the letter which you sent to the brethren of Caesarea, runs thus: "I entreat," you say, "that you teach me, your son and disciple, and one newly and recently coming, by God's help, to these mysteries, by such instruction as you ought and as is fitting, and as it befits prudent priests to answer: whether rather that opinion is to be held which says the soul is by transmission, and that through a certain hidden origin and secret order the other souls flow into the whole race of men from the transfusion of Adam the first-formed; or rather that definition is to be chosen, and that belief retained, which all your brethren and the priests set here retain and affirm, which testifies and believes that God was, and is, and will be the Author of all things and of all men." Of these two, then, which by consulting you have proposed, you wish that one be chosen, and that one of the two be answered to you; which ought to be done by those who know, if those two were so contrary to each other that, one being chosen, the other should consequently be rejected.

**5. 12.** But now, if someone should not choose one of these two, but answer that both are true—that is, that the other souls flow into the whole race of men from the transfusion of Adam the first-formed, and that nonetheless he believes and says that God was, and is, and will be the Author of all things and of all men—what do you judge must be contradicted in him? Shall we say, "If souls are propagated from parents, God is not the Author of all things, because he does not make souls"? For it will be answered, if we say this: "Then, because bodies are propagated from parents, God is not the Author of all things, if for this reason he must be said not to make bodies." But who would deny that God is the Author of all human bodies—yet say that he is the author only of that one which he first fashioned from the earth, or certainly also of his very spouse, because he himself formed her too from his side; but not also of the rest, because we cannot deny that from those the other bodies of men have flowed?

**6. 13.** And thereby, if those with whom you have a conflict in this question so assert the propagation of souls from the derivation of that one as to deny that God now makes and forms them, press on in refuting, convicting, and correcting them, as much as, with the Lord's help, you can. But if they affirm that certain beginnings are drawn from that one, and thereafter from parents, and yet that single souls in single men are created and formed by God the Author of all things, inquire what is to be answered to them—especially from the holy Scriptures—which is not ambiguous nor can be otherwise understood. Or if you have already found it, as I asked above, direct it to us too. But if it still lies hidden from you, as from me, press on indeed with all your might to refute those who say that souls are not from the divine work, which you said in your first letter they murmured among their more secret fables, and then, on account of this foolish and impious opinion, withdrew from your fellowship and the service of the Church; and against them in all ways defend and protect what you set down in the same letter: that God made souls, and makes them, and will make them; and that there is nothing in heaven or on earth which has not stood, or does not stand, by him as author. For this is most truly and most rightly believed, said, defended, and proved of absolutely every kind of creature. For God was, and is, and will be the Author of all things and of all men—which you set down in your last consultation to our fellow bishops of the province of Caesarea, and in a manner exhorted that they should rather choose this, by the example of all the brethren and fellow priests who are with you, and who hold it.

**6. 14.** But it is one question, where it is asked whether God is the Author and Maker of all souls and bodies, which the truth holds; or whether some natures arise which he himself does not make, which opinion is utterly of error: but it is another, where it is asked whether God makes human souls from propagation or without propagation, which souls it is nevertheless not lawful to doubt are made by him. In which question I wish you to be sober and watchful; and not so to destroy the propagation of souls that you incautiously run into the Pelagian heresy. For if of human bodies, whose propagation is known to all, we nevertheless say—and truly say—that God is the creator, not only of that first man, or of the first spouses, but of all propagated from them: I think it is easily understood that those who defend the propagation of souls do not wish us to destroy them by this, since God makes souls when he also makes bodies, which we cannot deny are made from propagation; but that other proofs must be sought, by which those who think souls are propagated may be repelled, if the truth declares that they err: about which matter those men were rather, if it could be, to be questioned, on account of whose injury, being deceased—as you write in the letter which you sent to me later—"you dreaded to convert men to the better part. For these deceased men, you said, were such and so great and so learned bishops, that, being a new and untrained teacher, you feared to wish to corrupt their traditions." And so, if I could know by what testimonies such, and so great, and so learned men were asserting that opinion about the propagation of souls . . . which, however, in the letter given to the Caesareans, by no means regarding their authority, you said was "a new invention and an unheard-of doctrine"; whereas surely, even if it is an error, we know that it is not new, but old and ancient.

**7. 15.** But when in some question certain causes not undeservedly compel us to doubt, neither ought we on this account to doubt whether we ought to doubt. For about doubtful matters one must without doubting doubt. You see how the Apostle does not doubt to doubt about himself, whether he was caught up into the third heaven *in the body or out of the body—whether this or that, I do not know, God knows*[^3]. Why, then, may it not be lawful for me, so long as I do not know, to doubt whether my soul came into this life from propagation or without propagation, when I do not doubt that in either way it was made by the highest and true God? Why is it not lawful for me to say, "I know that my soul subsists from the work of God, and is altogether the work of God; whether from propagation, like the body, or apart from propagation, like that which was given to the first man, I do not know, God knows"? Which of these do you wish me to confirm? I could, if I knew. But if you yourself know, lo, you have me more desirous to learn what I do not know than to teach what I know. But if you do not know, like me, pray like me, that, whether through any servant of his or through himself, that Master may teach us, who said to his disciples, *Do not wish to be called rabbi by men; for one is your Master, Christ*[^4]—if indeed he knows it is expedient for us that we should know even such things, who knows not only what he teaches, but also what it is expedient for him to tell us.

**7. 16.** For I confess my desire to your Charity. I desire indeed to know this too, which you ask; but I would much rather know, if it could be, when there will be presented—he who is *desired by all nations*[^5]—and when the kingdom of the saints will be, than whence I began to come into this earth: and yet that, when his disciples, our Apostles, asked it of him who knows all things, they received the answer, *It is not for you to know the time or the times which God has placed in his own power*[^6]. What if he knows this too, that it is not for us to know—he who surely knows what is useful for us to know? And that indeed I know through him, that it is not for us to know the times which the Father has placed in his own power. But whether the origin of souls, which I do not yet know, is for us to know—that is, whether it pertains to us to know it—not even this do I know. For if I knew at least this, that it is not for us to know it, I would cease not only to affirm, so long as I do not know, but even to inquire. But now, although it is so obscure and deep that I beware there more the rashness of teaching than I have the desire of learning, yet I wish to know even this, if I can. And although that is much more necessary which that holy man says, *Make known to me, O Lord, my end*[^7] (for he does not say "my beginning"), would yet that not even my beginning, as far as pertains to this question, lay hidden from me.

**8. 17.** But even about my own beginning I am not ungrateful to my Teacher, in that I know the human soul is a spirit, not a body, and that it is rational, or intellectual; and that it is not the nature of God, but rather a creature in some measure mortal, insofar as it can be changed for the worse, and estranged from the life of God, by participation in which it is blessed; and in some measure immortal, since it cannot lose the sense by which, after this life, it is either well or ill. I know also that it did not deserve to be enclosed in flesh for acts done before the flesh; but neither that it is therefore in man without the stain of sin, even if its life upon the earth were of one day, as it is written. And thereby I know that no one is born from Adam through the series of generation without sin; whence even for infants it is necessary to be reborn in Christ through the grace of regeneration. These so many, and not small, things about the beginning or origin of our souls—among which there are more pertaining to that knowledge which stands by faith—I both rejoice to have learned and confirm that I know. Wherefore, if I do not know, in the origin of souls, whether God makes them for men from propagation or without propagation, which souls I nevertheless do not doubt are made by him—I do indeed rather choose to know this too than not to know it: but so long as I cannot, I better doubt about it than dare to confirm something as certain which is perhaps contrary to that matter about which I ought not to doubt.

**8. 18.** You, then, my good brother, since you consult me and wish that I define one of these—whether the other souls come to be from that one man, like bodies, through propagation, or without propagation, like that one soul of that one man, single souls for single men, made by the Creator (for that they are made by him, whether thus or thus, we do not deny)—allow that I too consult, how the soul draws sin originally from that whence it is not itself originally drawn. For that all souls draw original sin from Adam we likewise do not deny, lest we rush into the detestable Pelagian heresy. If this which I ask, you too do not know, allow me patiently to be ignorant of both—both what you ask and what I ask: but if you already know what I ask, when you have taught me this too, then that which you wish me to answer, I will answer, fearing nothing there. I ask, then, that you not be angry, because I could not confirm what you ask, but could show what you should ask; which, when you have found it, do not doubt to confirm what you were asking.

**8. 19.** And this indeed I judged should be written to your Holiness, who already, as if certain, think the propagation of souls must be disapproved. But for the rest, if I had to write back to those who assert it, perhaps I would show how they are ignorant of what they think they know; and how much they ought to dread, lest they dare to assert it.

**8. 20.** Indeed, in the reply of my friend, which I have inserted into this letter, lest perhaps it move you that he mentioned two books sent by me, to which he answered he had no free time to reply: one is about this question, not both; but in the other I sought, by consulting and treating, that which I asked of him. But as for what he admonishes and exhorts, "that we should rather give our effort that this most pernicious heresy be removed from the Churches," he means that very Pelagian heresy, which I admonish you, brother, as much as I can, to avoid most cautiously, when you either think or already dispute about the origin of souls; lest it steal upon you that it must be believed that any soul whatever, except the one Mediator's, does not draw original sin from Adam—bound by generation, to be loosed by regeneration.
