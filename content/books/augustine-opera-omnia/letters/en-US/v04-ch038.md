# Letter 246

AUGUSTINE, TO LAMPADIUS.

**1.** About the question of fate and fortune, by which I noticed your mind to be not lightly moved both when I was present, and now have learned more pleasingly and certainly from your letter, I owe you a reply of no small volume; which the Lord will grant that I so unfold as he knows befits you for the salvation of your faith. For it is no small evil to be brought, by perverse opinions, not only to commit by the flattery of pleasure, but also to be turned away, toward defending sin, from the medicine of confession.

**2.** This indeed you will know, as briefly as quickly, that all laws and all institutions of discipline, praises, blames, exhortations, terrors, rewards, punishments, and all the other things by which the human race is administered and governed, are utterly weakened and subverted, and nothing of justice at all remains in them, unless the will is the cause of sinning. With how much more license and equity, then, do we disapprove the errors of the astrologers, than we are compelled to condemn and cast away the divine laws, or even the care of our households; which not even the astrologers themselves do? For when one of them has sold foolish fates to moneyed men; as soon as he has recalled his eye from the ivory tablets to the management and care of his own house, he corrects his wife not only with words but even with blows; I do not say if he notices her jesting too wantonly, but if she looks too immoderately through the window. Yet if she should say to him: "Why do you beat me? beat Venus if you can, by whom I am compelled to do this": then indeed he does not care what vain words he composes for deceiving outsiders, but what just blows he lays on for correcting his own.

**3.** When, therefore, anyone, when he has begun to be reprehended, turns the cause to fate, and therefore does not wish to be blamed, because he says he was compelled by fate to do that for which he is accused; let him return to himself, let him keep this in his own household, let him not chastise a thieving slave, let him not complain of a contumelious son, let him not threaten a wicked neighbor. For doing which of these does he act justly, if all those by whom he suffers injuries are impelled to do it not by their own fault but by fate? But if by his own right and with the diligence of a householder he exhorts to good, and deters from evil, whatever men he has for the time in his power, commands his will to obey, honors those who obey him at his nod, takes vengeance on those who despise him, repays grace to the well-doing, hatred to the ungrateful; shall I wait to dispute against fate, when I find that he expresses so much, not by words but by deeds, that he seems almost to break with his own hands all the pebbles of the astrologers over their heads? If, then, your avidity is not content with these few things, and desires some book about this matter for it to read at greater length; you must patiently await our vacations, and God must be asked, who would deign to grant both leisure and the means of satisfying your mind on this matter. Yet I shall be more eager, if you would not be reluctant both often to remind me by your letters of your love, and to teach me by writing back what you think of this letter.
