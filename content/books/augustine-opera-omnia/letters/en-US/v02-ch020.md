# Letter 119

To the holy and most blessed lord pope Augustine, Consentius.

**1.** I had already suggested in a brief discourse the kind of my petition to your brother, the holy bishop Alypius—admirable to me in all the virtues of his mind—hoping that he would deign to be a helper of my prayers with you. But because the cause which compelled him to go to the country estate cheated me of your presence, I preferred to insert my prayer in a letter rather than to let the expectation of my mind waver; especially since what I ask, if you perceive that it ought to be granted me, the solitude of the very place in which you now are set may be able to aid your mind as it searches into the deepest mysteries. I, then, since I have altogether determined with myself that the truth of a divine matter ought to be perceived from faith rather than from reason—for if the faith of the holy Church were apprehended from the reasoning of disputation, and not from the piety of believing, no one but philosophers and orators would possess blessedness. But because it pleased God, who chose the weak things of this world to confound the strong, to save believers through the foolishness of preaching, not so much is reason to be sought about God as the authority of the saints is to be followed. For surely neither would the Arians, who think the Son, whom we confess to be begotten, lesser, persist in this impiety, nor would the Macedonians thrust the Holy Spirit, whom we believe to be neither begotten nor unbegotten, from the citadel of divinity as far as in them lies, if they preferred to accommodate their faith to the holy Scriptures rather than to their own reasonings.

**2.** Yet you, admirable man, if that Father of ours, the sole knower of secrets, who has the key of David, has granted you to penetrate the machine of the heavens with the gaze of a most serene heart, and, as it is written, with unveiled face to behold the glory of the Lord—as far as he who gave you a thought of this kind has given you the faculty of bringing it forth—announce to us some portion of that ineffable substance, and strive, with his help, to express, as far as you can, in words an image of his likeness; since, unless you are present as leader and master of so great a matter, our thought, as with bleary eyes, dreads to look upon it, struck back by the flashing of so great a light. Enter, then, into that most obscure cloud of the mysteries of God, which keeps off our gaze; the little questions in solving which I know I go astray—I who wish to follow the authority of Your Holiness by faith rather than to be depraved by the false image of a reason conceived in the heart—correct first in me, then in your books.

**3.** For I, hearing and believing in a very circumspect simplicity that the Lord Jesus Christ is light from light, as it is written, *Proclaim well day from day, his salvation*[^1]; and in the Wisdom of Solomon, *For she is the brightness of eternal light*[^2]; I believed God—what, though it is lawful to believe as is worthy, I was not able to do—yet to be the infinite magnitude of a certain inestimable light, whose quality the human mind, however sublimely thinking, could neither estimate, nor measure its quantity, nor feign its appearance; yet to be that certain thing, whatever it is, to which there is present an incomparable form, an inestimable beauty, which even with carnal eyes at least Christ beholds. When, then, about the end of the first book—as you doubtless deign to remember—wishing to prove that the Lord Jesus Christ, that is, the assumed man, so possesses divine power that the matter of human flesh which he had taken up remained (which in those vitals I had taught that nothing but weakness had perished), the knot of that question was cast against me. "If," he says, "that man whom Christ assumed was turned into God, then he ought not to have been local: why then did he say after the resurrection, *Do not touch me, for I have not yet ascended to my Father*[^3]?"

**4.** I, then, laboring to prove that Christ is everywhere by power, not by work; by divinity, not by flesh; wrote words of this kind about the unity of God and the trinity of persons: "God," I say, "is one, and the persons are three. God is undivided, the persons are distinct. God is within all things, beyond all things; he encloses the last, fills the middle, transcends the highest; beyond the universe and through the universe he is diffused: but the persons, consistent in themselves, are distinguished by property, not mingled by confusion. God, then, is one, and is everywhere; because there is no other besides him, and there is no place empty where another could be. All things are full of God, and besides God there is nothing. He himself is in the Father, he himself in the Son, he himself in the Holy Spirit: and through this, the Father, and the Son, and the Holy Spirit are not several gods, but he himself is the one God; and he who is the Father is not the same as he who is the Son, nor the Son the same as the Holy Spirit. The Father is in the Son, the Son in the Father, in both the Holy Spirit; because in three distinct in number, not in order—that is, in persons, not in power—one and indivisible God dwells. All things that are the Father's are the Son's; and the things that are the Son's are the Father's; and the things that are of both are the Holy Spirit's: because they possess the substance of the Godhead not as equal, but as the same—that is, as the one, not separated; and therefore neither in majesty nor in age does one precede another, because what is full cannot be divided, nor is there in fullness anything that could separate the fullness, and make a greater portion for one, a lesser for another. But in the persons it is not so; because the person of the Father is not that of the Son, nor is the person of the Son the same as that of the Holy Spirit. There is one power, which a threefold power possesses; there is one substance, in which are three that subsist. The Father, then, and the Son, and the Holy Spirit are everywhere by majesty, because they are one; in persons only they are with themselves, because they are three." And weaving the rest of this kind, I led the matter to this, that I should affirm the persons to be present and everywhere, but that the divinity, which is above the heavens, beyond the seas, beyond the depths, is one and the same in majesty. From which I showed it ought to be understood that the man whom Christ assumed, though turned into God, did not lose the assumed nature, yet could not be believed to be, as it were, a fourth person.

**5.** But you, a man to whom, I think, heaven itself has been granted to enter by the subtlety of your thoughts—for truthful is he who says, *Blessed are the pure in heart, for they will see God*[^4]—above all the stars you lift the height of a pure heart to that very contemplation; you say that God ought not to be thought of as any body. For even if anyone should feign in his mind a light a thousand times brighter and more intense than this sun's, no likeness of God can be comprehended there, because everything that can be seen is a body: but just as we cannot think of justice or piety as corporeal—unless perhaps we feign for ourselves some feminine bodies by gentile vanity—so God too is to be thought of, as far as we can, without any feigning of fancy. But to me, who, with a lukewarm heart, can scarcely at all perceive the subtlety of the disputation, it seemed that nothing living, according to substance, is in justice: and therefore I cannot yet think of God—that is, a living nature—as like justice; because justice lives not in itself, but in us; nay rather we live according to justice, but justice itself in no way lives by itself, unless perhaps it be asserted that that alone is justice which is not of this human equity, but which is God.

**6.** Whence I would wish to be confirmed by all means, not only by present words, but even by a fuller letter. For it is not right that only my own feet be recalled, at your admonition, from this way of error in which many of us walk. For since many in those islands in which we dwell, while they proceed by the right causeway to the way, run into the path of this tortuous error, will there be any Augustine there, to whose authority they may yield, whose teaching they may believe, by whose talent they may be overcome? Or do you perhaps, from that paternal affection, prefer to direct me by a hidden admonition rather than to rebuke me as a companion of a crooked journey? But to me, desiring to run for the benefit of my soul rather than for the praise of the world, your refutation is not useless, and therefore not even bitter; especially since it will procure for me and for the rest at once both life and praise. For no one, I think, can be so unjust as to prefer to brand me with folly for the fact that I once followed devious ways, rather than to hesitate to judge me prudent for the fact that I have chosen right ones. For neither are those to be esteemed fools whom the apostle Paul admonished, lest they run into uncertainty, saying, *So run that you may obtain*[^5]: wherefore this way which we run must not only be abandoned by us, but even barred and cut off, lest perhaps it cheat even those by the deceptive pretense of love. For you have been chosen, if I am not mistaken, not now as a reader of books already published by me, but as the emender of books to be approved. For in that letter which I set as a kind of little preface before those little books of mine, words of this kind are written: "It has pleased me," I say, "to stabilize the wavering skiff of our faith by the opinion of the blessed bishop Augustine." Why then, O man, the summit of this teaching which is in Christ, do you hesitate openly to convict a son to be corrected hereafter, since the anchor of your opinion, unless it presses its bite deeper, cannot more surely stabilize us? For it is no light or blameworthy question, in which the blindness of our thought not only has profited nothing, but even—as was most strongly said by you—has incurred the crime of idolatry. This I would wish to be discussed by you cautiously and prudently, so that the serenity of your teaching and talent may so wipe away the mist of our mind that what we now cannot think of, declared by the light of your understanding, we may be able to see with the eyes of the heart. Safe and blessed forever, mindful of me, may you possess the heavenly kingdom, holy and most blessed lord pope.
