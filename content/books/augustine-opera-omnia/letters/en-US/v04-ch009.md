# Letter 217

AUGUSTINE, A BISHOP, A SERVANT OF CHRIST, AND THROUGH HIM A SERVANT OF HIS SERVANTS, TO BROTHER VITALIS, GREETINGS IN HIM.

**1. 1.** When things not good were reported to me about you, I both asked the Lord, and, until good things are reported to me, I still ask, that you receive my letter not disdainfully and read it with profit. If he hears this prayer of mine for you, he also grants that I may offer thanksgiving for you. And if I obtain this, you will without doubt not contradict this very beginning of my letter. For I pray this for you, that you may be of right faith. If, then, it does not displease you that we should pray thus for those dear to us, if you recognize this prayer to be Christian, if you too either recall praying such things for those dear to you, or know that you ought to pray them, how do you say what I hear you say: "That for us to believe rightly in God and to assent to the Gospel is not a gift of God, but this is to us from ourselves, that is, from our own will, which he himself did not work in us in our heart"? And to this, when you have heard, "What then is it that the Apostle says: *God works in you both to will and to accomplish*[^1]?" you answer: "That God works, by his law, by his Scriptures, that we may will, which we either read or hear; but to assent to them or not to assent is so ours that, if we will, it comes about; but if we are unwilling, we make the working of God avail nothing in us. For he works, you say, as far as in him lies, that we may will, when his words become known to us; but if we are unwilling to acquiesce in them, we bring it about that his working profits nothing in us." If you say these things, you assuredly contradict our prayers.

**1. 2.** Say then most openly that we ought not to pray for those to whom we preach the Gospel that they may believe, but only to preach to them. Bring forth your arguments against the prayers of the Church; and when you hear a priest of God at the altar exhorting the people of God to pray for unbelievers, that God may convert them to the faith, and for catechumens, that he may breathe into them a desire for regeneration, and for the faithful, that they may persevere by his gift in what they have begun to be, mock these devout words and say that you do not do what he urges, that is, that you do not ask God for the unbelievers, that he may make them believers, on the ground that these are not benefits of the divine compassion but services of the human will. And, a man educated in the Church of Carthage, condemn too the book of the most blessed Cyprian *On the Lord's Prayer*[^2]; for in expounding it that teacher showed that those things are to be asked of God the Father which you say a man has from a man, that is, from himself.

**1. 3.** But if you think what I have said about the prayers of the Church and about the martyr Cyprian is too little, dare greater things: blame the Apostle, who said: *We pray to God that you may do no evil*[^3]. For you will not say that he does no evil who does not believe in Christ, or who deserts the faith of Christ; and so he who says, *that you may do no evil,* does not want these things to be done either: and it is not enough for him to command, but he confesses that he asks God that they not be done, knowing that the will of man is corrected and directed by him, so that he may not do them. For *by the Lord the steps of a man are directed, and he will desire his way*[^4]: he did not say, "And he will learn his way," or "will hold," or "will walk," or anything of this sort that you might say is indeed given by the Lord, but to a man already willing—so that, namely, the benefit of God by which he directs a man's steps, that he may learn, hold, and walk his way, the man might anticipate by his own will, and might merit this gift of God by his own preceding will. But for this reason he said: *By the Lord the steps of a man are directed, and he will desire his way,* that we might understand that the good will itself, by which we begin to will to believe (since what is the way of God but right faith?), is the gift of him who directs our steps for this very purpose first, that we may will. For Scripture does not say: "*By the Lord the steps of a man are directed,* because he willed his way"; but: *They are directed,* it says, *and he will desire.* Therefore it is not because he willed that they are directed, but because they are directed that he will desire.

**2. 4.** Here again you will perhaps say that this is done by the Lord while his teaching is read or heard, if a man assents by his own will to the truth he reads or hears. "For if the teaching of God were hidden from him," you say, "his steps would not be directed, by which, once directed, he would desire the way of God." And thereby you suppose that a man's steps are directed by the Lord to choosing the way of God only in this sense: that without the teaching of God the truth cannot become known to him, to which he assents by his own will. "And if he assents to it," you say (which is established in his free choice), "then surely his steps are rightly said to be directed by him, so that he desires his way, whose teaching, by a preceding persuasion and a following consent, he follows; which he does by natural liberty, if he wills, and does not do, if he does not will; about to receive a reward or a punishment for what he has done." This is that evil opinion of the Pelagians, ill-famed and deservedly reproved, and condemned even by Pelagius himself, who feared to be condemned in the judgment of the eastern bishops—the opinion by which they say that the grace of God is given not for individual acts, but lies in free choice, or in the law and teaching. And shall we, brother, be so heavy of heart that, concerning the grace of God—nay rather, against the grace of God—we should hold that Pelagian opinion which Pelagius himself condemned, with a feigned heart indeed, but nevertheless fearing his Catholic judges?

**2. 5.** And how, you will say, shall it be answered? In what way do you think more easily and openly than that we should so embrace what we treated above about praying to God that no creeping in of forgetfulness may withdraw it from our mind, and no cleverness of argument may wrest it away? For if—as is written, *By the Lord the steps of a man are directed, and he will desire his way*[^5]; and, *The will is prepared by the Lord*[^6]; and, *For it is God who works in you even to will*[^7]; and many things of this kind, by which the true grace of God is commended, that is, the grace which is given not according to our merits, but which itself gives the merits when it is given, because it anticipates a man's good will and does not find it in anyone's heart but makes it—if, then, God so prepared and so worked a man's will that he only applied his law and teaching to the man's free choice, and did not by that deep and secret calling so move his understanding that he might accommodate his assent to that same law and teaching, then without doubt it would be enough to read it, or to understand it by reading, or even to expound and preach it, and there would be no need to pray that God might convert the hearts of unbelievers to his faith, and grant to those converted, by the bounty of that same grace of his, a progressing perseverance. If, then, you do not refuse that these things are to be asked of the Lord, what remains, brother Vitalis, but that you confess them to be given by him from whom you agree they are to be asked? But if you deny that we ought to ask these things of him, you contradict his very teaching, because in it too we have learned to ask these things of him.

**2. 6.** You know the Lord's Prayer, and I do not doubt that you say to God: *Our Father, who are in heaven*[^8], and so on. Read its expositor, the most blessed Cyprian, and attend diligently and understand obediently how he expounded what is said there: *Your will be done, as in heaven, so on earth*[^9]; he will surely teach you to pray for unbelieving enemies of the Church, according to the precept of the Lord, who says, *Pray for your enemies*[^10]; and to pray this, that the will of God may be done, as in those who are already faithful and bear the image of the heavenly man, and are therefore worthy of the name of heaven, so also in those who through unbelief bear only the image of the earthly man[^11], and on this account are deservedly called earth. Surely these, for whom as enemies the Lord commanded us to pray, and concerning whom the most glorious martyr so expounded what we say in the prayer—*Your will be done, as in heaven, so on earth*—that we may ask even for them the faith that the faithful have: surely, then, these enemies of Christian devotion either are altogether unwilling to hear the law and teaching of God by which the faith of Christ is preached, or hear or even read it for this purpose, to mock and detest it and to blaspheme it with all the contradiction they can. In vain, therefore, and perfunctorily rather than truly, do we pour out prayers to God for them, that they may by believing assent to the teaching they oppose, if it does not pertain to his grace to convert to his faith the wills of men that are contrary to that faith. In vain too, and perfunctorily rather than truly, do we give great thanks to God with exultation when any of them believe, if he himself does not work this in them.

**2. 7.** Let us not deceive men, for we cannot deceive God. We do not pray to God at all, but only pretend to pray, if we believe that we ourselves, and not he, do what we pray for. We do not give thanks to God at all, but only pretend to give thanks, if we suppose that he does not do that for which we give him thanks. If lying lips are present in any human discourses, at least let them not be present in prayers. Far be it that what we ask God to do with our mouths and our voices we should deny that he does in our hearts; and—what is more serious—that, to deceive others as well, we should not be silent about this in our arguments, and that, while we wish to defend free choice before men, we should lose before God the help of prayer, and not have a true thanksgiving while we do not recognize true grace.

**3. 8.** If we truly wish to defend free choice, let us not attack that from which it becomes free. For he who attacks the grace by which our choice is freed to turn aside from evil and to do good himself wishes his choice still to be captive. Answer, I beg you, how the Apostle says: *Giving thanks to the Father, who has made us fit for a share in the lot of the saints in light; who has rescued us from the power of darkness, and transferred us into the kingdom of the Son of his love*[^12]—if it is not he who frees our choice, but the choice frees itself? Falsely, then, do we give thanks to the Father, as though he does what he does not do; and he erred who said that he himself makes us *fit for a share in the lot of the saints in light,* because he himself *rescued us from the power of darkness, and transferred us into the kingdom of the Son of his love.* Answer how we had free choice to turn aside from evil and to do good, when it was under the power of darkness; from which, if, as the Apostle says, God *rescued* us, he himself surely made it free. And if he works this so great good of ours only through the preaching of his teaching, what shall we say of those whom he has not yet rescued from that power of darkness? Is the divine teaching only to be preached to them, or must we also pray for them, that they may be divinely rescued from the power of darkness? If you say it is only to be preached, you contradict the command of the Lord and the prayers of the Church; but if you confess that we must pray for them, you confess surely that this is to be prayed for, that they may assent to that teaching, their choice freed from the power of darkness. So it comes about that they neither become faithful except by free choice, and yet they become faithful by the grace of him who freed their choice from the power of darkness. Thus the grace of God is not denied, but is shown to be true without any preceding human merits; and free choice is so defended that it is made firm by humility, not cast down by pride, and that *he who glories* may glory not in man, or in anything else, or in himself, but *in the Lord*[^13].

**3. 9.** For what is the power of darkness, but the power of the devil and his angels[^14], who, although they had been angels of light[^15], not standing in the truth through free choice[^16] but falling from it, became darkness? I do not teach you these things, but admonish you to recall what you know. To this power of darkness, then, through the ruin of him by whom the transgression was urged upon the first man from that power[^17], and in whom we all fell, the human race was made subject; on which account little children too are rescued from this power of darkness when they are reborn in Christ. Nor does this appear in their freed choice except when they have come to the years of an age that uses reason, having a will that assents to the saving teaching in which they have been nourished, and ending this life in it, if they were chosen in Christ before the foundation of the world that they might be holy and immaculate in his sight in love, predestined unto the adoption of sons[^18].

**3. 10.** But this power of darkness, that is, the devil, who is also called the prince of the power of the air, works in the sons of unbelief[^19]—this prince, the ruler of the darkness[^20], that is, of those sons of unbelief themselves, ruling them according to his own will, which not even he has free for doing good, but hardened to the utmost malevolence by the punishment of his own crime: hence no one of sound faith believes, or says, that these apostate angels are ever converted to their former devotion by a corrected will. What then does this power work in the sons of unbelief, but its own evil works, and first and foremost that very unbelief and faithlessness by which they are enemies of the faith—through which it knows that they could be cleansed, could be healed, could most perfectly reign free in eternity, which it vehemently envies them? And so it allows some of them, through whom it strives to deceive the more, to have certain seemingly good works, for which they are praised among the various nations, and especially in the Roman nation, who have lived in an illustrious and most glorious manner. But since, as most truthful Scripture says, *Everything that is not of faith is sin*[^21], and, *Without faith it is impossible* indeed *to please God*[^22]—not men—this prince does nothing so much as work that men may not believe in God, nor come by believing to the Mediator, by whom his works are undone.

**3. 11.** But the Mediator himself enters *into the house of the strong man*[^23], that is, into this world of mortals established, as far as concerns him, under the power of the devil; for of him it is written that he has the power of death[^24]. He enters *into the house of the strong man,* that is, into the one who holds the human race in his domination; and first he binds him, that is, restrains and confines his power, with the stronger bonds of his own power; and so he snatches away his vessels, whomever he has predestined to snatch, freeing their choice from his power, so that, with the strong man not hindering, they may believe in him by a free will. Accordingly, this is a work of grace, not of nature. It is a work, I say, of grace, which the second Adam brought us; not of nature, which the first Adam wholly ruined in himself. It is a work of grace that takes away sin and gives life to the dead sinner; not a work of the law that shows sin and does not give life from sin. For that great preacher of grace says: *I did not know sin except through the law*[^25]; and: *If a law had been given that could give life,* he says, *righteousness would altogether be from the law*[^26]. This is a work of grace, by receiving which men become friends of the saving teaching of the holy Scriptures even if they were enemies; not a work of the same teaching, by which those who hear and read it without the grace of God are made its worse enemies.

**4. 12.** The grace of God, therefore, is not in the nature of free choice and in the law and teaching, as Pelagian perversity foolishly thinks; but it is given for individual acts by the will of him of whom it is written: *You will set apart a voluntary rain, O God, for your inheritance*[^27]: because we both lost free choice for loving God through the greatness of the first sin, and the law and teaching of God, though holy and just and good[^28], nevertheless kills, if the Spirit does not give life[^29], through whom it comes about that it is held not by hearing but by obeying, not by reading but by loving. Wherefore, that we may believe in God and live devoutly, *it is not of him who wills nor of him who runs, but of God who shows mercy*[^30]: not because we ought not to will and run, but because he himself works in us both to will and to run. Hence the Lord Jesus himself too, distinguishing believers from unbelievers, that is, the vessels of mercy from the vessels of wrath[^31], says: *No one comes to me unless it has been given him by my Father*[^32]: and surely, that he might say this, his disciples had been scandalized at his teaching, who afterward did not follow him[^33]. Let us not, then, say that grace is teaching, but let us acknowledge the grace that makes the teaching profitable; and if this grace is lacking, we see that the teaching even does harm.

**4. 13.** For this reason God, as he foreknew in his predestination all his future works, so disposed them that he converts certain unbelievers to his faith by hearing the prayers of believers for them; so that those may be refuted, and, if he is favorable to them, corrected, who think that the grace of God is the nature of free choice, with which we are born, or that the grace of God is teaching—useful though it be—which is preached through tongues and letters. For we do not pray this for unbelievers, that their nature may come to be, that is, that they may be men; or that teaching may be preached to them, which to their own harm they hear if they do not believe; and very often we pray for those who, though they read or hear, are unwilling to believe: but we pray that the will may be corrected, that the teaching may be assented to, that the nature may be healed.

**4. 14.** But now, that they may persevere in what they have begun to be, the faithful pray even for themselves. For it is useful for all, or nearly all, on account of a most wholesome humility, that they should not be able to know what they will be. To this end it is said: *Let him who seems to stand take heed lest he fall*[^34]. On account of the usefulness of this fear—lest, having been reborn and having begun to live devoutly, we should be high-minded as though secure[^35]—certain ones who will not persevere are, by God's permission or provision and arrangement, mingled with those who will persevere; so that, terrified by their falling, we may walk the just way *with fear and trembling*[^36], until from this life, which *is a temptation upon earth*[^37], we pass to another, where there will no longer be pride to be repressed, nor must we struggle against its suggestions and temptations.

**4. 15.** But about this matter, that is, why certain ones who will not remain in the faith and in Christian holiness nevertheless receive this grace for a time, and are allowed to live here until they fall, when they could be snatched from this life *lest malice change their understanding*—which is written in the book of Wisdom about a holy man who died at an immature age[^38]—let each one inquire as he can; and if he finds, besides the one I have given, another probable explanation, not departing from the rule of right faith, let him hold it, and I with him, if it does not escape me: nevertheless, in that to which we have come, let us walk in it until God reveals to us, if we are wise in anything otherwise, as we are admonished by the apostolic Letters[^39]. But we have come into those things that we most firmly know to pertain to the true and Catholic faith, in which we must so walk, with him helping and showing mercy, to whom we say, *Lead me, Lord, in your way, and I will walk in your truth*[^40], that we may in no way deviate from them. TWELVE PROPOSITIONS AGAINST THE PELAGIANS.

**5. 16.** Since, then, by the favor of Christ we are Catholic Christians:  
**1.** We know that the unborn have done nothing good or evil in their own life[^41], nor come into the miseries of this life according to the merits of some prior life, which no one of them individually could have had as his own; but that, born carnally according to Adam, they contract the contagion of the ancient death at their first birth, and are not freed from the punishment of eternal death, which a just condemnation, passing from one into all, brings, except they are reborn in Christ[^42].  
**2.** We know that the grace of God is given neither to little children nor to adults according to our merits.  
**3.** We know that it is given to adults for individual acts.  
**4.** We know that it is not given to all men, and that to those to whom it is given, it is given not only not according to the merits of works, but not even according to the merits of the will of those to whom it is given: which appears most of all in little children.  
**5.** We know that to those to whom it is given, it is given by the free mercy of God.  
**6.** We know that to those to whom it is not given, it is not given by the just judgment of God.  
**7.** We know that *we shall all stand before the judgment seat of Christ, that each one may bear according to the things he has done through the body,* not according to the things he would have done if he had lived longer, *whether good or evil*[^43].  
**8.** We know that little children too will receive either good or evil according to the things they have done through the body. But they have done them not through themselves, but through those who, answering for them, are said both to renounce the devil and to believe in God; whence they are also counted in the number of the faithful, pertaining to the saying of the Lord: *He who believes and is baptized will be saved.* On account of which to those also who do not receive this Sacrament happens what follows: *But he who does not believe will be condemned*[^44]. Hence they too, as I have said, if they die at that tender age, are surely judged according to the things they have done through the body, that is, during the time they were in the body, when through the hearts and mouths of those carrying them they believed or did not believe, when they were baptized or not baptized, when they ate or did not eat the flesh of Christ, when they drank or did not drink his blood; according to these things, then, which they have done through the body, not according to the things they would have done if they had lived here long, they are judged[^45].  
**9.** We know that those are happy who die *in the Lord*[^46], and that nothing pertains to them of whatever they would have done had they lived a longer time.  
**10.** We know that those who believe in the Lord with their own heart do this by their own will and free choice.  
**11.** We know that for those who are unwilling to believe, we who already believe act with right faith, when we pray God that they may be willing.  
**12.** We know that for those of them who have come to believe, we both ought, and are accustomed, rightly and truly to give thanks to God, as for benefits.

**5. 17.** You recognize me, I think, in those things I have said that we know, as not having wished to mention everything that pertains to the Catholic faith, but only those things that pertain to this question, which is being treated between us, about the grace of God: whether this grace precedes or follows the will of man; that is, to say it more plainly, whether it is given to us because we will, or whether through it God brings it about even that we will. If, then, these twelve propositions, which I have said we know to pertain to right and Catholic faith, you too, brother, hold with us, I give thanks to God; which I would assuredly not give truly, unless it came about by the grace of God that you held them. And if you hold them, no contest at all remains between us about this question.

**6. 18.** For, to run through those same twelve in a brief exposition: how does grace follow the merit of the human will, when it is given even to little children, who cannot yet will or not will this? How are the merits of the will said to precede grace even in adults, if grace, that it may be true grace, is not given according to our merits? Which Catholic proposition Pelagius himself so feared that he condemned without hesitation those who say that the grace of God is given according to our merits, lest he be condemned by Catholic judges. How is the grace of God said to be in the nature of free choice or in the law and teaching, when Pelagius himself condemned this opinion too, without doubt confessing that the grace of God is given for individual acts, surely to those who already use free choice?

**6. 19.** How is it said that all men would have received it, if those to whom it is not given did not refuse it by their own will, since *God wills all men to be saved*[^47]; when it is not given to many little children, and most die without it who have no contrary will, and sometimes, though parents desire and hasten, and ministers too are willing and ready, it is not given because God is unwilling, when suddenly, before it is given, the child expires for whom it was being run to receive? Hence it is manifest that those who resist so clear a truth do not understand at all by what manner of speaking it was said that *God wills all men to be saved;* when so many are not saved, not because they themselves, but because God is unwilling, which is manifested without any obscurity in little children. But just as what was said—*All will be made alive in Christ*[^48]—when so many are punished with eternal death, was said for this reason, that all whoever receive eternal life do not receive it except in Christ: so what was said—*God wills all men to be saved*—when he is unwilling that so many be saved, was said for this reason, that all who are saved are not saved except by his willing; and if in some other way those apostolic words can be understood, yet so that they cannot be contrary to this most clear truth, in which we see that so many are not saved, men being willing but God unwilling.

**6. 20.** How does the human will merit that divine grace be given, if to those to whom it is given it is given by free mercy, that it may be true grace? How in this matter are the merits of the human will weighed, since to those to whom this grace is not given—differing for the most part by no merit, by no will, but having one and the same cause with those to whom it is given—it is nevertheless not given by the just judgment of God (for there is no iniquity with God[^49]); so that those to whom it is given may understand how freely it is given them, when it would surely not be given justly, since to those having a like cause it is justly not given?

**6. 21.** How is it not of the grace of God—not only the will to believe from the beginning, but also to persevere to the end[^50]—when the very end of this life is not in the power of man but of God, and God can surely confer even this benefit on one who will not persevere, that he be snatched from the body *lest malice change his understanding*[^51]? For a man will not receive either good or evil[^52] except according to the things he has done through the body, not according to the things he would have done if he had lived longer.

**6. 22.** How is it said that for this reason the grace of God is not given to some little children about to die and is given to others, because he foresees their future wills, which they would have if they lived; when each one receives either good or evil according to the things he has done through the body, not according to the things he would do if he had been longer in the body, as the Apostle determines[^53]? How are men judged according to their future wills, which they are said to have been about to have if they were kept longer in the flesh, when Scripture says, *Happy are the dead who die in the Lord*[^54]? Whose sure and secure happiness is no happiness at all, if God will judge even the things they did not do but were about to do, had this life been more prolonged for them; and he who is snatched away *lest malice change his understanding*[^55] receives no benefit, because he pays the penalties even for that malice from which perhaps, as it threatened, he was withdrawn: nor should we rejoice over those whom we have known to have died in right faith and a good life, lest they be judged according to certain crimes which they would perhaps have committed had they lived: nor are those to be grieved over or detested who ended this life in unbelief and lost morals, because perhaps, had they lived, they were about to do penance and live devoutly, and are to be judged according to these things: and that whole book *On Mortality* of the most glorious martyr Cyprian is to be rejected and cast aside[^56], in which his whole aim is that we should know that good faithful are to be congratulated when they die, since they are taken away from the temptations of this life, henceforth to abide in most blessed security. But since this is not false, and without doubt *happy are the dead who die in the Lord*, that error is to be laughed at and execrated by which it is thought that men are to be judged according to their future wills, which are not the future wills of those who die.

**6. 23.** How are those said to deny the free choice of the will who confess that every man, whoever believes in God with his own heart, believes only by his own free will; when rather those attack free choice who attack the grace of God, by which it truly becomes free for choosing and doing good things? How is it said that it comes about through the law of God and the teaching of the Scriptures, when that same Scripture said, *The will is prepared by the Lord*[^57]; and not rather through the hidden inspiration of the grace of God—since for those who, contradicting that same teaching, are unwilling to believe it, we ask God himself with right faith that they may be willing?

**6. 24.** How does God wait for the wills of men, so that they may anticipate him, to whom he may give grace; when we not undeservedly give him thanks for those to whom, not believing in him and persecuting his teaching with an impious will, he extended mercy in advance, and converted them to himself with most almighty ease, and made them willing out of unwilling? Why do we give him thanks for this, if he himself did not do it? Why do we magnify him the more, the more they were unwilling to believe, whom we rejoice to have believed, if the human will is not changed for the better by divine grace? The apostle Paul says: *I was unknown by face to the churches of Judea, which are in Christ; only they were hearing that he who once persecuted us now preaches the faith he once ravaged; and they glorified God in me*[^58]. Why did they glorify God, if God had not converted to himself the heart of that man by the goodness of his grace, when, as he himself confesses, he obtained mercy that he might be faithful[^59], with that *faith he once ravaged?* Even the very word he used—whom but God does it declare to have done this so great good? For what is *they glorified God in me,* but "in me they preached God as glorious"? But how did they preach him as glorious, if he himself had not done that great deed concerning Paul's conversion? And in what way had he himself done it, if he had not himself made him willing to believe out of unwilling?

**6. 25.** Surely it is manifest from those twelve propositions, which you will not be permitted to deny pertain to the Catholic faith, that it is established not only for all but also for each individual, that we confess the wills of men to be anticipated by the grace of God, and through it to be rather prepared than given on account of their merit. Or if you deny that any of those twelve is true—the number of which I commend for this reason, that they may be more easily committed to memory and more distinctly held—do not be reluctant to write back, that I may know, and may answer with whatever faculty the Lord has given me. For I do not indeed believe you to be a Pelagian heretic; but I want you to be such that nothing of that error passes over to you, or is left in you.

**7. 26.** But among these twelve you will perhaps find something that you think must be denied or doubted, and on which you compel us to argue more laboriously. Will you also forbid the Church to pray for unbelievers, that they may be faithful; for those who are unwilling to believe, that they may be willing to believe; for those who dissent from his law and teaching, that they may assent to his law and teaching, that God may give them what he promised through the prophet, *a heart to know* him, and *ears to hear*[^60], which they had surely received, of which the Savior himself said: *He who has ears to hear, let him hear*[^61]? When you hear a priest of God at his altar exhorting the people to pray to God, or himself praying with a clear voice, that he may compel unbelieving nations to come to his faith, will you not answer "Amen"? Or will you even argue things contrary to the soundness of this faith? Will you cry out or whisper that the most blessed Cyprian erred in this, where he teaches us to pray for the enemies of the Christian faith, that they too may be converted to it[^62]?

**7. 27.** Will you, finally, blame the apostle Paul, who had such vows for unbelieving Jews? Of whom he says: *The good will indeed of my heart, and my prayer to God, is made for them unto salvation*[^63]. He also says to the Thessalonians: *For the rest, brothers, pray for us, that the word of the Lord may run and be glorified, as also among you; and that we may be rescued from wicked and evil men: for not all have faith*[^64]. How would the word of God run and be glorified, unless those to whom it was preached were converted to the faith—since to those already believing he says, *as also among you?* He surely knows that this is done by him whom he wishes to be asked to do it, that he may also be rescued from wicked and evil men, who, even with these praying, were not going to believe. On account of which he added: *For not all have faith;* as if he said, "For not among all, even with you praying, will the word of God be glorified": because surely those were going to believe *who were ordained to eternal life*[^65], predestined *unto the adoption of sons through Jesus Christ in himself,* and chosen *in him before the foundation of the world*[^66]; but for this reason God, through the prayers of believers, makes those not yet believing to believe, that he may show that he himself does it. For no one is so unlearned, so carnal, so slow of wit, as not to see that God does what he commands himself to be asked to do.

**7. 28.** These and other divine testimonies, which it would be long to recount, show that God by his grace takes away the heart of stone from unbelievers[^67], and anticipates in men the merits of good wills; so that the will is prepared by a preceding grace, not that grace is given by a preceding merit of the will. This thanksgiving too indicates, as does prayer; prayer for unbelievers, thanksgiving for believers. For he who is to be prayed to that he may do it is to be given thanks when he has done it: whence the same Apostle says to the Ephesians: *For this reason I too, having heard of your faith in the Lord Jesus, and your love toward all the saints, do not cease to give thanks for you*[^68].

**7. 29.** But we are now speaking of the very beginnings, when men who were turned away and opposed are converted to God, and begin to will what they did not will, and to have the faith they did not have; and that this may come about in them, prayer is made for them, although it is not made by them: for they themselves, *how shall they call upon him in whom they have not believed*[^69]? But when what is prayed for has been done, thanks are given to him who did it, both for them and by them. But about the prayers of those now faithful, which they make both for themselves and for other faithful, that they may make progress in what they have begun to be, and about the thanksgivings, because they make progress, I do not think I must contend with you; that contest, against the Pelagians, is common to you and to us. For they attribute everything pertaining to the faithful and devout life of men so much to the free choice of the will that they think these things are to be had from ourselves, not to be asked of the Lord: but you, if what I hear about you is true, are unwilling that the beginning of faith—where there is also the beginning of a good, that is, a devout will—be a gift of God, but contend that we have from ourselves that we begin to believe; while you agree that God, through his grace, gives the other goods of a religious life to those who now ask, seek, and knock from faith. Nor do you attend to the fact that for this reason God is asked for unbelievers that they may believe, since God gives faith too; and for this reason thanks are given to God for those who have believed, since he gave faith too[^70].

**7. 30.** Wherefore, that I may at last conclude this discourse to you: if you deny that we must pray that those who are unwilling to believe may be willing to believe, if you deny that thanks must be given to God because those who were unwilling to believe willed to believe, you must be dealt with otherwise, so that you may not so err, or, if you persist in erring, may not send others into error. But if—what I rather believe of you—you think and agree that we ought and are accustomed to pray to God for those unwilling to believe, that they may be willing to believe, and for those who oppose and contradict his law and teaching, that they may believe it and follow it; if you think and agree that we ought also and are accustomed to give thanks to God for such people, when, converted to his faith and teaching, they become willing out of unwilling, you must without doubt confess that the wills of men are anticipated by the grace of God, and that God, who is asked to do it, brings it about that men will the good they did not will, and that we know it is fitting and just to give him thanks when he has done it. May the Lord give you understanding in all things, lord brother.
