# Letter 10\*

A MEMORANDUM FOR THE HOLY BROTHER ALYPIUS, FROM AUGUSTINE

**1.** As the holy brothers and our fellow bishops were returning—although I did not see them, yet, admonished by their letters that, if I wished to write anything to Your Holiness, I should send it to Carthage—I dictated these things. [Greeting them] I desire now and now even to see your Fraternity, with the hope of your return, which you signified in your letter, drawing near. I had indeed already written back that the books of Julian and Caelestius, which you sent through our son the deacon Commilito, reached me with your memorandum, and that I much wondered that you took no care to tell me anything about the correction of Turbantius, to whom Julian wrote those four books. For I heard from such a man—whom I cannot say lied—that he, condemning the same heresy by a sufficiently humble confession, was received into Catholic peace by Pope Caelestinus. For rather, when you wrote to me, I could suspect that you had forgotten this. Although, then, I had already written these things, yet even now I wished to remind you, lest perhaps Your Holiness receive this reply before those things which I did before. Meanwhile, among certain papers of ours I found a copy of the memorandum which you had made for yourself, when you were first sent from the council to the imperial court, and, having read it through, I saw that you could not then do many necessary things, and, certain things being removed which either were done or do not seem much to press, I thought it should be sent, lest perhaps they can now be done.

**2.** But I add another thing too: So great is the multitude of those who are commonly called "slave-dealers" in Africa, that they drain it in great part of the human race, by transferring those they buy into the provinces across the sea, and almost all of them free. For scarcely a few are found sold by their parents, whom nevertheless these men buy not, as the Roman laws permit, for labor of twenty-five years, but they buy them outright as slaves and sell them across the sea as slaves; but true slaves, from their masters, very rarely indeed. Further, from this multitude of dealers, so has the multitude of those seducing and plundering grown insolent, that they are reported, in herds, howling, in a terrible garb whether military or barbarian, to invade certain rural places, in which there are few men, and violently to carry off those whom they may sell to these dealers.

**3.** I omit what rumor recently reported to us, that in a certain little villa, by assaults of this kind, the men having been killed, women and children were carried off to be sold; but where this happened, if indeed it truly happened, was not said. But I myself, when, among those who were being freed by our church from that wretched captivity, I asked a certain girl how she had been sold to the dealers, she said she had been carried off from the house of her parents; then I asked whether she had been found there alone; she answered it was done with her parents and brothers present. Her brother too was there, who had come to receive her, and, because she was little, he himself opened to us how it had been done. For he said that at night robbers of this kind broke in, by whom—believing them to be barbarians—they rather, as best they could, hid themselves than dared to resist. But if there were no dealers, those things would not happen. Nor indeed do I think that this evil of Africa keeps silent in fame even there where you are; which was incomparably far less, when nevertheless the emperor Honorius gave to the prefect Hadrian a law restraining commerce of this kind, and decreed that the traffickers of such impiety were to be coerced with lead and proscribed and sent into perpetual exile; nor does he speak in that law of those who buy free men, seduced and plundered—which is practically all these men do—but generally of all who transfer households to be sold into the provinces across the sea; so that he ordered those slaves too to be associated with the fisc [assigned to the treasury], which surely he would by no means say of free men.

**4.** This law I have subjoined to this memorandum of mine, although it could perhaps be found more easily at Rome too; for it is useful and could remedy this pestilence, but we have begun to use it only so far as suffices for freeing men, not for coercing with such a punishment those dealers, on account of whom so many and so great crimes are perpetrated. For we terrify by this law those we can, and do not strike them, nay rather we fear lest perhaps others drag those men—though detestable and damnable—when caught by us, to the punishment owed by this law. Hence I write these things to Your Beatitude rather for this, that it may be established, if it can be done, by the most devout and Christian princes, that these men not come to the peril of damnation which is defined by this law, and especially to the coercion of lead, by which men easily die, when through the Church men are freed from them; and it is necessary, for the repressing of these same men, [...] this law perhaps to be brought into public, lest, while we, fearing these things, cease, the wretched free men be transported into perpetual servitude, since, with us doing nothing for them, who can easily be found who would not, if he has any power on the shores, rather sell them most cruel voyages than, out of Christian or human pity, put any of the wretches down from the ship, or not permit one to be put on the ship?

**5.** But of which powers or of which offices the care this law—or any other that may have been passed about this matter—can have its execution, so that Africa be no longer emptied of its natives, nor, in herds and droves, by a perpetual stream as it were, so great a multitude of men of both sexes lose their own liberty worse than by barbarian captivity, is theirs to provide. For from the barbarians very many are ransomed; but those transported into the provinces across the sea find no help of ransom; and resistance is made to the barbarians, when the Roman soldiery is conducted well and prosperously, lest Romans be held in barbarian captivity; but against these traffickers—of not just any animals but of men, and not of just any barbarians but of Roman provincials—dispersed everywhere, so that, into the hands of those promising prices, whether violently carried off or deceived by snares, they may be led anywhere and from anywhere, who resists, for Roman liberty (I will not say the common, but each one's own)?

**6.** Nay rather, it cannot sufficiently be said how many, by a wonderful blindness of desire and by I-know-not-what contagion of this disease as it were, have flowed down into this same wicked gain. Who would believe that a woman was found—and this among us, at Hippo—who used to seduce, shut up, afflict, and sell the women of Giddaba, as though for the sake of buying wood? Who would believe that a sufficiently respectable tenant of our church sold his wife, the mother too of his children, offended by no fault of hers, roused only by the fervor of this pestilence? A certain young man of nearly twenty years, a sensible accountant-notary, was seduced from our monastery and sold, who could scarcely be freed through the Church.

**7.** If I should wish to enumerate such crimes as we alone have experienced, I could by no means do so. Take one document, from which you may conjecture all the things that are perpetrated throughout all Africa and along all its shores. About four months before I wrote these things, men gathered from various lands, and especially from Numidia, by Galatian dealers (for these alone, or most of all, eagerly devote themselves to these gains), were brought, that they might be transported from the shore of Hippo. There was not lacking a faithful person, knowing our custom in alms of this kind, who reported this to the Church; immediately, partly from the ship into which they had been put, partly from the place where they had been hidden to be put on board, about a hundred and twenty men were freed by ours, with me indeed absent; among whom scarcely five or six were found to have been sold by their parents; but of the rest, the various misfortunes by which, through seducers and plunderers, they came to the Galatians, scarcely anyone hearing refrains from tears.

**8.** It is now for your holy Prudence to consider how great a transportation of wretched souls seethes along the other shores, if at Hippo Regius—where, by God's mercy, some diligence of the Church keeps watch, by which wretched men are freed from captivity of this kind, and the traffickers of such merchandise are punished, far less indeed than by the severity of this law, yet at least by the loss of their prices—so great a desire burns, so great a cruelty of the Galatians dares. By Christian love I beseech that I not have written these things to your Charity in vain. For patrons are not lacking to the Galatians, through whom they demand back from us those whom the Lord freed through the Church, given back even to their own people who now sought them and came to us for that reason with the letters of bishops. But certain of our faithful sons, with whom some of them then remained, commended to them (for the Church does not suffice to feed all whom it frees), they have already begun to disturb, when we dictated these things, even though letters having come from a power which they could fear [...]; nor yet have they altogether ceased from this demanding back.

**9.** All who deigned to greet us through the letter of Your Reverence I greet in return, according to their merits, in the love of Christ. My fellow servants who are with me greet Your Holiness along with me.
