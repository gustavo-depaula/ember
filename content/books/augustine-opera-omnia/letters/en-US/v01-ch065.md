# Letter 65

To the most blessed and venerably to-be-received lord, father and fellow priest, the old man Xantippus, Augustine sends greeting in the Lord

**1.** With the duty owed to your merits, greeting your Dignity, and much commending myself to your prayers, I intimate to your prudence that a certain Abundantius was ordained presbyter on the estate of Strabonia belonging to our care. Who, since he did not walk the ways of the servants of God, had begun to have a not good reputation; by which I, terrified, yet not rashly believing anything, but plainly made more solicitous, took pains, if in any way I could, to come to some certain indications of his evil conduct. And first I learned that he had embezzled the money of a certain rustic, deposited with him for a religious purpose, so that he could render no probable account thereof. Then he was convicted and confessed that, on the fast day of the Lord's Birthday, on which also the Church of Gippi, like the rest, was fasting, when, as if about to set out to his own Church, he had said farewell to his colleague, the presbyter of Gippi, about the fifth hour, and, having no clergyman with him, he stayed on the same estate, and both lunched and supped at the house of a certain woman of evil reputation, and remained in the same house. But in this woman's lodging a certain clergyman of ours of Hippo had already been removed; and this, because this man knew well, he could not deny. For the things he denied, I left to God, judging the things which he was not permitted to hide. I feared to entrust the Church to him, especially established amid the rage of the heretics barking around. And when he asked me to give a letter, his case being intimated, to the presbyter of the estate of Armemane in the field of Bulla, whence he had come to us, lest he should suspect anything more atrocious about him, that he might live there, if it can be done, more corrected, without the office of the presbyterate, I, moved by mercy, did it. But this it especially behooved me to intimate to your prudence, lest some deceit creep upon you.

**2.** But I heard his cause when there were a hundred days to the Lord's Easter, which is to be on the eighth day before the Ides of April. This, on account of the council, I took care to intimate to your Venerability, which I did not conceal even from him, but faithfully disclosed to him what had been established: that if within a year he should neglect to plead his cause, if perhaps he thinks he has anything to do, henceforth let no one hear his voice. But we, most blessed lord and venerably to-be-received father, if we do not think these indications of the evil conduct of clergy—especially when a not good reputation has begun to accompany them—are to be punished except in that way which is established in council; we begin to be compelled to wish to discuss things which cannot be known, and either to condemn uncertain things, or to pass over things truly unknown. I, at least, judged that a presbyter—and one who, on a fast day, on which the Church of that same place too was fasting, saying farewell to his colleague, the presbyter of that same place, dared to remain at the house of a notorious woman, having no clergyman with him, and to lunch and sup, and to sleep in one house—was to be removed from the office of the presbyterate, fearing to entrust the Church of God to him henceforth. But if perhaps it seems otherwise to the ecclesiastical judges, because it was established in council that the cause of a presbyter be ended by six bishops, let him who wishes entrust to him the Church committed to his care: I, I confess, fear to entrust any people whatever to such men, especially those whom no good reputation defends, so that this could be pardoned them; lest, if anything more ruinous should burst forth, I, languishing, should impute it to myself.
