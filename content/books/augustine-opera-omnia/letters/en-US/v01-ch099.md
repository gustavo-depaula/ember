# Letter 98

TO BISHOP BONIFACE, AUGUSTINE SENDS GREETING IN THE LORD.

**1.** You ask of me *whether parents harm their baptized little ones, when they try to heal them by the sacrifices of demons. And if they do not harm them, how the faith of the parents profits them when they are baptized, whose unbelief cannot harm them.* Here I answer that so great is the power of that Sacrament, that is, of saving Baptism, in the holy structure of the body of Christ, that, once begotten through the carnal pleasure of others, when he has once been regenerated through the spiritual will of others, henceforth he cannot be bound by the chain of another's iniquity, to which he consents by no will of his own. For *both the soul of the father is mine,* he says, *and the soul of the son is mine. The soul that sins, it shall die*[^1]: but that soul does not sin, when the parents, with it altogether unknowing, or anyone else, apply to it the sacrileges of demons. But for this reason it drew from Adam that which is loosed by the grace of that Sacrament, because the soul was not yet living separately—that is, another soul, of which it might be said, *And the soul of the father is mine, and the soul of the son is mine.* And so now, when a man is in himself, made another from him who begot him, he is not held liable for another's sin without his own consent. He drew guilt, therefore, because he was one with him, and in him, from whom he drew it, when what he drew was committed. But one does not draw from another, when, each living his own proper life, there is now whence it may be said: *The soul that sins, it shall die.*

**2.** But that he can be regenerated through the office of another's will, when he is offered to be consecrated, this one Spirit does, by whom the offered one is regenerated. For it is not written, Unless one is born again of the parents' will, or of the faith of those offering or ministering; but: *Unless one is born again of water and the Holy Spirit*[^2]. Water, therefore, exhibiting outwardly the sacrament of grace, and the Spirit working inwardly the benefit of grace, loosing the chain of guilt, reconciling the good of nature, regenerate the man in the one Christ, begotten from the one Adam. The regenerating Spirit, then, in the elders offering, and in the little one offered and reborn, is common: therefore, through this fellowship of one and the same Spirit, the will of those offering profits the little one offered. But when elders sin against a little one, offering it and trying to bind it by the sacrilegious chains of demons, the soul of the two is not common, so that they could even have a common guilt. For guilt is not shared through another's will in the way that grace is shared through the unity of the Holy Spirit. For the one Holy Spirit can be both in this and in that man, even if they do not know one another, through whom the grace of both is common. But the spirit of a man cannot be both this man's and that man's, through whom, with one sinning and the other not sinning, the guilt should nevertheless be common. And through this, a little one, once begotten from the flesh of his parents, can be regenerated by the Spirit of God, so that the obligation contracted from them is loosed. But once regenerated by the Spirit of God, he cannot be regenerated from the flesh of his parents, so that the obligation which has been loosed is contracted again. And therefore the little one does not lose the grace of Christ once received, except by his own impiety, if with the advance of age he turns out so evil. For then he will begin to have even his own proper sins, which are not taken away by regeneration, but healed by another cure.

**3.** Nevertheless, parents—or whatever elders—trying to bind their sons, or any baptized little ones whatever, by the sacrileges of demons, are rightly called spiritually murderers. For in them indeed they do not effect a killing, but, as far as it lies in themselves, they become killers. It is rightly said to them, when they are forbidden from this crime, Do not kill your little ones: for the Apostle too says: *Do not extinguish the Spirit*[^3]; not because he can be extinguished, but, as far as it lies in themselves, they are deservedly called his extinguishers who so act as to wish him extinguished. In this sense can rightly be understood what the most blessed Cyprian wrote in his letter On the Lapsed, when reproving those who in the time of persecution had sacrificed to idols: "And lest anything be lacking," he says, "to the heap of crime, infants too, placed in their parents' hands or handled by them, the little ones lost, at the very first beginning of their nativity, what they had obtained." They lost it, he said, as far as it pertained to the crime of those by whom they were compelled to lose it. They lost it in the mind and will of those who committed so great an outrage against them. For if they had lost it in themselves, they would surely have remained to be condemned by the divine sentence without any defense. But if holy Cyprian had judged this, he would not have immediately subjoined their defense, saying: "Will they not, when the day of judgment comes, say, We did nothing, nor, forsaking the food and cup of the Lord, did we of our own accord hasten to profane contagions; another's perfidy destroyed us, we found our parents to be parricides: they denied us the Church as mother, they denied us the Lord as father, so that, while little and improvident, and ignorant of so great an outrage, we were joined through others to the fellowship of the crime, and were caught by another's fraud?" He would not append this defense, unless he believed it most just, and that it would profit the little ones in the judgment of God. For if it is truly said, We did nothing; *The soul that sins, it shall die,* neither will those perish under the just judgment of God, whom their parents, by their own crime, as far as pertains to the little ones themselves, destroyed.

**4.** But that which is recalled in the same letter—that a certain little girl, left to her nurse when her parents fled in confusion, and forced by the same nurse into the sacrileges of demons, afterward, when the Eucharist was brought to her in the church, rejected it with marvelous motions—seems to me to have been done divinely for this reason: lest the elders should think that they sinned nothing against the little ones in that iniquity, but rather should understand, through that gesture, signifying in a way, of the bodies of those who could not speak, that they were marvelously admonished what they themselves ought to do, who, after that so great wickedness, rushed upon the saving sacraments, from which they ought surely, by repenting, to abstain. Nor, when divine providence does any such thing through infants, is it to be believed that they themselves do it by knowledge or reason. For neither, because God willed to coerce the madness of a certain prophet even by an ass speaking[^4], is the wisdom of asses therefore to be admired. Further, if through an irrational animal something most like a man sounded forth, which is to be attributed to a divine miracle, not to an asinine heart, so could the Omnipotent, through the soul of an infant—not where there was no reason, but where it was still asleep—show, by the gesture of its body, what those who had sinned both against themselves and against their little ones ought to take care of. But, since the infant does not return into the parent, so as to be one man with him and in him, but is altogether another, having his own flesh and his own soul: *The soul that sins, it shall die.*

**5.** Nor let this move you, that some bring little ones to receive Baptism not with that faith, that they be regenerated by spiritual grace to eternal life, but because they think that by this remedy they retain or recover temporal health. For they are not for that reason not regenerated, because they are not offered by these people with this intention. For through them the necessary ministries are celebrated, and the words of the sacraments, without which the little one cannot be consecrated. But that Holy Spirit who dwells in the saints—of whom that one silvered dove is forged by the fire of love[^5]—does what he does even through the service, sometimes, not only of those simply ignorant, but even of those damnably unworthy. For the little ones are offered to receive spiritual grace, not so much by those in whose hands they are carried (although also by them, if they too are good faithful), as by the whole society of the saints and faithful. For they are rightly understood to be offered by all to whom it is pleasing that they are offered, and by whose holy and undivided love they are helped to the communication of the Holy Spirit. The whole mother Church, therefore, which is in the saints, does this, because the whole bears all, the whole bears each. For if the sacrament of Christian baptism, when it is one and the same, is valid and suffices for consecration even among heretics—although it does not suffice for participation in eternal life; which consecration indeed makes the heretic guilty, having the Lord's mark outside the Lord's flock, yet sound doctrine admonishes that he is to be corrected, not consecrated again likewise—how much more in the Catholic Church is the grain to be purged carried even through the ministry of the stubble, so that it may be brought, the threshing-floor mediating, to the fellowship of the mass?

**6.** But I do not wish this to deceive you, that you think the chain of guilt drawn from Adam cannot otherwise be broken, unless the little ones be offered by their parents to receive the grace of Christ: for so, writing, you say: "So that, just as the parents were the authors to their punishment, they may likewise be justified through the faith of the parents": when you see that many are offered not by parents, but even by any outsiders whatever, as little slaves are sometimes offered by their masters. And sometimes, with their parents dead, little ones are baptized, offered by those who could show them a mercy of this kind. Sometimes too those whom their parents cruelly exposed, to be nourished by anyone, are sometimes gathered by holy virgins, and offered by them to Baptism; who certainly neither had any children of their own, nor dispose to have them: and through this you see nothing else done here except what is written in the Gospel, when the Lord had asked which had been the neighbor to the man wounded by robbers and left half-alive in the road; for it was answered: *He who showed mercy on him*[^6].

**7.** You seemed indeed to propose to me a most difficult question, at the end of your inquiry, with that intention, namely, by which you are wont vehemently to beware of a lie. "If I set before you," you say, "a little one, and ask whether, when he grows up, he will be chaste, or will not be a thief; without doubt you will answer: I do not know. And whether, established in that very infant age, he is thinking something good or evil; you will say: I do not know. If, then, you dare to promise nothing certain about his future morals, and about his present thought; what is it that, when they are offered to Baptism, the parents answer for them as sureties, and say that they do what that age cannot think, or, if it can, is hidden? For we ask those by whom they are offered, and say: Does he believe in God? Of that age, which knows not whether God exists, they answer: He believes; and to the rest it is so answered, the several things which are asked. Hence I wonder that the parents in these matters answer so confidently for the little one, as to say that he does such great good things which, at the hour at which he is baptized, the baptizer asks—yet at that same hour, if I should subjoin, Will he who is baptized be chaste, or will he not be a thief? I do not know whether anyone dares to say, One of these things he will be, or will not be, as he answers me without doubt that he believes in God, and that he turns himself to God." Then, concluding your writing, you add and say: "To these questions, then, I ask that you deign to answer briefly, in such a way that you do not prescribe to me from custom, but render a reason."

**8.** These your letters read and reread, and considered as much as the narrowness of time allowed, I remembered Nebridius my friend, who, although he was a most diligent and most keen inquirer into obscure matters pertaining especially to the teaching of piety, greatly hated a brief answer to a great question. And whoever demanded this, he bore most ill, and, if his person allowed it, he restrained him with an indignant face and voice, deeming unworthy one who sought such things, when he did not know how many things could and ought to be said about so great a matter. But I am not, like him, angry with you in the same way: for you are a bishop occupied with many cares, as I am; whence neither does it easily have leisure for you to read anything lengthy, nor for me to write it. For that man, then a young man, who was unwilling to hear such things briefly, and asked about many things in our conversation, asked, at leisure, of one at leisure: but you, considering now who, and from whom, you demand these things, bid me answer briefly about so great a matter. Behold, I do as much as I can; may the Lord help that I may be able to do what you ask.

**9.** Surely we often speak in such a way that, as the Pasch approaches, we say that the Lord's passion is tomorrow or the day after, when he suffered so many years ago, and that passion was done only once. Surely on the Lord's day itself we say, Today the Lord rose; when so many years have passed since he rose. Why is no one so inept as to argue that we, speaking thus, have lied, except because we name these days according to the likeness of those on which these things were done, so that the day itself is said which is not itself, but, by the revolution of time, like it, and it is said that on that day there is done, on account of the celebration of the sacrament, what was done not on that day, but already long ago? Was not Christ immolated once in himself, and yet in the sacrament he is immolated for the peoples not only at all the solemnities of the Pasch, but every day, nor surely does he lie who, being asked, answers that he is immolated? For if the sacraments did not have a certain likeness of those things of which they are sacraments, they would not be sacraments at all. But from this likeness, for the most part, they also receive the names of the things themselves. As, then, according to a certain manner, the sacrament of the body of Christ is the body of Christ, the sacrament of the blood of Christ is the blood of Christ, so the sacrament of faith is faith. But to believe is nothing else than to have faith. And through this, when it is answered that the little one believes, who does not yet have the affection of faith, it is answered that he has faith on account of the sacrament of faith, and that he turns himself to God on account of the sacrament of conversion, because that very answer too pertains to the celebration of the sacrament. As the Apostle says of Baptism itself: *We have been buried together with Christ through Baptism into death*[^7]. He does not say: We signified a burial; but plainly says: *We have been buried together.* The sacrament of so great a thing, then, he named by no other name than that of the thing itself.

**10.** And so, although that faith which consists in the will of believers does not yet make the little one a believer, yet the sacrament of that very faith now makes him faithful. For just as it is answered that he believes, so too he is called faithful, not by assenting to the thing itself with the mind, but by receiving the sacrament of the thing itself. But when a man has begun to be wise, he will not repeat that sacrament, but will understand it, and will be fitted, with a will in accord, to its truth. As long as he cannot do this, the sacrament will avail for his protection against the contrary powers; and it will avail so much that, if before the use of reason he migrates from this life, through that very sacrament, with the love of the Church commending him, he is freed by Christian help from that condemnation which entered the world through one man[^8]. He who does not believe this, and judges that it cannot be done, is surely unfaithful, even if he has the sacrament of faith; and far better than him is the little one, who, even if he does not yet have faith in thought, yet does not oppose to it the obstacle of a contrary thought, whence he wholesomely receives the sacrament of it. I have answered, as I think, your questions—as far as pertains to the less capable, and to the contentious, not enough; but as far as to the peaceable and understanding, perhaps more than enough. Nor have I, for my excuse, cast at you a most firm custom, but I have rendered, as I could, the reason of a most wholesome custom.
