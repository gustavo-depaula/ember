# Letter 110

To the most blessed and sweetest lord, the venerable and exceedingly longed-for brother and fellow priest Severus, and to the brothers who are with you, Augustine and the brothers who are with me send greetings in the Lord.

**1.** My letter, which our dearest son and fellow deacon Timothy carried, was already prepared for him as he was about to set out, when our sons Quodvultdeus and Gaudentius came to us with your letter. Hence it happened that, setting out at once, he did not bring my reply, since after their arrival he stayed with us but a very little while, and seemed at every hour about to depart. But even if I had replied through him, I would still be a debtor. For even now, when I seem to have replied, I am a debtor—I do not say of love, which we owe the more the more we have spent it, of which the Apostle shows us perpetual debtors, saying, *Owe no one anything, except to love one another*[^1]—but of your very letter: for when shall I suffice for your sweetness, and so great an avidity of your mind, which, when read, it announced to me? It indeed intimated to me a thing in you most well known; yet, though no intimater to me of a new thing, it was nevertheless a new exactor of a reply.

**2.** You wonder perhaps why I call myself unequal to the discharge of this debt, since you feel so much about me, you who have known me as my own soul: but this very thing is what has made for me great difficulty in answering your letter, because I am sparing to say how great you seem to me, on account of your modesty; and surely, by saying less, when you have conferred on me so great praise, what shall I remain but a debtor? Which I would not care about, if I did not know that those things you spoke about me to me were said from a most sincere love, and not from a flattery hostile to friendship. For in this way I would not become a debtor, because I would not owe to repay such things: but the more I know with how faithful a mind you speak, the more I see by how great a debt I am weighed down.

**3.** But see what has befallen me, that in a manner I have praised myself, I who have said that I was faithfully praised by you. But what else should I say than that of which I admonished concerning you, whom you know? Behold, I have made for myself a new question which you did not propose, and which perhaps you await that I should solve: so little was it that I was a debtor, unless I also heaped myself up with a larger debt; although this is easy to show, and, if I do not show it, easy for you to see, that both true things can be said unfaithfully, and untrue things faithfully. For he who believes as he speaks, even if he does not speak true things, speaks faithfully; but he who does not believe what he speaks, even if he speaks true things, speaks unfaithfully. Do I then doubt that you believe of me the things you wrote? Which, since I do not recognize them in myself, you could faithfully say untrue things about me.

**4.** But I do not wish you to be so deceived even by benevolence; to which benevolence I am a debtor, because I too could, both so faithfully and so benevolently, say about you the things that are true, except that, as I said above, I would spare your modesty. But when I am praised by the most genuine and most familiar of my soul, I hold it as though I were praised by myself. Which you perceive how troublesome it is, even if true things be said. How much more, since, even though you are another soul of mine—nay, though your soul and mine are one—you are so deceived about me, supposing that there are present in me things which are lacking, in the way that one man can be deceived even about himself? Which I do not wish, not only lest you whom I love be deceived, but also lest you pray less that I may be what you already believe me to be: nor in this am I a debtor to you, that, with the same advance of benevolence, I should believe and speak good things about you which you yourself still recognize to be lacking in you, but that, with a mind indeed so benevolent, I should say only those good things of yours, gifts of God, of which I am certain in you. Which I do not on this account fail to do, lest I be deceived about them, but lest you, praised by me, should seem to have praised yourself; and on account of that rule of justice by which I do not wish done to me what I would not wish. But if it ought to be done, I choose to be a debtor, as long as I think it ought not to be done; but if it ought not to be done, neither am I a debtor.

**5.** But I know what you can answer me to these things: you speak thus as though I had desired your lengthy letter about my praises. Far be it that I should believe this of you; but your letter, full—I will not say of how true, or how untrue—yet full of my praises, demanded this of me, that I should reprehend this in myself, even with you unwilling. For if you wished me to write something else, you desired me a bestower, not a repayer. Now the order of justice holds thus, that we first repay the debt; then afterward, to the one to whom we repay, if it so please, we may also give something. Although even such things as you desired me to write, if we more diligently ponder the Lord's precepts, we repay rather than give, if nothing is to be owed to anyone except to love one another. For love itself exacts the debt, that, serving brotherly charity, we help, in what we can, him who rightly wishes to be helped. But, my brother, I believe that you too know how great are the things in my hands, by which, on account of the various cares which the necessity of our service has, scarcely the fewest drops of time are dripped to me, which, if I spend them on other matters, I seem to myself to act against my office.

**6.** For as to what you wish, that I write you a lengthy letter, this indeed I owe, I confess; I owe it altogether to your so sweet, so sincere, so pure will. But because you are a good lover of justice, I admonish you from that very justice which you love to hear this from me the more gladly. You perceive that what I owe both to you and to others comes before what I owe to you alone; and time does not suffice me for all things, when not even for those which are prior. Whence all my dearest and most familiar friends, among whom in the name of Christ you are among the first to me, will do the duty of their office if they not only do not themselves impose other things to be written by me, but also forbid the rest, with as much authority and holy kindness as they can; lest I seem hard, when I do not give what is asked by individuals, while I wish rather to repay those things which I owe to all. Finally, when, as we hope and hold by promise, Your Venerableness comes to us, you will know with what works of letters, and how much, I am occupied, and will more insistently do what I have asked, that you also demolish from me others, whom you can, who wish to enjoin upon me something else to be written. May the Lord our God fill the so great and so holy bosom of your heart, which he himself has made, most blessed lord.

[^1]: Rom 13:8
