# Letter 129

To the honorable and most beloved son, the most illustrious and respectable man, the tribune and notary Marcellinus, Aurelius, Silvanus, and all the Catholic bishops.

**1.** The official notification, or the letter, of our brothers—whom we desire to be converted to Catholic peace from a pernicious dissension—has made us much solicitous, because they were unwilling to consent to the edict of your Nobility, by which you provided for the tranquility and quiet of our very disputation; lest perhaps, even if not all, yet some of them, through the tumult or din of the multitude, hinder the conference, which ought to be peaceful and peaceable. And would that this thought may not tempt them, and that this suspicion rather deceive us! And therefore, if they all wish to be present, that, when it shall seem good to them, we too being gathered there with them, we may at once go out thence concordant and peaceable, and, the schismatic division being corrected, bound by the brotherly bond of Christ's unity, with all good men marveling and rejoicing, but the devil alone and those like him grieving, let us together proceed into the Church to give thanks and render praises to God with the most ardent and most lucid love.

**2.** For what great thing is it, if a peaceable eye attend, and Christian thought not desert to consider and see, that—human accusations, whether true or false, being removed—the Church is to be sought in those writings where Christ its Redeemer became known? For just as we do not hear against Christ those who say that his body was stolen from the sepulcher by the disciples, so we ought not to hear against his Church those who say that it is nowhere but among the Africans alone, and the very fewest associates of the Africans: for the Apostle says that truthful Christians are members of Christ; just as, then, we do not believe that the dead flesh of Christ perished from the sepulcher by anyone's theft, so we ought not to believe that his living members perished from the world by anyone's sin. It is not, therefore, difficult—since Christ is the head, and the Church the body—to see commended together in the Gospel both the head against the calumnies of the Jews, and the body against the accusations of the heretics. For what is read, *It behooved Christ to suffer, and to rise from the dead on the third day*, is against those who say that the dead one was taken from the sepulcher; but what follows, *and that repentance and remission of sins be preached in his name through all nations, beginning from Jerusalem*[^1], is against those who say that the Church is not in the whole world: so that by one brief chapter and a few words, both the enemy of the head and the enemy of the body may be repelled, and, if he attend faithfully, corrected.

**3.** For that our brothers hold these enmities we grieve the more, the more it is agreed that they hold the same Scriptures with us, in which these most open testimonies are contained. For the Jews, who deny that Christ rose, at least do not receive the Gospel: but these our brothers are bound by the authority of both Testaments; and yet they wish to accuse us about the Gospel handed over, and refuse to believe it when recited. But now perhaps, the care of this conference being undertaken, they have searched the sacred Scriptures more diligently; and because in them they have found innumerable testimonies by which the Church was promised to be in all nations, and in the whole world—as we see it began to be rendered and presented in the Gospel and the apostolic Letters, and in the Acts of the Apostles, where the very places, and cities, and provinces are read through which it grew, beginning from Jerusalem, so as to spread thence even into Africa, not by migrating but by growing; but have not found any testimony of the divine words where it is said that it would perish from the other parts of the world, and remain in Africa alone, in the party of Donatus; and have seen how absurd it is that, for that which was to perish, so many divine testimonies should be recited, but for that which, as they suppose, was to be pleasing to the Lord, no testimony of it should be found: thinking of these things perhaps, to end vain and pernicious enmities, contrary to eternal salvation, they all wished to come together to the place of our conference, not that a new tumult may arise, but that the old discord may be ended.

**4.** For that too, by which they are wont to be gravely irritated against us, that the kings of the earth—who, it was so long before foretold, would serve Christ the Lord—establish laws against heretics and schismatics for Catholic peace, we believe that they have at some time considered is not to be blamed: because the ancient kings too, not only of the Hebrew nation but even of foreigners, terrified all the peoples of their kingdoms with most threatening precepts, lest anyone should not only do, but even say anything against the God of Israel, that is, the true God; and the forefathers of these men sent the very cause of Caecilian, whence this dissension was born, to the emperor Constantine, accusing through the proconsul Anulinus; which they seem to have done for no other reason than that the emperor Constantine should decree something by royal authority against those who should be overcome, on behalf of those who should overcome; and they could have found that whole cause (and perhaps did, by the necessity of the conference itself), the public archives being searched, to have been ended long ago, after the ecclesiastical judgments by which Caecilian was acquitted, that emperor too judging, to whose examination they both first sent the whole matter and afterward brought it. There too they could have found the cause of Felix of Aptunga, the ordainer of Caecilian—whom they had called in their council the fount of all evils—cleared, Aelianus the proconsul examining it by the precept of that same emperor.

**5.** Although, even if they attended to this—and, what was easy, noticed it—that in the holy Scriptures the Church of Christ was promised to be, with tares, and chaff, and bad fish mixed in, until the time of harvest, of winnowing, and of the shore: surely they could have considered that, even if Caecilian and his fellow bishops had had a bad cause, they could have prejudiced nothing against the Christian world, which God so long before promised to a few believers, and now, with many seeing, has rendered. Unless perhaps a sinning man availed more against the Church than God swearing for the Church, and iniquity, what it lost, than truth, what it promised. How foolish and impious it is to hold these things they have now perhaps seen: they have considered too that the Maximianists, condemned by them—the condemners of Primian—they took care to have driven even from the basilicas through earthly powers, and there, by their more recent example, learned more certainly that it is not a sin if the Church demands something of the sort against its rebels from powers of this kind: and that they afterward received some of the condemned—to whom, while condemning them, they gave a delay, along with very many others set in the same fellowship of schism—whom they said remained unpolluted in the communion of the sacrilegious shoot of Maximian, and the Baptism either by the condemned or by their associates, though given outside in schism, they did not dare to rescind and repeat; they surely judged well enough that those things which they said against us were condemned by their own example: and it is to be believed that they now understand how unworthy, how intolerable it is that, while they sit in episcopal chairs with these men, and with Primian himself together his condemners and the condemned in his cause, in order that there may be peace in the party of Donatus, the Christian world is defamed about Caecilian, lest the unity of Christ live in peace.

**6.** Thinking perhaps of all these things, and moved by the fear of God, they all wished to be present at the place of the conference, not by a disposition of tumult, but of peace. For as to the fact that they said they were all present for this reason, that their number might appear—since their adversaries have often lied that they are few: this, if it was ever said by our people, could most truly be said of those places where the number of our fellow bishops and clerics and laymen is far greater, and especially in the proconsular province: although, except for consular Numidia, even in the other African provinces they are most easily surpassed by the number of our people; or at any rate, in comparison with all the nations through which Catholic communion is spread, we most rightly say that they are very few. But now, if they had wished their number to become known, would it not become known more orderly and tranquilly through their subscriptions, which, by their mandate, with you perceiving it, you admonished by edict that they should append? What, then, does it mean that they desire all to be present at the place of the conference? For if they do not think of peace, what will they not disturb if they are going to speak, or what will they do there if they are going to be silent? For even if there be no clamor, the mere whisper itself of many will make a great enough din, by which that conference may be hindered.

**7.** But what is it that they thought should be set down in their notification, that they justly demanded that all be present because all were summoned to come? As though a few could be chosen who ought to be present, except by all who had come, that they should subscribe, with you present, to their choice, and so all be in the few, when the few were chosen by all. Either, then, tumult is thought of by them, or peace; of which we desire the latter, we guard against the former: and therefore, lest perhaps, which God forbid, that be prepared rather which we guard against than what we desire, we consent that all those be present, provided, however, that there be present from us so great a number as has seemed sufficient to your Respectability; so that, if anything tumultuous through the crowds shall emerge, it be rightly imputed only to those in whose party there shall be present, for a matter to be done by a few, an altogether superfluous multitude. But if—what we desire with all our wishes, what we ardently seek, what we suppliantly entreat of the Lord—the cause of making unity shall require that multitude, when they wish, we shall all be present, and to so great a good, with him helping who gives this, we shall eagerly fly together, saying: *You are our brothers*—now not to those who detest us, but who, hatred being ended, embrace us, that the name of the Lord may be honored, and appear to them in gladness, now experiencing with us *how good and pleasant it is for brothers to dwell as one*[^2]. (And in another hand): We wish you, son, well in God. (Likewise in another hand): I, Aurelius, bishop of the Catholic church of Carthage, have subscribed. (Likewise): I, Silvanus of the first seat of the province of Numidia, have subscribed.

[^1]: Luke 24:46-47
[^2]: Ps 133:1 (132:1)
