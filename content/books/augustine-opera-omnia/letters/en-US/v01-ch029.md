# Letter 29

Letter of the presbyter of Hippo Regius to Alypius, bishop of Thagaste, concerning the birthday of Leontius, formerly bishop of Hippo

**1.** About the business which meanwhile I cannot help being concerned about, I could write nothing certain, brother Macarius being absent, who is said to be returning soon; and what, with God's help, can be carried through, will be carried through. But about our solicitude for them, although our brothers who are fellow citizens and were present could set their own at ease; yet a thing worthy of epistolary conversation, by which we console one another, has been granted by the Lord; in the obtaining of which we believe we have been greatly helped by your very solicitude, which surely could not have been without intercession for us.

**2.** And so let us not fail to relate to your Charity what was done, that you may give thanks to God with us for the benefit received, you who poured out prayers with us for the receiving of it. When, after your departure, it had been reported to us that the people were in an uproar and saying that they could not bear that the festival should be forbidden which, calling it "Joy," they vainly try to hide under the name of drunkenness—as was being reported even while you were present—it opportunely happened for us, by the hidden ordinance of almighty God, that on the fourth day of the week that chapter in the Gospel was being treated in due course: *Do not give what is holy to the dogs, nor cast your pearls before swine*[^1]. There was, then, a treatment about dogs and swine, in such a way that both those quarreling with obstinate barking against God's precepts, and those given over to the filth of carnal pleasures, were compelled to blush; and it was so concluded that they saw how wicked it was to do within the walls of the church, in the name of religion, that which, if they persisted in doing it in their own houses, ought to keep them away from what is holy and from the ecclesiastical pearls.

**3.** But although these things were gratefully received, yet, because few had assembled, so great a matter was not yet settled. And when this sermon was bandied about outside by those who were present, each according to his ability and zeal, it had many contradictors. But after the day of Lent had dawned, and a frequent multitude came to the hour of the treatment, that passage in the Gospel was read where the Lord, having driven the sellers of animals from the temple and overturned the tables of the money-changers, said that the house of his Father had been made a den of thieves instead of a house of prayer[^2]: which chapter, when I had made them attentive by setting forth the question of drunkenness, I myself also recited, and added a discussion by which I might show how much more movingly and vehemently our Lord would drive drunken banquets, which are everywhere base, from the temple, from which he thus drove out permitted commerce, when those things were being sold which were necessary for the sacrifices lawful at that time; asking them to which they thought a den of thieves more like, those selling necessary things or those drinking immoderately.

**4.** And since I held in store the prepared readings to be supplied, I then added that the still carnal people of the Jews themselves, in that temple where the body and blood of the Lord was not yet offered, never celebrated banquets, not only with drunkenness, but not even sober ones; nor are they found in their history to have been intoxicated publicly in the name of religion, except when they kept festival to a fabricated idol[^3]. And while I was saying these things, I took the book and recited that whole passage. I added also, with what grief I could, that since the Apostle says, to distinguish the Christian people from the hardness of the Jews, that his Epistle was written not on tablets of stone, but on the fleshly tablets of the heart[^4], when Moses the servant of God, on account of those leaders, had broken the two tablets of stone[^5], how could we not break the hearts of these men, who, men of the New Testament, wished solemnly to display, on the days of the saints to be celebrated, those things which the people of the Old Testament celebrated both once and to an idol.

**5.** Then, the book of Exodus returned, exaggerating the crime of drunkenness as much as time allowed, I took up the apostle Paul, and showed among what sins it was placed, reading that passage: *If anyone is named a brother and is either a fornicator, or a servant of idols, or covetous, or a reviler, or a drunkard, or a robber, with such a one not even to take food*[^6]; warning, with groaning, with how great a danger we would banquet with those who got drunk even in their houses. I read also that which follows at no long interval: *Do not be deceived; neither fornicators, nor servants of idols, nor adulterers, nor the effeminate, nor liers with men, nor thieves, nor the covetous, nor drunkards, nor revilers, nor robbers will possess the kingdom of God. And such indeed you were; but you have been washed, but you have been justified in the name of the Lord Jesus Christ and in the Spirit of our God*[^7]. When these had been read, I said that they should consider how the faithful could hear, *but you have been washed*, who still allow the filth of such desire, against which the kingdom of heaven is closed, to be in their heart, that is, in the inner temple of God. Then we came to that chapter: *When you come together into one, it is not to celebrate the Lord's supper: for each one takes his own supper first in the eating; and one indeed is hungry, another is drunk. Have you not houses to eat and drink in? or do you despise the Church of God?*[^8] which, when recited, I more diligently urged that not even honorable and sober banquets ought to be celebrated in the church; since the Apostle did not say: Have you not houses to get drunk in? as if only getting drunk were not permitted in the church; but: *to eat and drink in*, which can be done honorably, but apart from the church, by those who have houses where they can be refreshed with necessary food; and yet that we have been brought to these straits of corrupt times and dissolute morals that we wish for, not yet modest banquets, but at least a household reign of drunkenness.

**6.** I recalled also the chapter of the Gospel which I had treated the day before, where it is said of false prophets: *By their fruits you will know them*[^9]. Then I called to mind that the fruits in that place are called nothing but works; then I asked among which fruits drunkenness was named, and recited that passage to the Galatians: *Now the works of the flesh are manifest, which are fornications, uncleannesses, lusts, the service of idols, sorceries, enmities, quarrels, jealousies, animosities, dissensions, heresies, envies, drunkennesses, revelings, and the like; which I foretell to you, as I have foretold, that those who do such things will not possess the kingdom of God*[^10]. After which words I asked how we could be recognized as Christians by the fruit of drunkenness, whom the Lord bade be recognized by their fruits. I added also, to be read, that which follows: *But the fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, long-suffering, kindness, goodness, faith, meekness, continence*[^11]: and I urged them to consider how shameful and lamentable it was that, of those fruits of the flesh, they desired not only to live by them privately, but even to render an honor of the Church to them, and, if the power were given, would fill the whole space of so great a basilica with crowds of feasters and drunkards; but of the spiritual fruits, to which they were invited both by the authority of the divine Scriptures and by our groans, they are unwilling to offer gifts to God, and to celebrate, by these most of all, the festivals of the saints.

**7.** When these things were done, I returned the book, and, prayer having been ordered, as much as I was able, and as much as the very danger pressed me and the Lord deigned to administer strength, I set before their eyes the common danger, both of those who were committed to us and of us who were going to render an account of them to the prince of shepherds; and by his humility, his signal insults, the blows and the spittings in his face, and the slaps, and the crown of thorns, and the cross and the blood, I beseeched them that, if they had offended themselves in anything, they would at least have pity on us, and would consider the ineffable love of the venerable old man Valerius toward me, who did not hesitate to lay upon me, on their account, the so dangerous burden of treating the words of truth, and who often told them that his prayers had been heard concerning our coming; whom he rejoiced to have come to him surely not for a common death or for a spectacle of their death, but for a common effort toward eternal life. Finally, I also said that I was certain, and trusted in him who cannot lie, who promised through the mouth of his prophet, saying of our Lord Jesus Christ: *If his sons forsake my law, and do not walk in my precepts, if they profane my statutes, I will visit their crimes with a rod, and their sins with scourges; but my mercy I will not take away*[^12]: that I trusted in him, therefore, that if they should despise these so great things which had been read and said to them, he would visit them with a rod and with a scourge, and would not permit them to be condemned with this world. In which complaint it was so done that, according to the greatness of the business and the danger, our protector and governor supplied courage and ability. I did not move their tears with my tears; but when such things were being said, I confess, anticipated by their weeping, I could not hold back my own. And when we had now wept together, in fullest hope of their correction, an end was made of my sermon.

**8.** But on the next day, when the day had dawned for which they were accustomed to prepare their throats and bellies, it was reported to me that some, even of those who had been present at the sermon, had not yet ceased from murmuring, and that the force of a most evil custom prevailed so greatly in them that, using only its voice, they would say: Why now? For surely those who in former times did not forbid these things were not non-Christians. Hearing this, I was altogether at a loss what greater engines to prepare for stirring them: I was nevertheless arranging, if they thought they should persist, to read that passage from the prophet Ezekiel: *The watchman is acquitted, if he has announced the danger, even if those to whom it is announced refuse to take heed*[^13], to shake out my garments and depart. But then the Lord showed that he does not desert us, and in what ways he exhorts us to presume upon him: for before the hour at which we were to mount the platform, those very men whom I had heard had complained about the assault on the old custom came in to me; whom, kindly received, I brought over in a few words to a sound opinion; and when the time of discussion came, omitting the reading I had prepared, because it now seemed unnecessary, I discoursed a little about this very question, that we could bring forward nothing either briefer or truer against those who say, Why now? than that we too should say: At least now.

**9.** Nevertheless, lest those who before us either permitted or did not dare to forbid such manifest crimes of the unskilled multitude should seem to be touched by any insult from us, I set forth to them by what necessity these things seemed to have arisen in the Church: namely, that after persecutions so many and so violent, when peace had been made, the crowds of the gentiles wishing to come to the Christian name were hindered by this, that they were accustomed to spend their festal days with their idols in an abundance of feasting and drunkenness, and could not easily abstain from these most ruinous and so very ancient pleasures, it had seemed good to our forefathers that this part of their weakness should be spared for the time being, and that in place of the festal days they were leaving behind they should celebrate other days in honor of the holy martyrs, with at least not a similar sacrilege, though with a similar luxury: now that they were bound together by the name of Christ and subjected to the yoke of so great an authority, the salutary precepts of sobriety might be handed over to them, which they would now be unable to resist on account of the honor and fear of him who commands them; and that therefore it was now time that those who do not dare to deny themselves Christians should begin to live according to the will of Christ, so that the things which were granted in order that they might become Christians should be rejected now that they are Christians.

**10.** Then I exhorted them that we should be willing to be imitators of the churches across the sea, in which these things have either never been admitted, or have already, through good rulers, with the people obeying, been corrected. And since examples of daily wine-drinking were brought forward from the basilica of the blessed apostle Peter, I said first that we had often heard it had been forbidden, but that, because the place is far removed from the bishop's dwelling, and in so great a city there is a great multitude of carnal men—especially pilgrims, who keep coming, new ones again and again, the more violently retaining that custom the more ignorantly—so monstrous a plague had not yet been able to be checked and quieted. But that we, if we honored the apostle Peter, ought to hear his precepts, and to look much more devoutly upon the Epistle in which his will appears than upon the basilica in which it does not appear; and immediately, having taken the book, I recited where he says: *For since Christ suffered for us in the flesh, arm yourselves also with the same intention; for he who has suffered in the flesh has ceased from the flesh, so as now to live the rest of his time in the flesh no longer by the desires of men, but by the will of God. For the time past suffices to have accomplished the will of men, walking in lusts, desires, drunkenness, revelings, and unspeakable services of idols*[^14]. When these had been done, since I saw all going with one mind into good will, despising the evil custom, I exhorted them to be present at the divine readings and psalms at the noon hour; that thus that day might be celebrated much more purely and sincerely; and surely from the multitude of those assembling it could easily appear who followed the mind and who the belly. And so, all having been read, the sermon was ended.

**11.** But on the afternoon of the day a greater multitude was present than before noon, and up to the hour at which we went out with the bishop, there was reading and psalm-singing in turn; and after we had gone out, two psalms were read. Then the old man compelled me, unwilling—for I now desired that so dangerous a day were finished—and bade me say something to them. I gave a brief sermon, by which I might give thanks to God. And since we heard that in the basilica of the heretics they were celebrating their accustomed banquets, since they still, even at that very time when these things were being done by us, persisted in their cups, I said that the beauty of the day is adorned by comparison with night, and the white color made more pleasing by the nearness of black; that thus our gathering for a spiritual celebration would perhaps have been less pleasant, had not, on the other side, a carnal gorging been set against it; and I exhorted them to seek such feasts eagerly, if they had tasted how sweet the Lord is; but that those were to be feared who, as though it were the highest good, pursue what will at some time be destroyed, since each becomes the companion of the thing he worships, and that the Apostle insulted such men saying: *whose God is the belly*[^15], though the same Apostle said in another place: *Food for the belly and the belly for food; but God will destroy both this and that*[^16]. That we ought therefore to follow that which is not destroyed, which, most remote from the affection of the flesh, is retained by the sanctification of the spirit; and to this effect, for the time, when the things which the Lord deigned to suggest had been said, the evening services which are customary each day were performed, and as we withdrew with the bishop, the brothers sang a hymn in the same place, a not small multitude of both sexes remaining and singing psalms until the day grew dark.

**12.** I have set forth to you, as briefly as I could, what who could doubt that you desired to hear? Pray that God may deign to turn away from our efforts all scandals and all weariness. We rest with you in great part with the alacrity of fervor, because such frequent gifts of the spiritual Church of Thagaste are reported to us. The ship with the brothers has not yet come. At Hasna, where the presbyter brother Argentius is, the Circumcellions, invading our basilica, shattered the altar. The case is now being conducted; that it may be conducted peacefully and as befits the catholic Church, for the suppressing of the tongues of an unpeaceful heresy, we greatly beg you to pray. We have sent the letter of the Asiarch. Most blessed ones, may you persevere in the Lord, mindful of us. Amen.
