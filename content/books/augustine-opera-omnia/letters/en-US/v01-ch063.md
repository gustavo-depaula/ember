# Letter 63

To the most blessed and venerable lord, and to be embraced with most sincere love, brother and fellow priest Severus, and the brothers who are with you, Augustine, and the brothers who are with me, send greeting in the Lord

**1.** If I say the things which the very cause compels me to say, where will be the solicitude of love? But if I do not say them, where will be the freedom of friendship? Yet, wavering meanwhile, I have chosen to clear myself rather than to accuse you. You wrote that you wonder that we, with our grief, wished to tolerate what could have been amended by correction: as though things ill done are not to be grieved over, even if afterward, as far as they can be, they are corrected; or as though that is not most of all to be tolerated which, when it is manifest that it was done amiss, cannot be made undone. Cease therefore to wonder, most sincere brother. For Timothy was ordained subdeacon at Subsana, against my counsel and will; while what should be done about him, deliberation still wavered amid our alternating opinions. Behold, I still grieve, although he has now returned to you; in which we do not repent of having obeyed your will.

**2.** Hear also what by chiding, what by admonishing, what by praying we corrected, even before he had set out from here; lest it still seem to you on that account that nothing was then corrected by us, because he himself had not yet returned to you. By chiding we corrected, first the man himself who did not obey you, that, brother Carcedonius being unconsulted, he set out before to your Holiness, whence the origin of this our tribulation arose; then the presbyter and Verinus, through whom we learned that it was done that he was ordained. For when, we chiding, all confessed that all these things were not rightly done, and asked that they be pardoned; we would act too proudly, if we did not believe them to be corrected. For neither could they bring it about that the things were not done; but neither did we, by chiding, do anything else, except that they might recognize and grieve that they had done amiss. But by admonishing we corrected, first all, that henceforth they not dare such things, lest they experience the wrath of God: then especially Timothy, who said that by an oath alone he was compelled to go to your Charity; that, if your Holiness, as we hoped it would be, considering the things we had spoken together, on account of the scandal of the weak, for whom Christ died, and on account of the discipline of the Church, which they perilously neglect, since here he had already begun to be a reader, should not wish him to be with you; he, now free from the bond of the oath, might with a most even mind serve God, to whom we are to render an account of our acts. Brother Carcedonius himself too, as much as we could, by admonishing we had brought to this, that he himself too should most patiently accept whatever the provision and necessity of preserving ecclesiastical discipline should compel to be done about him. But by praying we had corrected ourselves, that we might commend both the governings and the outcomes of our counsels to the mercy of God, and, if any indignation had bitten us, by fleeing under his medicinal right hand, be healed. Behold how many things, partly by chiding, partly by admonishing, partly by praying, we had corrected.

**3.** And now, considering the bond of love, that we not be possessed by Satan, for we are not ignorant of his devices[^1], what else ought we to do but obey your will, who thought that what was done could not be corrected, unless he, in whom you complain that an injury was done to you, were restored to your right? This too brother Carcedonius himself, although not without a not-light disturbance of mind, about which I ask that you pray for him, yet, thinking of Christ in you, did with equanimity. And when I still thought it should be considered whether, Timothy remaining with us, I should send another letter to your Brotherliness; he himself feared your fatherly commotion; and cut short my deliberation, not only allowing, but even insisting, that Timothy be restored to you.

**4.** But I, brother Severus, leave my cause to your judgment. For I am certain that Christ dwells in your heart; through whom I beseech you that you consult him, presiding over your mind subject to him, whether a man who, in the church committed to my dispensation, had already begun to read, and not once, but a second and a third time, had read at Subsana—accompanied also by the presbyter of the Church of Subsana—and at Turres, and at Cizan, and at Verbalis, can or ought to be judged not to have been a reader. And just as we corrected, by God's bidding, what was afterward done with us unwilling; so do you too, by the same one bidding, similarly correct what was first done without your knowing. For I do not fear lest you understand too little how great an entrance is opened to dissolving the order of ecclesiastical discipline, if the clergyman of another Church should swear to anyone that he will not withdraw from him, while he allows him to be with him, affirming that he does this lest he be the author of his perjury; whereas surely he who will not allow this, will not permit the man to remain with him either, because he could swear about himself, not about another, and he himself keeps the peaceful rule without any reproach.

[^1]: See 2 Cor 2:11
