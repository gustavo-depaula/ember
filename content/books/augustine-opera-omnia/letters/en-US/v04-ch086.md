# Letter 22\*

A MEMORANDUM TO THE BROTHERS ALYPIUS AND PEREGRINUS; AUGUSTINE SENDS GREETINGS IN THE LORD

**1.** On the day before the Nones of March, in Numidia, at Mazaca, there was a council of the bishops of Numidia, at which I could not be present, both because of other matters and because of the cold, which your Holiness knows I bear most ill. But there was such a complaint there about the shortage of clergy, on account of the law that compels them to be assigned to the public services of their persons, that they were compelled, through the brothers who had gathered, to send legates from there to the imperial court. One of these, our brother and fellow bishop Peter, when he was arranging to sail from our shore, asked me to write to your Charity; and if he finds you in such a place that you can, in God's mercy, assist this very cause—since it is not to be despised—in any way, the Lord will have rendered no small help to their labor.

**2.** For this is what more gravely vexes the reflection of us all: that, when idols were being served, there was no lack of an abundance of men holding exemptions, through whom the ministries of those sacrilegious rites were supplied and even overflowed; but we are hemmed into such straits that there is found, or scarcely found, no kind of men from whom the ordination of clergy may be made, especially in the cities, where there are either men of the curial order or commoners—men who among us cannot be distinguished from members of guilds, as your Holiness knows—whereas provision could be made for all needs in this way, that a number be fixed determining what proportion of men out of a given total it would be permitted to ordain. But why ordinations should be failing at this time is little attended to; namely, because there are lacking defenders who might in some way protect them from the wickedness of more powerful persons by whom they are ground down, and who, supported by suitable rank and chosen by their fellow citizens, might be able to assert against those by whom they are despised the laws passed on their behalf—men among whom they have a good reputation, so that in them there is both honesty and authority. When these are lacking to the cities, or to the territories belonging to those same cities, we groan in vain for those wretched people whom we cannot help.

**3.** For we are despised by the wicked, because they know that we, bound by our ecclesiastical profession, cannot do anything by which they might be endangered or punished. For if we wished to repel their violence by an ecclesiastical hand, they complain about us to those authorities by whom they are sent, that public necessities are being hindered through us, and they are readily believed; and feeling secure, they say whatever they wish, knowing that even in our own defense we are not permitted to lay their deeds bare before those by whom, once known, they could be punished. But if we ourselves complain about them unprompted, we shall seem to have taken on the role of accusers. So it comes about that we are able to be of some solace or protection to the very few who take refuge in the Church; but the others, far more numerous men found outside, or their goods, are laid waste while we groan and are unable to help.

**4.** Hence our people of Hippo wish—and I most of all wishing it—to have a defender, but we are uncertain whether it is permitted to obtain one who is in military service; and if it is, we all want our son Ursus, the son-in-law of Glyverius; but if it is not permitted except a private citizen, we think that one of our sons, that is, either Eusebius or Eleusinus, could fill this role—although even in the very curial orders of the cities suitable men in character and skill could be found, if there were given to them a rank in which there could be sufficient authority. I have written these things to your Venerableness so that, if the Lord gives you the opportunity, you may not find it burdensome to do this also.

**5.** Besides, the bishop Honorius of the province of Caesariensis, whom you well know, brother Alypius, is being demanded by the people of Caesarea, with great scandal to the Church, after the death of our brother of holy memory Deuterius, that he himself be established there as bishop. Certain religious persons have written to us from there how great an evil would be done if this were done. Meanwhile, when the bishops had gathered at that city for the sake of its very need, so that the people might choose whom they desired to have ordained for them, they were compelled, even by the grave injuries of a turbulent multitude, to place him there as if discharging the duties of the proper bishop, until the Apostolic See and the prelate of the Church of Carthage should be consulted, so that it might then be done, if they themselves were willing—which they will in no way be able to will against the Council of Nicaea and the other councils of bishops.

**6.** We, meanwhile, wrote to the bishops that it had not been sent by us for this reason—that the Apostolic See had replied to our report that a metropolitan bishop was not yet established there—and on this occasion we dissuaded them as far as we could from doing about this man what the seditious multitude demands; and a reply was written to us by few, indeed, but very well. For fifteen bishops wrote back to us, although one signed; it has, moreover, even the name of the first see at the head, but we have learned that he was absent; and they thought his name should be added for this reason, because he himself had written that some should gather at the city of Castellum, through whom a quick reply might be made to us. They also wrote to the same bishop Honorius that he should either depart from Caesarea or the clergy would not be in communion with him and he should know that he was not to be communicated with; and to the people themselves they gave a fitting letter, at the same time admonishing the clergy that they should request such a bishop as ought, with ecclesiastical discipline preserved, to be ordained for them.

**7.** But they, and especially the poor, when the very letters began to be read to them, were stirred up into a horrible sedition—although these things were done in his absence; for after he received our letters, he immediately departed from there and came to us and was with us at Hippo when I dictated this, promising that he would do nothing other than what we wished; he said, however, that he had not been able to be dismissed by them unless he swore that he would return to them if a reply were made to their legates by the fathers according to their will. As to his ambition, there is no need to say how many things are tossed about, since perhaps they cannot be proved. Let your Holiness suggest these things to the bishop Boniface, although nothing is to be feared, with the Lord's help, that in such a case there is need to creep up on such a man. His defenders wish to plead the case by examples of negligence, gathering from wherever they can places where such things have at some time been done, as if for that reason these things ought to be heaped up and not rather repressed and guarded against from happening hereafter—especially with him established in the Apostolic See, with whom all ambition is idle; and because illicit things are demanded to the ruin of ecclesiastical discipline, they try indeed even this, or rather establish this as it were the bulwark of their case: that the same Honorius had been ordained in that diocese which belonged to the Church of Cartenna.

**8.** But this came about because the predecessor of our brother Rusticus, who then presided there, ordered the bishop still to sit, and it was done with the willing consent of the one who then had it in his power that it should not be done. There, moreover, before this Honorius, his father had sat, and had been transferred from there to Caesarea, and had ordained his son in his own place. Therefore they think it permitted to them even now to do about the son what they did about the father, and that the bishop Rusticus had demanded that the same diocese be restored to him—but, as you know, he was reclaiming certain congregations, not the mother church itself, in which the bishop was established. They hold, moreover, letters of brother Rusticus that he is said to have given to those same congregations, that they should now know that they belonged henceforth to Cartenna, as if Honorius had been confirmed in a more important place. But brother Rusticus wrote back to us that false things had been bandied about concerning him, and he denies that the letters these men hold are his; although, even if it were so, the will of Rusticus, who wishes to change what was done under his predecessor, should certainly rather be corrected than that should be permitted which no one is unaware is being done illicitly.

**9.** A letter, moreover, was made in the name of the people of Caesarea (whether they themselves wrote it we do not know) to the primate of that same province, to the effect that in a public cause they wish to send the same bishop Honorius to the imperial court, through whom they can have their needs relieved; and they requested at length that he give him an official pass, adding that, if he were unwilling to give it, they would compel him to sail even without a pass. But he did not give it, nor did Honorius himself tell us that he wished to sail in a public cause, but in his own cause, namely vehemently to repay the costs of a lawsuit to that Felix, or to the old man called Quietus, who had submitted a petition against him before us among the people. But we were dealing, if it could be done, so that the very case might be ended among us, provided that Honorius would give a letter to the people of Caesarea that they should provide a bishop for themselves, such a letter as we wished to be given by him.

**10.** But I am especially anxious for those who sent us letters from there about him, lest perhaps he sail and contrive some dangers for them from the imperial court; for, when it became known at Caesarea that they had written to me, a very great multitude cried out about those seditious men, declaring them to be heretics and to have made records about them; and when they wished these to be published to them, he did not give them, and they heard that he had carried them off with himself; but when we questioned him, he said that he had made no records about them. Nevertheless I am very anxious, because they asked me by letter to take care in this matter, lest catholic men incur some danger.

**11.** Indeed, those who favor him have written to him at Hippo, as he reported to us, that the letters sent from there to us about removing him from the Church of Caesarea—bearing the names of fifteen bishops—are false; and in truth it moves even us that no subscription of the primate, whose name is set at the beginning, is read, and there is only one, and that of uncertain authorship; and on this account it is most difficult that this very case can be ended here, which the animosity of men and the very necessity rather compels to be terminated by the judgment of the Apostolic See. About this matter we are not troubled, for we are certain of what can be decided by that See; but, as I have said, the danger to men moves me, lest catholics suffer some evils as if they were heretics—which may the Lord avert by his mercy, whether through your Charity or, what is best of all, through the insistence and most merciful and most just vigilance of the Apostolic See itself.
