# Letter 157

Augustine, bishop, servant of Christ and of his Church, to his most beloved son Hilary, greetings in the Lord.

**1. 1.** From your letter I have learned not only of your well-being but also of your religious zeal concerning the word of God, and your pious care for your salvation, which is in Christ Jesus our Lord. And so, giving thanks to God, I have not delayed to send a reply.

**1. 2.** If, then, you ask whether in this life anyone advances so far in the perfection of righteousness that he lives here altogether without any sin, consider what the apostle John said, whom the Lord loved especially among his disciples: *If we say that we have no sin, we deceive ourselves, and the truth is not in us*[^1]. If, then, those of whom you wrote to me say that they are without sin, you see that they deceive themselves and the truth is not in them. But if they confess that they are sinners, so that they may be able to merit the mercy of God, let them refrain from deceiving others also, in whom they try to instill this pride. For the Lord's Prayer is necessary for everyone, which the Lord gave even to the very rams of the flock, that is, to his apostles, so that each one may say to God: *Forgive us our debts, as we also forgive our debtors*[^2]. For anyone to whom these words in the prayer were not necessary would have to be declared to live here without sin. If the Lord had foreseen that there would be any such persons, better surely than his apostles proved to be, he would have taught them another prayer in which they would not ask to have their sins forgiven, since all had already been forgiven them in baptism. For if holy Daniel, not before men as though in feigned humility, but before God, that is, in the prayer in which he was beseeching God, confessed not only the sins of his people but even his own, as was set down by his truthful lips, it seems to me that nothing else should be said to these people except what the Lord commands to a certain proud man through the prophet Ezekiel: *Are you wiser than Daniel?*[^3]

**1. 3.** But plainly, whoever, helped by the mercy and grace of God, has abstained from those sins that are also called crimes, and has not neglected to cleanse by works of mercy and pious prayers those sins without which life here is not lived, will deserve to depart from here without sin, although while he was living here he had some sins; for just as these were not lacking, so also the remedies by which they might be purged were present. But whoever, having found in this as it were an occasion, when he hears that no one here lives without sin by his own choice, gives himself over to his lusts and binds himself to wicked crimes, and perseveres in these criminal and abominable ways right up to his last day, whatever alms he may give in the meantime, both leads an unhappy life and ends it still more unhappily.

**2. 4.** But these people are to be tolerated in any case when they say that there is or has been here someone, apart from the one Holy of Holies, who has had no sin at all. But that which they say—that free choice suffices for a man to fulfill the Lord's precepts, even if he is not helped to good works by the grace of God and the gift of the Holy Spirit—must be wholly anathematized and detested with every execration. For those who assert this are utterly estranged from the grace of God; because, *being ignorant of God's righteousness, and seeking to establish their own*, as the Apostle says of the Jews, *they have not submitted to the righteousness of God*[^4]. For the fullness of the law is nothing but love; and surely *the love of God has been poured out in our hearts*, not through ourselves nor by the powers of our own will, but *through the Holy Spirit who has been given to us*[^5].

**2. 5.** Free choice, therefore, has power for good works if it is helped from above, which comes about by asking humbly and acting; but abandoned by divine help, however much it may excel in knowledge of the law, it will in no way have the solidity of righteousness, but the puffing up of impious pride and a deadly swelling. The same Lord's Prayer teaches this. For we say to God in vain, *Lead us not into temptation*[^6], if this is so placed in our own power that we are able to fulfill it without any help from him. For it is said, *Lead us not into temptation*, which is understood as, Do not permit us to be led into it by abandoning us. *For God is faithful*, says the Apostle, *who will not let you be tempted beyond what you are able, but with the temptation will also make a way of escape, that you may be able to endure it*[^7]. Why did he say that God does this, if it lies in our power alone, without his help?

**2. 6.** For the law itself was given as a help to those who use it lawfully, so that through it they may know either what righteousness they have already received, for which they may give thanks, or what is still lacking to them, which they should earnestly ask for. But those who hear what the law says, *You shall not covet*[^8], in such a way that, because they have learned it, they think it suffices for them, and do not believe and ask that the strength to do what is commanded be given them by the help of God's grace—for them *the law came in, that the offense might abound*[^9], which was said of the Jews. For it is too little that they do not fulfill what the law commands, *You shall not covet*; but in addition they grow proud, and *being ignorant of God's righteousness*—that is, the righteousness that God gives, who justifies the ungodly—*and seeking to establish their own*, as though made by the powers of their own will, *they have not submitted to the righteousness of God. For Christ is the end of the law, unto righteousness to everyone who believes*[^10], who surely came for this, that *where the offense abounded, grace might superabound*[^11]. If the Jews were enemies of this grace, being ignorant of God's righteousness and seeking to establish their own, why are these people also enemies, if they have believed in the one whom the Jews killed? Or is it that those receive the reward who, after killing Christ, accused their own impiety and submitted themselves to his grace once recognized; while these undergo judgment who wish to believe in Christ in such a way that they try to kill his very grace?

**2. 7.** For those who rightly believe, believe in him for this: that they may hunger and thirst for righteousness and be filled by his grace. For everyone, as it is written, *who calls upon the name of the Lord shall be saved*[^12]—not, surely, with the salvation of the body, which many have who do not call upon the name of the Lord, but with that salvation of which he himself says, *Those who are well have no need of a physician, but those who are sick*; and, explaining accordingly what he meant: *I came not to call the righteous, but sinners*[^13]. He called the righteous, then, healthy, but sinners sick. Let the sick man, then, not presume on his own strength; for *he shall not be saved by the multitude of his strength*[^14]. For if he presumes on it, let him take care that this strength is not such as the healthy usually have, but such as the frenzied usually have, who, though they are insane, think themselves so healthy that they neither seek a physician but even strike him as a nuisance—just as these people in a way strike Christ with insane pride, when they contend that the so kindly help of his grace is not necessary for doing righteousness, once the precept of the law has been given. Let them cease, then, to be so insane, and let them understand that they have free choice for this, as far as they can: not that they should spurn the help by a proud will, but that they should call upon the Lord by a pious will.

**2. 8.** For this free will will be the freer the healthier it is, and the healthier the more subject to the mercy and grace of God. For it faithfully prays and says, *Direct my steps according to your word, and let no iniquity have dominion over me*[^15]. For how is it free, over which iniquity has dominion? But that it may not have dominion over it, see by whom it is invoked. For he does not say, Direct my steps according to free choice, that no iniquity may have dominion over me; but, *Direct my steps*, he says, *according to your word, and let no iniquity have dominion over me*. He prays, he does not promise; he confesses, he does not profess; he longs for the fullest freedom, he does not boast of his own power. For not everyone who trusts in his own strength, but everyone who calls upon the name of the Lord, shall be saved. *But how are they to call*, he says, *upon him in whom they have not believed?*[^16] For this, then, do those who rightly believe believe: that they may call upon him in whom they have believed, and may have power to do what they have received in the lawful precepts; since what the law commands, faith obtains.

**2. 9.** For, to leave aside for now the many precepts of the law and to recall from it the one the Apostle chose to recall: when the law says, *You shall not covet*[^17], what else does it seem to command but continence from unlawful desires? For the mind is borne, as if by a weight, wherever it is borne, by love. We are commanded, therefore, to take away from the weight of desire what we may add to the weight of love, until the former is consumed and the latter perfected; *for the fullness of the law is love*[^18]. And yet, concerning continence itself, see what is written: *And when I knew*, he says, *that no one could be continent unless God granted it, and this very thing was wisdom, to know whose gift it was, I went to the Lord and besought him*[^19]. Did he say: And when I knew that no one could be continent except through his own free choice, and this very thing was wisdom, to know that this good was from myself? He plainly did not say this, which certain people in their vanity say, but what had to be said in the truth of holy Scripture: *When I knew*, he says, *that no one could be continent unless God granted it*. God, therefore, commands continence and gives continence; he commands through the law, he gives through grace; he commands through the letter, he gives through the Spirit: for the law without grace makes the offense abound, and the letter without the Spirit kills. He commands for this reason: that we, having tried to do what is commanded, and wearied under the law by our own weakness, may know to ask for the help of grace; and if we have been able to do any good work, may not be ungrateful to the one who helps. This man too did this; for wisdom taught him whose gift it was.

**2. 10.** For the choice of the will is not taken away because it is helped; rather, it is helped because it is not taken away. For one who says to God, *Be my helper*[^20], confesses that he wills to fulfill what he commanded, but asks help from the one who commanded so that he may be able. So this man too, when he knew that no one could be continent unless God granted it, went to the Lord and besought him: surely it was willingly that he went, willingly that he besought, nor would he have asked unless there had been a will. But unless he had asked, how much could the will have done? For even if it can before it asks, what does it profit, unless he gives thanks for what he can to the one from whom must be asked what he cannot yet do? Hence even one who is already continent does not, of course, have continence unless the will is present; but unless it had received, what would the will have? *For what have you*, he says, *that you did not receive? And if you received it, why do you boast as if you had not received it?*[^21]—that is, Why do you boast as though you had from yourself what, if you had not received it, you could not have from yourself? This was said so that *he who boasts should boast not in himself but in the Lord*[^22]; and that one who does not yet have something to boast of should not hope from himself, but should pray to the Lord. For it is better for one to have less to ask of God than to have more to attribute to himself; since it is profitable to rise from the depths rather than to fall from on high: for *God*, as it is written, *resists the proud, but gives grace to the humble*[^23]. To the abounding of offenses, then, the law teaches us what we ought to will, unless grace helps, so that we may be able to do what we will, and may fulfill what we have been able to do. And it will help if, not presuming on our own strength, we are not high-minded, but consent to humble things, and give thanks for what we can already do, and humbly, with a longing will, ask God for what we cannot yet do; supporting our prayer with the fruitful works of mercy, giving that it may be given to us, forgiving that it may be forgiven us.

**3. 11.** But as to what they say—that an infant taken away by death unbaptized cannot perish, since it is born without sin—the Apostle does not say this; and I think it is better that we believe the Apostle rather than these people. For that teacher of the Gentiles, in whom Christ was speaking, says: *Through one man sin entered into the world, and through sin death, and so it passed to all men, in whom all sinned*; and a little later he says: *For the judgment indeed was from one offense unto condemnation, but grace is from many offenses unto justification*[^24]. Accordingly, if these people should perhaps find an infant not begotten from the concupiscence of that one man, let them say that he is not liable to that damnation, nor to be freed from that damnation through the grace of Christ. For what is *from one offense unto condemnation*, but in that offense by which Adam offended? And what is *from many offenses unto justification*, but that the grace of Christ looses not only that one offense, by which infants begotten from that one man are bound, but also the many offenses that men, as they grow, add by their evil ways? Yet he says that even that one offense, by which the carnal stock that draws its origin from that first man is bound, suffices for condemnation. Therefore the baptism of little ones is not superfluous, so that those who through generation are bound to that condemnation may through regeneration be loosed from that same condemnation: for just as no man is found who is generated carnally apart from Adam, so no man is found who is regenerated spiritually apart from Christ. But carnal generation is liable to that one offense and to its damnation; while spiritual regeneration causes to be abolished not only that one offense, on account of which infants are baptized, but also the many offenses that men, by living badly, have added to that in which they were generated. Therefore he goes on to say: *For if by the offense of one death reigned through one, much more shall they who receive the abundance of grace and of righteousness reign in life through one, Jesus Christ. So then, as through the offense of one unto condemnation upon all men, so through the righteousness of one unto justification of life upon all men. For as by the disobedience of one man many were made sinners, so by the obedience of one many are made righteous*[^25].

**3. 12.** What will they say to this? Or what remains to them, except to contend that the Apostle erred? The vessel of election, the teacher of the Gentiles, the trumpet of Christ, cries out: *The judgment from one unto condemnation*; and these people cry back, asserting that little ones, whom they confess to draw their stock from that one man of whom he speaks, do not go into condemnation, even if they have not been baptized in Christ. *The judgment*, he says, *from one unto condemnation*: what does he mean by *from one*, but, from one offense? For it follows: *But grace is from many offenses unto justification*[^26]. Therefore from one side the judgment leads from even one offense unto condemnation; from the other side grace leads from even many offenses unto justification. Wherefore, if they do not dare to resist the Apostle, let them explain to us why the judgment leads from one offense unto condemnation, when men come to judgment to be damned for many offenses. Or if they think it was so said because the beginning of sin was made from Adam, which the rest imitated, so that from that one offense those would be drawn into judgment and condemnation who by imitating committed many sins—why was it not likewise said of grace and the justified? Why did he not say similarly: And grace from one offense unto justification? For just as men's many offenses are found in between that one which they imitated and the judgment by which they are punished—for they came from one to many, so as to be led from many to judgment and damnation—so those same many offenses are in the same way in between this very one in imitation of which they were committed, and the grace by which they are forgiven; since from that one they came to many, so as to come from many to the grace of justification. Since, then, in both, that is, both in judgment and in grace, as far as concerns the one and the many offenses, the account is one and the same, let these people tell us why he said that the judgment leads from one offense unto condemnation, but grace from many offenses unto justification. Or let them acquiesce that it was so said because in this matter two men are set up: Adam, from whom carnal generation consists, and Christ, from whom spiritual regeneration. But because the former is only man, while the latter is both God and man, that regeneration does not loose one offense alone, which is from Adam, in the way that that generation binds by one offense, which is from Adam. Rather, for that generation the connection of one offense suffices for condemnation; for whatever men afterward add from their evil works pertains not to that generation, but to human conduct. But for this regeneration it does not suffice to loose only that offense which is drawn from Adam, but whatever also is added afterward from the iniquitous works of human conduct. Therefore the judgment is from one unto condemnation, but grace from many offenses unto justification.

**3. 13.** *For if by the offense of one death reigned through one*—the offense from which little ones are cleansed through baptism—*much more shall they who receive the abundance of grace and of righteousness reign in life through one, Jesus Christ*[^27]: much more surely shall they reign in life, because it will be the kingdom of eternal life, whereas death passes in them temporally and will not reign forever. And so, *as through the offense of one unto condemnation upon all men*—from which condemnation little ones are to be freed through the sacrament of baptism—*so through the righteousness of one unto justification of life upon all men*[^28]. And here he said all, and there all; not because all men come to the grace of Christ's justification, since so many, estranged from it, die eternally, but because all who are reborn unto justification are reborn only through Christ, just as all who are born unto condemnation are born only through Adam. For there is no one in that generation apart from Adam, no one in this regeneration apart from Christ: therefore all, and all; and the same all he afterward calls also many, adding: *For as by the disobedience of one man many were made sinners, so also by the obedience of one man many are made righteous*[^29]. Who are these many, but those whom just before he had called all?

**3. 14.** See how he commends the one and the one, that is, Adam and Christ—the former unto condemnation, the latter unto justification—although Christ came in the flesh so long after Adam: so that we may know that even the righteous of old, whoever they could be, were freed only through the same faith by which we also are freed, namely faith in the incarnation of Christ, which was foretold to them, as it is announced to us as accomplished. For this reason he calls Christ man, though he is also God, lest anyone suppose that the righteous of old could be freed only through Christ as God, that is, through the Word who was in the beginning, and not also through faith in his incarnation, by which Christ is called also man. For that sentence cannot be destroyed, of which he also says elsewhere: *Through one man death, and through one man the resurrection of the dead. For as in Adam all die, so also in Christ all shall be made alive*[^30]. He means, of course, the resurrection of the righteous, where there is eternal life; not the resurrection of the wicked, where there will be eternal death: therefore he says, shall be made alive; because those shall be damned. Hence also in the ancient sacraments the circumcision of little ones was commanded to be done on the eighth day, since Christ, in whom is effected the stripping off of carnal offense which circumcision signifies, rose on the Lord's day, which is the eighth after the seventh of the sabbath. This faith, then, belonged also to the righteous of old. Hence the Apostle also says: *Having the same spirit of faith, as it is written, I believed, therefore I have spoken; we also believe, therefore we also speak*[^31]. He would not say, the same spirit of faith, unless he were reminding us that the righteous of old also had that same spirit of faith, that is, of the incarnation of Christ. But because to them future things were foretold which are announced to us as already accomplished, and what was veiled in the time of the Old Testament is revealed in the time of the New Testament, therefore its sacraments were varied, so that some were in the Old Testament and others in the New; though the faith itself is not varied, but is one: for *as in Adam all die, so also in Christ all shall be made alive*.

**3. 15.** But as to what he adds to those words we were treating, saying, *But the law came in, that the offense might abound*[^32], this no longer pertains to that offense which is drawn from Adam, of which he was saying above, *Death reigned through one*[^33]; for whether we understand the natural law, which appears in the years of those who can already use reason, or the written law, which was given through Moses—since neither could it give life and free from the law of sin and death, which is drawn from Adam, but rather added increases of transgression: *For where there is no law*, says the same Apostle, *there is no transgression*[^34]. Accordingly, since there is a law also in the reason of a man who now uses the choice of freedom, written naturally in the heart, by which it is suggested that no one should do to another what he himself does not wish to suffer, according to this law all are transgressors, even those who have not received the law given through Moses, of whom it is read in the Psalm: *I counted all the sinners of the earth transgressors*[^35]. For not all the sinners of the earth transgressed the law given through Moses; yet unless they had transgressed some law, they would not be called transgressors: *For where there is no law, there is no transgression*. When the law given in paradise was transgressed, then, man is born from Adam with the law of sin and death, of which it is said: *I see another law in my members, fighting against the law of my mind, and leading me captive in the law of sin which is in my members*[^36]. Yet unless this is later strengthened by evil habit, it is more easily overcome—though not except by the grace of God. But when that other law is transgressed, which is in the use of reason of the rational soul at the age of a man who now uses reason, all the sinners of the earth become transgressors. And when even that law which was given through Moses is transgressed, the offense abounds much more. *For if a law had been given which could give life, righteousness would altogether be from the law. But Scripture has shut up all things under sin, that the promise by faith in Jesus Christ might be given to those who believe*[^37]—these words, if you recognize them, are the Apostle's. Concerning which law he says again: *The law was added because of transgression, until the seed should come to whom the promise was made, ordained through angels in the hand of a mediator*[^38]—commending Christ, by whose grace all are saved, whether little ones from the law of sin and death with which we are born, or older ones who, by misusing the choice of the will, transgressed the natural law of reason itself, or those who received the law given through Moses and, transgressing it, were killed by the letter. But when a man transgresses even the precepts of the Gospel, let him be reckoned dead four days; yet even of him one must not despair, on account of the grace of him who did not say slowly, but cried with a loud voice, *Lazarus, come forth*[^39].

**3. 16.** The law, then, came in that the offense might abound: whether when men neglect what God commands, or when, presuming on their own strength, they do not implore the help of grace, and add pride to weakness. But when by the divine call they understand why they should groan, and call upon him in whom they rightly believe, saying: *Have mercy on me, O God, according to your great mercy*[^40]; and, *I said, Lord, have mercy on me, heal my soul, for I have sinned against you*[^41]; and, *In your righteousness give me life*[^42]; and, *Remove from me the way of iniquity, and according to your law have mercy on me*[^43]; and, *Let not the foot of pride come to me, and let not the hand of sinners move me*[^44]; and, *Direct my steps according to your word, and let no iniquity have dominion over me*[^45] (*for by the Lord are a man's steps directed, and he will choose his way*[^46]); and many other things, which were written for this reason, to admonish us that, for fulfilling what is commanded us, help must be asked from the one by whom it is commanded—when, therefore, a man stretches himself out to him and so groans, what follows comes to pass: *Where the offense abounded, grace superabounded*[^47]; and, *Her many sins are forgiven, for she loved much*[^48]; and the love of God is poured out in the heart, whence the fullness of the law may come to be—not through the powers of the choice that is in us, but through the Holy Spirit who has been given to us. For he knew the law who said, *For I delight in the law of God according to the inner man*; and yet he added, *But I see another law in my members, fighting against the law of my mind, and leading me captive in the law of sin which is in my members. Wretched man that I am, who will free me from the body of this death? The grace of God through Jesus Christ our Lord*[^49]. Why did he not rather say, Through my free choice, except because freedom without grace is not freedom, but defiance?

**3. 17.** When, therefore, the Apostle had said, *The law came in, that the offense might abound; but where the offense abounded, grace superabounded*[^50], he went on and added: *that, as sin reigned unto death, so also grace might reign through righteousness unto eternal life, through Jesus Christ our Lord*[^51]. Now when he had said, *that, as sin reigned unto death*, he did not say, Through one man, or through the first man, or through Adam: because he had already said, *The law came in, that the offense might abound*; and this abounding of the offense pertains not to the propagation from the first man, but to the transgression of human conduct, which was added in those of more advanced age, out of the abundance of iniquity, to that one offense by which alone little ones are held bound. But because this whole, even what does not pertain to the origin of that one offense, is nevertheless such as the grace of the Savior is fit to loose, therefore, when he had said, *so also grace might reign through righteousness unto eternal life*, he added, *through Jesus Christ our Lord*.

**3. 18.** Let no one's arguments, then, brought forward against these apostolic words, hinder little ones from the salvation which is in Christ Jesus our Lord: for we ought to speak on their behalf all the more, the more they cannot speak for themselves. *Through one man sin entered into the world, and through sin death; and so it passed to all men, in whom all sinned*[^52]. As they cannot be free from that one man, so they cannot be free from that same sin, unless they are loosed from his guilt through the baptism of Christ. *For until the law sin was in the world*[^53]: this was not said because sin was thereafter in no one, but because what could be taken away only by the Spirit of grace could not be taken away by the letter of the law. Therefore, lest anyone, trusting in the strength—I do not say of his will, but rather of his vanity—should suppose that the law could suffice for free choice, and should mock the grace of Christ, the Apostle says: *For until the law sin was in the world; but sin was not imputed when there was no law*[^54]. He did not say, It was not; but, It was not imputed; because there was no law by whose accusation it might be shown—whether the law of reason in the little one, or the law of the letter in the people.

**3. 19.** *But death reigned*, he says, *from Adam until Moses*[^55]; because not even the law given through Moses could take away the reign of death, which the grace of Christ alone took away. But over whom it reigned, see: *even over those*, he says, *who did not sin after the likeness of the transgression of Adam*[^56]. Death reigned, then, even over those who did not sin. But why it reigned, he shows when he says, *after the likeness of the transgression of Adam*. For this is the better understanding of these words: that, when he had said death reigned over those who did not sin, as if to admonish us why it reigned over those who did not sin, he added, after the likeness of the transgression of Adam; that is, because there was in their members the likeness of the transgression of Adam. It can also be understood thus: death reigned from Adam until Moses, even over those who did not sin after the likeness of the transgression of Adam—because in themselves, since they had now been born and did not yet use reason, which he used when he sinned, nor had they received the precept that he transgressed, but were held bound by original fault alone—through which the reign of death is in them only insofar as they are not those who, reborn by the grace of Christ, belong to his kingdom; since temporal death, although it too is propagated from the original offense, destroys the body in them, but does not draw the soul to punishment, where he wished the reign of death to be understood: so that the soul reborn through grace may not die unto Gehenna, that is, may not be estranged or separated from the life of God; but the temporal death of the body, even in those who are redeemed by the death of Christ, is left for a time for the exercise of faith and the contest of the present struggle, in which the martyrs too have striven; and it too will be consumed in the renewal of the body which the resurrection promises. For there death will be wholly swallowed up in victory, from which the grace of Christ now takes away the kingdom, lest it draw the souls of his own to the punishments of hell. Some manuscripts, indeed, do not have, *over those who did not sin*, but, over those who sinned, after the likeness of the transgression of Adam; by which words this sense is in no way taken away. For according to this they are understood to have sinned after the likeness of the transgression of Adam, according to what was said above, *in whom all sinned*[^57]. But the Greek manuscripts, from which the Scripture was translated into Latin, mostly have what we have said.

**3. 20.** But as to what he added concerning Adam, *who is the figure of him who was to come*[^58], this too is understood in more than one way. For either it is a figure of Christ by contrast, so that as in him all die, so also in Christ all are made alive; and as through his disobedience many were made sinners, so through the obedience of Christ many are made righteous: or he called him the figure of him who was to come because he himself imposed a figure of death on his posterity. Yet that is the better understanding, that he is believed to be a figure by contrast, which the Apostle greatly commends. Finally, lest the contrary things in this figure be weighed altogether as equal, he adds and says: *But the gift is not like the offense: for if by the offense of one many died, much more did the grace of God and the gift in the grace of one man, Jesus Christ, abound unto many*[^59]: so that it may be understood, not unto more in number, since the wicked who will be damned are more in number, but it will abound more, because in those who are redeemed through Christ the figure of death from Adam avails temporally, but the figure of life through Christ will avail eternally. Although Adam, then, is a figure of him who was to come by contrast, Christ nevertheless bestows more on the regenerate than he had harmed in the generated. *And the gift is not like the result of the one who sinned: for the judgment indeed was from one unto condemnation, but grace is from many offenses unto justification*[^60]. Not only in this, he says, is the figure unequal, because the former harmed temporally those whom Christ redeemed eternally, but also because the posterity, by that one's single offense, unless they are redeemed by Christ, are handed over to condemnation; whereas the redemption of Christ dissolves even the many offenses which the abundance of transgressing iniquity added to that one; concerning which we have already disputed above.

**3. 21.** Against these words of the Apostle and their sound understanding, acquiesce to no one, if you wish to live for Christ and in Christ. For if, as they say, the Apostle had recalled these things so that we might understand that sinners pertain to the first man for this reason—that we did not draw the offense from him by being born, but sin by imitating him—he would rather have set down the devil, who both sinned first and from whom the human race drew no propagation of substance, but followed him by imitation alone; whence he is called the father of the ungodly, just as Abraham is called our father on account of the imitation of faith, not on account of carnal origin. For this reason it was said of the devil himself: *But they imitate him who are of his portion*[^61]. Then, if on account of imitation the Apostle were recalling the first man in this place, because he was the first sinner among men, so as to say that all sinful men pertain to him for that reason—why did he not set down holy Abel, who was the first righteous man among men, to whom all the righteous would pertain on account of the imitation of righteousness? But he set down Adam, against whom he set down none but Christ; because as that man by his offense corrupted his posterity, so that God-man by his righteousness saved his inheritance: the one by transmitting the uncleanness of the flesh, which the impious devil could not do; the other by bestowing the grace of the Spirit, which the righteous Abel could not do.

**3. 22.** Many things on these questions we have said in other little works of ours and in ecclesiastical sermons: for there were even among us certain people who, wherever they could, would scatter these new seeds of their error; some of whom, through our ministry and that of our brothers, the mercy of the Lord healed of that plague. Yet I think there are not lacking some here, especially at Carthage; but they now mutter in secret, fearing the most firmly grounded faith of the Church. For one of them, named Celestius, had already begun to creep up to the honor of the presbyterate in the church of that same city; but by the most faithful liberty of the brothers, on account of these very disputes against the grace of Christ, he was brought before an episcopal court. Yet he was compelled to confess, on account of little ones being baptized, that redemption is necessary for them too. There, although he was unwilling to say anything more expressly about original sin, nevertheless by the very name of redemption he conceded no little against himself. For from what would they be redeemed, except from the power of the devil, in which they could not be except by the bond of original sin? Or by what price are they redeemed, except by the blood of Christ, of which it is most plainly written that it was poured out *for the remission of sins*[^62]? But because he departed more convicted and detested by the Church than corrected and at peace, I feared lest perhaps he might be the very one there who is trying to disturb your faith: and so I thought I should make mention of his name. But whether it is he himself, or others who share his error—for they are more than we can hope; and where they are not refuted, they seduce others also to their sect, and so multiply, so that I do not know where they will break out—we nevertheless prefer that they be healed within the framework of the Church than that they be cut off from her body as it were as incurable members, if, however, necessity itself permits this. For it is to be feared that more may rot while the rotten ones are spared. But the mercy of our Lord is powerful, to free them rather from this plague: which it will do without doubt, if they faithfully attend to and hold what is written: *Whoever calls upon the name of the Lord shall be saved*[^63].

**4. 23.** Hear now a few words about the rich, which comes next in your letter to be inquired into. For you have set down that they say this: that a rich man remaining in his riches cannot enter the kingdom of God unless he sells all that he has, nor does it profit him even if he should perhaps fulfill the commandments out of those very riches. The arguments of these people are escaped by our fathers Abraham and Isaac and Jacob, who departed from this life so long before. For all these had no few riches, as the most faithful Scripture testifies: yet that many would come from the east and the west, and would recline in the kingdom of heaven not above them, or apart from them, but with them, he himself, who for our sake became poor though he was truly rich, foretold by a most truthful promise. And although the proud rich man, who was clothed in purple and fine linen and feasted sumptuously every day, was tormented when dead among the dead, nevertheless, if he had pitied the poor man covered with sores who lay despised before his gate, he too would have deserved mercy. And if poverty, not righteousness, had been the merit of that poor man, he certainly would not have been carried by the angels into the bosom of Abraham, who had been rich here. But so that it might be shown to us that neither was poverty in itself divinely honored in the one, nor were riches damned in the other, but that piety in the one and impiety in the other had their respective ends: so the fire of torment received the impious rich man, while the bosom of the rich man received the pious poor one. And this man, indeed, when he lived here as a rich man, so had riches and so little valued them in comparison with God's precepts that he was unwilling even by the immolation of his own son to offend the God who commanded it, whom he both hoped and wished to be the heir of his riches.

**4. 24.** Here, of course, they say that the ancient fathers did not sell all that they had and give to the poor because the Lord had not commanded this of them. For, the New Testament not yet being revealed—which ought not to come about except in the fullness of time—neither was their virtue to be revealed, by which virtue God knew in their hearts that they could most easily do this, he who bore them so distinguished a testimony that, although he is the God of all the saints, he deigned to speak as of his chief friends: *I am the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, the God of Jacob: this is my name forever*[^64]. But after the great sacrament of piety was manifested in the flesh, and the coming of Christ shone forth for the calling of all the Gentiles, in whom those fathers too had believed—but kept the olive of this faith, to be manifested in its own time, as it were in the root of that tree of which the Apostle speaks—it was said to the rich man: *Go, sell all that you have and give to the poor, and you will have treasure in heaven; and come, follow me*[^65].

**4. 25.** If they say this, they seem to say something reasonable. But let them hear the whole, let them attend to the whole; let them not open their ears in part and grow deaf in part. To whom did the Lord command these things? Surely to that rich man who was seeking to receive counsel for obtaining eternal life: for he had said this to the Lord: *What shall I do to obtain eternal life?* But he did not answer him: If you wish to come to life, go, sell all that you have; but: *If you wish to come to life, keep the commandments*[^66]. When the young man said that he had kept these, which the Lord recalled to him from the law, and asked what was still lacking to him, he received the answer: *If you wish to be perfect, go, sell all that you have and give to the poor*[^67]. And lest he should think that he would in this way lose those things which he greatly loved: *And you will have*, he says, *treasure in heaven*. Then he added: *And come, follow me*; lest it be thought to profit anyone anything, when he has done these things, if he does not follow Christ. But he indeed went away sad, who had seen how he had kept those precepts of the law: for I think that he had answered that he had kept them more arrogantly than truly. Nevertheless the good Master distinguished the precepts of the law from that more excellent perfection: for there he said, *If you wish to come to life, keep the commandments*; but here, *If you wish to be perfect, go, sell all that you have*, and so on. Why, then, do we deny that the rich, although they fall short of that perfection, nevertheless come to life, if they keep the commandments, and give that it may be given to them, and forgive that it may be forgiven them?

**4. 26.** For we believe the apostle Paul to have been a minister of the New Testament, when, writing to Timothy, he said: *Charge the rich of this world not to be high-minded, nor to hope in the uncertainty of riches, but in the living God, who furnishes us with all things abundantly to enjoy. Let them do good, let them be rich in good works, let them give readily, share, lay up for themselves a good foundation for the future, that they may lay hold of the true life*[^68]: of which it was also said to that young man, *If you wish to come to life*. I think that, in charging these things, the Apostle was instructing the rich, not deceiving them: for he did not say, Charge the rich of this world to sell all that they have, give to the poor, and follow the Lord; but, not to be high-minded, nor to hope in the uncertainty of riches. It was that pride, by which the rich man despised the righteous poor man lying before his gate, and that hope in the uncertainty of riches, by which on account of his purple, fine linen, and sumptuous feasts he thought himself blessed—not the riches themselves—that led him to the torments of hell.

**4. 27.** Or is it perhaps because the Lord added and said, *Amen I say to you, a rich man will enter the kingdom of heaven with difficulty; and again I say to you, it is easier for a camel to pass through the eye of a needle than for a rich man to enter the kingdom of heaven*[^69], that these people therefore think that a rich man, even if he does those things which the Apostle wrote should be charged upon the rich, cannot enter the kingdom of heaven? What, then, is it? Does the Apostle speak against the Lord, or do these people not know what they are saying? Let the Christian choose which of these he should believe. I think it better for us to believe that these people do not know what they say than that Paul speaks against the Lord. Then, why do they not also in what follows hear the Lord himself saying to the disciples, saddened over the misery of the rich: *What is impossible for men is easy for God*[^70]?

**4. 28.** But, they say, this was said for this reason: because it would come to pass that, on hearing the Gospel, the rich, having sold their patrimony and distributed it to the poor, would follow the Lord and enter the kingdom of heaven, and so what seemed difficult would come about—not that, remaining in their riches and keeping the precepts of the Apostle, that is, not being high-minded, nor hoping in the uncertainty of riches, but in the living God, doing good, giving readily, and sharing with the needy, they would lay hold of the true life; but rather that they would fulfill these apostolic precepts too only by selling all their goods.

**4. 29.** If they say this—and I know that they do say it—they do not first attend to how the Lord, against their dogma, proclaimed his grace. For he did not say: What seems impossible to men is easy for men if they will; but: *What is impossible for men*, he says, *is easy for God*[^71]: showing that, when these things are rightly done, they are not done by the power of man, but by the grace of God. Let these people, then, attend to this; and if they rebuke those who glory in their riches, let them take care not to trust in their own virtue: for both alike are rebuked in the Psalm: *Those who trust in their own strength, and who glory in the abundance of their riches*[^72]. Let the rich, then, hear: *What is impossible for men is easy for God*; and whether, remaining in their riches and doing good works out of them, or having sold them and distributed them among the needs of the poor, they enter the kingdom of heaven, let them attribute their good to the grace of God, not to their own strength. For what is impossible for men is easy not for men, but for God. Let these people too hear this; and if they have already either sold all that is theirs and given to the poor, or are still doing it and arranging it, and in this way are preparing to enter the kingdom of heaven, let them not attribute this to their own virtue, but to that same divine grace. For what is impossible for men is easy not for them, since they too are men, but for God. For the Apostle also says this to them: *Work out your own salvation with fear and trembling. For it is God who works in you both to will and to work, for his good will*[^73]. Surely they say that for this reason they received from the Lord the counsel of perfection about selling their goods—that they might follow the Lord, since there was added, *and come, follow me*: why, then, in the good works they do, do they presume only on their own will, and not hear the Lord, whom they say they follow, rebuking and testifying: *Without me you can do nothing*[^74]?

**4. 30.** But if the Apostle said, *Charge the rich of this world not to be high-minded, nor to hope in the uncertainty of riches*, in such a way that they should sell all that they possess, and by distributing these proceeds to the needy do what follows, *give readily, share, lay up for themselves a good foundation for the future*[^75], and does not believe that they can enter the kingdom of heaven otherwise—then he deceives those whose households he so carefully orders with the soundness of doctrine, admonishing and charging how wives should show themselves to husbands, husbands to wives, children to parents, parents to children, slaves to masters, masters to slaves: for how can these things be done without a household and without some family estate?

**4. 31.** Or does this move them, that the Lord says: *Whoever has left all that is his for my sake will receive in this world a hundredfold, and in the world to come will possess eternal life*[^76]? It is one thing to have left, another to have sold: for among the things he commanded to be left, a wife too is mentioned, whom it is permitted by no human laws to sell, and by the laws of Christ not even to put away, except for the cause of fornication. What, then, do these precepts mean (for they cannot be contrary to one another), except that sometimes a moment of necessity arises, where either the wife is to be put away or Christ: so that, to leave other cases aside, if a Christian husband should displease his very wife, and she should set before him either divorce from herself or divorce from Christ? Here what should he choose but Christ, and praiseworthily put away the wife for Christ's sake? For where both are Christians, the Lord has commanded that no one put away his wife, except for the cause of fornication. But where either of them is an unbeliever, let the counsel of the Apostle be attended to: *If an unbeliever consents to dwell with a faithful husband, let the husband not put away the wife. Likewise also the faithful wife the husband, if he consents to dwell with her. But if the unbeliever*, he says, *departs, let him depart: for a brother or sister is not in bondage in such cases*[^77]: that is, if the unbeliever is unwilling to be with the faithful spouse, here let the faithful one recognize his own freedom, lest he so reckon himself subject to bondage as to abandon the faith itself, for fear of losing the unbelieving spouse.

**4. 32.** This is understood also of children and parents, this of brothers and sisters: that all are to be left for Christ's sake, when this condition is set—that one should leave Christ if he desires to keep them with him. This, then, is to be understood in this place also of house and fields, this also of those things which are possessed by right of money. For similarly even of these he does not say: Whoever sells for my sake whatever it is lawful to sell; but: *Whoever leaves*. For it can happen that it is said to a Christian by some power, Either you shall not be a Christian, or if you wish to remain in this, you shall not have your house and possessions. Then truly even those rich who had so resolved to remain in their riches as to win God's favor by the good works done from them, should rather leave these for Christ's sake than Christ for the sake of these; so that they may receive in this world a hundredfold, by the perfection of which number all things are signified (for the whole world of riches belongs to the faithful man; and they become in this way *as having nothing, yet possessing all things*[^78]), and in the world to come may possess eternal life; lest, Christ being left for the sake of these things, they be cast down into eternal death.

**4. 33.** For by this law and condition, not only those who by excellence of mind have received the counsel of perfection, so as to distribute what they have sold to the poor and, with shoulders freer from every burden of this world, to bear the light burden of Christ, but also whoever is weaker, and less fit for that most glorious perfection, who nevertheless remembers that he is truly a Christian—when he hears it set before him that, unless he leaves all these things, he is going to leave Christ—will rather lay hold of *a tower of strength against the face of the enemy*[^79]: because, when he built it on his faith, he computed the cost by which it could be completed, that is, he came to the faith with this mind, that he might renounce this world not only in words; for even if he bought something, he was as one not possessing, and if he used this world, he was as one not using it, not hoping in the uncertainty of riches, but in the living God.

**4. 34.** For since everyone who renounces this world doubtless renounces all that is his, in order to be able to be Christ's disciple (for he himself, when he had set forth the parables about the cost necessary for building a tower and about preparing for war against another king, added thus: *Whoever does not renounce all that he has cannot be my disciple*[^80]), surely he renounces also his riches, if he has any—either so that, in no way loving them, he distributes them all to the needy and unburdens himself of superfluous loads; or so that, loving Christ more, he transfers his hope from them to him, and so uses them that, giving readily and sharing, he lays up treasure in heaven; and is prepared, just as parents and children and brothers and wife, in this way also to leave these things, if such a condition is set that he cannot have them except by leaving Christ. For if he renounces this world otherwise, when he comes to the sacrament of faith, he does what blessed Cyprian laments concerning the lapsed, saying: *Renouncing the world in words only, and not in deeds*. For of this it is said, when, a temptation coming, he fears to lose these things more than to deny Christ: *Behold a man who began to build and could not finish*[^81]. He is also the one who, while his adversary was still far off, sent envoys seeking peace; that is, while temptation was not yet afflicting, but still threatening and menacing, in order not to be without those things which he loves more, he consents to leave Christ and deny him. And there are many such, who even think that the Christian religion ought to favor them for increasing their wealth and multiplying earthly delights.

**4. 35.** But such are not the rich Christians who, although they hold these things, are nevertheless not so held by them as to prefer them to Christ; for with a truthful heart they have renounced the world, so as to place no hope in such things. These instruct their wives and children and whole households with sound discipline to hold the Christian religion. Their houses, fervent with hospitality, receive the righteous man *in the name of a righteous man, that they may receive a righteous man's reward*[^82]. They break their bread to the hungry, clothe the naked, redeem the captive, *lay up for themselves a good foundation for the future, that they may lay hold of the true life*[^83]. And if perhaps for the faith of Christ monetary losses must be endured, they hate their riches; if this world threatens the bereavement or separation of their loved ones for Christ's sake, they hate parents, brothers, children, wives; and finally, if they must bargain with the adversary over the very life of this body, lest, being abandoned, Christ abandon them, they hate their own soul as well. For of all these they have received a commandment, that otherwise they cannot be disciples of Christ.

**4. 36.** And yet, because it is commanded them to hate even their own souls for Christ, they do not have them to be sold, or to be poured out by laying hands on themselves; but they are prepared to lose them by dying for the name of Christ, lest, by denying Christ, being dead they should live. So too their riches, which they were not prepared to sell at Christ's admonition, they ought to be prepared to lose for Christ, lest, Christ being lost, they perish together with them. From this we have rich men and women, and most illustrious persons, raised on high by the glory of martyrdom. Thus many who were reluctant to be perfected beforehand by the sale of their goods, were suddenly perfected by imitating the passion of Christ; and those who had wrought some weakness of flesh and blood with their riches suddenly strove against sin, for the faith, even unto blood. But those to whom the crown of martyrdom did not come, and who did not receive that so great and so splendid counsel of perfection about selling their goods, and yet, free from damnable crimes, fed the hungry Christ, gave drink to the thirsty, clothed the naked, took in the stranger—these will not indeed sit with Christ as those who will judge on high, but will stand at his right hand to be mercifully judged. *For blessed are the merciful, for God will have mercy on them*[^84]. And *judgment without mercy will be done to him who has not done mercy: but mercy triumphs over judgment*[^85].

**4. 37.** Accordingly, let these people cease to speak against the Scriptures, and in their exhortations to greater things, let them so stir men up that they do not condemn the lesser. For surely they cannot persuade to holy virginity by exhortation in any other way than by condemning the bonds of marriage, when, as the Apostle teaches, *each one has his own gift from God, one in this way, another in that*[^86]? Let them, then, walk the way of perfection, having sold all their goods and mercifully distributed them. But if they are truly Christ's poor, and gather not for themselves but for Christ, why do they punish his weaker members before they have received the judgment seats? For if they are to be such as those to whom the Lord says, *You shall sit upon twelve thrones, judging the twelve tribes of Israel*[^87], and such as those of whom the Apostle says, *Do you not know that we shall judge angels?*[^88], let them rather prepare themselves to receive into the eternal dwellings not criminal but religious rich men, by whom they have been *made friends from the mammon of iniquity*[^89]. For I think that some of those who chatter these things shamelessly and imprudently are supported in their necessities by rich and pious Christians. For the Church has, as it were, her soldiers, and, as it were, her provincials: whence the Apostle says, *Who ever serves as a soldier at his own expense?* She has also a vineyard and planters, she has a flock and shepherds: whence he says accordingly, *Who plants a vineyard and does not eat of its fruit? Who feeds a flock and does not partake of its milk?*[^90] Although to dispute such things as these people dispute is not to be a soldier, but to rebel; not to plant a vineyard, but to root it up; not to gather those to be fed, but to separate from the flock those to be lost.

**4. 38.** But just as those very men who are fed and clothed by the religious services of the rich (for they receive nothing for their needs except from those who sell their goods) are nevertheless not judged and condemned by the more excellent members of Christ, who, with a greater virtue which the Apostle much commends, support themselves with their own hands: so neither ought these people to condemn Christians of lesser merit, by whose means they are supported; but rather, by living rightly and teaching rightly, to say to them: *If we have sown spiritual things for you, is it a great thing if we reap your carnal things?*[^91] For with much less shamelessness do the servants of God who live by selling the honest works of their hands condemn these people, from whom they receive nothing, than these people, who, unable to work with their hands because of some bodily weakness, condemn the very ones by whose means they live.

**4. 39.** I who write these things vehemently loved the perfection of which the Lord spoke when he said to the rich young man, *Go, sell all that you have and give to the poor, and you will have treasure in heaven; and come, follow me*[^92], and I did so not by my own strength, but with his grace helping. For not because I was not rich will it therefore be imputed less to me: for not even the apostles themselves, who did this first, were rich. But he leaves the whole world who leaves both what he has and what he wishes to have. But how much I have advanced in this way of perfection, I myself know better than any other man; but God knows better than I. And to this purpose I exhort others with all the strength I can, and in the name of the Lord I have companions to whom this has been persuaded through my ministry: yet in such a way that above all sound doctrine is held, and we do not by vain defiance judge those who do not do these things, telling them that it profits them nothing that they live chastely, though in marriage, that they govern their households and families in a Christian manner, that by works of mercy they lay up treasure for themselves for the future; lest, by disputing these things, we be found not expounders of the holy Scriptures, but their accusers. This I have mentioned because these people, when they are forbidden to say such things by those who have not received this counsel of the Lord, answer that those are unwilling to have such things disputed because they favor their own vices and refuse to fulfill the Lord's precepts: as if—to say nothing of those who, though weaker, nevertheless use riches religiously—even the greedy and avaricious themselves, using these things badly and fixing their muddy heart on earthly treasure, since the Church must necessarily carry these too until the end, just as those nets carry the bad fish to the shore, are not more tolerable in her than these people, who, by talking and disseminating such things, so wish to seem great—because they have sold their riches, or however small their patrimonies, at the Lord's command—that they strive by this unsound doctrine to disturb and overturn his inheritance, which is spread and diffused even to the ends of the earth.

**5. 40.** Hence, since on this occasion, but yet now briefly, I have said what I also think about the Church of Christ in this world—that is, that until the end of this world it must necessarily carry both good and bad, since you asked this too among your questions—I will at last at some point conclude this lengthy letter. Avoid swearing as much as you can. For it is better not to swear even what is true, since by the habit of swearing one both often falls into perjury and is always near to perjury. But these people, as far as I have heard some of them, are wholly ignorant of what it is to swear: for they think they are not swearing when they have in their mouth, God knows; and, God is witness; and, I call God to witness against my soul; because it is not said, By God, and because such things are found in the apostle Paul. But there too is found against them what they confess to be an oath, where the Apostle says: *I die daily, by your glory, brethren, which I have in Christ Jesus our Lord*[^93]. For in the Greek manuscripts it is found that this is indeed an oath: lest anyone in the Latin tongue understand it said, by your glory, as it is said, by my coming again to you, and many similar things, where it is said, by something, and it is not an oath. But not because the Apostle, a man most firm in the truth, swore in his letters, ought swearing to be a sport to us. For it is much safer, as I have said, as far as concerns us, never to swear; so that there may be in our mouth, *Yes, yes; no, no*[^94], as the Lord admonishes. Not because it is a sin to swear what is true, but because it is a most grave sin to swear falsely; into which he falls the sooner who is accustomed to swear.

**5. 41.** You have received what seems to me; let better men expound these things better—not these people, whose opinion I now know is to be rejected, but others who can truly do so. For I too am more ready to learn than to teach; and you will confer a great benefit if you do not let me remain ignorant of the things that are there disputed by the holy brothers against the empty talk of these people. May you live rightly and faithfully in the Lord, most beloved son.
