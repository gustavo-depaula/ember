# Letter 4\*

TO CYRIL, A MOST BLESSED LORD, AND A BROTHER AND FELLOW PRIEST TO BE HONORED AND VENERATED WITH THE DUE DUTIES OF LOVE, AUGUSTINE SENDS GREETINGS IN THE LORD.

**1.** I most greatly commend myself to your holy prayers, rendering to your reverence the duty of greeting through a servant of God, Justus by name, whom I came to know very recently as a good brother; who, when he had come from there all the way to us, and was returning thence again from us, afforded a most welcome occasion by which we might discharge these duties to Your Beatitude. Nor do I think the cause that drove him to come to us should be kept silent; for I learned it from his own report.

**2.** Your Sincerity recalls, as I think, that you sent us the ecclesiastical proceedings held in the province of Palestine, where Pelagius, thought a Catholic, was acquitted, when he had hidden himself in the cunning hiding places of words and had deceived our brothers who then presided as judges, no adversary on the other side refuting him. These proceedings having been considered and examined as diligently as I could, I wrote a book about them to our venerable brother and fellow priest Aurelius, bishop of the Church of Carthage[^1], in which, as the Lord granted, I showed what the Catholic judges followed in Pelagius's answers, so as to acquit him as a Catholic. For many, entangled in his error, boasted that, he having been acquitted, his heretical dogmas too had been confirmed as Catholic by the judgment of Catholic bishops; and, with these boasting everywhere, very many, ignorant of what had been done, believed it was so, with great scandal to the churches.

**3.** To take away this opinion, then, I labored over the aforesaid book, in which, as I could, I demonstrated that, even with Pelagius acquitted (not before God, whom no one deceives, but before men, whom he could deceive), those pestilent dogmas were nevertheless altogether damned, even with him himself anathematizing them. But when this servant of God, Justus, the bearer of this letter to your venerability, had this book of mine, he offended certain ones, because in it it is argued that not all sinners are punished by eternal fire, and they said—as he reported to me—that this place of the book was not so explained by me, but falsified by him. Hence, disturbed, he sailed to us with that very codex, lest he have it perhaps faulty, although he was well conscious to himself that no falsity had been committed in it by him. And so, collating it with our codices, with me too coming fully to know it, he found that he had it intact.

**4.** Accordingly, since it touches us, far be it that a malevolent—but rather a suspicion full of love, yet not to be despised—should think that this sentence, which we state, that not all, but certain sinners are damned by everlasting punishment, displeases those who say that even this mortal life has the saints without sin, so that, for the indulgence of their sins (since there are none), not even the Lord's Prayer is necessary for them, in which the whole Church cries out: *Forgive us our debts*[^2]; without doubt Your Holiness provides that they should be corrected from the depravity of this error. For it is certain that this too flows from that unsound Pelagian doctrine, which asserts that all sinners are punished by eternal fire for this reason, that for those who truly confess they are not without sin no hope of pardon may be left, so that they either swell with pride, thinking that this life of theirs has no sin, or waste away in despair, as though already destined for everlasting punishment. The blessed Apostle says indeed: *The fire will test of what sort each one's work is. If anyone's work which he has built upon abides, he will receive a reward; but if anyone's work is burned up, he will suffer loss, but he himself will be saved, yet so as through fire*[^3], but these apostolic words are so to be taken that what is said be understood not of the fire of the last judgment, but, before that judgment, either in this life or after death. Yet by all means that error is to be avoided by which all sinners are thought to be about to go into the punishment of everlasting fire, if they have not led here a life that has no sin at all. It is also to be seen whether perhaps those who think this be found to savor other Pelagian dogmas too, no less or even more unsound, and dire contagions creep through the incautious crowd, while the evil is not repressed nor healed which has, in certain ones, been found out by the care of brotherly love.

**5.** So, then, I commend brother Justus to your most devout Holiness, that you not only defend him from those slandering him, but also that those very ones whom he not undeservedly holds suspect—lest perhaps they both lose their own souls and let the Pelagian poison into these—you would deign to correct with pastoral diligence and fatherly gentleness, or even, if it is needful, with medicinal harshness; or, if you find them sound in the faith, take from his mind the scruple of this suspicion. For both are Latins, and they came to those places from the Western Church, in which we too are. Hence they ought above all to be commended by us to Your Reverence, lest they seem to have chosen those lands for themselves, that they may lurk with impunity among the Greeks, where, when they argue these things, they are less understood, and therefore their error is not easily refuted. But we do this for this reason, that we may be saddened over the destruction of no one, but rather rejoice over the salvation of all, as far as it can be done.

[^1]: Augustine, The Deeds of Pelagius
[^2]: Matt 6:12
[^3]: 1 Cor 3:13-15
