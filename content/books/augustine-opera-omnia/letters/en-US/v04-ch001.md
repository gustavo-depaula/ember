# Letter 211

**1.** Just as severity stands ready to punish the sins it finds, so love does not wish to find anything to punish. This is the reason I did not come to you when you were seeking my presence, not for the joy of your peace but for the increase of your dissension. For how could I have overlooked it and left it unpunished, if so great a tumult had arisen among you in my presence as arose in my absence, which, though it escaped my eyes, nonetheless battered my ears with your voices? Indeed, your rebellion might perhaps have been even greater in my presence, since it would have been necessary not to grant you what you were demanding—things most ruinous in their example, contrary to sound discipline, and not to your advantage; and so I would have found you such as I do not want, and I myself would have been found by you such as you did not want.

**2.** Since, then, the Apostle writes to the Corinthians, saying, *I call God to witness against my soul that it was to spare you that I have not yet come to Corinth; not because we lord it over your faith, but we work with you for your joy*[^1], I too say this to you, that *it was to spare you that I have not come to you*. I also spared myself, *that I might not have sorrow upon sorrow*[^2]; and I chose not to show my face to you, but to pour out my heart to God for you, and to plead the cause of your great peril not before you in words, but before God in tears, lest he turn into grief that joy of mine by which I am accustomed to rejoice over you and, amid the great scandals with which this world everywhere abounds, sometimes to find consolation, when I consider your abundant community, your chaste love, and your holy manner of life, and the more generous grace of God that has been given you, so that you have not only scorned carnal marriage but have also chosen to live together of one mind in a household, so that you have one soul and one heart toward God.[^3]

**3.** When I consider these good things in you, these gifts of God, my heart, amid the many storms by which it is shaken from other evils, is wont to find some rest. *You were running well; who has bewitched you? This persuasion is not from God, who called you*[^4]. *A little leaven*...—I do not want to say what follows[^5]; for I rather desire and pray and exhort that this very leaven be changed for the better, lest the whole lump, as it had almost already done, be changed for the worse. If, therefore, you have sprouted again into sound thinking, *pray that you may not enter into temptation*[^6], lest you fall again *into quarrels, jealousies, animosities, dissensions, slanders, seditions, whisperings*[^7]. For we did not so plant and water the Lord's garden in you that we should reap such thorns from you. But if your weakness is still in turmoil, pray that you may be delivered from temptation. Yet those who disturb you, if they still disturb you, unless they correct themselves, will bear their judgment, whoever they may be.[^8]

**4.** Consider what an evil it is that, while we rejoice over the Donatists' return to unity, we should mourn internal schisms within the monastery. Persevere in your good purpose, and you will not desire to change the superior under whom, abiding in that monastery through so many years, you have grown both in number and in age—she who received you as a mother, not in her womb but in her heart. For all of you who came there found her either serving and pleasing my holy sister the superior, or already herself the superior who received you: under her you were instructed, under her you were veiled, under her you were multiplied; and yet you make such an uproar that she should be changed for you, when you ought rather to mourn if we wished to change her for you. For she is the one you know, she is the one to whom you came, she is the one by having whom for so many years you have grown. You have received nothing new except a superior; or if it is on his account that you seek novelty, and out of resentment against him you have rebelled in this way against your mother, why did you not rather ask that he be changed for you? But if you shrink from this—because I know how reverently you love him in Christ—why not rather her? For in governing you, the new superior's first efforts are so disturbed that he would rather abandon you himself than endure this reputation and resentment from you, so that it might be said that you would not have sought another superior had you not begun to have him as superior. May God therefore calm and compose your minds; let not the work of the devil prevail in you, but let *the peace of Christ conquer in your hearts*[^9]; and do not, out of grief of mind because what you want is not done, or out of shame at having wanted what you ought not to have wanted, blushing run to your death, but rather by repenting take up your strength again, and do not have the repentance of Judas the traitor, but rather the tears of Peter the shepherd.[^10]

**5.** These are the things we command you to observe, you who have been established in the monastery. First, the reason you have been gathered into one is that you may dwell together of one mind in the household and have *one heart and one soul toward God*[^11]. And do not call anything your own, but let all things be common to you, and let food and clothing be distributed to each of you by your superior, not equally to all, because you are not all equally strong, but to each according to her need. For so you read in the Acts of the Apostles: *They had all things in common, and distribution was made to each as anyone had need*[^12]. Those who had anything in the world, when they entered the monastery, should willingly want it to be held in common. But those who had nothing should not seek in the monastery what they could not have outside; nevertheless, what their weakness requires should be supplied to them, even if their poverty, when they were outside, could not find even the necessities; only let them not on that account think themselves happy because they have found food and clothing of a kind they could not find outside.

**6.** And let them not lift up their necks because they are now associated with those whom they did not dare to approach outside, but let them have their hearts on high and not seek earthly goods, lest monasteries begin to be useful to the rich and not to the poor, if the rich are humbled there and the poor are puffed up there. But again, those too who seemed to be something in the world should not look down on their sisters who have come to that holy community out of poverty; rather, let them be eager to glory not in the rank of their wealthy parents but in the fellowship of their poor sisters. And let them not be exalted if they have contributed something of their own resources to the common life, lest they be prouder of their wealth because they share it with the monastery than if they were to enjoy it in the world. For every other kind of iniquity is exercised in evil works so that they may be done; but pride lies in wait even for good works so that they may perish. And what does it profit to scatter by giving to the poor and to become poor, if the wretched soul is made prouder by despising riches than it had been by possessing them? Live therefore all of you of one mind and in concord, and honor in one another God, whose temples you have become.[^13]

**7.** Be diligent in prayers at the appointed hours and times. In the oratory let no one do anything but that for which it was made, from which it also takes its name; so that if any, even apart from the appointed hours, should wish to pray when they have leisure, they may not be hindered by those who think they may do something else there. When you pray to God with psalms and hymns, let what is uttered with the voice be turned over in the heart; and do not sing anything except what you read is to be sung; but what is not so written that it should be sung, let it not be sung.

**8.** Subdue your flesh by fasting and by abstinence from food and drink, as far as your health permits. But when someone cannot fast, let her nonetheless take no food outside the hour of the meal, unless she is sick. When you come to table, until you rise from it, listen without uproar and quarreling to what is read to you according to custom; and let not your throats alone take food, but let your ears also receive the word of God.

**9.** Those who are weak from a previous habit, if they are treated differently in their diet, ought not to be a source of annoyance to the others, nor should it seem unjust to those whom a different habit has made stronger. And let these not think the others happier because they take what they themselves do not take, but let them rather congratulate themselves that they are strong where the others are not. And if to those who have come to the monastery from a more delicate way of life there is given something in food, clothing, bedding, or coverings that is not given to others who are stronger and therefore happier, those to whom it is not given ought to consider how far those others have come down from their worldly life to this one, even though they have not been able to attain to the frugality of others who are stronger in body. Nor should the latter be disturbed that they see the former receiving more—not because they are honored, but because they are tolerated—lest there arise that detestable perversity whereby in the monastery, where the strong become as rich as they can in toil, the delicate become poor. Indeed, just as the sick must necessarily take less lest they be weighed down, so after sickness they must be so treated that they may recover more quickly, even if they came from the lowliest poverty of the world, as though the more recent illness had conferred on them what an earlier habit conferred on the wealthy. But when they have regained their former strength, let them return to their happier habit, which befits the servants of God all the more the less they are in need; nor let the will keep them, now vigorous, where necessity had relieved them when they were weak. Let those count themselves richer who are stronger in bearing scarcity. For it is better to need less than to have more.

**10.** Let your dress not be conspicuous; and do not seek to please by your clothes, but by your conduct. Let your head coverings not be so thin that the hairnets beneath show through. Keep your hair covered on every side, and let it neither be scattered by carelessness nor arranged by effort. When you go out, walk together; when you come to where you are going, stand together. In your gait, in your bearing, in your dress, in all your movements let nothing be done that would entice anyone's lust, but only what befits your holiness. Even if your eyes are cast upon some man, let them be fixed on none. For when you go out, you are not forbidden to see men, but to desire them or to wish to be desired by them. It is not by touch alone but also by feeling and by gaze that a woman is desired and desires. And do not say that you have chaste minds if you have unchaste eyes, for an unchaste eye is the messenger of an unchaste heart. And when, even with the tongue silent, hearts announce themselves to one another by mutual gaze as unchaste, and according to the desire of the flesh they delight in one another with ardor, then, even though the bodies remain untouched by any unclean violation, chastity itself flees from their conduct. Nor ought she who fixes her eye upon a man and loves to have his fixed upon herself to think that she is not seen by others when she does this; she is seen indeed, and by those by whom she does not suppose herself to be seen. But suppose she is hidden and seen by no human being—what will she do about that observer on high, from whom nothing can be hidden? Or is he to be thought not to see because he sees with the more patience the more wisely he sees? Let the holy woman, then, fear to displease him, lest she wish to please a man wrongly; let her consider that he sees all things, lest she wish to look on a man wrongly. For in this matter too the fear of him has been commended, where it is written: *An abomination to the Lord is one who fixes the eye*[^14]. When, therefore, you are together in church, and wherever men are also present, guard your chastity for one another. For God, who dwells in you, will in this way also guard you by means of yourselves.

**11.** And if you notice in any of you this wantonness of the eyes of which I speak, admonish her at once, so that what has begun may not go further but be corrected immediately. But if after the admonition you see her doing the same thing again, on whatever other day, then whoever has been able to discover this must, as with one already wounded, bring her forward to be healed—yet only after the matter has first been shown to a second or a third, so that she may be convicted by the mouth of two or three[^15] and restrained with fitting severity. And do not judge yourselves to be ill-disposed when you report this. For indeed you are rather not innocent if by your silence you allow your sisters to perish, whom you could correct by reporting them. For if your sister had a wound on her body that she wanted to hide because she feared to have it lanced, would it not be cruel of you to keep silent, and merciful to disclose it? How much more, then, ought you to make it known, lest it fester more ruinously in her heart? But before it is shown to the others, by whom she is to be convicted if she denies it, it ought to be disclosed to the superior, if she has neglected to be corrected after being admonished, in case she can be more privately reproved and not become known to the rest. But if she denies it, then others are to be brought in to the one denying, so that now before all she may be charged not by one witness but convicted by two or three.[^16] And once convicted, she ought to undergo a corrective punishment at the discretion of the superior or the priest; and if she refuses to bear it, and does not herself depart, let her be cast out of your community. For this too is done not cruelly but mercifully, lest by a pestilent contagion she destroy many. And what I have said about not fixing the eye should also be diligently observed in detecting, forbidding, reporting, convicting, and punishing the other sins, with love for the persons and hatred for the vices. But whoever has gone so far into evil as to receive letters or any little gifts secretly from anyone, if she confesses this of her own accord, let her be pardoned and prayed for. But if she is caught and convicted, let her be more severely corrected at the discretion of the superior or the priest, or even the bishop.

**12.** Keep your clothes in one place under one or two custodians, or as many as can suffice to shake them out lest they be damaged by moths; and just as you are fed from one storeroom, so be clothed from one wardrobe. And if it can be so, let it not concern you which garment is brought out for you to wear as the season requires—whether each of you receives the one she had laid aside or another that someone else had had—provided that what each one needs is not denied her. But if from this quarrels and grumbling arise among you, and someone complains that she has received something worse than she had before, and judges herself unworthy to be clothed as her sister was clothed, then test yourselves by this to see how much you lack in that inner *holy garment*[^17] of the heart, you who quarrel over the garment of the body. Yet if your weakness is tolerated, so that you receive what you had laid aside, still keep in one place under common custodians what you lay aside; in such a way, to be sure, that no one works at anything for herself—whether what she is to wear, or where she is to lie, or what she is to be girded or covered with, or to cover her head—but let all your work be done in common, with greater zeal and more frequent eagerness than if you were making things for yourselves. For love, of which it is written that *it does not seek its own*[^18], is understood in this way: that it sets common things before its own, not its own before the common. And therefore the more you care for the common property rather than your own, the more you will know that you have made progress, so that in all the things that passing necessity uses, the love that abides may surpass them. The consequence, then, is that if anyone, man or woman, has conferred on his own daughters or on any relatives established in the monastery something—whether a garment or anything else to be reckoned among necessities—it should not be received secretly, but it should be in the power of the superior, so that, brought into the common store, it may be furnished to whoever needs it. But if anyone conceals a thing conferred on her, let her be condemned by the judgment of theft.

**13.** Let your garments be washed at the discretion of the superior, whether by yourselves or by launderers, lest an excessive craving for clean clothing contract inner stains of the soul. Let the washing of bodies too, and the use of baths, not be constant, but be allowed at the customary interval of time, that is, once a month. But where the necessity of an illness compels the body to be washed, let it not be put off longer; let it be done without grumbling on the advice of the physician, so that, even if she is unwilling, she does at the superior's bidding what must be done for her health. But if she is willing, and perhaps it is not expedient, let her craving not be obeyed; for sometimes, even if it does harm, what gives pleasure is believed to do good. Finally, if there is a hidden pain in the body of a servant of God, let her be believed without doubt when she says what is paining her; yet if it is not certain whether what gives her pleasure is expedient for healing that pain, let the physician be consulted. And let them not go to the baths, or wherever it is necessary to go, fewer than three. Nor should she who has need to go somewhere go with those she herself wishes, but with those the superior orders. The care of the sick, whether of those recovering after illness or of those laboring under some infirmity even without fever, ought to be entrusted to someone, so that she herself may request from the storeroom what she sees each one needs. And let those who are in charge of the storeroom, or of the clothing, or of the books, serve their sisters without grumbling. Let books be requested at a fixed hour each day; let those who request them outside that hour not receive them. But let those under whose custody are the things requested not delay to give clothing and footwear when they are necessary to one in need.

**14.** Either have no quarrels, or end them as quickly as possible, lest anger grow into hatred, and make a beam out of a splinter[^19], and make the soul a murderer. For it is not to men alone that what is written pertains: *Whoever hates his brother is a murderer*[^20]; rather, the female sex too received this precept with the male sex, which God made first. Whoever has injured another by insult, or curse, or even by charging her with a crime, let her remember to cure what she has done by making amends as quickly as possible, and let the one who was injured forgive without dispute. But if they have injured one another, they ought to remit their debts to one another, for the sake of your prayers; and the more often you have them, the holier you ought to have them. Better is she who, though often tempted by anger, yet hastens to obtain forgiveness from the one to whom she acknowledges she has done a wrong, than she who grows angry more slowly and is more reluctantly inclined to ask pardon. She who is unwilling to forgive a sister should not hope to receive the effect of her prayer; but she who is never willing to ask pardon, or does not ask it from the heart, is without reason in the monastery, even if she is not cast out of it. Accordingly, refrain from harsh words; and if they have issued from your mouth, do not be reluctant to bring forth remedies from the same mouth from which the wounds were made. But when the necessity of discipline compels you to say harsh words in restraining the younger ones, even if you feel that you have exceeded the measure in those very words, it is not required of you to beg pardon of them, lest, while excessive humility is preserved among those who ought to be subject, the authority of governing be broken; nevertheless, pardon is to be sought from the Lord of all, who knows with how much goodwill you love even those whom you have perhaps rebuked more than is just. But the love among you ought to be not carnal but spiritual; for the things that women do who are forgetful of modesty, even women with women, jesting shamefully and playing, ought not to be done not only by widows and untouched handmaids of Christ established in a holy purpose, but not at all by married women either, nor by virgins about to marry.

**15.** Let the superior be obeyed as a mother, with due honor preserved, lest in her God be offended; much more the priest, who has the care of you all. That all these things, then, may be observed, and that, if anything has not been observed, it may not be carelessly passed over but be seen to and set right, pertains chiefly to the superior, in such a way that she refers to the priest who watches over you whatever exceeds her measure or her strength. But let her not count herself happy in a power that dominates, but in a love that serves. In honor before men let her be set above you; in fear before God let her be laid beneath your feet. Toward all let her show herself a pattern of good works.[^21] Let her rebuke the restless, console the fainthearted, support the weak, be patient toward all[^22]; let her hold discipline gladly, impose it with fear. And although both are necessary, yet let her seek more to be loved by you than feared, always considering that she will render an account of you to God. Hence by obeying you have mercy not only on yourselves but also on her, since the higher the place she holds among you, the greater the peril in which she stands.

**16.** May the Lord grant that you observe all these things with love, as lovers of spiritual beauty, breathing forth the good fragrance of Christ by a good manner of life, not as bondwomen under the law, but as women established free under grace.[^23] And that you may be able to look at yourselves in this little book as in a mirror, lest through forgetfulness you neglect anything, let it be read to you once a week; and where you find yourselves doing the things that are written, give thanks to the Lord, the giver of all good things; but where any of you sees that something is lacking to her, let her grieve over the past and be on guard for the future, praying that the debt may be forgiven and that she may not be led into temptation.[^24]
