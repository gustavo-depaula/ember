# Letter 166

*A Book on the Origin of the Human Soul*

Augustine to Jerome

**1. 1.** Our God, who has called us into his kingdom and glory, I have both asked and ask, that this which I write to you, holy brother Jerome, consulting you about the things I do not know, he may will to be fruitful to us. For although I consult you, who are much greater than I in age, yet I myself too, already an old man, consult: but for learning what is needful, no age can seem to me too late; because, although it more befits old men to teach than to learn, yet it befits them more to learn than to be ignorant of what they teach. Indeed I bear nothing more troublesome, in all the straits which I suffer in the most difficult questions, than the absence of your charity at so great a distance, so that I can scarcely give, scarcely receive, your letters, at intervals not of days, not of months, but of several years; whereas, if it could be done, I would wish to have you present daily, with whom I might speak whatever I wished. Nor for that reason, however, ought I not to do what I could, even if I could not do all that I wished.

**1. 2.** Behold, there came to me a religious young man, in catholic peace a brother, in age a son, in honor our fellow presbyter, Orosius, vigilant in mind, ready in speech, burning with zeal, desiring to be a useful vessel in the house of the Lord, for refuting the false and pernicious doctrines which have slaughtered the souls of the Spaniards much more unhappily than the barbarian sword their bodies. For from there he hastened to us all the way from the shore of the ocean, stirred by the report that from me he could hear whatever he wished about the things he wished to know. Nor did he take no fruit from his coming. First, that he should not believe too much of me from report: then, I taught the man what I could; and what I could not, I advised him whence he could learn it, and exhorted him to go to you. In which matter, since he received my counsel, or rather command, willingly and obediently, I asked him that, coming from you, he would return to his own through us. Holding to this promise of his, I believed an occasion had been granted me by the Lord, by which I might write to you about the things I desire to know through you. For I was seeking whom I might send to you, nor did a suitable one easily occur to me, both in faithfulness of acting and in alacrity of obeying and in practice of traveling. When, therefore, I found this young man to be the very one such as I was asking from the Lord, I could not doubt.

**2. 3.** Receive, then, the things which I ask you not to be reluctant to open and discuss. The question about the soul moves many, among whom I confess that I too am one. For what I hold most firmly about the soul, I will not keep silent: then I will subjoin what I still wish to be cleared up for me. The soul of man is immortal, according to a certain manner of its own. For not in every way as God, of whom it was said that *he alone has immortality*[^1]. For about the deaths of the soul holy Scripture recalls many things; whence is that: *Let the dead bury their dead*[^2]: but it so dies, estranged from the life of God, that nevertheless it does not altogether cease to live in its own nature; so it is found mortal from some cause, that it is also not without reason called immortal. The soul is not a part of God. For if it were this, it would be in every way unchangeable and incorruptible. And if it were so, it would neither fail toward the worse, nor advance toward the better; nor would it either begin to have in itself something it did not have, or cease to have what it had, as far as pertains to its own affections: but that it is otherwise, there is no need of testimony from without; whoever attends to himself recognizes it. But it is said in vain by those who would have the soul to be a part of God, that this defect and baseness which we see in the most worthless men, this weakness and sickness which we feel in all men, is not from itself, but from the body: what does it matter whence it is sick, which, if it were unchangeable, could not be sick from any source whatever? For what is truly unchangeable and incorruptible can be changed or corrupted by the accession of nothing: otherwise not only Achilles, as the fables tell, but all flesh would be invulnerable, if no mischance befell it. The nature, then, is not unchangeable which is in any way, by any cause, in any part, changeable: but it is wicked to believe God otherwise than truly and supremely unchangeable. The soul, therefore, is not a part of God.

**2. 4.** That the soul too is incorporeal, although it can be persuaded to the slower-witted with difficulty, I confess is nevertheless persuaded to me. But lest I either superfluously raise a controversy of words, or deservedly suffer one—since, when the matter is settled, there is no need to contend about the name—if every substance, or essence, or whatever is more aptly named is a body, namely whatever in some way is in itself, then the soul is a body. Likewise, if it pleases to call incorporeal only that nature which is supremely unchangeable and is everywhere whole, the soul is a body; since it is not anything of such a kind. Further, if a body is nothing but what, through space of place, by some length, breadth, and height, is so set or moved that with a greater part of itself it occupies a greater place, and with a shorter a shorter, and is less in a part than in the whole, then the soul is not a body. For throughout the whole body which it animates, it is extended not by local diffusion, but by a certain vital intention: for through all its particles it is wholly present at once, and is not less in the lesser and greater in the greater; but somewhere more intently, somewhere more remissly, and in all it is whole, and in each it is whole. For not otherwise does it, which feels in a part of the body and not the whole, nevertheless feel wholly: for when something is touched in a tiny point in the living flesh, although that place is not only not that of the whole body, but scarcely seems to be in the body, yet it does not lie hidden from the whole soul; nor does what is felt run through all parts of the body, but is felt only there where it occurs. Whence, then, does what occurs not in the whole soon reach the whole, except because the soul is whole even there where it occurs, nor, to be whole there, deserts the rest? For the other parts too live, it being present, where nothing of the kind has occurred. And if this should occur, and both occur at once, both at once would not be hidden equally from the whole. Accordingly it could not be whole at once in all and in each particle of its body, if it were diffused through them as we see bodies diffused through spaces of places, occupying smaller places with their smaller parts, and larger with their larger. Wherefore, if the soul is to be called a body, it is certainly not a body such as the earthly, nor such as the moist, or airy, or ethereal. For all such are greater in greater places and lesser in lesser, and none of them is wholly present in any part of itself; but as there are parts of places, so they are occupied by parts of bodies. Whence it is understood that the soul, whether it is to be called a body or incorporeal, has a certain nature of its own, created of a substance more excellent than all these elements of the worldly mass, which truly cannot be thought of in any phantasm of corporeal images, which we perceive through the senses of the flesh, but is understood by the mind and felt by life. Nor do I say these things in order to teach you what is known to you, but to open what I most firmly hold about the soul, lest anyone, when I come to the things I inquire about, think that I hold nothing about the soul, either by knowledge or by faith.

**2. 5.** I am also certain that the soul, by no fault of God, by no necessity either of God's or its own, but by its own will, has collapsed into sin; and that it cannot be freed from the body of this death either by the power of its own will, as though sufficient for this of itself, or by the death of the body itself, but by the grace of God through Jesus Christ our Lord; and that there is no soul at all in the human race for whose liberation the mediator of God and men, the man Christ Jesus, is not necessary. But whatever soul, without the grace of the Mediator and his sacrament, goes out of the body at any age of the body, goes both to future punishment, and at the last judgment will receive a body for punishment. But if, after the human generation which was made from Adam, it is regenerated in Christ, belonging to his society, it will both have rest after the death of the body, and will receive a body for glory. These are the things which I hold most firmly about the soul.

**3. 6.** Now receive, I beg, what I inquire, and do not spurn me; so may he not spurn you who for our sake deigned to be spurned. I ask where the soul contracted the guilt by which it is drawn into condemnation, even an infant's taken away by death, if the grace of Christ does not come to its aid through the sacrament by which little ones too are baptized. For you are not among those who have just now begun to babble certain novelties, saying that there is no guilt drawn from Adam which is loosed by baptism in the infant. If I knew that you held this—or rather, unless I knew that you do not hold it—I would by no means ask this of you, or think it should be asked. But because we hold your opinion on this matter to be in concord with the most well-founded catholic faith, by which, refuting also the empty talk of Jovinian, you adduced a testimony from the book of Job, *No one is clean in your sight, not even an infant whose life is of one day upon the earth*[^3], then you added: And we are held guilty in the likeness of the transgression of Adam; and your book on the prophet Jonah declares this quite remarkably and clearly, where you said that little ones are rightly compelled to fast on account of that very original sin: not unfittingly do I ask you where the soul contracted this guilt, on account of which it must be freed, even at that age, through the sacrament of Christian grace.

**3. 7.** I indeed, some years ago, when I was writing certain books on Free Will, which have gone out into the hands of many and are now held by very many, so thought that the four opinions about the incarnation of the soul should be treated—whether the rest are propagated from that one which was given to the first man; or whether new ones too are made even now for each individual; or whether, existing already somewhere, they are either sent divinely or fall of their own accord into bodies—that whichever of them was true would not hinder my intention, by which I was then acting with as much strength as I could against those who try to bring in, against God, a nature of evil endowed with its own principle, that is, against the Manichees; for of the Priscillianists I had as yet heard nothing, who fable blasphemies not much unlike those of the others. Therefore I did not add a fifth opinion, which you mentioned among the rest in your letter, lest you should pass over any, where, answering a man of religious memory and most dear to us in the charity of Christ, Marcellinus, who inquired about this question, you wrote that the soul is a part of God: first, because the question is not about its incarnation but about its nature when this is asked; then, because those against whom I was acting hold this, and I was especially acting to separate the blameless and inviolable nature of the Creator from the vices and defect of the creature, since they contend that the very substance of the good God, in the part by which it was captured, was corrupted and oppressed by the substance of evil—to which they attribute its own principle and princes—and led to a necessity of sinning. This error of heretical opinion being excepted, then, I desire to know which of the four remaining opinions is to be chosen. For whichever is to be chosen, far be it that it should impugn this faith of which we are certain: that for every soul, even that of a little infant, there is necessary a liberation from the bond of sin, and that this is none except through Jesus Christ, and him crucified.

**4. 8.** Accordingly, not to make it long, you certainly hold this: that God makes individual souls even now for individuals being born. To which opinion, when it is objected that God consummated all creatures on the sixth day and rested on the seventh, you adduce the testimony from the Gospel: *My Father works until now*[^4]. For so you wrote to Marcellinus: in which letter you also deigned most benevolently to make mention of me, because he had me here in Africa, who could more easily set forth that opinion to him. But if I could have, he would not have inquired this of you, placed so far away; if, indeed, he wrote this to you from Africa. For when he wrote, I do not know; I only know that about this he well knew my hesitation: whence he wished to act without consulting me. Although, even if he had consulted me, I would rather have exhorted him, and given thanks that it could be conferred upon us all, had you not preferred to write back briefly rather than to answer fully: I believe lest you should labor superfluously where I was, whom you thought to know best what he had asked. Behold, I wish that opinion to be mine too, but I do not yet confirm that it is.

**4. 9.** You have sent disciples to me, that I may teach them the things I have not yet myself learned. Teach, then, what I am to teach: for many demand of me that I teach, and to them I confess that I am ignorant of this, as of many other things: and perhaps, although in my presence they are abashed, yet among themselves they say: *You are a teacher in Israel, and are you ignorant of these things?*[^5]—which indeed the Lord said to one who was one of those who delighted to be called Rabbi: whence he had come to the true teacher by night, because perhaps he blushed to learn, who had been accustomed to teach. But it delights me rather to hear the teacher than to be heard as a teacher. For I recall what he said to those whom he chose before the rest: *But you*, he says, *do not be called Rabbi by men; for one is your teacher, Christ*[^6]. Nor did another teach Moses even through Jethro, nor another Cornelius even through Peter who came first, nor another Peter even through Paul who came later: for by whomever the truth is spoken, it is spoken by the gift of him who is the Truth itself. What if for this reason we still do not know these things, and could not find them either by praying, or by reading, or by thinking and reasoning—so that we may be proved, not only with how great charity we teach the unlearned, but with how great humility we learn even from the learned?

**4. 10.** Teach, then, I beg, what I am to teach, teach what I am to hold, and tell me, if souls are made individually even today for individuals being born, where in little ones they sin, so that they need in the sacrament of Christ the remission of sin, sinning in Adam from whom the flesh of sin was propagated: or, if they do not sin, by what justice of the Creator they are so bound by another's sin, when they are inserted into the mortal members propagated from there, that damnation follows them unless aid be brought through the Church; whereas it is not in their power that the grace of baptism can come to their aid. By what equity, then, are so many thousands of souls damned, which in the deaths of little ones go out of bodies without the indulgence of the Christian sacrament, if, newly created with no preceding sin of their own, but by the will of the Creator they clung individually to individuals being born, to animate whom he created and gave them—he who surely knew that each of them, by no fault of its own, was going to go out of the body without the baptism of Christ? Since, therefore, we can neither say of God that he either compels souls to be made sinful, or punishes the innocent; nor is it lawful for us to deny that those which go out of bodies without the sacrament of Christ, even of little ones, are drawn only into damnation: I beseech you, how is this opinion defended, by which souls are believed to be made, not all from that one of the first man, but as that one for one, so individuals for individuals?

**5. 11.** But those things which are said as other objections against this opinion, I think I can easily refute; such as that by which certain people seem to press it: how did God consummate all his works on the sixth day, and rest on the seventh, if he still creates new souls? To whom, if we say what you set down from the Gospel in the aforesaid letter, *My Father works until now*[^7], they answer: it is said, he works, by administering the things instituted, not by instituting new natures, lest the Scripture of Genesis be contradicted, where it is read most plainly that God consummated all his works. For even that it is written that he rested, is surely to be understood of creating new creatures, not of governing them; because then he made the things that were not, from making which he rested: because he had consummated all the things which, before they were, he saw must be made, so that thenceforth he should create and make whatever he made, not the things that were not, but from the things that already were. Thus both are shown to be true—both that it was said, he rested from his works, and that it was said, he works until now: since the Gospel cannot be contrary to Genesis.

**5. 12.** But to those who say these things so that it may not be believed that God now, like that one, makes new souls which were not, but creates them from that one which already was, or sends them from some fountain or treasury which he then made, it is easily answered that even in those six days God created many things from those natures which he had already created, as from the waters birds and fish, but from the earth trees, hay, animals: but that he then made the things which were not, is manifest. For there was no bird, no fish, no tree, no animal: and it is rightly understood that he rested from these things created which were not, and were created, that is, ceased, lest further the things which were not should be created. But now, when it is said that God does not send souls already existing in some fountain, nor sprinkle them from himself as his own particles, nor draw them originally from that one, nor fetter them in carnal chains for offenses committed before the flesh, but creates new ones individually for individuals, each its own for each being born, he is not said to do anything which he had not done before. For already on the sixth day he had made man to his own image; which surely is understood to have been done according to the rational soul. This he does now too, not by instituting what was not, but by multiplying what was. Whence both is true—that he rested from instituting the things which were not—and this is true—that he works until now, not only by governing the things he made, but also by creating something more numerously, not which he had not yet, but which he had already created. Either in this way, then, or in any other way whatever, we get out from under what is objected to us about God's rest from his works, lest on that account we should not believe that new souls are now still made, not from that one, but as that one.

**5. 13.** For as to what is said—Why does he make souls for those whom he knows will quickly die?—we can answer that hereby the sins of the parents are either convicted or scourged. We can also rightly leave these things to the moderation of him whom we know to give a most ornate and most orderly course to all things passing temporally, among which are also the births and deaths of animals; but that we cannot perceive these things, which, if we could perceive, we would be soothed with an ineffable delight. For not in vain was it said of God through the prophet, who had learned these things divinely inspired: *Who brings forth the world by number*[^8]. Whence music, that is, the knowledge or sense of modulating well, was granted by God's bounty, for the admonition of a great matter, even to mortals having rational souls. Whence, if a man skilled in making a song knows what delays to assign to which sounds, so that what is sung may run and pass most beautifully by sounds departing and succeeding; how much more does God, whose wisdom, by which he made all things, is far to be preferred to all arts, permit no spaces of time in natures being born and dying—which, like syllables and words, pertain to the particles of this age—to pass, in this as it were marvelous song of passing things, either more briefly or more at length than the modulation foreknown and predetermined demands! And since I have said this even of a tree's leaf, and of the number of our hairs, how much more of the rising and setting of a man, whose temporal life is not extended more briefly or more at length than God the disposer of times knows to be consonant with the governance of the universe!

**5. 14.** That too which they say—that everything which began to be in time cannot be immortal, because all things that have arisen perish, and things grown grow old, so that in this way they may compel us to believe that the human mind is therefore immortal because it was created before all times—does not move our faith: for, to say nothing else, the immortality of Christ's flesh began to be in time, which nevertheless now does not die, and *death will no longer have dominion over him*[^9].

**5. 15.** But that which you set down in the book against Rufinus—that certain people calumniate this opinion, because it seems unworthy that God should give souls to adulterous conceptions, whence they try to construct that, by the merits of a life lived before the flesh, souls can justly be brought as it were to workhouses of this kind—does not move me, who think of many things by which this calumny can be refuted. And what you yourself answered—that the fault of the sowing is not in the wheat which is said to have been carried off by theft, but in him who stole the grain; nor on that account ought the earth not to cherish the seeds in its bosom, because the sower cast them with an unclean hand—is a most elegant likeness. And even before I read it, this objection about adulterous offspring made no straits for me in this question, since I regard generally that God makes many good things even out of our evils and our sins. But the creation of any animal whatever, if it has a pious and prudent considerer, stirs up ineffable praise of the Creator; how much more the creation, not of any animal, but of man! But if the cause of creating is sought, none is more quickly and better answered than that every creature of God is good. And what is more worthy than that the good God should make good things, which no one can make but God?

**6. 16.** These and other things I can, as I can, say against those who try to undermine this opinion by which souls are believed to be made for individuals as that one was. But when it comes to the punishments of little ones, I am, believe me, hemmed in by great straits, nor do I at all find what to answer: I do not mean only those punishments which that damnation has after this life, to which they must necessarily be drawn, if they go out of the body without the sacrament of Christian grace; but those very ones which in this life are set before our eyes, to our grief, which, if I should wish to enumerate, time would fail me sooner than examples. They languish with illnesses, are tortured by pains, are racked by hunger and thirst, are crippled in their limbs, are deprived of their senses, are vexed by unclean spirits. It must surely be shown how they justly suffer these things without any evil cause of their own. For it is not lawful to say either that these things happen with God not knowing, or that he cannot resist those doing them, or that he either does or permits these things unjustly. Is it that, as we rightly say that irrational animals are given for the uses of more excellent natures, even if vicious—as in the Gospel we see most plainly that swine were granted to the demons for the use they desired—can we rightly say this also of man? For he is an animal, but rational, though mortal. There is a rational soul in those limbs which pay penalties by such afflictions; God is good, God is just, God is omnipotent: to doubt this is altogether the part of a madman. Let a just cause, then, be told of such great evils which happen in little ones. Indeed, when older people suffer these things, we are wont to say either that, as in Job, merits are being tested, or that, as in Herod, sins are being punished: and from certain examples which God willed to be manifest, it is granted to man to conjecture others which are obscure; but this in older people. But of little ones, explain what we should answer, if there are no sins to be punished in them by such great punishments: for surely there is no justice to be tested at those ages.

**6. 17.** And of the diversity—or rather the absurdity—of mental capacities, what shall I say? Which indeed lies hidden in little ones, but, drawn from the very natural beginnings, appears in grown men, some of whom are so slow and forgetful that they could not learn even the first elements of letters; while certain ones are of such fatuity that they do not differ much from cattle, whom they commonly call idiots. It is answered perhaps: The bodies do this. But did the soul, according to this opinion which we wish to defend, choose a body for itself, and in choosing, when it was deceived, err? Or, when it was compelled to enter a body by the necessity of being born, other bodies being preoccupied by the crowds of souls, did it itself find no other, and, as a place at some spectacle, so occupy not the flesh it wished, but the flesh it was able to? Can we either say, or ought we to think, these and such things? Teach, then, what we ought to think, what we ought to say, so that the account of new souls, made individually for individual bodies, may stand for us.

**7. 18.** I indeed said something, not about mental capacities, but at least about the punishments of little ones which they suffer in this life, in those books on Free Will. Of what kind it is, and why it does not suffice me in this question which we have in hand, I will intimate, and I will insert into this letter that very passage excerpted from the third book. For it is thus: "But concerning the torments of the body by which little ones are afflicted, who through their age have no sins—if the souls by which they are animated did not exist before the men themselves began to be—a greater complaint is wont to be laid down, and as it were mercifully, when it is said, What evil have they done, that they should suffer these things? as if there could be a merit of innocence before anyone could do any harm! But since God works some good in the amending of older people, when by the pains and deaths of their little ones who are dear to them they are scourged, why should these things not happen, since, when they have passed, it will be as if they had not happened for those in whom they happened; but those on whose account they happened will either be better, if, amended by temporal inconveniences, they choose to live more rightly, or will have no excuse in the punishment of the future judgment, if, amid the anguishes of this life, they have been unwilling to turn their desire to eternal life? But who knows what God reserves for those very little ones, in the secret of his judgments, by way of good compensation—those by whose torments either the hardness of older people is bruised, or their faith is exercised, or their mercy is proved? Since, although they have done nothing rightly, yet they have suffered these things not as sinning in anything. For not in vain does the Church commend as received into the honor of the martyrs even those infants who were killed when our Lord Jesus Christ was sought by Herod to be slain."

**7. 19.** These things I then said, when I wished to fortify this very opinion of which we now treat. For, as I recalled a little before, whichever of those four opinions about the incarnation of the soul was true, I was striving to show the substance of the Creator blameless, and most remote from the society of our sins. And therefore whichever of them could be convicted by the truth and repudiated did not pertain to the care of my intention which I then had; since, all having been examined by a more diligent disputation, whichever of them rightly conquered the rest would be most secure for me, when, even according to all of them, I was demonstrating that what I was maintaining persisted unconquered. But now I wish, if I can, by right reason to choose one out of all; and therefore, looking more attentively at the defense of this very one of which we now treat, in those words of mine which I recalled from that book, I do not see it valid and firm.

**7. 20.** For as its support there is that which I said there: "But who knows what God reserves for those very little ones, in the secret of his judgments, by way of good compensation—those by whose torments either the hardness of older people is bruised, or their faith is exercised, or their mercy is proved?" But I see this said not without reason of those who suffer something of the kind for the name of Christ and true religion, even without knowing it, or who are already imbued with the sacrament of Christ; because without the society of the one Mediator they cannot be freed from damnation, so that this compensation can be granted them, even for those evils which here, in various afflictions, they have endured. But now, since this question cannot be resolved unless answer be made also about those little ones who breathe their last after the gravest torments without the sacrament of Christian society—what compensation is to be thought of for those for whom, moreover, damnation has been prepared? For about the baptism of little ones too, in that same book, not indeed sufficiently, but as much as seemed enough for that work, I somehow answered, that it profits even those who do not know it, and do not yet have their own faith: yet about the damnation of those little ones who depart from this life without it, I did not then think anything should be said, because what is now being treated was not being treated.

**7. 21.** But to omit and disregard the things which they suffer for a brief time, nor are recalled once they are past—can we similarly disregard that *through one man came death, and through one man the resurrection of the dead? For as in Adam all die, so also in Christ all will be made alive*[^10]? For through this apostolic, divine, and clear sentence it shines forth quite evidently that no one goes into death except through Adam, no one goes into eternal life except through Christ. For this is, all, and all: because as all men, through the first, that is, through carnal generation, pertain to Adam, so all men come to the second, that is, the spiritual generation, whoever come to Christ. Therefore it was said, both here all, and there all: because as all who die, die only in Adam, so all who will be made alive, will be made alive only in Christ. And through this, whoever should tell us that anyone can be made alive in the resurrection of the dead except in Christ is to be detested as a plague of the common faith. Likewise, whoever should say that in Christ even little ones who go out of life without participation in his sacrament will be made alive—he surely both goes against the apostolic preaching and condemns the whole Church, where, for that reason, there is haste and running to baptize little ones, since it is believed without doubt that they can otherwise in no way be made alive in Christ. But whoever is not made alive in Christ, it remains that he abide in that condemnation of which the Apostle says: *Through the offense of one, upon all men unto condemnation*[^11]. That little ones are born liable to this offense, both the whole Church believes, and you yourself, already disputing against Jovinian and expounding the prophet Jonah, as I recalled a little before, defined by a most truthful faith; I believe also in other places of your little works which I have either not read, or do not at present recall. The cause, then, of this damnation in little ones I inquire; because I neither see that there is any sin of the souls at that age, if new ones are made individually for individuals, nor do I believe that any is damned by God which he sees to have no sin.

**8. 22.** Or perhaps it must be said that in the little one the flesh alone is the cause of sin; but that a new soul is made for it, by which—living according to the precepts of God, in the help of the grace of Christ—even the flesh, tamed and subjugated, may be able to gain the merit of incorruption: but because in the little one the soul cannot yet do this, unless it has received the sacrament of Christ, through this grace there is acquired for his flesh what could not yet be acquired by his conduct; but if the soul of a little one goes out without that sacrament, the soul itself indeed will be in eternal life, from which no sin could separate it, but its flesh will not rise in Christ, that sacrament not having been received before death?

**8. 23.** This opinion I have never heard, never read. But plainly I have heard and believed, and on that account have also spoken: that *the hour comes when all who are in the tombs will hear his voice; and those who have done good will come forth to the resurrection of life*—that is the resurrection of which it is said, *and through one man the resurrection of the dead*; that is the one by which *in Christ all will be made alive*—*but those who have done evil, to the resurrection of judgment*[^12]. What, then, is here to be understood of those infants who, before they could do either good or evil, were stripped of the body without baptism? Nothing here was said of such. But if their flesh therefore will not rise, because they did neither anything good nor anything evil, then neither will that of those rise who, having received the grace of baptism, died at that age in which they could do nothing well or ill. But if those will rise among the saints, that is, among those who have done good—among whom shall those too rise, unless among those who have done evil—lest we should believe that some human souls will not receive their bodies, either to the resurrection of life, or to the resurrection of judgment? Which opinion, before it is refuted, displeases already by its very novelty: then who would bear it, if those who run to baptism with their little ones should believe that they run for the sake of their flesh, not for their souls? Blessed Cyprian indeed, founding no new decree, but keeping the most firm faith of the Church, to correct those who thought that a little one should not be baptized before the eighth day of its nativity, said that it was not the flesh, but the soul, that was not to be lost; and held, with certain of his fellow bishops, that one rightly born can be baptized as soon as born.

**8. 24.** But against some opinion of Cyprian, where perhaps he did not see what should have been seen, let each think what he pleases; only let no one think against the most manifest apostolic faith, which proclaims that from the offense of one all are led into condemnation: from which condemnation none frees but the grace of God through Jesus Christ our Lord, in whom alone all are made alive, whoever are made alive. Let no one think against the most well-founded custom of the Church, where, if it were run to baptism only for the sake of the bodies of little ones, the dead too would be offered to be baptized.

**8. 25.** Since these things are so, the cause must be sought and rendered why souls which are newly created for individuals being born are damned, if little ones die apart from the sacrament of Christ: for that they are damned, if they go out of the body thus, both holy Scripture and the holy Church are witness. Whence that opinion about the creation of new souls, if it does not assail this most well-founded faith, let it be mine too; if it assails it, let it not be yours either.

**8. 26.** I do not wish it said to me that for this opinion we must take what is written: *Who formed the spirit of man within him*[^13]; and, *Who formed their hearts individually*[^14]. Something most strong and most invincible must be sought, which does not compel us to believe God the damner of any souls without some fault. For either it is worth just as much, or perhaps it is more, to create than to form; and yet it is written: *Create in me a clean heart, O God*[^15]: nor on that account can it be thought that the soul in this place wishes itself to be made before it was something. As, therefore, while already existing, it is created by the renewal of righteousness, so while already existing, it is formed by the conformation of doctrine. Nor does that which is written in Ecclesiastes—*Then the dust will return to the earth as it was, and the spirit will return to the Lord who gave it*[^16]—confirm this opinion which we wish to be ours: for this rather supports those who think that all souls are from one. For as the dust, they say, returns to the earth, as it was; and yet the flesh, of which this was said, does not return to the man from whom it was propagated, but to the earth, from which the first man was made: so the spirit too, propagated from the spirit of that one, nevertheless does not return to him, but to the Lord by whom it was given to him. But because this testimony so sounds for them that it does not in every way seem to be contrary to this opinion which I wish to defend, I thought your prudence should only be admonished, lest by such testimonies you try to rescue me from these straits. For although no one brings it about, by wishing, that what is not true be true, yet, if it could be done, I would wish this opinion to be true; just as I wish that, if it is true, it be defended by you most clearly and most invincibly.

**9. 27.** But this difficulty follows also those who suppose that souls already existing elsewhere, and prepared from the beginning of the divine works, are sent by God into bodies. For of these too the same thing is asked: if the blameless souls come obediently to where they are sent, why are they punished in little ones, if, unbaptized, they have ended this life? The difficulty is exactly the same in both opinions. They seem to themselves to get out of this question more easily who assert that souls are entangled in individual bodies according to the merits of a prior life. For they think that this is to die in Adam: namely, to pay penalties in the flesh which was propagated from Adam: from which guilt, they say, the grace of Christ frees the little with the great. This indeed is rightly, truly, and most excellently said, that the grace of Christ frees from the guilt of sins the little with the great. But that souls sin in another, higher life, and are thence cast headlong into carnal prisons—I do not believe, I do not acquiesce, I do not consent. First, because these people say that this comes about through I know not what cycles, so that after I know not how many volumes of ages one must return again to this burden of corruptible flesh and to paying penalties: than which opinion I do not know what more horrible can be thought. Then, what just man, finally, has died, about whom we ought not (if these people speak true) to be anxious, lest, sinning in the bosom of Abraham, he be cast down into the flames of that rich man? For why could he not sin even after this body, if he could even before? Lastly, it is far one thing to have sinned in Adam, whence the Apostle says, *in whom all sinned*; and another to have sinned outside Adam, I know not where, and therefore to be thrust into Adam, that is, into the flesh propagated from Adam, as into a prison. But that opinion, that all souls are made from one, I do not wish even to discuss, unless it be necessary; and would that this one, of which we now treat, if it is true, be so defended by you that this be no longer necessary.

**9. 28.** But although I desire, ask, with ardent vows long for and await, that through you the Lord may take from me the ignorance of this matter: nevertheless, if—which God forbid—I shall by no means have deserved it, let me ask patience for myself from the Lord our God; in whom we so believe that, if he does not open some things to us even knocking, we ought in no way to murmur against him. Let us remember that it was first said to the Apostles themselves: *I have many things to say to you, but you cannot bear them now*[^17]. Among these, as far as concerns me, let me reckon even this: nor, since I know this, let me be indignant as unworthy, lest by this very thing I be convicted still more unworthy. For there are many other things I likewise do not know, which I cannot recall or enumerate: and I would tolerably be ignorant of this too, did I not fear lest some of these opinions, against that which we hold by a most firm faith, creep upon incautious minds. But before I know which of them is rather to be chosen, I profess that I think this not rashly: that the one which is true does not oppose the most robust and most well-founded faith, by which the Church of Christ believes that not even little human beings, very recently born, can be freed from damnation except through the grace of the name of Christ, which he commended in his sacraments.
