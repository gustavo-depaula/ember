# Letter 151

TO MY LORD, DESERVEDLY ILLUSTRIOUS AND, BY THE HONOR DUE FROM ME, MOST WORTHY SON CAECILIANUS, FROM AUGUSTINE, GREETINGS IN THE LORD.

**1.** The complaint about me put into your letter is the more welcome to me, the fuller it is of love. If, then, I should try to clear myself of what I kept silent about, what else shall I try than to show that you had no cause for which you ought to be vexed at me? But since I love this in you more—that you deigned to be offended at my silence, which I had believed had no weight at all among your cares—I will desert my own cause, if I contend to clear myself. For if you ought not to have been indignant that I did not write to you, you set no value on me, who bear it indifferently whether I speak or am silent. Further, because you took it ill that I was silent, that indignation is not indignation. I do not, then, so much grieve that I did not offer it, as I rejoice that you longed for our conversation. For that I, an old friend—and what should be kept silent by you, but acknowledged by us—deserved to be charged with having consoled with no address such and so great a man, placed among foreigners and laboring under public cares, is to me an honor, not a sorrow. Forgive, then, one who gives thanks that you did not think me unworthy to be vexed at when silent. For now, among so many and so great affairs—not yours, but public, that is, of all—I have believed that my letters can be not only not burdensome but even welcome to your benevolence, by which you surpass your eminence.

**2.** For when I had received a letter sent to me by the brothers from the holy and, for his outstanding merits, venerable pope Innocent, which by sure signs is declared to have been given to me through your Excellency, I had for this reason supposed that no page of yours was conveyed along with it, because you had not wished, occupied with weightier matters, to be bound by the care of writing and replying. For surely it seemed fitting that I, to whom you deigned to send the writings of a holy man, should receive them joined with yours. Hence, then, I had concluded that my letters were no burden to your mind, unless it were necessary for the sake of commending someone, to whom I could not deny this by the office of intercession; which is wont to be our custom of granting to all, and a certain profession, even importunate, yet not to be disapproved. And so I did: for I commended a friend of mine to your kindness, from whom I have now received his reply too, giving thanks, which I myself also give.

**3.** But if I thought any evil of you, especially in that cause which, though not expressed, your letter nevertheless suggested to me, far be it that I should write any such thing to you where I asked any benefit either for myself or for anyone. For either I would keep silent, watching for a time when I could have you present; or, if I thought it must be dealt with by letter, I would rather do that, and so do it that you could scarcely bear my grief. For therefore, after that man's impious and cruel treachery—against which, even through the solicitude you shared with us, we vehemently but in vain pressed, lest he strike our heart with that grief and butcher his own conscience with so great a crime—I at once set out from Carthage, my departure concealed, lest so many and so great men, who dreaded his sword within the church, should hold me back with violent weepings and groanings, thinking that my presence could profit them something, so that he whom I could not worthily enough reproach for his soul I should be compelled even to entreat for their bodies. Yet the walls of the church sufficiently protected that bodily safety of theirs. But I was constrained by hard straits, because neither would he endure me as he ought, and moreover I would be compelled to do what would not be fitting. I grieved also gravely for the lot of my venerable fellow bishop, the ruler of so great a Church, to whose office it was said to pertain, after the man's so nefarious deceit, still to show himself humble, that the rest might be spared. I confess: since I could bear so great an evil with no strength of breast, I departed.

**4.** This would be even now the cause of my silence toward you, as it was then of my departure, if I believed that you had acted with him so as to avenge such nefarious injuries. Those believe this who do not know how, and how often, and what things you said to us, when with anxious care we were urging that, the more familiarly you clung to him, the more frequently you came to him, the more often you conversed with him alone, the more he should care for your reputation; lest to those who were called your enemies he should give such an end as would make you be thought to have done nothing else with him. Which indeed neither I believe, nor my brothers who heard you in conversation, and by hearing and by every nod saw the signs of your kindly breast. But, I beseech you, forgive those who believe it: for they are men, and in the minds of men there are such hiding-places, and such recesses, that all the suspicious, when deservedly blamed, even think they ought to be praised, because they are cautious. The causes stood: we knew that from one of those whom he suddenly ordered to be seized you had suffered a most grievous injury. His brother too, in whom he most persecuted the Church, was said to have answered you somewhat harshly, as it were setting himself before you. Both were thought to be suspected by you. When, having been summoned, they had departed, you remaining there, and speaking with him more secretly, as was reported, suddenly they were ordered to be detained. Men spoke of your friendship not as recent, but as old. So great a conjunction confirmed the report, and so assiduous a conversation of one alone with one alone. His power was then great. The ease of calumny shone out. It was no great matter to procure someone to say what, salvation being promised, that man had charged should be said. All things at that time conspired so that, even by one witness, as by an odious and credible charge, anyone whatever might be taken out of the way without risk to him who ordered it.

**5.** We meanwhile, since there was a rumor that the ecclesiastical hand could snatch them away, were mocked with false promises; so that, he not only as it were willing but even pressing, a bishop was sent for them to the imperial court, this promise being interposed to episcopal ears, that until something should be done for them there, no examination of their cause would be stirred up. Finally, the day before they were struck down, your Excellency came to us; you gave hope, such as you had never before given, that he could grant them to you who were about to set out, since you had said to him gravely and prudently that the whole of what he so assiduously, familiarly, and secretly conversed with you about did not honor you so much as burden you, and amounted to this: that, the counsel of their death being shared and discussed between you, no one would doubt what would follow after all this. When you indicated to us that you had said these things—for in narrating, reaching toward that place where the sacraments of the faithful are celebrated, while we were astonished, you so swore that you had said these things—that not only then, but even now, after so horrendous an unforeseen end, when I recall all your nods, I seem to myself too shameless if I believe any evil of you. By which words of yours you said he was so moved that, as a familiar provision for your journey, he was going to grant you their safety.

**6.** Whence I testify to your Affection that, on the next day, on which the nefarious offspring of that travail was born, when it was suddenly announced to us that they had been led from prison to that judge, although we were perturbed, yet I, considering both what you had said to us the day before, and what day was about to dawn after that—since it was the day before the solemnity of blessed Cyprian—supposed that he had even chosen the day on which he would both grant your petitions, and, suddenly gladdening the whole Church of Christ, would wish to ascend to the place of so great a martyr, more sublime by the kindness of sparing than by the power of slaying; when behold, a messenger rushed upon us, by which we knew that they had been struck down sooner than we could even seek how they were being heard. For a place was provided, and a near one, not assigned to the punishments of men, but rather as an ornament of the city, where it is rightly believed that for this reason he had ordered certain men to be slaughtered some days before, lest in these there should be an odious novelty: men whom it was his plan could thus be stolen from the Church, if they were ordered not only to be struck down suddenly, but even to be struck down in a near place. And so he sufficiently showed that he did not fear to inflict torment on that mother whose intervention he feared: namely the holy Church, among whose faithful, baptized in her bosom, we surely knew him too. After this end, then, of so great an undertaking, when it had been dealt with us so diligently that even through you, though without your knowing it, we were made the day before almost secure and almost certain of their safety—who of such men, as the multitude of men is, would not hold it undoubted that, even by you, words were given to us, and their life taken from them? Therefore, as I said, good man, even though we do not believe this, forgive those who believe it.

**7.** But far be it from my heart, and from my life, such as it is, that I should intercede with you for anyone, or ask any benefit from you, if I believed you the author of so great an evil, and of so wicked a cruelty. But plainly I confess: if even hereafter you are in that familiarity in which you were before, with your leave let my grief be free; you compel us much to believe what we did not wish. But it is consistent that I should not believe this either, who do not believe those things of you. Your friend, by the unforeseen success of sudden power, persecuted no more their life than your reputation. Nor, in saying these things, do I, forgetful of my own mind and profession, inflame your hatreds against him; but I summon you to a more faithful love. For he who so deals with the evil that they repent of their wickedness knows how to take counsel even by indignation: for as the evil harm by flattering, so the good profit by opposing. For he struck his own soul, with the same iron with which he most insolently slew others, more grievously and more deeply: which, after this life, if he does not correct it by repenting, and has not used well the patience of God, he will be compelled to find and feel. But often the present life even of the good has, by the judgment of God, been permitted to be snatched away by the evil, lest to suffer such things should be thought an evil. For what can it harm those about to die, to die in the flesh? Or what do those who fear to die accomplish, except that they die a little later? Whatever harms the dying comes from life, not from death; in which, if they have had such souls as those to which Christian grace comes to aid, surely their death was not the setting of a good life, but the occasion of a better.

**8.** And the manners of the elder indeed appeared more friendly to this world than to Christ: although he too had, in no small part, corrected his earlier youthful and worldly life by taking a wife. Yet perhaps the merciful God did not will him to be his brother's companion in death except in pity. But that other lives religiously, and much in Christian heart and life. This report preceded him, so that he came in the cause of the Church; this followed when he had come. What uprightness in him in his manners, what faith in friendship, what zeal in learning, what sincerity in religion, what chastity in marriage, what continence in judgment; toward enemies patience, toward friends affability, toward the holy humility, toward all love; in bestowing benefits readiness, in asking them modesty, in things rightly done love, in sins grief! What grace of honesty, what splendor of favor, what care of piety; what mercy in aiding, what benevolence in forgiving, what confidence in praying! What he knew with profit, with what modesty he spoke! What he was ignorant of without profit, with what diligence he searched out! How great in him was the contempt of present things! How great the hope and desire of eternal goods! That he might not, all worldly actions left behind, take up the belt of the Christian warfare, the marriage bond hindered him, by which, already entangled, he had begun to desire better things, when it was no longer lawful to break those, though inferior, things.

**9.** One day his brother, when they were now held together in custody, said to him: If I suffer these things for the deserts of my sins, you, whose life we know to be so attentively and ardently Christian, by what evil deserts have you been brought to this? But he: Do you, he said, esteem it a small benefit conferred on me by God—if, however, this testimony of yours about my life is true—that what I suffer, even if I suffer it unto the shedding of blood, my sins may there be punished, and not be reserved for me unto the judgment to come? Here perhaps someone may believe that he was conscious to himself of certain hidden sins of unchastity. I will tell, then, what the Lord God willed me, to my great consolation, to hear from his mouth, and plainly to know. When, as human things are, anxious about this very thing, I was dealing alone with him alone, now placed in that same custody, lest there be anything for which he ought to please God by a greater and more notable repentance, he—as he was of singular modesty, since, although he blushed at my suspicion, false though it was, yet most gratefully received the admonition—modestly and gravely smiling, and grasping my right hand with both his hands, said: I testify, by the sacraments which are brought through this hand, that I have known no intercourse except with my wife, neither before nor after.

**10.** What evil, then, befell this man in death, and not rather very much good, since, having these gifts unto Christ—without whom they are had in vain—he departed from this life? These things I would not mention to you, if I believed that you were offended by his praises. But since I do not believe this, neither do I believe, surely, that he could be slain with you—I do not say pressing, but at least willing or wishing it. Accordingly, you judge with us, the more sincerely the more innocently, that he dealt more cruelly with his own soul than with that man's body; when, despising us, despising his own promises, despising your so many and so great petitions and admonitions, despising finally the Church of Christ (and in it whom, but Christ?), he arrived by this death at the end of his machination. Or is that man's honor to be compared even to this man's prison, when the one raged exalted, the other rejoiced shut up? With horrible and penal darkness the conscience of a wicked man surpasses not only all prisons, but even the lower world. And what did it harm you, whose reputation, though it grievously wounded, yet did not destroy your innocence? Although your reputation itself too is safe, both with those who knew you better than we, and with us ourselves, who beheld your cares with us, lest so monstrous a crime should be perpetrated, expressed with such great affection that we almost discerned with our eyes the invisible things of your heart. He harmed himself, then, in whatever he harmed; he transfixed his own soul, his own life, his own conscience; finally his very own reputation, which, good, even the worst are wont to desire, he laid waste with that blind cruelty. For he is so much the more hateful to all the good, the more he tried to please the impious, or rejoiced to have pleased them.

**11.** But where could it better appear that he did not have the necessity which he feigned himself to have—so that under its pretext, as a good man, he might do so great an evil—than that not even he was pleased, by whose command he dared to excuse himself? Let the holy deacon Quintianus report to your Excellency; for he himself had been joined to the bishop whom we had sent for them, how neither did pardon seem to be granted to them, lest even so they should be marked with some charge; but only a memorandum by which he was to be ordered to dismiss them free from all trouble. By a gratuitous cruelty, then, by no necessity (although there may perhaps have been other causes, which we suspect, and which there is no need to commit to a letter), he savagely afflicted the Church with grief. To the bosom of which Church his brother, fearing to perish, had fled, that he might find him alive, the counselor of so great a crime: of which Church he himself too, when he had offended his patron, sought the asylum, nor could it be denied him. If you love this man, detest him; if you do not wish him to be punished eternally, shudder at him. Thus your reputation, thus his life, must be taken counsel for: for he who loves in him what God hates not only hates him, but also himself.

**12.** Since this is so, I neither believe of your kindness that you were the author or sharer of so great a crime, nor that you deceived by malicious cruelty: which be far from your life and manners! Nor do I wish your friendships to be such that by them he too should glory in evil unto his own destruction, and human suspicion be confirmed; but rather such that by them he may be disposed to repentance—and such and so great a repentance as so horrendous wounds require for their medicine: for you will be the more his friend, the more you are the enemy of his crimes. It would be of interest to your Excellency to make known by reply where you were on that day on which that was committed; how you received it, what you did afterward, or, when you saw him, what you said to him, what you heard from him. For I, from the time I suddenly departed thence the next day, have been able to hear nothing about you that pertains to this matter.

**13.** But as for what I read in your letter, that you are now compelled to believe that you were as it were avoided by me, and that I was refused you at Carthage, you rather compel me by these words not to keep silent about the causes of my absence. Of which one is, that the labor which it is necessary to endure in that city—and which, if I wished to set forth, as much again would have to be said—I can now no longer sustain, since to my own weakness, which is known to all who know me more familiarly, there has been added also old age, which is the common weakness of the human race. The other cause is, that I have resolved, if the Lord will, to spend all the time given me from those occupations which the necessity of the Church, which I serve by my proper office, demands, upon the labor of studies pertaining to ecclesiastical sciences; in which I judge that, if it please the mercy of God, I shall profit even posterity in something.

**14.** But there is one thing, if you wish to hear the truth, which I bear most grievously in you: that, although you are now both of this age, and of this life and uprightness, you still wish to be a catechumen; as though the faithful could not, the more faithful and better they are, the more faithfully and better administer the commonwealth. For what good do you accomplish in these so great cares and labors of yours, except that it may be well with men? For if you do not accomplish this, it would be better to sleep nights and days, than to keep watch in public labors that will profit no advantage of men. Nor indeed do I doubt your Excellency.
