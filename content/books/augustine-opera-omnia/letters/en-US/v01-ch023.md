# Letter 23

To the most beloved lord and honorable brother Maximinus, Augustine the presbyter of the catholic Church sends greeting in the Lord

**1.** Before I come to the matter about which I wished to write to your Benevolence, I will briefly give the reason for the heading of this letter, lest it move either you or anyone else. I have written *lord*, because it is written: *You were called to freedom, brothers; only do not use your freedom as an opportunity for the flesh, but through love be servants of one another*[^1]. Since, then, by this very service of a letter I serve you through love, not absurdly do I call you lord, on account of the one and true Lord of ours who commanded us these things. But as for my having written *most beloved*, God knows that I not only love you, but love you as myself; since I am well conscious to myself that I wish for you the good things I wish for myself. And as for my having added *honorable*, I did not add it in order to honor your episcopate; for to me you are not a bishop—and do not take this said as an insult—but from the disposition which ought to be in our mouth: *Yes, yes; No, no*[^2]. For you are not unaware, nor is any man who knows us unaware, that you are not my bishop, nor I your presbyter. I therefore gladly call you honorable from that rule by which I know you to be a man, and know that man was made to the image and likeness of God[^3], and is set in honor by the very order and right of nature, if indeed, by understanding the things that are to be understood, he preserves his honor. For so it is written: *Man, set in honor, did not understand; he was compared to the senseless beasts, and was made like to them*[^4]. Why, then, should I not call you honorable insofar as you are a man, especially since I dare not despair of your salvation and correction so long as you are in this life? But that I should call you *brother*, you are not unaware that it has been divinely commanded us to say, even to those who deny that they are our brothers: You are our brothers; and this has great force for the cause for which I wished to write to your Fraternity. For now, having given the reason why I made such a doorway for the letter, hear most calmly what follows.

**2.** When in this region I was detesting, in whatever words I could, the custom of men—a custom to be mourned and bewailed—who, though they glory in the Christian name, do not hesitate to rebaptize Christians, there were no lack of those who praised you and told me that you do not do these things. I confess, at first I did not believe it. Then, considering that it could come about that the fear of God should seize a human soul thinking about the life to come, so that it would restrain itself from a most open crime, I rejoiced and believed that with such a purpose you were unwilling to be too estranged from the catholic Church. I was indeed seeking an occasion of speaking with you, so that, if it could be done, the small dissension that had remained between us might be removed; when behold, a few days ago it was reported that you had rebaptized our deacon of Mutugenna. I grieved vehemently, both at his pitiable fall and at your unexpected crime, brother. For I know what the catholic Church is. The nations are the inheritance of Christ, and the ends of the earth are Christ's possession[^5]. You too know this, or if you do not know it, take note; it can most easily be known by those who are willing. To rebaptize, then, a heretic who has received these signs of holiness which Christian teaching has handed down is altogether a sin; but to rebaptize a catholic is a most monstrous crime. Yet not quite believing it, because I held a good opinion of you, I went to Mutugenna myself; and the wretched man I could not see, but from his parents I heard that he had now even been made your deacon. And yet I still feel so well about your heart that I do not believe him to have been rebaptized.

**3.** Therefore I beseech you, most beloved brother, by the divinity and humanity of our Lord Jesus Christ, that you deign to write back to me what was done, and to write back in such a way as knowing that I wish to read your letter to our brothers in the Church. I have written this so that, when I afterward do what you would not expect me to do, I might not offend your Charity and lay before our common friends a just complaint about me. What, then, should hinder you from writing back, I do not see. For if you do rebaptize, there is nothing about the men of your college for you to fear, since you will have written back that you do what they would bid you do even if you were unwilling. And when you have defended, with as many documents as you could, that it ought to be done, they will not only not be angry but will even praise you. But if you do not rebaptize, seize Christian freedom, brother Maximinus, seize it, I beg you; in the contemplation of Christ, fear no man's reproach and dread no man's power. The honor of this world passes away, ambition passes away. At the future judgment of Christ, neither tiered apses, nor veiled chairs, nor throngs of nuns running to meet and singing will be brought forward for the defense, when consciences begin to accuse and the arbiter of consciences to judge. The things that here honor us, there burden us; the things that here relieve us, there weigh us down. These things which are displayed for our honor for the time being, on account of the benefit of the Church, will perhaps be defended by a good conscience; but they will not be able to defend a bad one.

**4.** Therefore, what you do with so pious and so religious a mind—if indeed you do it—namely, that you do not repeat the baptism of the catholic Church, but rather approve it as that of the one most true mother, who offers her bosom to all nations to be reborn and pours her breasts upon the reborn, as the one possession of Christ, stretching itself to the ends of the earth[^6]; if you truly do this, why do you not burst forth into an exulting and free voice? Why do you press the so useful splendor of your lamp under a bushel[^7]? Why do you not, having torn off and cast away the old skins of timid servitude, rather clothe yourself in Christian confidence, go forth, and say: "I know one baptism, consecrated and sealed in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit; where I find this form, I must approve it; I do not destroy what I recognize as the Lord's, I do not blow contempt upon the standard of my king"? Even those who divided Christ's garment did not violate it[^8]; and they had not yet believed that Christ would rise, but saw him dying. If the garment of him hanging on the cross was not torn by the persecutors, why is the sacrament of him sitting in heaven destroyed by Christians? If in the times of the old people I were a Jew, when I could be nothing better, I would surely have received circumcision. This seal of the righteousness of faith[^9] had so great a force at that time, before it was made void by the Lord's coming, that an angel would have strangled the infant son of Moses, had not the mother, seizing a flint, circumcised the boy and by this sacrament driven off the impending destruction[^10]. This sacrament even held back the river Jordan and led it back to its source[^11]. This sacrament the Lord himself, although by being crucified he made it void, nevertheless received by being born. For those seals were not condemned, but gave way as more fitting ones succeeded. For just as the first coming of the Lord took away circumcision, so the second coming will take away baptism. For just as now, after the freedom of faith has come and the yoke of servitude has been removed, no Christian is circumcised in the flesh; so then, when the just reign with the Lord and the impious are condemned, no one will be baptized, but that which these things prefigure—that is, the circumcision of the heart and the cleanness of conscience—will remain forever. If, then, at that time I were a Jew, and a Samaritan came to me and wished, abandoning that error which the Lord too disapproved when he said, *You worship what you do not know; we worship what we know, for salvation is from the Jews*[^12]—if, then, the Samaritan, whom the Samaritans had circumcised, wished to become a Jew, the audacity of repetition would surely be idle, and we would be compelled not to repeat but to approve that which had been done among the heresy, which God had commanded. But if, in the flesh of a circumcised man, I should find no place where I might repeat the circumcision, because that member is one; far less is a place found in one heart where the baptism of Christ may be repeated. Therefore you who wish to double baptism must altogether seek out double hearts.

**5.** Cry out, then, that you do rightly, if you do not rebaptize; and write back to me about it not only without trembling, but even with joy. Let none of your councils terrify you, brother. For if this displeases them, they are not worthy to have you; but if it pleases them, we trust in the mercy of the Lord, who never deserts those who fear to displease him and strive to please him, that peace will soon be between you and us: lest, on account of our honors—about which burden a dangerous account is rendered—the wretched peoples who believe in Christ have common food in their houses, and yet cannot have the table of Christ in common. Do we not groan that husband and wife, in order to join their bodies faithfully, often swear to one another by Christ, and yet tear apart that very body of Christ by a divided communion? If, through your modesty and prudence, and through the love which we owe to him who poured out his blood for us, so great a scandal, so great a triumph of the devil, so great a ruin of souls be taken out of the way in these regions, who could explain in words what a palm the Lord prepares for you—that from you there should proceed so imitable an example of medicine for healing the other members which, decayed, lie pitiably throughout all Africa? How I fear, since you cannot see my heart, that I may seem to you to speak with insult rather than with love! But surely I find nothing more to do than to offer my discourse to you for inspection, and my mind to God.

**6.** Let us take out of the way the empty objections which the unskilled parties are accustomed to hurl against one another; do not you object the times of Macarius, nor I the savagery of the Circumcellions: if this does not pertain to you, neither does that to me. The Lord's threshing floor has not yet been winnowed; it cannot be without chaff. Let us pray, and act as much as we can, that we may be wheat. About our rebaptized deacon I cannot be silent: for I know how dangerous silence is for me. For I do not intend to pass my time in ecclesiastical honors as in windy seasons, but I intend to render an account to the prince of all shepherds about the sheep committed to me. If perhaps you would not wish me to write these things to you, you must, brother, pardon my fear. For I greatly fear that, while I am silent and dissemble, others too may be rebaptized by you. I have therefore resolved, as far as the Lord deigns to grant strength and means, so to conduct this cause that, by our peaceful conferences, all who are in communion with us may know how far the catholic Church differs from heresies or schisms, and how greatly the ruin of tares, or of branches cut off from the Lord's vine, is to be guarded against. If you undertake this conference with me with a willing mind, so that, with us in concord, the letters of us both may be read to the peoples, I shall exult with ineffable joy. But if you do not accept this with an even mind, what shall I do, brother, except read our letters even against your will to the catholic people, that it may be the better instructed? But if you do not deign to write back, I have resolved to read at least my own, so that, your diffidence being known, men may at least blush to be rebaptized.

**7.** And I will not do this while the soldier is present, lest any of you suppose that I wished to act more tumultuously than the reasoning of peace requires; but after the soldier's departure, so that all who hear us may understand that it is not my purpose that men be compelled unwilling to anyone's communion, but that the truth may become known to those who seek it most quietly. The terror of temporal powers will cease on our side: let the terror of the assembled Circumcellions also cease on your side. Let us deal with the matter, let us deal by reason, let us deal by the authorities of the divine Scriptures, let us, quiet and tranquil as far as we can, ask, seek, knock[^13], that we may receive and find, and it may be opened to us; that perhaps it may come about that, the Lord aiding our concordant efforts and prayers, so great a deformity and impiety of the African regions may begin to be abolished from our lands. If you do not believe that I wish to act after the departure of the soldiers, write back after the departure of the soldiers. For if I should wish to read my letter to the people while the soldier is present, my letter, when produced, will show me to be a violator of good faith. May the mercy of the Lord turn this away from my character and from my purpose, which he has deigned to inspire in me through his yoke.

**8.** My bishop would perhaps rather have sent a letter to your Benevolence, had he been present, or I would have written at his bidding or with his permission. But in his absence, since the deacon's rebaptism is recent, or is said to be, I did not allow the action itself to grow cold by delay, stirred by the sharpest stings of grief over a brotherly and true death. This grief of mine, with the help of the Lord's mercy and providence, the compensation of peace will perhaps soothe. May God and our Lord deign to inspire in you a peaceful mind, most beloved lord and brother.
