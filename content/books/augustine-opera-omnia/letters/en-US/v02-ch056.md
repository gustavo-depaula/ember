# Letter 155

AUGUSTINE, BISHOP, SERVANT OF CHRIST AND OF HIS FAMILY, TO HIS BELOVED SON MACEDONIUS, GREETINGS IN THE LORD.

**1. 1.** Although I do not recognize in myself the wisdom you attribute to me, yet for your so great and so sincere benevolence toward me I give, and owe, very great thanks; and I am delighted that the labors of my studies have pleased so great a man: but much more, that I recognize your mind panting after the love of eternity and truth, and the affection of that very love after that divine and heavenly commonwealth, of which Christ is the ruler, and in which alone one must live always and blessedly, if here one lives rightly and devoutly; I see it drawing near, and I embrace it burning to possess that commonwealth. For from there flows even true friendship, not to be weighed by temporal advantages, but to be valued by gratuitous love. For no one can be truly a friend of a man unless he is first a friend of truth itself: which, if it is not done freely, can be done by no means.

**1. 2.** Concerning this matter the philosophers too have said many things; but among them true piety, that is, the true worship of the true God, from which all the duties of living rightly ought to be drawn, is not found: for no other reason, as far as I understand, except that they wished in a certain way to fashion the blessed life for themselves, and thought it rather to be achieved than to be obtained; whereas the giver of it is none but God. For he does not make a man blessed except he who made man. For he who bestows on the good and evil so great goods of his creature—that they exist, that they are men, that they are vigorous in senses, strong in powers, abounding in resources—will give himself to the good, that they may be blessed, since it is also his gift that they be good. But those who, in this toilsome life, in these dying members, under this burden of corruptible flesh, wished to be the authors and as it were founders of their own blessed life, seeking it as if by their own virtues, and already retaining it, not seeking and hoping it from that fountain of virtues, could in no way feel God resisting their pride. Whence they fell into a most absurd error, so that, while they assert that the wise man is blessed even in the bull of Phalaris, they are compelled to confess that the blessed life is sometimes to be fled. For, exaggerating the evils of the body, they yield, and in their gravest troubles decide that one must depart from this life. Here I will not say how great a wickedness it is for a man to kill himself, innocent, when he ought by no means to kill even a guilty man; whence in the first book of those three which you most kindly and most studiously read through, we have already said many things. But surely let it be considered, and judged not proudly but soberly, how that is a blessed life which the wise man does not retain and enjoy, but is compelled to be without by laying hands on himself.

**1. 3.** There is in Cicero, as you know, in the last part of the fifth book of the Tusculans, a passage to be noted on account of what I am saying. For when he was treating of the blindness of the body, and affirming that the wise man can be blessed even when blind, he said many things which he would rejoice to perceive by the ears. And likewise, if he were deaf, he transferred to the eyes the things in which he would take delight. But if he were deprived of both senses, and became blind and deaf, he did not dare to pass sentence here and call him blessed. But he added also the gravest pains of the body, which, if they do not destroy him, he will destroy himself, and, freed by this "virtue," arrive at the harbor of not feeling. The wise man, then, yields to the most monstrous disasters and succumbs, so far that he is compelled by them even to perpetrate homicide upon himself. Whom now shall he spare, that he may be without those evils, who does not spare himself? Surely he is always blessed; surely the blessed life, set in his own power, he can lose by no force of any calamity. Behold, in blindness and deafness and the most atrocious torments of the body, he either lost the blessed life; or, if even in these afflictions it is still blessed, it comes about, through the disputations of such most learned men, that there is sometimes a blessed life which the wise man cannot bear; or, what is more absurd, which the wise man ought not to bear, and which he flees, breaks off, casts away, and withdraws himself from even by the sword, or by poison, or by some other voluntary death; so that in the harbor of not feeling there is either nothing at all, which the Epicureans, and any others of like folly, supposed; or he is therefore blessed because he has been freed from that blessed life as from some plague. O too proud boasting! If the blessed life is in the torments of the body, why does the wise man not remain in it to enjoy it? But if it is wretched, what, I beseech you, but pride hinders him from confessing it, from praying to God, from supplicating the just and merciful one, who is able both to avert or mitigate the evils of this life, or to arm with fortitude for bearing them, or to free from them in every way, and after these to give the truly blessed life, where no evil is admitted, and where the supreme good is never lost?

**1. 4.** This is the reward of the devout, in the hope of attaining which we lead this temporal and mortal life not so much delightfully as tolerably, and then bear its evils bravely, by good counsel and divine gift, when we rejoice in the faithful promise of God of eternal goods and in our own faithful expectation. Exhorting us to this, the apostle Paul says: *Rejoicing in hope, patient in tribulation*[^1]: for he shows why we are patient in tribulation, by premising, "rejoicing in hope." To this hope I exhort you through Jesus Christ our Lord. For this God himself, the master—the majesty of his divinity lying hidden, and the weakness of his flesh appearing—not only taught by the oracle of his word, but also strengthened by the example of his passion and resurrection. For in the one he showed what sort of things we should bear, in the other what sort we should hope for. Whose grace those men too would merit, were they not, lifted up and puffed up with pride, vainly trying to make this blessed life for themselves, which God alone has truly promised to bestow on his worshippers after this life. For sounder is that view of the same Cicero, where he says: For this life indeed is death, which I could lament, if it pleased me. How, then, if it is rightly lamented, is it proved blessed; and not rather, since it is rightly lamented, convicted of being wretched? Wherefore accustom yourself, I beseech you, good man, to be blessed meanwhile in hope, that you may be so also in fact, when to most persevering piety shall be requited the reward of eternal felicity.

**2. 5.** If by a long letter I am burdensome, you yourself brewed this for yourself, who called me wise. For from this I dared to speak these things to you, that I might display to you wisdom—not such as I have, but show such as it ought to be. But this, in the present age, is the true worship of the true God, that in the age to come there may be of it a sure and entire fruit. Here most constant piety, there everlasting felicity. Of this wisdom, which alone is true, if I have anything, I took it from God, I did not presume it of myself; and I faithfully hope it to be perfected in me by him, by whom I humbly rejoice it to have been begun; neither, in that which he has not yet granted, unbelieving, nor, in that which he has already granted, ungrateful. For not by my own talent or merit, but by his gift am I, if I am anything praiseworthy. For certain most acute and most excellent talents went into the greater errors, the more presumptuously they ran as if by their own powers, and did not suppliantly and truly ask God to show them the way. But what are the merits of any men whatever, since he who came, not with due wage, but with gratuitous grace, alone, free from sins and the liberator from sins, found all sinners?

**2. 6.** If, then, true virtue delights us, let us say to him what we read in his sacred Scriptures: *I will love you, O Lord, my virtue*[^2]: and if we wish to be truly blessed (which we cannot not wish), let us hold with faithful heart what we have learned in those same Scriptures: *Blessed is the man whose hope is the name of the Lord, and who has not regarded vanities and lying follies*[^3]. But how great a vanity, how great a madness, and how great a lie it is, for a mortal man, leading a toilsome life with changeable spirit and flesh, laden with so many sins, subject to so many temptations, liable to so many corruptions, and destined to most just punishments, to trust in himself that he may be blessed—when not even that which he has as the chief thing in the dignity of his nature, that is, mind and reason, can he vindicate from errors, unless God, the light of minds, be present! Let us cast away, then, I beseech you, the vanities and lying follies of the false philosophers: because neither will there be virtue for us, unless he himself be present by whom we are aided; nor blessedness, unless he himself be present whom we may enjoy, and may absorb all our changeable and corruptible nature—which through itself is feeble and a certain material of miseries—by the gift of immortality and incorruption.

**2. 7.** But since we know you to be a lover of the commonwealth, see how clear it is in those sacred Scriptures that a man is not blessed from one source, a city from another. For a certain one in them, full of the Holy Spirit, speaks these things, praying: *Deliver me from the hand of the sons of strangers, whose mouth has spoken vanity, and their right hand is the right hand of iniquity. Whose sons are like new plants established in their youth. Their daughters arrayed and adorned like the likeness of a temple. Their storehouses full, overflowing from this into this. Their sheep fruitful, multiplying in their goings forth; their oxen fat. There is no breach of wall, nor passage, nor outcry in their streets. They have called the people blessed that has these things: blessed is the people whose God is the Lord himself*[^4].

**2. 8.** You see that, by the exaggeration of earthly felicity, the people is not called blessed except by the sons of strangers, that is, those not pertaining to the regeneration by which we are made sons of God: from whose hand this man prays to be delivered, lest by them he be dragged into this opinion and into impious sins. For by speaking vanity, they called the people blessed that has those things which he mentioned above, in which consists that felicity which the lovers of this world alone seek. And therefore their right hand is the right hand of iniquity, because they set before what should have been set after, just as the right hand is set before the left. For if they are had, the blessed life is not to be placed in them: they ought to be subject, not preeminent; following, not leading. But to this man who prayed thus, and desired to be delivered and distinguished from the sons of strangers who called the people blessed that has these things, as though we should say, You yourself, what do you think? what people do you call blessed?—he did not say: Blessed is the people whose is the virtue of its mind. For if he had said this, he would indeed have distinguished this people too from that which places the blessed life in this visible and bodily felicity; but he would not yet have transcended all vanities and lying follies: for *cursed*, as the same Scriptures teach elsewhere, *is everyone who places his hope in man*[^5]; therefore neither ought anyone to do so in himself, because he too is a man. Accordingly, that he might pass beyond the bounds of the lie of all vanities and follies, and there place the blessed life where it truly is, *Blessed*, he said, *is the people whose God is the Lord himself*[^6].

**3. 9.** You discern, then, whence must be sought what all, learned and unlearned, desire, and which many, by erring and being proud, are ignorant whence it is sought and where it is received. But both are reproached together in a certain divine psalm, both those who trust in their own virtue, and those who glory in the abundance of their riches[^7]; that is, both the philosophers of this age, and those who abhor such philosophy and call the people blessed to whom earthly opulence is at hand. And through this, from the Lord our God, by whom we were made, let us seek both the virtue by which we may overcome the evils of this life, and the blessed life by which, after this life, we may fully enjoy in his eternity; so that both in virtue and in the reward of virtue, as the Apostle says, *he who glories may glory in the Lord*[^8]. This let us wish for ourselves, this for the city of which we are citizens: for a city is not blessed from one source, a man from another; since a city is nothing other than a harmonious multitude of men.

**3. 10.** And so, if all your prudence, by which you try to take counsel for human affairs; if all your fortitude, by which you are terrified by the iniquity of no adversary; if all your temperance, by which, amid so great a ruin of the most wicked custom of men, you abstain from corruptions; if all your justice, by which, judging rightly, you distribute to each his own—labors and strives for this, that those for whom you wish it to be well may be safe in body, and, secure and at peace from all wickedness of anyone, may have sons like new plants established, daughters adorned like the likeness of a temple, storehouses full overflowing from this into this, fruitful sheep, fat oxen, and that no breach of wall deform the estate, nor the outcry of litigants resound in their streets—then your virtues will not be true, just as neither is the blessedness of those people. For here, to say the truth, that modesty of mine which in your letter you praised with kindly speech ought not to hinder me. If, I say, any administration of yours, equipped with the virtues I have mentioned, is determined by this end of intention, that men according to the flesh may suffer no unjust troubles; and you do not think it pertains to you to what they refer this quiet which you strive to afford—that is (not to be roundabout in words) how they may worship the true God, where is all the fruit of a quiet life—so great a labor profits you nothing for the truly blessed life.

**3. 11.** I seem to say this rather immodestly, and as it were forgetful of the custom of my intercessions. But if modesty is nothing other than a certain fear of displeasing, in this cause, by fearing, I am not modest. For I fear lest, first to God, then to that very friendship which you deigned to enter with me, I justly displease, if I should be less free in admonishing what I judge most healthful to admonish you. Let me be more modest when I intercede with you for others; but when for yourself, the freer the more friendly; because the more friendly the more faithful: although these very things I would not say, were I not acting more modestly. Which, if it is "the greatest efficacy in difficult matters among the good," as you yourself wrote, let it aid me, for you with you, that I may enjoy you in him who afforded me this door and confidence toward you: especially since what I suggest I judge to be now easy for your mind, supported and equipped by so many divine gifts.

**3. 12.** For if the virtues which you have received—perceiving from whom you received them, and giving thanks to him—you confer upon his worship even in these worldly honors of yours, and rouse and lead the men subject to your power to worship him by the example of your religious life, and by the very zeal of taking counsel, whether by fostering or by terrifying, and wish nothing else in this, that through you they live more securely, than that hence they may merit him with whom they will live blessedly: then those virtues will both be true, and, by the aid of him by whose bounty they were granted, will so grow and be perfected that they will lead you, without any doubt, to the truly blessed life, which is none but eternal: where now neither are evils prudently distinguished from goods, which will not be; nor are adversities bravely borne, because there will be nothing but what we love, not also what we bear; nor is lust temperately bridled, where we shall feel no incitements of it; nor is help justly given to the needy, where we shall have no one poor and needy. There will be one virtue there, and the same will be virtue and the reward of virtue, which the man who loves this says in the sacred utterances: *But for me it is good to cleave to God*[^9]. This there will be the full and everlasting wisdom, and the same truly the life now blessed: for it is the arrival at the eternal and supreme good, to cleave to which forever is the end of our good. Let this be called also prudence, because most foresightedly it will cleave to the good which is not lost; and fortitude, because most firmly it will cleave to the good from which it is not torn away; and temperance, because most chastely it will cleave to the good where it is not corrupted; and justice, because most rightly it will cleave to the good to which it is deservedly subject.

**4. 13.** Although even in this life virtue is nothing other than to love what ought to be loved: to choose this is prudence; to be turned away from it by no troubles is fortitude; by no enticements, temperance; by no pride, justice. But what should we choose to love above all, except that than which we find nothing better? This is God, to whom if, by loving, we either prefer or equal anything, we know not how to love ourselves. For it is so much the better for us, the more we go to him than whom nothing is better. But we go not by walking, but by loving. Whom we shall have the more present, the purer we are able to have that same love by which we tend toward him: for he is neither extended nor enclosed by bodily places. To him, then, who is everywhere present and everywhere whole, it is permitted to go, not by feet, but by morals. But our morals are wont to be judged, not from what each one knows, but from what he loves: nor do good or evil morals make us, except good or evil loves. By our crookedness, then, we are far from the rightness of God: whence, by loving the right, we are corrected, that by the right we may be able to cleave to the right.

**4. 14.** To him, then, with as great effort as we can, let us also act that they may arrive whom we love as ourselves, if we now know how to love ourselves by loving him. For Christ, that is, the Truth, says that on these two precepts the whole Law and the Prophets hang: that we love God with the whole heart, with the whole soul, with the whole mind, and love our neighbors as ourselves[^10]. The neighbor here, indeed, is to be weighed not by the nearness of blood, but by the fellowship of reason, in which all men are partners. For if the reasoning of money makes partners, how much more the reasoning of nature, common by the law not of trading but of being born! Hence that comic poet too (for, as to brilliant talents the splendor of truth is not lacking), when he composed the saying spoken by one old man to another:  
*Have you so much leisure from your own affairs  
that you concern yourself with others' business, which has nothing to do with you?*  
he made the other reply:  
I am a man; I count nothing human alien to me.  
To which sentence they relate that even whole theaters, full of the foolish and unlearned, applauded. For so naturally does the fellowship of human minds touch the feeling of all, that no man there but felt himself the neighbor of any man whatever.

**4. 15.** Since, then, with that love which the divine law commands, a man ought to love God, and himself, and his neighbor; yet not on this account were three precepts given, nor was it said, On these three, but: *On these two precepts hang the whole Law and the Prophets*[^11], that is, on the love of God with the whole heart, with the whole soul, and with the whole mind, and of the neighbor as oneself: namely, that it might be understood that there is no other love by which each one loves himself, except that he loves God. For he who loves himself otherwise is rather to be said to hate himself: for he becomes unjust, and is deprived of the light of righteousness, when, turned away from the higher and more excellent good, and thence turned either to himself, he is surely turned to lower and needy things; and there comes to be in him what is most truly written: *But he who loves iniquity hates his own soul*[^12]. Since, then, no one loves himself except by loving God, there was no need that, the precept being given about the love of God, a man should also be commanded to love himself, since in that he loves himself, in which he loves God. He ought, then, to love his neighbor also as himself, so that whatever man he can, he may lead, whether by the consolation of beneficence, or by the information of teaching, or by the coercion of discipline, to worship God; knowing that on these two precepts hang the whole Law and the Prophets.

**4. 16.** He who chooses this with sober discernment is prudent; he who is turned away from it by no affliction is brave; he who by no other delight, is temperate; he who by no elation, is just. By these virtues divinely imparted—through the grace of the mediator of God with the Father, and of the man Jesus Christ with us, through whom, after the enmities of iniquity, we are reconciled to God in the Spirit of love—by these virtues, I say, divinely imparted, both a good life is now led, and afterward its reward, which can be none but eternal, the blessed life, is paid in full. For here these same virtues are in act, there in effect; here in work, there in wage; here in office, there in the end. And so all the good and holy, even in any torments whatever, supported by divine help, are called by the hope of that blessed end, by which end they will be blessed: for if they were always in those same torments and most atrocious pains, no sound reason would doubt that, with whatever virtues, they were wretched.

**4. 17.** Piety, then, that is, the true worship of the true God, profits unto all things; both that which averts or eases the troubles of this life, and that which leads to that life and salvation where we shall now suffer nothing of evil, and shall fully enjoy goods supreme and everlasting. To attain this more perfectly and retain it most perseveringly I exhort you as myself: of which, were you not already a partaker, and did you not judge that these temporal honors of yours ought to serve it, you would not say to the Donatist heretics, in order to reduce them to the unity and peace of Christ, by an edict: "For your sake this is done; for your sake priests of uncorrupted faith, for your sake the Emperor Augustus, for your sake we too, his judges, labor"; and many other things which you so set down in that same edict, that it appears that you, in the belt of an earthly judge, in no small part have the heavenly commonwealth in mind. Wherefore, if I have wished to speak with you at greater length about true virtues and the truly blessed life, I ask not to be reckoned burdensome to your occupations: rather indeed I trust that I am not, since you bear so great a mind, and one wonderfully to be praised, that you neither desert those cares, and are the more gladly and familiarly occupied with these.
