# Letter 13

Augustine to Nebridius

**1.** To write to you of the usual things is not pleasing; of new things is not permitted. For the one I see does not suit you, the other I have no leisure for. For since I left you, no opportunity, no leisure has been given me for stirring up and turning over those things we are accustomed to inquire into between us. The winter nights are indeed exceedingly long, nor are they wholly slept away by me; but when there is leisure, things present themselves to be thought about which are necessary for breaking up that very leisure. What, then, am I to do? Am I to be mute with you, or silent? You want neither, I will neither. So come, and receive what the last part of the night could hammer out concerning me, for as long as it ran on, the time in which this letter was written.

**2.** You must remember what has often been bandied about between us in conversation, and what set us panting and feverish—namely, the question of the soul's either having some perpetual body or quasi-body, which you recall is even called a vehicle by some. As for this matter, it is clear that, if it is moved through place, it is not intelligible. Now whatever is not intelligible cannot be understood. But what escapes the understanding, if at least it does not escape the senses, is not altogether forbidden to be estimated thereby with some verisimilitude. But what can neither be understood nor sensed begets an opinion too rash and trifling; and this thing we are dealing with is of that kind, if indeed it exists at all. Why, then, I ask you, do we not declare a holiday for this little question, and lift our whole selves, having prayed to God, into the supreme serenity of the supreme living nature?

**3.** Here perhaps you will say that, although bodies cannot be perceived, we can nevertheless perceive intelligibly many things pertaining to body—as is the fact that we know there is body. For who would deny this, or who would confess this to be verisimilar rather than true? Thus, while body itself is verisimilar, it is nevertheless most true that there is some such thing in nature: therefore body is judged to be sensible, but that there is body is judged intelligibly; for it could not otherwise be perceived. So that body of some sort, about which we are inquiring—on which the soul is thought to lean in order to pass from place to place—although it would be sensible, if not to our senses, yet to certain far more vigorous ones, whether it nevertheless exists can be known intelligibly.

**4.** If you say this, let it come to mind that what we call understanding takes place in us in two ways: either by the mind and reason themselves, inwardly, of themselves—as when we understand that the understanding itself exists; or by a prompting from the senses—as that which has just been mentioned, when we understand that body exists. Of these two kinds, we understand the first through ourselves, that is, by consulting God concerning what is with us; the second, concerning what is reported by the body and the sense, none the less by consulting God. And if this holds, no one can understand whether that body exists unless his sense has reported something about it to him. And if among the class of living beings there is any such—since we perceive that we are not of that class—I think that point too is established which I had begun to make above: that this question does not pertain to us. I would have you think these things over again and again, and take care that I may know what you beget by thinking.
