# Letter 167

*A Book on the Opinion of James*

Augustine to Jerome

**1. 1.** What I wrote to you, brother Jerome, to be honored by me in Christ, asking about the human soul—if new ones are made individually even now for individuals being born, where they contract the bond of sin which we do not doubt must be loosed through the sacrament of the grace of Christ even in infants recently born—since it proceeded into no small volume, I was unwilling to burden with any other question: but what presses more sharply is much less to be neglected. Accordingly I ask, and through God I beseech, that you set forth for me—which I judge will profit many—or, if you already have it set forth, whether by you or by some other, that you direct it to us: how is that to be taken which is written in the letter of the apostle James, *For whoever keeps the whole law, but offends in one point, has become guilty of all*[^1]? Which matter is so great and of such moment that I much repent of not having written to you about it long ago.

**1. 2.** For this question turns on the present life to be lived—how we may come to eternal life—not on the past to be searched out, which oblivion has utterly sunk, such as is that which I thought should be asked about the soul. But it is narrated that something was elegantly said which fits this matter quite aptly. When a certain man had fallen into a well, where there was so much water that it rather received him so that he should not die, than choked him so that he should not speak, another came up, and seeing him, wondering, said: How did you fall in here? But he said: I beg you, think how you may free me from here; do not ask how I fell in here. So, since we confess, and hold by the catholic faith, that even the soul of a little infant is to be freed by the grace of Christ from the guilt of sin, as from a well, it is enough for it that we know the manner in which it is made safe, even if we never know how it came into that evil. But I thought it should be asked for this reason: lest perhaps from those opinions about the incarnation of the soul we hold some too incautiously, which would altogether contradict the freeing of the little one's soul, denying that it is in that evil. This, therefore, being most firmly held—that the soul of a little one is to be freed from the guilt of sin, and to be freed in no other way except by the grace of God through Jesus Christ our Lord—if we can also know the cause and origin of the evil itself, we more readily and more instructedly resist empty talkers, who are not disputants but wranglers; but if we cannot, the duty of mercy ought not on that account to grow sluggish, just because the beginning of the misery lies hidden. But against those who seem to themselves to know what they do not know, we are the safer for this, that we do not ignore our ignorance. For it is one thing which it is evil not to know; another which either cannot be known, or there is no need to know, or is indifferent to the life we seek: but this which I now inquire about, from the letter of the apostle James, turns on this very life in which we live, and in which we strive to please God so that we may live forever.

**1. 3.** How, then, is it to be understood, I beseech you: *Whoever keeps the whole law, but offends in one point, has become guilty of all*[^2]? Is it that one who has committed theft—nay rather, who has said to the rich man, *Sit here*, but to the poor, *Stand there*[^3]—is guilty of homicide, and of adultery, and of sacrilege? And if he is not, how is one who offends in one point become guilty of all? Or does what he said about the rich and the poor not pertain to those things of which, if anyone offend in one, he becomes guilty of all? But we must recall whence that sentence came, and what prior things gave birth to it, and connected to which it depends. *My brothers*, he says, *do not hold the faith of our Lord Jesus Christ of glory with respect of persons. For if there enters your assembly a man with a gold ring in fine clothing, and there enters also a poor man in mean attire, and you pay attention to the one clothed in splendid clothing, and say to him, You sit here well; but to the poor man you say, You stand there, or, Sit under my footstool: do you not judge among yourselves, and have become judges of wicked thoughts? Listen, my beloved brothers: did not God choose the poor in this world, rich in faith, and heirs of the kingdom which God has promised to those who love him? But you have dishonored the poor man*[^4]: namely on account of him to whom it was said, *Stand there*, when to the one having the gold ring it had been said, *You sit here well*. And then it follows, treating and unfolding more broadly that same sentence: *Do not*, he says, *the rich oppress you by their power, and drag you to judgment? Do they not themselves blaspheme the good name which has been invoked over you? If indeed you fulfill the royal law, according to the Scripture, You shall love your neighbor as yourself, you do well: but if you accept persons, you commit sin, being convicted by the law as transgressors*[^5]. See how he calls transgressors of the law those who say to the rich, *Sit here*, and to the poor, *Stand there*. See: lest they should think it a contemptible sin to transgress the law in this one matter, he went on and added: *But whoever keeps the whole law, but offends in one point, has become guilty of all. For he who said, You shall not commit adultery, said also, You shall not kill. And if you do not kill, but commit adultery, you have become a transgressor of the law*[^6]: on account of what he had said, *convicted by the law as transgressors*. Since these things are so, it seems to follow (unless it be shown to be understood in another way) that one who has said to the rich man, *Sit here*, and to the poor man, *Stand there*, paying to the latter not the honor he paid to the former, is to be judged both an idolater, and a blasphemer, and an adulterer, and a homicide, and—not to recall all, which is long—guilty of all crimes: for, offending in one, he has become guilty of all.

**2. 4.** But indeed, does one who has one virtue have all, and one who does not have one have none? If this is true, that sentence is confirmed. But I wish it to be expounded, not confirmed; which through itself, with us, is firmer than all the authorities of the philosophers. And that point about virtues and vices, if it is truly said, does not have as its consequence that on this account all sins are equal. For that point about the inseparability of the virtues—even if perhaps I am mistaken, yet if I rightly remember, which I scarcely remember—pleased all the philosophers who said that those same virtues are necessary for the living of life. But this point about the parity of sins only the Stoics dared to dispute, against all the sense of the human race: which vanity of theirs you most clearly convicted from the holy Scriptures, in that Jovinian, who in this opinion was a Stoic, but in catching at and defending pleasures an Epicurean. In which most sweet and most splendid disputation of yours it appeared sufficiently evident that it did not please our authors—or rather the Truth herself, who spoke through them—that all sins are equal. But how it can come about that, even if this about the virtues is true, we are nevertheless not on that account compelled to confess the equality of all sins, I will try, as much as I can, with the Lord's help, to open: and if I accomplish this, you will approve; but where I fall short of the case, you will supply.

**2. 5.** Certainly they persuade us of this—that one who has one virtue has all, and that all are lacking to him to whom one is lacking—because prudence can be neither cowardly, nor unjust, nor intemperate: for if it were any of these, it would not be prudence. Further, if prudence will be such only when it is also brave, and just, and temperate, then surely, where it is, it has the rest with it. So too fortitude cannot be imprudent, or intemperate, or unjust: so temperance must be prudent, brave, and just: so justice is not, unless it be prudent, brave, and temperate. Thus, where any one of them is true, the others likewise are; but where the others are lacking, that one is not true, even if in some way it seems to be like it.

**2. 6.** For there are, as you know, certain vices contrary to the virtues by an open distinction, as imprudence to prudence. But there are certain ones contrary only because they are vices, yet similar by a certain deceptive appearance, as to that same prudence not imprudence, but cunning. For I now call cunning that which is more usually understood and named in malicious people; not as our Scripture is wont to speak, which often sets down cunning in a good sense: whence, *Be cunning as serpents*[^7]; and that, *That it may give cunning to the innocent*[^8]. Although even among them the most eloquent of the Roman tongue said, *Nor yet were guile or cunning lacking to him for taking precaution*, setting down cunning in a good sense: but among them it is most rare, among ours most frequent. Likewise, among the parts of temperance, most openly contrary is extravagance to frugality: but that which is commonly called stinginess is indeed a vice, yet similar to frugality, not by nature, but by a most deceptive appearance. Likewise by a manifest dissimilarity injustice is contrary to justice: but the lust of avenging oneself is wont as it were to imitate justice, but it is a vice. Cowardice is perspicuously contrary to fortitude: but harshness differs by nature, deceives by likeness. Constancy is a certain part of virtue; from it, inconstancy is far averse, and undoubtedly stands against it: but stubbornness affects to be called constancy, and is not; because the one is a virtue, the other a vice.

**2. 7.** So that it may not be necessary to recall the same things again, let us set down, by way of example, something from which the rest can be understood. Catiline, as those wrote of him who were able to know him, could bear cold, thirst, hunger, and was patient of fasting, of cold, of want of sleep, beyond what is credible to anyone, and through this seemed both to himself and to his own to be endowed with great fortitude. But this fortitude was not prudent; for it chose evils for goods: it was not temperate; for it was defiled by most base corruptions: it was not just; for he had conspired against his homeland. And therefore it was not even fortitude; but harshness imposed on itself the name of fortitude, in order to deceive the foolish: for if it were fortitude, it would be not a vice, but a virtue; but if it were a virtue, it would never be left by the other virtues, as by inseparable companions.

**2. 8.** Wherefore, while it is asked, even concerning the vices, whether they likewise all are where one will be, or none are where one will not be, it is laborious to show this, because to one virtue two vices are wont to be opposed—both that which is openly contrary, and that which is shadowed by an appearance of likeness. Whence that fortitude of Catiline, because it was not fortitude, which did not have the other virtues with it, was more easily seen to be so: but that it was cowardice, where there was the exercise of enduring and tolerating any most grievous troubles whatsoever, beyond what is credible to anyone, can be persuaded with difficulty. But perhaps to those who look more keenly, cowardice appears to be that very harshness; because it had neglected the labor of those good pursuits by which true fortitude is acquired. Nevertheless, because there are bold men who are not timid, and again timid men who lack boldness, when each is a vice—since one who is brave by true virtue neither rashly dares, nor inconsiderately fears—we are compelled to confess that the vices are more than the virtues.

**2. 9.** Whence sometimes a vice is removed by a vice; as by the love of praise, the love of money: sometimes one yields so that more succeed; as one who was a drunkard, if he has drunk moderately, may have learned both stinginess and ambition. Vices, therefore, can also yield to vices succeeding, not to virtues; and therefore they are more. But where a virtue has once entered, since it brings the rest with it, surely all the vices that were there will yield: for not all were there, but sometimes just as many, sometimes more succeeded fewer, or fewer more.

**3. 10.** Whether these things are so must be inquired more diligently. For that is not a divine sentence by which it is said, One who has one virtue has all; and to him in whom one is lacking, none is present: but this seemed so to men—very ingenious indeed, zealous, but yet men. But I do not know how I should say—I do not say of a man, from whom virtue (*virtus*) is said to be named, but even of a woman who keeps for her husband the faith of the marriage bed, if she does this on account of the precept and promise of God, and is first faithful to him—that she does not have chastity, or that chastity is no virtue, or a small one; so too of a husband who keeps this same faith to his wife: and yet there are very many such, of whom I would say that none is without some sin, and surely that sin, of whatever kind, comes from some vice. Whence conjugal chastity in religious men and women, since it is without doubt a virtue—for it is neither nothing nor a vice—does not nevertheless have all the virtues with it. For if all were there, there would be no vice; if no vice, no sin at all: but who is without some sin? Who, then, is without some vice, that is, a certain kindling or as it were root of sin, when he who reclined on the Lord's breast cries out: *If we say that we have no sin, we deceive ourselves, and the truth is not in us*[^9]? Nor need this be argued at length with you; but I say it on account of others who may perhaps read this. For you indeed, in that same splendid work against Jovinian, diligently proved this too from the holy Scriptures: where also, from this very letter, whose words are the ones whose meaning we now inquire into, you set down what is written: *For in many things we all offend*[^10]. For he does not say, You offend, but we offend, though it was the apostle of Christ speaking: and although in this place he says, *But whoever keeps the whole law, but offends in one point, has become guilty of all*, there he says, not in one, but in many; and not certain ones, but all, offend.

**3. 11.** But far be it that any believer should suppose that so many thousands of servants of Christ, who truly say that they have sin, lest they deceive themselves and the truth be not in them, have no virtue, since wisdom is a great virtue: for wisdom itself said to man, *Behold, piety is wisdom*[^11]. Far be it, then, that we should say that so many and so great faithful and pious men of God do not have piety, which the Greeks call either *εὐσέβειαν*, or more expressly and fully *θεοσέβειαν*: but what is piety, except the worship of God? And whence is he worshiped, except by love? Love, therefore, *from a pure heart and a good conscience and unfeigned faith*[^12], is a great and true virtue, since it is itself also the end of the precept. Rightly was it called *strong as death*[^13]: either because no one conquers it, as none conquers death; or because in this life, up to death, is the measure of love, as the Lord says, *Greater love than this no one has, than that one lay down his life for his friends*[^14]; or rather, because as death tears the soul from the senses of the flesh, so love from carnal concupiscences. To this knowledge is subservient, when it is useful; for without it knowledge puffs up: but what love has filled by building up, there knowledge will find nothing empty to puff up. Moreover, he showed useful knowledge by defining it, where, when he had said, *Behold, piety is wisdom*, he immediately added: *But to abstain from evils is knowledge*[^15]. Why, then, do we not say that one who has this virtue has all, since the fullness of the law is love? Or is it that the more it is in a man, the more he is endowed with virtue; but the less, the less virtue is in him, since it is itself virtue: and the less virtue is in him, the more is vice? Where, then, it shall be full and perfect, nothing will remain of vice.

**3. 12.** Accordingly the Stoics seem to me to be deceived, because they are unwilling that a man advancing in wisdom should have wisdom at all, but that he then has it when he is altogether perfect in it: not that they deny he is advanced; but unless, by emerging from a certain deep, he suddenly flashes out into the free airs of wisdom, they hold him in no part wise. For just as it makes no difference for the drowning of a man whether he has water deep over him by many stadia, or by one palm, or by a finger: so they say that those who tend toward wisdom advance indeed, as rising from the bottom of a whirlpool into the air; but unless by advancing, as by emerging, they have escaped all folly, like the water pressing upon them, they have no virtue, nor are they wise: but when they have escaped, they soon have it whole, nor does anything of folly remain, whence any sin at all could exist.

**3. 13.** This likeness, where folly is set as water and wisdom as air, so that the mind, emerging as it were from the drowning of folly, suddenly breathes again into wisdom, does not seem to me sufficiently fitted to the authority of our Scriptures: but rather that one, by which vice or folly is compared to darkness, but to light virtue or wisdom, as far as such likenesses can be drawn from corporeal things to intelligible ones. Not, therefore, as one rising from the waters into the air, where, when he has passed their surface, he is suddenly inspired with as much as suffices; but as one proceeding from darkness into light, by gradually advancing is illuminated. Until this is done most fully, we nevertheless already say of him, as of one going out from a most hidden cave, that he is breathed upon by the nearness of the light, the more so the more he approaches the exit: so that what shines in him is surely from the light toward which he proceeds; but what is still obscure is from the darkness whence he goes out. And so both, *No living person will be justified in the sight of God*[^16]; and yet, *the just lives by faith*[^17]. And the saints are clothed with righteousness, one more, another less: and no one here lives without sin, and this one more, another less; but the best is the one who has the least.

**4. 14.** But why have I, as if forgetting to whom I speak, become like a teacher, when I had proposed what I wish to learn from you? But because, concerning the parity of sins—whence the question fell into what I was treating—I had determined to set forth my opinion for you to examine, I will now at last conclude it. For even if it is true that one who has one virtue has all, and one who does not have one has none, not even so are sins equal. For where there is no virtue, nothing indeed is right; nor yet on that account is one thing not more crooked than the crooked, and more distorted than the distorted. But if, which I think is truer and more congruous with the sacred Scriptures, the intentions of the soul are like the members of the body, not because they appear in places, but because they are felt in affections; and one is illuminated more, another less, another wholly lacks light and is overshadowed by a shadowy obstacle: surely, just as each one is affected by the illumination of pious love—in one act more, in another less, in some nothing—so it can be said that he has one virtue and does not have another, has one more, another less. For we can rightly say, There is greater love in this man than in that; and, Some in this man, none in that, as far as pertains to the love which is piety: and in one and the same man, that he has greater chastity than patience, and greater today than yesterday, if he advances; and still does not have continence, and has no small mercy.

**4. 15.** And, to embrace generally and briefly what notion I have of virtue, as far as pertains to living rightly: virtue is love, by which that which is to be loved is loved. This is in some greater, in others less, in others none; but the fullest, which now cannot be increased, as long as a man lives here, is in no one; but as long as it can be increased, surely that which is less than it ought to be is from vice. From which vice *there is no just man on earth who does good and does not sin*[^18]: from which vice *no living person will be justified in the sight of God*[^19]. On account of which vice, *if we say that we have no sin, we deceive ourselves, and the truth is not in us*[^20]: on account of which, however much we advance, it is necessary for us to say, *Forgive us our debts*[^21]; although in baptism all things said, done, thought have already been forgiven. And so he who sees rightly sees whence, and when, and where that perfection is to be hoped for, to which there is nothing that can be added. But if there were no precepts, surely there would be no place where a man might more surely inspect himself, and see from what he should turn away, toward what he should strive, for what he should give thanks, what he should pray for. Great, then, is the usefulness of the precepts, if so much is granted to free choice that the grace of God may be more honored.

**5. 16.** If these things are so, how does one who has kept the whole law become guilty of all if he offends in one point? Or perhaps, because the fullness of the law is love, by which God and neighbor are loved—in which precepts of love the whole law and the prophets hang—he rightly becomes guilty of all who acts against that on which all hang? But no one sins except by acting against it: because *You shall not commit adultery, you shall not kill, you shall not steal, you shall not covet, and if there is any other commandment, it is summed up in this saying, You shall love your neighbor as yourself. Love of neighbor works no evil. But the fullness of the law is love*[^22]. But no one loves his neighbor except by loving God, so as to spend on him whom he loves as himself, as much as he can, that he too may love God: whom if he himself does not love, he loves neither himself nor his neighbor. And through this, one who has kept the whole law, if he has offended in one point, becomes guilty of all; because he acts against love, on which the whole law hangs. He becomes guilty of all, then, by acting against that on which all hang.

**5. 17.** Why, then, should sins not be called equal? Or perhaps because one who sins more grievously acts more against love, one who sins more lightly less? And by this very fact he admits a more and a less by which one becomes guilty of all: but the one sinning more grievously, or in more points, is more guilty; the one sinning more lightly, or in fewer points, less guilty—with so much greater guilt, namely, the more he has sinned, and with so much less, the less; yet even if he offends in one point, he is guilty of all, because he acts against that on which all hang? And if these things are true, in that way too that is resolved which a man even of apostolic grace says: *For in many things we all offend*[^23]. For we all offend, but one more grievously, one more lightly, according as each has sinned more or less: with so much greater guilt in committing sin, the less he is in loving God and neighbor; and again, with so much less in the perpetration of sin, the greater he is in the love of God and neighbor. And so, with so much fuller iniquity, the more empty of love. And then we are perfect in love, when nothing remains of weakness.

**5. 18.** Nor indeed, as I judge, must it be thought a light sin to hold the faith of our Lord Jesus Christ with respect of persons, if we refer that difference of sitting and standing to ecclesiastical honors: for who would bear that a rich man be chosen for the seat of honor in the Church, a poorer man more instructed and more holy being despised? But if he speaks of daily seatings together, who does not sin in this? If, however, he sins, unless when within himself he so judges that the other seems to him to be the better the richer he is: for this he seems to have signified, when he says, *Do you not judge among yourselves, and have become judges of wicked thoughts?*[^24]

**6. 19.** The law of liberty, then, is the law of love, of which he says: *If, however, you fulfill the royal law according to the Scriptures, You shall love your neighbor as yourself, you do well. But if you accept persons, you commit sin, being convicted by the law as transgressors*[^25]. And after that sentence most difficult to understand, of which I have said enough of what I thought should be said, recalling that same law of liberty: *So speak*, he says, *and so do, as those who are to be judged by the law of liberty*[^26]. And since he knows what he said a little before, that *in many things we all offend*, he suggests the Lord's as it were daily medicine for the daily, though lighter, wounds. *For judgment*, he says, *without mercy to him who does not do mercy*[^27]. For hence also the Lord: *Forgive*, he says, *and it will be forgiven you; give, and it will be given you*[^28]. *But mercy triumphs over judgment*[^29]: it was not said, Mercy conquers judgment, for it is not adverse to judgment; but, triumphs, because more are gathered through mercy—but those who have shown mercy. *For blessed are the merciful, for God will have mercy on them*[^30].

**6. 20.** And this surely is just: that it be forgiven them, because they forgave; and given to them, because they gave. For there is in God both mercy in judging, and judgment in pitying. On account of which it is said to him: *Mercy and judgment I will sing to you, O Lord*[^31]: for whoever, as it were too just, awaits judgment without mercy as though secure, provokes a most just wrath, fearing which he said, *Enter not into judgment with your servant*[^32]. Whence it is said to a contumacious people: *Why do you wish to contend with me in judgment?*[^33] For when the just king shall sit on his throne, *who will boast that he has a chaste heart? Or who will boast that he is clean from sin?*[^34] What hope is there, then, unless mercy triumphs over judgment? But toward those who have done mercy, by truly saying, *Forgive us our debts, as we also forgive*; and by giving without murmuring; for *God loves a cheerful giver*[^35]. Finally, holy James from this place speaks of the works of mercy, that he may console those whom that sentence had vehemently terrified; when he admonishes how even the daily sins, without which one does not live here, are expiated by daily remedies: lest a man, who, when he offends in one point becomes guilty of all, by offending in many—since *in many things we all offend*—should, on account of the great heap of his guilt gathered bit by bit, be carried to the tribunal of so great a judge, and not find the mercy which he did not do; but rather, by forgiving and giving, deserve to have his sins forgiven him, and the promises rendered to him.

**6. 21.** I have said many things, by which I would perhaps cause you tedium, who do not expect to learn these things, which nevertheless you approve, since you have been accustomed to teach them. But if there is anything in them, as far as pertains to the matter itself—for in what eloquence they are unfolded, I do not much care—if, then, there is anything in them which offends your erudition, I ask that by writing back you admonish me, and not be reluctant to correct me. For unhappy is he who does not both worthily honor such great and so holy labors of your studies, and give thanks for them to the Lord our God, by whose gift you are such. Whence, since I ought more willingly to learn from anyone what I uselessly do not know, than more readily to teach anyone what I do know, how much more justly do I demand of you this debt of love—by whose teaching, in the name and help of the Lord, ecclesiastical letters in the Latin tongue have been helped as much as they never could before! But especially that sentence, *Whoever keeps the whole law, but offends in one point, has become guilty of all*, if your charity knows that it can be expounded better in some other way, I beseech through the Lord that you would deign to communicate it to us.
