# Letter 161

To the holy lord, brother to be venerably beloved and fellow priest Augustine, and to the brothers who are with you, from Evodius and the brothers who are with me, greetings in the Lord.

**1.** Through Jobinus, who had been sent to the estate of Marcianum, I some time ago, by a letter sent, asked one question about reason and God, of which I have not yet deserved an answer. But because we had in our hands, reading them, the letters of your holiness, one to the illustrious man Volusianus and another to Italica, an illustrious woman in Christ, it occurred to me that, in the earlier letter, concerning the carnal conception of our Lord Jesus Christ our God in the Virgin, and the nativity from her, you set down, "If a reason is sought, it will not be marvelous; if an example is demanded, it will not be unique"; and it seemed that something of the kind could be said about every nativity, whether of a man, or of any living thing, or of a seed. For if the reason for it be sought, it will not be traced out, and it will surely be marvelous; and if an example be demanded, since there is nothing of the kind among examples, it will be unique. For neither can any union of man and woman, or of any conception at any time—what is formed in secret—be set forth by reason; nor indeed will any reason be found of the seeds born from the earth, first rotting, afterward bearing fruit. For is it not marvelous too that a single worm, without any parent—if something be sought for uniqueness—is found perfect, formed by virginal conception inside an apple? Whence I think it was said as an example: *I am a worm, and not a man*[^1]. Of conceptions, then, whether of joined or of single beings, I do not know what reason could be furnished. Not only of the conception of the Virgin, then, is no reason rendered, but I think a reason can be supplied of no conception whatever.

**2.** If an example is demanded: behold, mares are said to conceive from the wind, and hens from ashes, and ducks from water, and some other animals to bring forth their own offspring without male seed. Certainly, if they do not bring them forth whole, yet they can conceive unimpaired: how, then, is it set down, "If an example is demanded, it will not be unique," when so many examples come forth? It is known to all that certain animals are born within bodies—not only of females, but even of men, I mean internally: is it that there too the seed makes the conception? Behold examples, behold marvels, of which no reason at all is rendered. Or if to this it is answered that in a virgin human being nothing of the kind ever happens, yet there are examples in other natures of things, since things born are also conceived without seeds, of which a reason can by no means be rendered at all. Yet even in generation it is possible to find something of this kind, which so brings forth offspring that the integrity of the nature is not violated. For I am wont to hear that the spider, without any male conception and without corruption of the bringing-forth, produces marvelously, according to the manner of its nature, all those threads from which, when they are stretched out, it is wont to hang, and shows that this has been granted to it alone, as it were as something unique. Therefore, if this too be sought, it is not only marvelous, but such examples of things can by no means be given. For I fear lest for this reason these things have come first among examples: so that those who would not have believed that a virgin could conceive might by these examples be convinced that it is indeed marvelous, but yet will not be unique: for all the works of God are marvelous, since they were made in wisdom. If, then, this is cast at us, what shall we answer?

**3.** This too presses me most vehemently, that the same can be said of the substance of the Lord's own glorified body: that it too could see the substance of God (for you said in the letter to Italica that it cannot, and it is certain): so that when we begin to render a reason why it cannot, this is cast at us—that if what was done in that conception and nativity is marvelous and unique, and they wish no reason and no example to be sought, since this was granted to one alone: so that, just as no reason is rendered to them about the conception and generation, but it is found unique in him alone, so about his vision no reason should be rendered nor any example required, but that it should be permitted to him alone, uniquely, to see the substance of the divinity with the body. If it be answered that a reason can be rendered, because a corporeal thing can by no means behold an incorporeal thing, I fear it may be answered that that conception too can be set forth by reason and confirmed by examples. For either reason will fail among men, and examples will cease, and they will confirm that the Only-begotten can be seen by his body to be God; or, if a reason be rendered here, it will follow that a reason can be rendered by certain too-clever men of that conception too, and of the generation that followed. What, then, is to be answered to such men, I inquire. For I do not sow contentions, but I ask how the insidious are to be answered. I, however, so believe the conception and bringing-forth of the Virgin as I have always believed; and that God cannot be seen even by the glorified body, I in any case gather by reason. Yet I think we must withstand men who are wont either to stir up seditions out of questions, or with fervent zeal to be awake to learning. Pray for us. May the peace and love of Christ make your holiness mindful of us, holy lord, and venerably most blessed brother.

[^1]: Ps 22:6 (21:7)
