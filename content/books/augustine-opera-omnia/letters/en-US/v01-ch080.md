# Letter 80

TO THE HOLY AND GOD-BELOVED BROTHERS PAULINUS AND THERASIA, DESERVEDLY VENERABLE AND MUCH LONGED-FOR, AUGUSTINE SENDS GREETING IN THE LORD.

**1.** When my dearest brother Celsus asked again for a reply, I hastened to render my debt—but truly I hastened. For while I thought he would stay with us some days yet, having suddenly learned of the chance of a ship, he indicated to me at night his departure for the following day. What was I to do, since I could not hold him; and since he was hurrying to you, with whom it was better for him to be, I ought not, even if I could? Accordingly, I snatched up at once these few things to be dictated and sent, confessing myself the debtor of a longer letter, when, after the return of our venerable brothers and colleagues Theasius and Evodius, I shall first have been satisfied with news of you in part. For we hope, in the name and with the help of Christ, that you will come to us more abundantly, even now, in their breasts and mouths. While I was writing this, I had also, a few days before, given another letter through our like-minded son Fortunatianus, priest of the Church of Thagaste, about to sail to Rome. Now therefore I ask what I am wont to ask, that you do what you are wont to do. Pray for us, that God may see our humility and our toil, and forgive all our sins[^1].

**2.** I desire to converse with you about such matters, if you would deign, by letters, as we might converse if we were present to your senses. Behold, that little question which I recently proposed—as if I, present, were laying it before you, present, amid sweet discourses—you have solved with a plainly Christian understanding and devotion, but too hastily and briefly; for the grace of your mouth could dwell there somewhat longer and more abundantly, if, when you had said that you had resolved to persevere in that place which you happily occupy, in such a way that, if God should be pleased to have anything else for you, you would prefer his will to your own, you would explain this very thing somewhat more openly. In what way, then, are we to know the will of God, which is to be set before our own will—whether only in that matter which we ought therefore to bear willingly, because even unwilling we would be compelled? For there it indeed comes to pass what we do not wish; but we correct ourselves so as to wish it, because he wills it, whose will it is neither right to refuse in its excellence, nor permitted to evade in its omnipotence; just as another girded Peter and bore him where he did not wish, yet he went where he would not, and willing he underwent a harsh death[^2]. Or even there, where there is the power not to change one's resolution, although something else occurs in which the will of God may rather appear, calling us to change our resolution—not because ours was evil, but a resolution in which one would rightly remain, unless we were called by him to another. For it was not evil for Abraham to nourish and bring up his son as long as he could, as far as it lay in himself, even to the end of his own life; but, suddenly bidden to kill him, he changed a resolution not previously evil, but one that would have been evil if, after the command, it had not been changed[^3]. On this point too I do not doubt that nothing else seems so to you.

**3.** But for the most part, not by a voice from heaven, not through a prophet, not through a revelation either of a dream or of that transport of mind which is called ecstasy, but by the things themselves happening and calling us to something other than we had determined, we are compelled to recognize that the will of God is other than ours was: as if we had determined to set out, and something should arise which the truth, consulted about our duty, forbade us to abandon; or, to us determining to remain there, something should be announced which, by the same truth consulted, would compel us to set out. Concerning this third kind of cause for changing one's resolution, I ask that you converse with me more fully and clearly about what seems to you. For it often troubles us, and it is difficult not to omit something that ought rather to have been done, while we are unwilling to change that in which we had first determined to remain—not indeed something evil, yet now evil for this reason, that what ought rather to be done is abandoned when it presents itself; whereas, if it did not present itself, one would persevere in that former course not only without blame, but even with praise. Here it is difficult not to be deceived; here the prophetic voice altogether prevails: *Who understands his faults*[^4]? Hence I pray that you make me a sharer in your thoughts: what in such matters you are wont to do, or find ought to be done.

[^1]: See Ps 25:18 (24:18)
[^2]: See Jn 21:18
[^3]: See Gen 22:1-12
[^4]: Ps 19:12 (18:13)
