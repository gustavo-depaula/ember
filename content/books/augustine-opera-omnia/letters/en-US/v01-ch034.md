# Letter 34

To the honorable and most beloved lord Proculeianus, Augustine

**1.** God knows, to whom the secrets of the human heart are manifest, that as much as I love Christian peace, so much am I moved by the sacrilegious deeds of those who persevere unworthily and impiously in dissension from it; and that this motion of my mind is peaceful, and that I do not act so that anyone may be compelled unwilling to catholic communion, but that the open truth may be declared to all who err, and that, manifested through our ministry, with God's help, it may itself sufficiently persuade that it should be embraced and followed.

**2.** For what is more execrable, I ask you, to be silent about other things, than what has now happened? A young man is rebuked by his bishop, mad with frequent beatings of his mother, and not even in those days, when even the severity of the laws spares the most wicked, recalling his impious hands from the womb from which he was born. He threatens the same mother that he will cross over to the party of Donatus, and that he will destroy her whom he is wont to beat with incredible fury. He threatens her, crosses over to the party of Donatus, is rebaptized in his fury, and, raging for his mother's blood, is dressed in white robes. He is set within the railings, eminent and conspicuous, and, before the eyes of all who groan, the meditator of matricide is set forth as if renewed.

**3.** Do these things, at last, please you, most grave man? By no means would I believe this of you: I know your consideration. The carnal mother is being beaten, it is believed, in the members with which she bore and nourished the ingrate; mother Church, the spiritual one, forbids this, and she too is beaten in the sacraments with which she bore and nourished the ingrate. Does he not seem to you to have said, gnashing parricidally: What shall I do to the Church which forbids me to beat my mother? I have found what I shall do: let her too be struck with what injuries she can be; let something be done in me from which her members may grieve. I will go to those who know how to blow contempt upon the grace in which I was born there, to destroy the form which I received in her womb. I will torture both my mothers with savage torments: let the one who bore me later bring forth the earlier. To the grief of the one, I will die spiritually; to the slaying of the other, I will live carnally. What else do we await, honorable man Eusebius, except that against the wretched woman, decrepit with age, destitute by widowhood, from beating whom he was forbidden in the Catholic Church, the Donatist may now be armed without fear? For what else did he conceive in his raging heart, when he said to his mother: I will transfer myself to the party of Donatus, and I will drink your blood? Behold, now bloodstained in conscience, whitened in dress, he has accomplished part of his promise; the other part remains, that he drink his mother's blood. If, then, these things please, let him be urged by his clergy and his sanctifiers to discharge, within his octave, the whole of what he vowed.

**4.** The right hand of the Lord is indeed powerful, which may restrain his fury from the wretched and desolate widow, and may deter him, in the ways it knows, from so wicked a design; nevertheless, struck with so great a grief of mind, what should I do but at least speak? Or do they do these things to her, and to me it is said: Be silent? May the Lord turn this madness from me, that, when he himself commands me through his Apostle, and says that those teaching what they ought not must be refuted by the bishop[^1], I, terrified by their indignations, should be silent? For that I wished so sacrilegious a wickedness to be attached to the public Records, I wished for this purpose: that no one, especially in other cities where it shall be opportune, may suppose that I, lamenting these things, am inventing something, since even at Hippo itself it is now said that Proculeianus did not order this which the public office reported.

**5.** But what can we do more modestly than that I conduct so grave a cause through you, a man both endowed with the most illustrious dignity and tranquil with the most considerate will? I ask, therefore, as I have already asked through our brothers, good and honorable men whom I sent to your Excellence, that you deign to inquire whether Proculeianus's presbyter Victor did not receive this order from his bishop which the public office reported; or whether perhaps, when Victor himself said otherwise, those who took it down recorded something false in the Records, since they are of the same communion. Or if he consents that we treat peaceably this whole question of our dissension, so that the error which is already manifest may become more manifestly known, I gladly embrace it. For I heard that he said that, without a popular uproar, ten grave and honorable men from each side should be present with us, and that we should search out, according to the Scriptures, what is in the truth. For that which some again reported to me that he had said—why I did not go to Constantina, when they themselves were there in greater numbers, or that I ought to go to Milevis, because there, as they allege, they are soon to hold a council—it is ridiculous to say, as though any care belonged to me but my own, of the Church of Hippo. For me the whole reasoning of this question is chiefly with Proculeianus. But if perhaps he thinks himself unequal, let him implore the help of whichever of his colleagues he wishes. For in other cities we do only as much as pertains to the Church as either we are permitted, or is imposed on us, by the bishops of those same cities, our brothers and fellow priests.

**6.** Although this man too, who calls himself a bishop of so many years, what he fears in me, a recruit, that he should be unwilling to confer discourse with me, I do not sufficiently understand: if it is the learning of liberal letters, which perhaps he himself either did not learn, or learned less, what has this to do with that question which must be discussed either from the holy Scriptures or from ecclesiastical or public documents, in which he has been engaged for so many years, whence he ought to be more skilled in them? Finally, there is here my brother and colleague, Samsucius, bishop of the church of Tursa, who learned none of the things which this man is said to dread: let him be present, let him deal with him. I will ask him, and, as I trust in the name of Christ, he will easily grant me to take my place in this matter, and the Lord will help him, contending for the truth, unpolished though he be in discourse, yet, as we trust, instructed in true faith. There is therefore no reason why he should refer it to certain others I know not, rather than that we accomplish between ourselves what pertains to us. Nor yet, as I said, do I avoid even those, if he himself demands their help.

[^1]: See Titus 1:9–11
