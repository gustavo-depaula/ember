# Letter 72

To the truly holy and most blessed pope Augustine, Jerome

**1. 1.** *You direct frequent letters to me, and often compel me to answer a certain letter of yours, copies of which had come to me, as I have already written before, through Sisinnius the deacon, without your subscription, and which you indicate that you sent first through brother Profuturus, secondly through a certain other; and meanwhile Profuturus, drawn back from his journey, and made a bishop, was carried off by a swift death; that other, whose name you keep silent, feared the perils of the sea, and changed his plan of navigation. Since these things are so, I cannot sufficiently wonder how that same letter is said to be held by very many both at Rome and in Italy, and to me alone has not come, to whom alone it was sent; especially since the same brother Sisinnius said, among your other treatises, that he found it not in Africa, not with you, but on an island of the Adriatic, about five years before this.*

**1. 2.** *From friendship all suspicion is to be removed, and one must so speak with a friend as with a second self. Some of my familiars, and vessels of Christ, of whom at Jerusalem and in the holy places there is a very great abundance, suggested that this was done by you not with a simple mind, but seeking the praise and the petty rumors and the little glory of the people, that you might grow at our expense; that many might know that you provoke, I fear; that you write as a learned man, I keep silent as an unskilled one; and that there has at last been found one who imposes a limit on my garrulity. But I, to confess simply, for this reason first did not wish to answer your Dignity, because I did not clearly believe the letter to be yours, nor (as the common saying about certain things is) a sword smeared with honey. Then I was guarding against this, lest I should seem to answer impudently a bishop of my communion, and to reprove some things in the letter of one reproving; especially since I judged certain things in it to be heretical.*

**2. 3.** *Finally, lest you should justly complain and say, What then? you had seen my letter, and recognized the signs of a known hand in the subscription, so as so easily to wound a friend, and to turn another's malice into an insult to me? Therefore, as I have already written before, either send the same letter subscribed with your hand; or cease to provoke an old man hiding in his little cell. But if you wish either to exercise or to show off your learning, seek young men, and eloquent, and noble, of whom there are said to be very many at Rome, who can and dare to engage with you, and to bear the yoke with a bishop in the disputation of the holy Scriptures. I, once a soldier, now a veteran, ought to praise your victories and those of others, not myself again, with a worn-out body, do battle*[^1]; lest, if you frequently urge me to write back, I recall that story, that Quintus Maximus broke Hannibal, youthfully exulting, by his patience[^2]. Age bears away all things, the mind too. Often, as a boy, I remember that I laid the long suns to rest with singing: now so many songs are forgotten by me; the voice too now flees Moeris himself[^3]. *And, to speak more from the holy Scriptures, that Barzillai the Gileadite, delegating to his young son the benefits of king David and all his delights, showed that old age ought not to seek these things, nor to take up what is offered*[^4].

**2. 4.** *But as for your swearing that you did not write a book against me, nor send to Rome one you did not write, but that, if perhaps some things are found in your writings which differ from my sense, I was not wounded by you, but it was written by you what seemed right to you: I beg that you hear me patiently. You did not write a book; and how have writings of your reproach of me been brought to me through others? why does Italy have what you did not write? by what reasoning do you ask that I write back to those things which you deny that you wrote? Nor am I so dull that, if you have felt differently, I should think myself wounded by you. But if you reprove my words at close quarters, and demand a reason for my writings, and compel me to amend what I have written, and provoke me to a* palinode*, and restore my eyes to me; in this friendship is wounded, in this the rights of intimacy are violated. Lest we seem to contend childishly, and to give our partisans or detractors in turn material for contending; I write these things, because I desire to love you purely and in a Christian way, and to retain nothing in my mind which differs from my lips. For it is not fitting that, from adolescence even to this age, sweating with labor in a little monastery with holy brothers, I should dare to write anything against a bishop of my communion, and that bishop whom I began to love before I knew, who first provoked me to friendship, whom, rising in the learning of the Scriptures after me, I rejoiced over. Therefore either deny your book, if perhaps it is not yours, and cease to demand a reply to those things which you did not write; or, if it is yours, ingenuously confess it, so that if I write something in my own defense, the fault may be in you who provoked, not in me who was compelled to answer.*

**3. 5.** *You add besides that you are ready, if anything in your writings has moved me, or I should wish to correct it, to accept it as a brother, and not only will rejoice in my benevolence toward you, but beseech that I do this very thing. Again I say what I feel: you provoke an old man, you goad one who is silent, you seem to flaunt your learning. But it is not of my age to be thought ill-disposed toward one whom I ought rather to favor. And if in the Gospels and the Prophets perverse men find something on which to lean for reproach, do you wonder if in your books, and especially in the exposition of the Scriptures, which are most obscure, certain things seem to differ from the line of right? And this I say, not because I now judge that certain things in your works are to be reproved. For I have never given attention to reading them: nor is there with us an abundance of these copies, except the books of your Soliloquies, and certain Commentaries on the Psalms; which, if I wished to discuss, I would teach to differ—not from me, who am nothing, but from the translations of the ancient Greeks. Farewell, my friend, most dear, in age a son, in dignity a parent: and observe this, asked of me by you, that whatever you write to me, you cause to come to me first.*

[^1]: See Virgil, Aeneid 12.439–440
[^2]: See Virgil, Aeneid 6.845–846
[^3]: Virgil, Eclogues 9.51–54
[^4]: See 2 Sam 19:34–37
