# Letter 183

To his most beloved brothers Aurelius, Alypius, Augustine, Evodius, and Possidius, bishops, from Innocent, greetings.

**1.** The letters of your fraternity, full of faith, and confirmed with all the vigor of catholic religion, sent from two councils through our brother and fellow bishop Julius, we received with a most grateful mind, because their tenor and whole context stands, with sound reasoning, in the consideration of the daily grace of God, and in the correction of those who think otherwise; so that it can both take away from them all error, and, by some example given of our law, present them a worthy teacher whom they ought to follow. But about these things we have already, as I think, said enough above, when, answering your reports, we wrote back what we thought both about their perfidy and about your opinion. But there comes up and is supplied, again and again, what may be said against them, nor can there ever be lacking what may conquer, since what is conquered by the strength of our faith and by truth itself more fully is so wretched and impious. For one who has spurned and despised all hope of life, confounding his heart by a hostile and damnable disputation, when he believes that there is nothing he may receive from God, nor anything left that he may ask for the healing of himself; who has taken this from himself, what has he left further?

**2.** If, then, there are any whom so great a perversity has bound in defense of themselves, who give and join themselves to this dogma, hoping that this pertains to catholic doctrine, which, abhorrent and far off, is utterly proved adverse—infected by their admonitions and words, and led to fall—they will hasten to return to the right path of the way, lest the error, besieging their mind as it were fed upon their senses, invade them longer. For if Pelagius, wherever he has stood his ground, has deceived the minds of those who easily or simply believed him disputing, by this affirmation: whether they be in this city (whom, not knowing them, we can neither make manifest nor deny, since even if they exist they lie hidden, nor do they ever dare either to defend him preaching these things, or to boast such things with any of ours present, nor can anyone easily be detected in so great a multitude of the people, nor anywhere be recognized), or whether they live in any place of the earth; by the mercy and grace of our God we believe that they are easily corrected, the condemnation of him being heard who shall have been found the pertinacious and resisting author of this dogma; nor does it matter where these are, provided that, wherever they can be found, they are to be healed.

**3.** To us, however, it cannot be persuaded that he has been cleared, although there have been brought to us, by I know not what laymen, proceedings by which he would have us believe that he was both heard and absolved: which, whether they are true, we doubt, because they came under no prosecution of that council, nor have we received any letters about this matter from those before whom he pleaded the causes of this thing. But if he could have been confident of his own cleansing, we believe rather that he would have done this, which could have been much truer, that by his letters he would compel those who judged the case to indicate it. But since there are some things set down in those very proceedings, which, being charged, he partly suppressed by avoiding them, partly confounded with total obscurity by twisting many words upon himself; some he cleared rather by false arguments than by true reasoning, as for the time could seem—denying some, turning others by a false interpretation.

**4.** But would that, which is rather to be wished, he might now turn himself to the true way of the catholic faith from that error of his path, so as to desire and wish to be cleared, considering the daily grace of God, and acknowledging his help, that it may seem true, and he be approved by all, corrected by manifest reasoning, not by the indication of proceedings, but with his heart converted to the catholic faith! Whence we can neither blame nor approve their judgment, since we do not know whether the proceedings are true; or, if they are true, whether it is established that he rather slipped away than cleared himself with all truth. And if he is confident and knows that he is not worthy of our condemnation, so that he may say that he has now refuted this whole which he had said, he ought not to be summoned by us, but rather himself to hasten, that he may be absolved. For if he still thinks in such a way, when will he commit himself to our judgment, whatever letters he has received, since he knows he must be condemned? But if he were to be summoned, it would better be done by those who seem to be nearer and not separated by a long space of earth. But care will not be lacking, if he furnishes matter for the medicine. For he can condemn what he had thought, and, his letters given, ask pardon for his error, as befits one returning to us, dearest brothers.

**5.** The book, indeed, which was said to be his, transmitted to us by your charity, we have unrolled: in which we read many things written against the grace of God, many blasphemous things, nothing that would please, and almost nothing that would not utterly displease, to be condemned and trampled by anyone; the like of which no one but he who had written these things would receive into his mind and think. For to dispute more broadly in this place about the law, as it were with Pelagius set before us and resisting, we have not thought necessary, since we converse with you who know the whole and rejoice with an assent equal to ours. For these examples are better set down when we treat with those who, it is established, are ignorant of these matters. For about the possibility of nature, about free choice, and about all the grace of God, and daily grace, to whom that thinks rightly would it not be most abundant to dispute? Let him, then, anathematize these things which he thought, that those who had been undone by his discourses and precepts may at last know what the true faith holds. For they will more easily be called back, when they perceive these things condemned by their author. But if he wishes pertinaciously to persist in this impiety, we must act so that aid may be brought at least to those whom not his, but rather this man's, error has led in; lest this medicine too perish for them, whose such care he neither admits nor asks. (And in another hand:) May God keep you safe, dearest brothers. Given on the sixth day before the kalends of February.
