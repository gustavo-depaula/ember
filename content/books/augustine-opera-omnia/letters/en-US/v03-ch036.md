# Letter 187

*A Book on the Presence of God*

Augustine to Dardanus

**1. 1.** I confess, most beloved brother Dardanus, more illustrious to me in the love of Christ than in the dignity of this world, that I have answered your letter more slowly than I ought. The causes of which I would not wish you to inquire, lest you bear me more grievously excusing myself at length, than you endured me writing back too slowly; since I would rather you easily pardon my offense, than judge about my defense: whatever those causes were, believe that there could have been no contempt of you with me. Nay rather, I would have written back most quickly, if I had despised you. Nor now, because at length, at some time, I have written back, have I therefore at least at some time labored at something worthy of you as a reader, and which I could deservedly entitle with your name; but I preferred now to write back somehow, rather than to endure that this summer too pass with me a debtor of this office. Nor did your dignity terrify me and make me hesitating, since your humanity is more pleasing than that is suspect. But from that very thing whence I love you more, hence I find it more difficult whence I may suffice for so great an avidity of your religious love.

**1. 2.** To this was added that, besides the flame of mutual love, by which it comes about that we love even those whom we have never seen, while we believe that they have what we love—where you surely anticipated me, and made me fear that the opinion and expectation you have of me may be deceived in me—besides this, then, you set forth for me in your letter such questions, that, if they had been set forth by anyone whatever, they would become no small business even for my leisure, which is lacking to me. But when they are set forth by you, for whom, searching the depths, the surface of their solution is by no means enough, and they are set forth to one most occupied and walled in and bound by the masses of other cares; it belongs to your prudence and benevolence to consider how peaceable you ought to hold me, either that I did not answer for a long time, or if even now, for the greatness of your intention, I shall not answer.

**2. 3.** You ask, then: "The mediator of God and men, the man Christ Jesus, how is he now believed to be in heaven, since, hanging on the wood, and now about to die, he said to the believing thief: *Today you will be with me in paradise*[^1]?" And from this perhaps you say that paradise is to be understood as composed in some part of heaven; or, because God is everywhere, that that man too who is in God is everywhere diffused; from this surely wishing it to be understood that he too could be in paradise who is everywhere.

**2. 4.** Here I ask, or rather acknowledge, in what way you take the man Christ. Not, surely, as certain heretics, the Word of God and flesh, that is, without a human soul, so that the Word was for the flesh in place of a soul: or the Word of God and a soul and flesh, but without a human mind, so that the Word of God was for the soul in place of a human mind. Not, surely, do you so take the man Christ, but as you spoke above, where you said that you take Christ to be the omnipotent God by such a reasoning of belief that you would not believe him God, unless you had believed him also perfect man. Surely when you say perfect man, you wish the whole human nature to be understood there: but he is not a perfect man, if either a soul be lacking to the flesh, or to the soul itself a human mind.

**2. 5.** If, then, we think it was said according to the man whom the Word, God, assumed: *Today you will be with me in paradise*, paradise is not to be supposed from these words to be in heaven: for not even on that very day was the man Christ Jesus going to be in heaven; but in hell according to the soul, and in the tomb according to the flesh. And that the flesh was laid in the tomb on that day, the Gospel most plainly shows: but that the soul descended into hell, the apostolic doctrine proclaims; since blessed Peter adduces a testimony to this matter from the Psalms, where he shows it was foretold: *For you will not leave my soul in hell, nor will you give your holy one to see corruption*[^2]. That was said of the soul, because it was not left there, whence it returned so quickly: that was said of the body, because by a swift resurrection it could not be corrupted in the tomb. But no one understands paradise to be in the tomb. And if anyone were so absurd as to try to be wise in this way, because that tomb was in a garden; surely from this he would be recalled from that opinion, that the one to whom it was said, *Today you will be with me in paradise*, was not on that day with Christ in that tomb: nor would the burial of the flesh, perceiving nothing of joy or grief in death, be offered to his faith as a great gift, while he was thinking of rest where there is perception.

**2. 6.** It remains, therefore, that if it was said according to the man, *Today you will be with me in paradise*, paradise is to be understood as in hell, where Christ was going to be on that day according to his human soul. But whether that bosom of Abraham, where the impious rich man, while he was in the torments of hell, saw the poor man resting, is to be reckoned by the name of paradise, or to be thought to pertain to hell, I would not easily say. For of that rich man we read it was said: *And the rich man died, and was buried in hell*; and, *while he was in the torments in hell*. But in the death or rest of the poor man hell is not named: but, *It happened*, he says, *that the poor man died, and was carried by the angels into the bosom of Abraham*. Then to the burning rich man Abraham says: *Between us and you a great chasm has been fixed*[^3]: as it were between hell and the seats of the blessed. For not easily anywhere in the Scriptures is the name of hell found set down in a good sense. Whence too it is wont to be asked, if only penal places are rightly understood as hell, how we may piously believe that the soul of the Lord Christ was in hell. But it is well answered that he descended for this reason, that he might come to the aid of those to whom it was fitting: whence blessed Peter says that he loosed the pains of hell, by which it was impossible for him to be held. Further, if both regions, of those grieving and of those resting—that is, both where that rich man was tortured, and where that poor man rejoiced—are to be believed to be in hell; who would dare to say that the Lord Jesus came only to the penal parts of hell, and was not among those who rest in Abraham's bosom? Where, if he was, he himself is to be understood as the paradise which he deigned to promise to the thief's soul on that day. And if these things are so, the name of paradise is general, where one lives happily. For not because that was called paradise where Adam was before sin, was the Scripture therefore prohibited from calling the Church too paradise, with the fruit of its apple trees.

**3. 7.** But the sense is much more expedited, and free from all these ambiguities, if it be taken that Christ said, not according to that which he was as man, but according to that which he was as God: *Today you will be with me in paradise*. For the man Christ was on that day going to be in the tomb according to the flesh, in hell according to the soul: but the same Christ as God is everywhere always. For he is the light which shines even in the darkness, although the darkness does not comprehend it. He is the Power and Wisdom of God, of which it is written that *it reaches from end to end mightily, and disposes all things sweetly*[^4]; and reaches everywhere by its purity, and nothing defiled runs into it. Wherever, then, paradise is, whoever of the blessed is there, is there with him who is everywhere.

**3. 8.** For since Christ is God and man; God, surely, whence he says, *I and the Father are one*[^5]; but man, whence he says, *The Father is greater than I*[^6]; and the same is the only-begotten Son of God from the Father, and son of man from the seed of David according to the flesh: both are to be observed in him when he speaks, or when the Scripture speaks of him, and it must be looked at what is said according to what. For as one man is a rational soul and flesh, so one Christ is the Word and man. Accordingly, as far as concerns the Word, Christ is creator; for *all things were made through him*[^7]: but as far as concerns the man, Christ is created; for *he was made of the seed of David according to the flesh*[^8], and made in the likeness of men. Likewise, because in the man there are two, soul and flesh; according to the soul he was sad unto death, according to the flesh he suffered death.

**3. 9.** Nor yet, when we say Christ the Son of God, do we separate the man; or when we say the same Christ the son of man, do we separate God. For according to the man he was on earth, not in heaven where he now is, when he said: *No one ascends into heaven, except him who descended from heaven, the Son of man who is in heaven*[^9]: although according to that which he was the Son of God, he was in heaven; but according to that which he was the son of man, he was still on earth, and had not yet ascended into heaven. Similarly, since according to that which he is the Son of God, he is the Lord of glory; but according to that which he is the son of man, he was crucified; the Apostle nevertheless says: *For if they had known, they would never have crucified the Lord of glory*[^10]. And through this both the son of man was, according to God, in heaven, and the Son of God, according to the man, was being crucified on earth. As, then, it could rightly be said that the Lord of glory was crucified, when that passion pertained to the flesh alone: so it could rightly be said, *Today you will be with me in paradise*, since, according to human lowliness, he was going to be on that day through the flesh in the tomb, through the soul in hell; but according to divine unchangeableness, he had never withdrawn from paradise, because he is everywhere always.

**3. 10.** Do not, then, doubt that the man Christ Jesus is now there whence he is to come; and recall by memory, and faithfully hold, the Christian confession, that he rose from the dead, ascended into heaven, sits at the right hand of the Father, nor will come from anywhere but thence to judge the living and the dead. And so he will come, that angelic voice testifying, as he was seen to go into heaven, that is, in the same form and substance of flesh; to which surely he gave immortality, he did not take away its nature. According to this form he is not to be supposed everywhere diffused. For it must be guarded against, lest we so build up the divinity of the man, that we take away the truth of the body. But it is not consequent that what is in God should be everywhere, as God. For of us too the most truthful Scripture says that *in him we live, and move, and are*[^11]; nor yet are we everywhere as he is; but that man is in God in one way, since God too is in the man in another way, by a certain proper and singular mode. For God and man are one person, and both are one Christ Jesus; everywhere through that which he is God, but in heaven through that which he is man.

**4. 11.** Although even in that very thing which is said—that God is everywhere diffused—a carnal thought must be resisted, and the mind called away from the senses of the body, lest we suppose that God is diffused through all things by a spacious magnitude, as soil, or moisture, or air, or this light is diffused (for every magnitude of this kind is less in its part than in the whole); but rather so as wisdom is great, even in a man whose body is small. And if there are two wise men, of whom one is larger in body, neither wiser, that wisdom is not greater in the larger, less in the smaller, or less in one than in two; but as much in this one as in that, and as much in each as in both: for neither, if they are altogether equally wise, do both know more than each; just as, if they are equally immortal, both do not live more than each.

**4. 12.** Finally, the very immortality of the body, which preceded in the flesh of Christ, and is promised to be ours at the end, although it is a great thing, is surely not great in mass; but although it is held corporeally, it is a certain incorporeal excellence. For since the immortal body itself is less in a part than in the whole, its immortality is as full in a part as in the whole; and although some members are greater than others, yet some members are not more immortal than others. As now, when we are sound in every part according to the manner of present health in the body, not because the whole hand is greater than the finger do we say that the health of the whole hand is greater than that of the finger; but in those unequal members the health itself is equal, when the shorter are so compared to the larger, that what cannot be as great as another, can nevertheless be as sound. But there would be greater health in the larger members, if the larger were the sounder: but since it is not so, but the larger and the smaller are equally sound, the quantity in the masses of the members is surely unequal, but in the unequal the health is equal.

**4. 13.** Since, then, a body is some substance, its quantity is in the magnitude of its mass; but its health is not its quantity, but its quality. The quantity of a body, therefore, could not obtain what its quality could. For its parts being so distant, which cannot be at once, since each holds its own spaces of places, the smaller smaller, and the larger larger, it could not be whole or as great in each individual part; but the quantity is ampler in the ampler parts, shorter in the shorter, and in no part as great as throughout the whole: but the quality of the body, which is called health, when the body is wholly sound, is as great in the larger as in the smaller parts; for those which are less great are not therefore less sound, or those which are ampler therefore sounder. Far be it, then, that what the quality of a created body can do in the body, the very substance of the Creator cannot do in itself.

**4. 14.** God, then, is diffused through all things. For he himself says through the prophet: *Heaven and earth I fill*[^12]; and what I set down a little before of his wisdom: *It reaches from end to end mightily, and disposes all things sweetly*[^13]; and likewise it is written: *The Spirit of the Lord has filled the world*[^14]; and it is said to him in a certain psalm: *Where shall I go from your spirit, and where shall I flee from your face? If I ascend into heaven, you are there; if I descend into hell, you are present*[^15]. But God is so diffused through all things, that he is not the quality of the world, but the substance creating the world, ruling the world without labor, and containing it without burden. Yet not through spaces of places, as if diffused by mass, so that in half the body of the world he is half, and in the other half half, and so whole throughout the whole; but whole in heaven alone, and whole in earth alone, and whole in heaven and in earth, and contained by no place, but whole everywhere in himself.

**4. 15.** So the Father, so the Son, so the Holy Spirit, so the Trinity one God. For neither did they divide the world among themselves into three parts, which the individual ones might fill individually, as though the Son or the Holy Spirit would not have where to be in the world, if the Father had occupied the whole. The true, incorporeal, and unchangeable divinity is not so. For they are not bodies, whose magnitude is ampler in three than in each; nor do they hold places by their masses, so that, by distant spaces, they cannot be at once. For if the soul, set in the body, finds not only no straits, but even a certain breadth—not of corporeal places, but of spiritual joys—when there comes to pass what the Apostle says: *Do you not know that your bodies are the temple of the Holy Spirit who is in you, whom you have from God?*[^16]; nor can it be said except most foolishly that the Holy Spirit has no place in our body, which our soul has wholly filled: how much more foolishly is it said that the Trinity is anywhere hindered by any straits, so that the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit cannot be everywhere at once!

**5. 16.** But that is much more wonderful, that, although God is everywhere whole, he nevertheless does not dwell in all. For it cannot be said to all what the Apostle says, either what I have already said, or even this: *Do you not know that you are the temple of God, and the Spirit of God dwells in you?*[^17] Whence too, on the contrary, he says of certain ones: *But whoever does not have the Spirit of Christ, this one is not his*[^18]. Who, further, would dare to suppose—except whoever is utterly ignorant of the inseparability of the Trinity—that the Father or the Son could dwell in anyone in whom the Holy Spirit does not dwell, or the Holy Spirit in anyone in whom the Father and the Son do not also? Whence it must be confessed that God is everywhere by the presence of his divinity, but not everywhere by the grace of his dwelling. For on account of this dwelling, where without doubt the grace of his love is acknowledged, we do not say, Our Father who art everywhere, although this too is true, but: Our Father who art in heaven; that we may rather recall his temple in prayer, which we ourselves too ought to be, and insofar as we are, so far do we pertain to his society and the family of adoption. For if the people of God, not yet made equal to his angels, still in this pilgrimage is called his temple, how much more is his temple in heaven, where there is the people of angels, to whom we are to be joined and made equal, when, the pilgrimage finished, we shall have received what was promised!

**5. 17.** Since, then, he who is everywhere does not dwell in all; even in those in whom he dwells, he does not dwell equally. For whence is that which Elisha asked, that the Spirit of God which was in Elijah might be doubly in him? and whence, among all the saints, are some holier than others, except by having God the indweller more abundantly? How, then, did we above say truly that God is everywhere whole, when in some he is ampler, in some less? But what we said is not negligently to be considered, that he is everywhere whole in himself. Therefore he is not in those things which some grasp more, some less. For he is said to be everywhere, because he is absent from no part of things; whole, because he does not present his part to a part of things, and to another part another part, equal to equals, but to the lesser a lesser, and to the greater a greater; but he is wholly present, equally, not only to the universe of creation, but even to any part of it. And those are said to be far from him who, by sinning, have become most unlike; and these to draw near to him who, by living piously, receive his likeness. As eyes are rightly said to be so much the farther from this light, the blinder they are. For what is so far from light as blindness, even if the light be present and pour over extinguished eyes? But eyes are deservedly said to draw near the light, which advance, by the access of health, by receiving the keenness of sight.

**6. 18.** Further, what we thought might be too little distinctly understood when we said that God is everywhere whole, unless we added, In himself, I see must be expounded more diligently. For how is he everywhere, if in himself? Everywhere, namely, because he is nowhere absent. But in himself, because he is not contained by those to whom he is present, as though he could not be without them. For take away the spaces of places from bodies, they will be nowhere, and because they will be nowhere, they will not even be. Take away the bodies themselves from the qualities of bodies, there will be no place where they may be, and therefore it is necessary that they not be. For when a body is equally sound, or equally white, throughout its whole mass, there is not in one part of it greater health or whiteness than in another, nor greater in the whole than in a part of it; because the whole is not constituted sounder or whiter than the part. But if it be unequally sound or unequally white, it can come to pass that in a smaller part there be greater health or whiteness, when smaller members are sounder or whiter than larger; so far is it that what is said to be great or small in qualities consists in mass. Nevertheless, if the very mass of the body, however great or small it be, be utterly taken away, there will be no place where its qualities may be, although they are not to be measured by mass. But God, if he be grasped less by him to whom he is present, is not on that account himself less. For he is whole in himself, nor is he in those in whom he is so as to need them, as though he could not be except in them. But just as he is not absent from him in whom he does not dwell, and is wholly present, although that one does not have him; so to him in whom he dwells he is wholly present, although that one does not wholly grasp him.

**6. 19.** For he does not divide himself, for dwelling, through the hearts or bodies of men, granting one part of himself to this one, another to that, as this light through the entrances and windows of houses: but rather, if a deaf man does not grasp any sound, since it is a corporeal and transitory thing, and a hard-of-hearing man does not grasp the whole, and among those who hear, when they are equally near it, one grasps it more than another, the more acute he is, but less, the more obtuse his hearing, although it does not sound to one more variously, more or less, but is equally present to all in the place in which they are; how much more excellently does God, by his incorporeal nature and unchangeably living, who cannot, like sound, be stretched and divided through the delays of times, nor needs airy space as his own place, where he may be present to those present, but remaining in himself by eternal stability, can be wholly present to all things, and wholly to each, although those in whom he dwells have him according to the diversity of their capacity, some more, some less, whom he himself builds for himself, by the grace of his goodness, as a most beloved temple!

**6. 20.** And divisions of gifts indeed have been spoken of, as it were through the parts and members of one body, where we are both at once all one temple, and individually individual temples; because God is not greater in all than in each: and it comes about, for the most part, that the many grasp him less, one more. But when the Apostle had said: *But there are divisions of gifts*, he immediately added: *but the same Spirit*: likewise, when he had recalled those very divisions of gifts: *But all these*, he says, *one and the same Spirit works, dividing his own to each as he wills*[^19]. Dividing, then, not himself divided, because he is one and the same. But those divisions are spoken of, as of members in a body; because the ears do not avail the same as the eyes, and so the other members are concordantly distributed to diverse offices: which nevertheless, when we are sound, rejoice together in a common and equal health, one, and not diverse, nor greater in one place, lesser in another, but although they are themselves unequal. The head of this body is Christ, the unity of this body is commended by our sacrifice: which the Apostle briefly signified, saying: *One bread, one body, we many are*[^20]. Through our head we are reconciled to God, because in him the divinity of the Only-begotten was made a partaker of our mortality, that we too might be partakers of his immortality.

**6. 21.** This sacrament is far from the hearts of the proud wise, and therefore not Christian, and through this not truly wise. I speak even of those wise who knew God; because, knowing God, as the Apostle says, *they did not glorify him as God, or give thanks*[^21]. But you know in what sacrifice it is said: "Let us give thanks to the Lord our God." From the lowliness of which sacrifice the pomp and cothurnus of those people is far away. And therefore it is a very wonderful thing how God is the indweller of certain ones not yet knowing God, and of certain ones knowing him is not. For neither do those pertain to the temple of God who, knowing God, did not glorify him as God, or give thanks; and to the temple of God pertain little ones sanctified by the sacrament of Christ, regenerated by the Holy Spirit, who certainly through their age cannot yet know God: whence those could know him whom they could not have, these could have him before they knew him. But most blessed are those for whom to have God is the same as to know him: for that knowledge is the fullest, the truest, the happiest.

**7. 22.** Hence now we must treat also that question which you added after the subscription of your letter: "If little ones still do not know God, how could John, even before he was born, at the coming and presence of the mother of the Lord, exult in his mother's womb?" For when you had recalled that you had read my book On the Baptism of Little Ones, you added and said: "About pregnant women I desire to know what you think, since the mother of John the Baptist answered the faith of belief on behalf of the child."

**7. 23.** These are certainly the words of Elizabeth, the mother of John: *Blessed are you among women, and blessed is the fruit of your womb. And whence is this to me, that the mother of my Lord should come to me? For behold, as the voice of your salutation came to my ears, the infant exulted in joy in my womb*[^22]. But that she might say this, as the Evangelist prefaced, *she was filled with the Holy Spirit*[^23], by whose revealing she without doubt knew what that exultation of the infant signified; that is, that there had come the mother of him whose precursor and shower he was to be. There could, then, be that signifying of so great a matter to be known by the elders, not known by the little one. For neither, when this was narrated before in the Gospel, was it said: "The infant believed in his womb"; but, exulted: nor did she herself say: "The infant exulted in faith in my womb"; but, exulted in joy. But we see exultation not only of little ones, but even of cattle, surely not coming from any faith, or religion, or any rational knowledge whatever. But this plainly stood out as unusual and new, because it was in the womb, and at the coming of her who was going to bear the Savior of men. Therefore wonderful, therefore to be reckoned among great signs: therefore this exultation, and as it were a return-salutation rendered to the mother of the Lord, just as miracles are wont to come about, was done divinely in the infant, not humanly by the infant.

**7. 24.** Although even if the use of reason and will was so far accelerated in that child, that within the maternal members he could already recognize, believe, consent—which in other little ones the age is awaited that they may be able—even this is to be reckoned among the miracles of the divine power, not to be drawn to examples of human nature. For when God willed, even the dumb beast of burden spoke rationally; nor on that account are men admonished to await even asinine counsels in their deliberations. Wherefore I neither despise what was done in John, nor from there fix a rule of what is to be thought about little ones: nay rather I therefore wonderfully proclaim it in him, because in others I do not find it. There is indeed something similar also in that struggle of the twins in Rebecca's womb; but this too was so far a portent, that on that account the woman sought a divine oracle, and heard that by those two little infants two peoples were figured.

**7. 25.** But that little ones, who do not yet know even human things, do not know divine things, if we should wish to show it by words, I fear lest we seem to do injury to our very senses, when by speaking we persuade that, where the evidence of truth most easily surpasses all the powers and office of speech. Do we not see that, even when they have begun to produce some kind of signs of articulate voice, and to pass from infancy to the beginning of speaking, they still feel and say such things, in which, if they had remained, and with advancing years persisted in being such, no one—even one too foolish—would doubt them to be foolish? Unless perhaps it remains that, in the wailing of infancy, or in the very silence still of the womb, we believe the little ones to have been wise, but afterward, when they began to speak with us, to have come, by growing, to this ignorance which we see. Which to suppose, you see how absurd it is, since the sense of children bursting out into some kind of words—which is surely almost nothing toward the things the elders are wise about—is nevertheless, compared to that in which they are born, able to be called understanding. But whence, in that so great safeguard of salvation, when Christian grace comes to their aid, that they struggle against it with such voices and movements as they can is not imputed to them, and all their resistance is counted for nothing, until the sacraments be performed in them, by which what was drawn from the original damnation may be expiated—except because they so far do not know what they do, that they are not judged even to do it? Further, if they already used the choice of reason and will, by which assent ought to be applied to that sanctification, how great an evil it would be to resist so great a grace, and how not only what was done would profit nothing, but guilt would even increase from it, what Christian would not know?

**8. 26.** We say, then, that in baptized little ones, although they do not know it, the Holy Spirit dwells. For they so do not know him, although he is in them, as they do not know their own mind too; whose reason in them, which they cannot yet use, is as it were a certain spark put to sleep, to be roused by the access of age. Nor ought this to seem a wonder in little ones, since the Apostle says even to certain older ones: *Do you not know that you are the temple of God, and the Spirit of God dwells in you?*[^24]—of whom a little before he had said: *But the animal man does not perceive the things that are of the Spirit of God*[^25]; whom he calls little ones too, not by the age of the flesh, but of the mind. They did not, therefore, perceive by knowledge the Holy Spirit who dwelt in them; and, the Holy Spirit dwelling in them, they were still animal, not yet spiritual, because they could not yet perceive by knowledge their own indweller, the Spirit.

**8. 27.** But he is said to dwell even in such for this reason: because in them he acts secretly, that they may be his temple; and this he perfects in those advancing and persevering in advancing. *For by hope we have been saved*, as the Apostle says; although in another place he says: *He saved us by the washing of regeneration*[^26]. When, then, here he says, He saved us, as though salvation itself were already given, how it is to be understood, he expounds there, where he says: *For by hope we have been saved. But hope which is seen is not hope; for what anyone sees, why does he hope for it? But if we hope for what we do not see, by patience we await it*[^27]. For many things are said in the divine Scriptures as though they were done, when they are understood to be still in hope. Whence is that too which the Lord says to the disciples: *All things which I heard from my Father I have made known to you*[^28]: which is so far said according to the hope of future things, that he afterward says to them: *I have many things to say to you, but you cannot bear them now*[^29]. He acts, then, in those mortals in whom he still dwells, the very building of his dwelling, which he perfects not in this, but in another life after this, when death shall be swallowed up in victory, and it shall be said to it: *Where, O death, is your victory? Where, O death, is your sting?* But what is the sting of death, except sin?[^30]

**8. 28.** On account of which, even now, reborn of water and the Spirit, and all our sins—whether of origin from Adam, in whom all sinned, or of our deeds, words, and thoughts—deleted in the cleansing of that washing; yet, because we remain in this human life which is a temptation upon the earth, we deservedly say: *Forgive us our debts*. And this prayer the whole Church says, which the Savior cleanses by the washing of water in the word, that he may present it to himself glorious, not having spot, or wrinkle, or any such thing; then, surely, when it shall be perfected in fact, where now, by advancing, one walks in hope. For how is it now not having spot, or wrinkle, or any such thing, which, whether in all the men pertaining to it who already use the reason of the mind and the choice of the will, and bear the burden of mortal flesh, or surely—which even the contentious must necessarily confess—in many of its members truly says: *Forgive us our debts*?

**8. 29.** Since, then, those advancing, in whom, while mortal, he dwells, while from day to day they are renewed, he justifies more and more, hears them praying, cleanses them confessing, that he may present to himself a temple immaculate forever; he is deservedly said not to dwell in those who, knowing God, did not glorify him as God, or give thanks. For by worshiping and serving the creature rather than the Creator, they were unwilling to be the temple of the one true God: and so, while they wish to have him with many, they more easily brought it about that they did not have him, than that they should mingle him with many or false gods. And he is deservedly said to dwell in those whom, called according to his purpose, he took up to be justified and glorified, even before they are able to know his incorporeal nature, which is everywhere whole, as far as it can be known in part, through a mirror and in an enigma, by a man in this life, when he has advanced most. For there are, in whom he dwells, many such as the Apostle says: *I could not speak to you as to spiritual, but as to carnal. As to little ones in Christ, I gave you milk to drink, not food: for you were not yet able; but neither indeed are you yet able*[^31]. To these surely he says also that: *Do you not know that you are the temple of God, and the Spirit of God dwells in you?* If the last day of this life finds such people, even before they come to the spiritual age of mind, where they are nourished not with milk, but with solid food; their indweller will perfect in them whatever of understanding they had less here, since they did not withdraw from the unity of the body of Christ who was made the way for us, and from the society of the temple of God. Whence, that they may not withdraw, they perseveringly hold in the Church the rule of faith common to little and great, and walk in that to which they have come, until God reveal to them, if they are wise in anything otherwise; they do not dogmatize their carnal thoughts; because they do not harden by remaining in contentious defenses, but in a way by walking, that is, by advancing, they sweat, obtaining the perspicuity of understanding through the piety of faith.

**9. 30.** Since these things are so, those two things which come about in one man, to be born and to be reborn, pertain to two men: one to that first Adam, the other to the second who is called Christ. But, the Apostle says, *not first what is spiritual, but what is animal; afterward the spiritual. The first man of the earth, earthly; the second man from heaven, heavenly: as is the earthly, such also are the earthly; and as is the heavenly, such also are the heavenly. As we have borne the image of the earthly, let us bear also the image of him who is from heaven*[^32]: likewise he says: *Through one man death, and through one man the resurrection of the dead. For as in Adam all die, so also in Christ all will be made alive*[^33]. He said all, and all, for this reason: because into death no one but through that one, into life no one but through this one. In the first it lay open what man's choice was worth toward death; but in the second, what the help of God was worth toward life. Finally, the first man, only man; but the second, God and man: for sin was committed, God being abandoned; righteousness does not come about without God. And through this we would neither die, unless by carnal propagation we came from his members; nor would we live, unless by spiritual connection we were members of this one. Therefore it was needful for us to be born, and to be reborn, but for him, for our sake, only to be born. For we, by being reborn, pass from sin to righteousness; but he made a passage from no sin to righteousness: but in that he was baptized, he commended the sacrament of our regeneration, more deeply by his lowliness; signifying, nevertheless, our old man by his passion, our new man by his resurrection.

**9. 31.** For the disobedience of the concupiscence which dwells in mortal flesh, by which it comes about that those same members are moved apart from the choice of the will, is reduced by conjugal righteousness to such a measure, that they be generated by parents lawfully joined, for whom it is necessary to be regenerated. Yet Christ did not wish to come by a meeting of male and female of this kind to his flesh; but from a virgin desiring nothing of the kind in his conception, he took the likeness of the flesh of sin for us, by which the flesh of sin might be cleansed in us. For as through the offense of one, the Apostle says, upon all men unto condemnation; so through the righteousness of one upon all men unto justification of life. For no one is born except by carnal concupiscence working, which is drawn from the first man who is Adam; and no one is reborn except by spiritual grace working, which is given through the second man, who is Christ. Wherefore, if by being born we pertain to that one, by being reborn to this one, and no one can be reborn before he is born; surely that one was born singularly, who did not need to be reborn; because he did not make a passage from sin, in which he never was, nor was he conceived in iniquity, nor did his mother nourish him in sins in the womb: because the Holy Spirit came upon her, and the power of the Most High overshadowed her; whence what was born of her, holy, is called the Son of God. For the good of marriage does not extinguish, but moderates, the evil of the disobedient members, so that, carnal concupiscence being in a way limited, there may at least be conjugal chastity. But the Virgin Mary, to whom it was said, *And the power of the Most High will overshadow you*[^34], in conceiving the holy offspring, burned with no ardor of this concupiscence under such a shade. With this corner stone excepted, then, I do not see how men are built into a house of God to have God dwelling in them, except when they have been reborn; which they cannot be before they are born.

**10. 32.** Whatever opinion, then, we have about pregnant women—or rather about men still set within the maternal members—whether we think that they can or cannot be granted some mode of sanctification; whether on account of John, who, not yet brought forth into this light, nevertheless exulted in joy (which surely who would believe could have come about except by the working of the Holy Spirit?), or on account of Jeremiah, to whom the Lord says: *Before you came out of the womb, I sanctified you*[^35]: yet that sanctification by which we are made both individually temples of God, and all together one temple of God, is only of the reborn; which men cannot be unless born. But no one will well end the life in which he was born, unless reborn before he ends it.

**10. 33.** But if anyone says that a man is already born even when he is still in the mother's womb, and adduces a testimony from the Gospel, because it was said to Joseph about the pregnant Virgin, mother of the Lord: *For what is born in her is of the Holy Spirit*[^36]; does there accede to this nativity a second nativity? Otherwise it will not now be a second, but a third. But the Lord, when he spoke of this: *Unless one*, he says, *be reborn anew*[^37]; reckoning, namely, as the first that nativity which comes about by the mother bringing forth, not conceiving and pregnant, which comes about from her, not which comes about in her. For neither do we say that a man whom the mother bore is reborn, as though he were born again, who had already once been born in the womb: but, that nativity not being reckoned which makes her pregnant, a man is said to be born by birth, that he may be able to be reborn of water and the Spirit. According to which nativity from the mother, even the Lord himself is said to be born in Bethlehem of Judah. If, then, a man can be regenerated by the grace of the Spirit in the womb, since it still remains for him to be born, he is therefore reborn before he is born; which can in no way come about. Accordingly, into the framework of the body of Christ, as into a living structure of the temple of God, which is his Church, men born are transferred, not by works of righteousness which they did, but by being reborn through grace, as from the mass of ruin to the foundation of the building. For besides this building, which is constructed to be blessed for the eternal habitation of God, all the life of man is unhappy, and is to be called death rather than life. Whoever, then, is to be dwelt in by God, that the wrath of God may not remain over him, will not be alien from this body, from this temple, from this city. But everyone not reborn is alien.

**11. 34.** Further, the sacrament of our regeneration the Mediator, manifested, wished to be manifest. But to the ancient righteous it was something hidden, although they too were saved by that same faith, which was to be revealed in its own time. For we do not dare to prefer the faithful of our time to the friends of God through whom these things were prophesied to us, since God commends himself to be the God of Abraham and the God of Isaac and the God of Jacob, so that he says this is his name forever. But if circumcision is believed to have been for the ancient saints in place of baptism, what will be answered about those who, before this was commanded, pleased God, yet not without faith? Because without faith, as it is written to the Hebrews, it is impossible to please God. But having, the Apostle says, the same Spirit of faith, according to what is written, *I believed, therefore I have spoken; we also believe, therefore we also speak*[^38]. He would not say, the same, unless their Spirit of faith too were that same one. But just as they, when that same sacrament was hidden, believed the incarnation of Christ to be future, so we too believe it accomplished: and both by us and by them his coming to judgment is awaited as future. For there is no other mystery of God except Christ, in whom the dead in Adam must be made alive; because as in Adam all die, so also in Christ all will be made alive, as we discussed above.

**12. 35.** Wherefore God, who is everywhere present, and everywhere whole, does not dwell in all, but only in those whom he makes his most blessed temple, or his most blessed temples, rescuing them from the power of darkness, and transferring them into the kingdom of the Son of his love, which begins from regeneration. But his temple is spoken of in one way by signification, when it comes about through the hands of men from inanimate things, as the tabernacle, of woods, veils, skins, and movable things of this kind; as also through king Solomon, a temple constructed of stones, woods, metals: but in another way by the very true thing which was figured by those significations. Whence it is said: *And you, as living stones, are built up a spiritual house*[^39]: and whence likewise it is written: *For we are the temple of the living God, as God says: I will dwell in them and walk among them; and I will be their God, and they shall be my people*[^40].

**12. 36.** Nor ought it to move us, that through certain ones not pertaining to this temple, or not yet pertaining—that is, in whom God does not dwell, or does not yet dwell—he works some power; as through that one who cast out demons in the name of Christ, although he did not follow Christ, whom he ordered to be permitted, on account of the commendation, useful to many, of his name. He says too that many will say to him on the last day: *In your name we did many mighty works*[^41]; to whom surely he would not answer: I do not know you, if they pertained to the temple of God, which by dwelling he blesses. Cornelius too, the centurion, before he was incorporated into this temple by regeneration, saw an angel sent to him, and heard him saying that his prayers were heard, and his alms accepted. For God does these things as everywhere present, or through his holy angels.

**12. 37.** For that sanctification of Jeremiah, before he came out of the womb, although some take it as a type of the Savior, who did not need regeneration; yet even if it be taken of the Prophet himself, it can not unfittingly be understood also according to predestination: as the Gospel calls those not yet regenerated sons of God, where Caiaphas, when he had said about the Lord: *It is expedient for you that one man die for the people, and the whole nation not perish*; the Gospel, following on, immediately added: *But this he said not of himself; but being high priest of that year he prophesied that Jesus was to die for the nation; and not only for the nation, but that he might gather into one the sons of God who were dispersed*[^42]. He called sons of God surely, besides the Hebrew nation, those set also among all the other nations, not yet faithful, not yet baptized. How, then, sons of God, except according to predestination, according to which the Apostle too says that *God chose us in Christ before the foundation of the world*[^43]? But that gathering into one was going to make them sons of God. For neither was it said into one as some corporeal place, since the prophet foretold concerning such a calling of the nations: *And they will adore him, each one from his own place, all the islands of the nations*[^44]; but, gather into one, was said into one spirit, and into one body, of which the one head is Christ. Such a gathering is the building of the temple of God. Such a gathering not carnal generation, but spiritual regeneration makes.

**13. 38.** God dwells, then, in individuals as in his temples, and in all together gathered into one, as in his temple. Which temple, as long as, like the ark of Noah, it floats in this world, there comes about what is written in the Psalm: *The Lord inhabits the flood*: although also, on account of the many peoples of the faithful among all nations, whom the Apocalypse signifies by the name of waters, it can be fittingly understood: *The Lord inhabits the flood*. But there follows: *And the Lord will sit as king forever*[^45]: surely in his very temple now set in eternal life after the floating of this world. God, therefore, is everywhere present, and everywhere wholly present; nor everywhere dwelling, but in his temple, to which by grace he is kind and propitious: but he is grasped, dwelling, by some more, by some less.

**13. 39.** But of our very head the Apostle says: *That in him dwells all the fullness of the divinity bodily*[^46]. Not bodily because God is corporeal; but either he used a transferred word, as it were he dwelt in a man-made temple not bodily but shadowily, that is, by prefiguring signs—for he calls all those observances *shadows of things to come*[^47], even by a transferred word; for the supreme God, as it is written, does not dwell in man-made temples—or surely it was said bodily, because in the body of Christ too which he assumed from the Virgin, as in a temple, God dwells. For hence it is that, when to the Jews seeking a sign he had said: *Destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up*; the evangelist, expounding accordingly what this was, says: *But this he said of the temple of his body*[^48].

**13. 40.** What, then, is it? Do we think this is the difference between the head and the rest of the members, that in any member, however principal—as in some great prophet or apostle—although divinity dwells, yet not, as in the head which is Christ, all the fullness of the divinity? For in our body too sense is in the individual members; but not so great as in the head, where it is altogether fivefold: for there are sight, and hearing, and smell, and taste, and touch; but in the rest there is touch alone. Or, besides this—that, as in a temple, in that body dwells all the fullness of the divinity—is there something else that is the difference between that head and the excellence of any member? There is plainly: that by a certain singular assumption of that man one person was made with the Word. For of no saint could it be said, or can be, or will be able to be: *The Word was made flesh*: no saint, by any preeminence of grace, received the name of Only-begotten, that what the Word of God itself is before the ages, this it should be called together with the assumed man. Singular, then, is that assumption, nor can it in any way be common with any holy men, however preeminent in wisdom and holiness. Where there is a sufficiently perspicuous and clear proof of divine grace. For who would be so sacrilegious as to dare to affirm that any soul could, by the merit of free choice, bring it about that it be another Christ? That, then, one single soul should pertain to the person of the only-begotten Word, by what pact, through free choice given commonly to all and naturally, could it have merited, unless a singular grace had bestowed this; which it is lawful to proclaim, about which it is wicked to wish to judge?

**13. 41.** If these things we have, according to our strength, as much as the Lord helped, rightly treated; when you extend yourself to think of God everywhere present, and not diffused by distant spaces, as it were by some mass or extension, but everywhere whole, turn your mind away from all the images of bodies which human thought is wont to turn over. For neither so is wisdom, nor justice, nor so, finally, love thought of, about which it is written: *God is love*[^49]. But when you think of his dwelling, think of the unity and gathering of the saints: especially in heaven, where for this reason he is chiefly said to dwell, because there his will is made the perfect obedience of those in whom he dwells; then on earth, where, building, he dwells in his house, to be dedicated at the end of the age. But that Christ our Lord, the only-begotten Son of God, equal to the Father, and the same son of man, than whom the Father is greater, is both everywhere whole present as God, and in that same temple of God as the indwelling God, and in some place of heaven on account of the manner of a true body, do not doubt. But when it delights me to speak with you, I do not know whether I have kept the measure of sufficient discourse, as if I were compensating for a long silence by loquacity. But because by the religion and benevolence with which you anticipated me, you are so engrafted into my heart, that I converse with you as with a friend truly: whatever in the work of our pen you recognize to have been usefully wrought, give thanks to God. But if you perceive any faults of mine, as a most dear friend pardon them; wishing, namely, medicine for me with that same sincerity of love with which you also grant pardon.
