# Letter 143

To the most excellent lord, and deservedly distinguished, and much most dear son Marcellinus, Augustine sends greetings in the Lord.

**1.** Your letter, which I received through my holy brother and fellow bishop Boniface, I sought, when I was writing back, and did not find. Yet I recalled that you asked in it how the magicians of Pharaoh found, all the water of Egypt being turned into blood, whence to do something similar. This question is wont to be solved in two ways: either because sea water could be offered; or, what is more credible, because in that region in which the children of Israel were, those plagues did not happen. For this is most plainly expressed in certain places of that Scripture, even where it is not said what ought to be understood.

**2.** But that letter of yours which the priest Urbanus brought has a question set forth to me from books not divine, but mine, which I wrote On Free Will. But in such questions I do not labor much: because, even if my opinion cannot be defended by clear reasoning, it is mine; not that of the author whose sense it is not right to disapprove, even when, it not being understood, something is thence held which is to be disapproved. I, accordingly, confess that I try to be of the number of those who write by progressing, and progress by writing. Whence, if anything has been set down by me either too incautiously or too unlearnedly, which not only by others who can see it is deservedly reprehended, but even by myself, because I too ought at least afterward to see it, if I progress; it is neither to be wondered at nor grieved over: but rather to be pardoned and congratulated; not because there was error, but because it was disapproved. For he loves himself too perversely who wishes others too to err, that his error may lie hidden. For how much better and more useful, that where one has himself erred, others may not err, by whose admonition he may be rid of his error! Which if he is unwilling, let him at least not have companions of his error. For if God shall grant me what I wish, that I may collect and demonstrate, by some work instituted for this very purpose, whatever in all my books most rightly displeases me; then men will see how I am not a respecter of my own person.

**3.** But you who love me much, if you assert me to be such, against those by whose malice, or unskillfulness, or understanding I am reprehended, that you say I have nowhere erred in my writings; you labor in vain, you have undertaken a not good cause, you are easily overcome in it, with me myself as judge. Since it does not please me, when I am esteemed by my dearest ones to be such as I am not. For surely they love not me, but another for me under my name, if they love not what I am, but what I am not. For insofar as they know me, or believe true of me, I am loved by them: but insofar as they attribute to me what they do not recognize in me, they love another such as they think me, in place of me. Tullius, the greatest author of Roman eloquence, says of someone: "He never uttered any word which he would wish to recall." Which praise, although it seems most illustrious, is yet more credible of one too fatuous than of one perfectly wise. For even those whom the crowd calls fools, the more they dissent from common sense, and are more absurd and insipid, the more they utter no word which they would wish to recall; because to repent of a bad, or foolish, or inconvenient saying, is surely of the sensible. But if it be taken in a good part, that we believe someone to have been such that, while he spoke all things wisely, he never uttered any word which he would wish to recall; this is rather to be believed, with most wholesome piety, of the men of God, who spoke by the keen Holy Spirit, than of him whom Cicero so praises. From this excellence I am so far that, if I bring forth no word which I would wish to recall, I am more like a fool than a wise man. For his writings are most worthy of the highest authority who uttered no word, not which he would wish to recall, but which he ought to recall. Whoever has not yet attained this, let him have the second part, of modesty, because he could not have the first, of wisdom: because he was not able diligently to say all things not to be repented of, let him repent of those which he shall have recognized ought not to have been said.

**4.** Since, then—not, as certain of my dearest ones suppose, no words, or few, but rather perhaps more words than even the slanderous suppose—I have said which I would prefer to recall, if I could; that Tullian saying does not flatter me, by which it was said, "He never uttered any word which he would wish to recall": but the Horatian saying plainly distresses me: "A word once sent forth knows not how to return." Hence it is that I hold the books of most dangerous questions—namely, those On Genesis, and On the Trinity—longer than you wish and bear; so that, if they cannot but have some things which are deservedly reprehended, they may at least be fewer than they could be, if they were brought forth by headlong haste too inconsiderately. For you—as your letter indicates (for this my holy brother and fellow bishop Florentius too wrote to me)—therefore urge that they be brought forth, that they may be able, while I live in this flesh, to be defended by me, when perhaps in certain things they shall begin to be accused, either by biting enemies, or even by friends who understand too little. Which you surely say on this account, that you do not think there is anything in them which by true reasoning can be blamed: otherwise you would exhort me not to the publication of them, but rather to a more diligent emendation. But I look rather to true judges, severe by truth, among whom I wish first to set even myself; so that to them only those things may come to be reprehended which by me, however diligently searching, could in no way be seen.

**5.** Since these things are so, yet this which in the third book On Free Will, when I was dealing with rational substance, was so set down by me that I said: *But in the lower bodies the soul, after sin, ordered, rules its own body, not in every way at its choice, but as the laws of the universe permit*: let those diligently notice who think that I established and fixed something about the human soul as certain—either that it comes from the parents by propagation, or that it sinned in the acts of a higher and heavenly life, so that it deserved to be enclosed in corruptible flesh; and let them see that the words were so weighed by me that, holding that which I hold for certain—that after the sin of the first man the rest of men were born and are born in the flesh of sin, for whose healing came, in the Lord, the likeness of the flesh of sin—all things should sound in such a way as to prejudice none of those four opinions which afterward I set out and distinguished, confirming none, but meanwhile, the discussion of them being set aside, determining what I was dealing with, so that, whichever of them were true, God without doubt should be praised.

**6.** For whether all souls are propagated from that one, or are made individually in individuals, or, created outside, are sent in, or of their own accord plunge into bodies; without doubt this rational creature—that is, the nature of the human soul—in the lower, that is, earthly bodies, after sin, ordered, rules its own body, not in every way at its choice, as long as the sin of the first man stands. For it was not said, "After his own sin," or, "After he sinned"; but it was said, "After sin": so that, whatever reason might afterward declare, if it could, whether his sin, or the sin of the parent of his flesh, that which was said might be understood rightly said, "After sin, ordered in the lower bodies, it rules its own body, not in every way at its choice," because both the flesh lusts against the spirit, and we groan being burdened, and the body which is corrupted weighs down the soul: and who could narrate all the discomforts of carnal weakness? which surely will not be, when this corruptible shall have put on incorruption, that the mortal be absorbed by life. Then, accordingly, the spiritual body will rule in every way at its choice; but now not in every way, but as the laws of the universe permit, by which it has been established that bodies, having arisen, perish, and, having grown, grow old. For the soul of that first man, before sin, even though not yet a spiritual but an animal body, yet ruled it at its choice. But after sin—that is, after sin was committed in that flesh, from which thereafter the flesh of sin was to be propagated—the rational soul is so ordered in the lower bodies that it does not in every way rule its own body at its choice. But if they do not yet acquiesce, in little children, who have as yet committed no sins of their own, that there is already the flesh of sin, because for this too, to be healed, when they are baptized, that likeness of the flesh of sin is a necessary medicine; not even so have they reason to be angry at these words of ours. For it is agreed, unless I am mistaken, that the same flesh, even if it is weak not by vice but by nature, yet began to be born after sin: because neither was Adam so created, nor did he beget anyone before sin.

**7.** Let them seek, then, other things which they may rightly reprehend, not only in others brought forth too hastily, but even in these my very books On Free Will. For I do not deny that they will find whence they may confer a benefit on me; since, if those books—because they have already gone forth into the hands of many—cannot be corrected, I at least, since I still live, can. But these words, so cautiously set down by me that I prescribe nothing to any of the four opinions or reasonings about the origin of the soul, let those only reprehend who think that this very hesitation of mine about so obscure a matter is to be reprehended: against whom I do not defend myself, that I do rightly to hesitate in this question, since I altogether do not doubt both that the soul is immortal—not as God is, who alone has immortality, but in a certain manner of its own kind—and that it is a creature, not the substance of the Creator, and whatever else most certain I hold about its nature. But because the obscurity of the most foggy question about the origin of the soul compels me to do this, let them rather stretch out a hand to one confessing, and desiring to know whatever that is; and let them teach, if they can, or show, if they have learned anything about this matter either by certain reasoning or by the most plain divine word. For if reasoning is rendered against the authority of the divine Scriptures, however acute it be, it deceives by a likeness to the true; for it cannot be true. Again, if to a most manifest and certain reasoning the authority of the holy Scriptures is opposed, as it were, he who does this does not understand, and opposes to the truth not the sense of those Scriptures, to which he could not penetrate, but rather his own, nor objects what he finds in them, but what he finds in himself as it were for them.

**8.** For example: attend diligently to what I shall say. It is written near the end of the book which is called Ecclesiastes, when Scripture was speaking of the dissolution of man, which happens through this death by which the soul is sundered from the body: *And let the dust return into the earth, as it was; and let the spirit return to God who gave it*[^1]. The opinion of this authority is without doubt certain, nor does it deceive anyone by falsehood: but if anyone should wish so to interpret it as to try to establish thence the propagation of souls—that from that one which God gave to the first man, all the rest come—it seems to favor him that there it was said of the flesh by the name of dust (for surely dust and spirit are nothing else in this place than soul and flesh), so that in that way he may affirm that the soul returns to God because it is of the propagation of that soul which God gave to the first man, in the same way as the flesh is turned into earth, since it too is of the propagation of that flesh which in the first man was made from the earth: and so he may contend that, from what is most well known about the flesh, we ought to believe that which is hidden about the soul. For about the propagation of the flesh there is no doubt, but about the soul there is doubt. But both are so set in this testimony that the individual things seem to be rendered to the individual by a parallel reasoning: namely, that the flesh be turned into earth, as it was; for thence it was taken, when the first man was made: and that the spirit return to God, who gave it, when he breathed into the face of the man whom he had formed the breath of life, and the man was made into a living soul, so that thereafter from each thing the propagation of each thing should run on.

**9.** Nevertheless, if that is true, that God gives souls, not propagated from that one, but created elsewhere, individual ones to individuals, this opinion too agrees with what was said, "Let the spirit return to God who gave it." Only the remaining two opinions, then, seem to be excluded; because, if proper souls were made in individual men, when they are created, it is thought it ought not to have been said, "Let the spirit return to God who gave it," but "to God who made it": for "gave" sounds, as it were, as though that already existed outside which could be given. Then that which was said, "let it return to God," they urge with the word and press, saying: How will it return, where it never before was? For it ought rather, they assert, to have been said, "And let it go to God," or "let it proceed to God," than "Let it return to God," if it is to be believed that this spirit never before was there. Likewise, how souls glide of their own accord into the body, when it is written, "gave it," is not so easy to explain. And through this, as I said, those two opinions labor under the words of this testimony: one, by which the individual souls are thought to be made in their individual bodies; the other, by which they are thought to plunge of their own accord into bodies. But to those other two—whether they come from the propagation of that one, or, already created beforehand and established with God, are given to individual bodies—these words are fitted without labor.

**10.** And yet, if the assertors of that opinion by which it is believed that souls are made in their individual bodies should affirm that it was so said of the spirit—which is set for the soul—"God gave it," in the way it is rightly said that he gave us eyes, or ears, or hands, or anything else; which members surely he had not already made outside, and held stored somewhere, that, when there was need, he might give them—that is, add and join them—but there made them in the body, to which he is said to have given them: what is to be answered to him I do not see; unless perhaps either other testimonies be brought forth, or a certain reasoning whence this opinion may be refuted. Likewise those who think that souls flow of their own accord into bodies take what was said, "God gave it," in the way it was said, *God handed them over to the concupiscence of their heart*[^2]. Accordingly one word is left, which was said, "let it return to God," in what way it can be taken that it returns where before it was not, if souls are made each in its own body; by which one word alone one of those four opinions is straitened. But not even on account of this one word do I rashly judge that opinion is to be refuted, lest perhaps it can be shown, by some kind of locution which holy Scripture is wont to use, that this too could have been rightly said, so that the created spirit may be understood to return to God, as to the author by whom it was created, not as to him with whom it primarily was.

**11.** These things I have written for this reason, that whoever shall wish to establish and defend any of those four opinions about the soul, let him bring forth such things, either from the Scriptures received into ecclesiastical authority, which cannot be taken otherwise—as is, that God made man—or a reasoning so certain that there exists either no contradiction, or one deservedly judged like to madness; as if anyone should say that neither can anyone know the truth, nor be deceived, except one living. For, that we may see how true this is, the authority of the Scriptures is not necessary; rather, common sense itself by perspicuous reasoning so proclaims it to be true that whoever contradicts it is held most demented. If anyone can furnish this in that most obscure question which is about the soul, let him help my unskillfulness: but if he cannot, let him not blame my hesitation.

**12.** As for the virginity of holy Mary, if what I have written does not persuade anyone that it could have happened, then everything that has happened marvelously in bodies must be denied. But if it is not believed for the reason that it happened only once, ask the friend whom this still troubles whether nothing is to be found in secular literature that likewise happened only once and yet was believed, not with the vanity of fable but, as they suppose, with the trust due to history; ask him, I beg you. For if he denies that any such thing is to be found in those writings, he must be set straight; but if he admits it, the question is resolved.

[^1]: Eccl 12:7
[^2]: Rom 1:24
