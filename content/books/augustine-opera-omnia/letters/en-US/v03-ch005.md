# Letter 160

Evodius to bishop Augustine, greetings.

**1.** Perfect is that reason which furnishes the knowledge of all things, and especially of the eternal things that are grasped by the understanding: that this reason is eternal, and ought to have been eternal; that what neither began, nor is changed, nor is varied is eternal—reason itself teaches and shows; and reason must be eternal, not only because it teaches and demonstrates eternal things, but much more because eternity itself cannot be without reason; since, I think, there would be no eternity if reason itself had not been eternal. Then, that God is, or ought to have been, and ought to come about in no other way than that God should be, reason demonstrates. And whether or not there were those who knew this, nevertheless, since God is eternal, it must not be doubted that the reason is eternal which would maintain that God ought to be, and so that it would show itself to be coeternal with him.

**2.** But there are certain things which are compelled by reason to be, so that the reason is prior, and the effect of that thing which reason shows is to be is posterior: as, for example, when the world was made, reason had it that the world should be made. Reason, therefore, is prior to the world. Those things, then, which reason knew were to be, followed after, so that reason is first, and afterward the working of the world. Now, then, since reason shows that God is, or that it is necessary that God should be, which shall we set before which? Reason before God, or reason before the world, or God before reason, without which it is probable that God in no way is? For if to be eternal is, for God, to be eternal by reason, what is reason? Therefore it is either God, or of God, as reason itself teaches: and if it is itself God, reason shows that God is reason, and these can be coeval and coeternal. But if this reason is the likeness of God, it shows likewise that the reason is of God: and this will be coeval and coeternal. But that God could likewise not be unless God were, reason itself shows: and if this reason be taken away, which it is wicked to say, God will not be, if reason did not show that God is necessary. Then, therefore, God is, when his reason shows that God is. God therefore is, because he is: without doubt there is reason which taught that he is.

**3.** What, then, is first in God, if it can be said—reason, or God? But God will not be unless there were the reason that teaches that God ought to be. Reason too will not be unless God will be. There is therefore nothing first and last there. This divine nature, then, in a way has reason and God at once. But one begets one, either reason God, or God reason. But let reason and God be called perhaps the subject, or in the subject. God and reason are one in one. But God rightly begets reason, since reason demonstrates that God is. And God is understood by reason as the Son by the Father, and reason by God as the Father by the Son. For reason itself with God is God. For God never was without reason, nor reason without God. For then God is, if reason is; and then the Son is, if the Father is; so that if reason be taken away—as has been said, which it is not lawful to say—not even God himself is: for by his reason there is act and operation, so that he is God. Again, let us say the same things. If reason was not, God was not; and if God is not, reason was not. Reason therefore and God are an everlasting thing; and God and reason in a like way are an everlasting thing. But the connection and union of reason to God, and of God to reason, of the Father to the Son, of the Son to the Father, furnish each other, in a way, beginnings and causes of existing; since the one cannot be without the other. We fail in words, and whatever is said is said for this: that there not be silence. But shall we call God the seed of reason, or reason the seed of God? Since neither can fruit be without a root, nor is a root anything without fruit. (Let the likeness be drawn, so that something of understanding about God may be marked: for in a grain of wheat too there lives a fructifying reason, by which it is not allowed to be barren; but again, if there were no grain of wheat, reason would have nothing from which it might produce these things.)

**4.** Since, therefore, the reason which is God either shows God to be reason, or reason to be God, in a way showing the one by the other; the Father is not shown except through the Son, nor is the Son shown except through the Father; and then the Son is as it were in silence, when through the Father one comes to the Son, so that the one is in a way hidden, the other demonstrated: so that the one, demonstrating himself, demonstrates also the other, and the one cannot be known so that the other lies hidden; because, *He who has seen me*, he says, *has seen the Father also*[^1]; and, *No one comes to the Father except through me*[^2]; and, *No one comes to me unless the Father has drawn him*[^3]. We have entered upon an arduous and exceedingly difficult business, that we might understand something of God which we do not understand. Nevertheless, just as all things that are are not understood and are unknowable without some form, so much more without the Son, that is, without reason, is God unknown. For what? Was the Father ever without reason, irrational? Who would dare to say this? Therefore God is to be known by reason, one from one, or one in one to be at once one: because God is one, in whom must necessarily be that love which reason itself teaches must always be had, or which God commands must be shown.

[^1]: Jn 14:9
[^2]: Jn 14:6
[^3]: Jn 6:44
