# Letter 194

TO SIXTUS, MOST BELOVED LORD IN THE LORD OF LORDS, HOLY BROTHER AND FELLOW PRESBYTER, AUGUSTINE SENDS GREETING IN THE LORD.

**1. 1.** In the letter I sent by our most dear brother Albinus the acolyte, I promised that I would send a longer one by our holy brother and fellow presbyter Firmus, who brought us the letter of your Sincerity, full of the sincerity of your faith, which conferred on us joy as great as we are more able to have than to express. For—this must be confessed to your Charity—we were exceedingly saddened when rumor was spreading abroad that you favored the enemies of Christian grace. But, that this sadness might be wiped from our hearts, that same rumor did not keep silent that, first of all, you yourself before a very large congregation had been the first to pronounce an anathema upon them; then, when the letters of the Apostolic See concerning their condemnation had been sent to Africa, your own letters too followed, addressed to the venerable old man Aurelius; and though these were brief, they sufficiently indicated your vigor against their error. But now, when in your letters the very faith of the Roman Church—to which especially the blessed apostle Paul spoke much and in many ways concerning the grace of God through Jesus Christ our Lord—spoke with us more openly and more fully as to what you thought about and against that doctrine, not only did all that cloud of sadness flee from our hearts, but so great a light of gladness shone there that that grief and fear seemed to have done nothing in us except to make the ensuing joys burn the more amply.

**1. 2.** And so, most dear brother, although we do not see you with the eyes of the flesh, yet in mind, in the faith of Christ, in the grace of Christ, in the members of Christ, we hold, embrace, and kiss you; and now that the most holy and most faithful bearer of our mutual conversations is returning from us to you—whom you wished to be with us not only the conveyor of your writings but also the narrator and witness of what has been done—we render our reply and converse with you somewhat longer, urging that you press on in teaching those whom, as far as we have learned, you have sufficiently pressed in frightening. For there are some who think that impieties most justly condemned are still to be defended rather freely; and there are those who penetrate homes more secretly, and what they now fear to cry out in the open they do not cease to sow in secret; and there are those who have fallen wholly silent, restrained by great fear, but who still retain in their heart what they no longer dare to bring forth with their mouth—men who nevertheless may be very well known to the brethren from their earlier defense of that very doctrine. Accordingly, some are to be more severely restrained, others more watchfully tracked down, others to be handled indeed more gently but no less diligently taught, so that, if they are not feared lest they ruin others, still they are not neglected lest they perish.

**2. 3.** For when they think that free choice is taken from them if they do not agree that a man does not have even a good will itself without the help of God, they do not understand that they are not strengthening human choice but puffing it up, so that it is carried along through empty things and not set in the Lord as on a stable rock: for *the will is prepared by the Lord*[^1].

**2. 4.** But when they suppose that they believe God to be a respecter of persons if they believe that, without any preceding merits, *he has mercy on whom he wills*[^2], and calls those whom he deigns to call, and makes whom he wills religious, they pay too little attention to the fact that the owed penalty is rendered to the condemned, and unowed grace to the one set free, so that neither may the former complain that he is unworthy, nor the latter boast that he is worthy; and that there is rather no respecting of persons there, where one and the same mass of damnation and offense enfolds them, so that the one set free may learn from the one not set free that the same punishment would befit him too, had not grace come to his aid. But if it is grace, then it is rendered for no merits, but bestowed by gratuitous goodness.

**2. 5.** "But it is unjust," they say, "that in one and the same evil cause this one should be set free and that one punished." Surely, then, it is just that both be punished: who would deny this? Let us, then, give thanks to the Savior, since we perceive that there is not rendered to us what we recognize was owed to us too in the damnation of those like us. For if both were set free, it would surely be hidden what is owed to sin through justice; if no one, what grace would bestow. So that, then, in this most difficult question we may rather use the words of the Apostle: *God, willing to show his wrath and to make known his power, endured with much patience the vessels of wrath, which are fitted for destruction; and to make known the riches of his glory upon the vessels of mercy*[^3]; to whom the thing formed cannot say, *Why have you made me thus?*[^4] since he has power *from the same mass to make one vessel for honor and another for dishonor*[^5]. And here, since this whole mass is deservedly damned, justice renders the owed dishonor, grace bestows the unowed honor—not by the prerogative of merit, not by the necessity of fate, not by the rashness of fortune, but by *the depth of the riches of the wisdom and knowledge of God*, which the Apostle does not open but marvels at as closed, crying out: *O the depth of the riches of the wisdom and knowledge of God! How inscrutable are his judgments, and how unsearchable his ways! For who has known the mind of the Lord? Or who has been his counselor? Or who has first given to him, that he should be repaid? For from him, and through him, and in him are all things: to him be glory forever and ever. Amen*[^6].

**3. 6.** But they are unwilling that to him be the glory in the justifying of the ungodly by gratuitous grace—those who, *being ignorant of his righteousness and wishing to establish their own*[^7], or, now pressed by the voices of religious and pious men crying out, confess that they are divinely helped to having or doing righteousness in such a way that some merit of their own precedes, as if they were the first to wish to give, that it might be repaid to them by him of whom it is said, *Who has first given to him, that he should be repaid?*[^8]—supposing that their merits go ahead toward him of whom they hear, or rather are unwilling to hear: *For from him, and through him, and in him are all things*[^9]. And from the riches of which there is a depth of his wisdom and knowledge, from these are the riches of his glory upon the vessels of mercy, which he calls into adoption; which riches he wills to make known even through the vessels of wrath that are fitted for destruction. And what are the unsearchable ways, if not those of which it is sung in the Psalm: *All the ways of the Lord are mercy and truth*[^10]? Therefore his mercy and truth are unsearchable: since *he has mercy on whom he wills*[^11], not by justice but by the grace of mercy; and *whom he wills he hardens*[^12], not by iniquity but by the truth of retribution. Which mercy and truth nevertheless so meet each other—for it is written, *Mercy and truth have met together*[^13]—that neither does mercy impede the truth by which the worthy is struck, nor truth the mercy by which the unworthy is set free. What merits of his own, then, will the one set free boast, when, if things worthy of his merits were rendered, he would be nothing but condemned? Are there then no merits of the righteous? There certainly are, because they are righteous. But that they might become righteous, there were no merits: for they were made righteous when they were justified; but, as the Apostle says, *justified freely by his grace*[^14].

**3. 7.** Since, therefore, these men are enemies and hostile to this grace, Pelagius nevertheless—in the ecclesiastical court in Palestine (for otherwise he would not have come away from there unpunished)—anathematized those who say that the grace of God is given according to merits. But in their later disputations too nothing else is found than that grace is given for merits—the grace of whose commendation the apostolic letter to the Romans especially speaks, so that from there his preaching might spread, as from the head of the world, throughout the whole world: for it is the grace by which the ungodly is justified, that is, by which one who was before ungodly becomes righteous. And therefore no merits precede the receiving of this grace, since to the merits of the ungodly not grace but punishment is owed: nor would this be grace if it were not given gratuitously, but rendered as a debt.

**3. 8.** But when these men are asked what grace Pelagius thought was given without any preceding merits, when he anathematized those who say that the grace of God is given according to our merits, they answer that this very grace given without any preceding merits is the human nature in which we were created: for before we existed we could not merit anything, so as to exist. Let this deceit be cast out of the hearts of Christians: for the Apostle by no means commends this grace, by which we were created so as to be human, but the grace by which we were justified when we were evil human beings. For this is the grace through Jesus Christ our Lord. For Christ died not for no one, that they might be created as human beings, but for the ungodly, that they might be justified: for he was already a human being who said, *Wretched man that I am, who will free me from the body of this death? The grace of God through Jesus Christ our Lord*[^15].

**3. 9.** They can indeed say that the remission of sins is the grace that is given for no preceding merits: for what good merit can sinners have? But the remission of sins itself is not without some merit, if faith obtains it: for the merit of faith is not nothing; by which faith that man said, *God, be merciful to me a sinner*[^16], and went down justified by the merit of faithful humility, since *he who humbles himself shall be exalted*[^17]. It remains, then, that faith itself, from which all righteousness takes its beginning—on account of which it is said to the Church in the Song of Songs, *You shall come and pass over from the beginning of faith*[^18]—it remains, I say, that faith itself we should attribute not to the human choice which these men extol, nor to any preceding merits, since from it begin whatever good merits there are; but that we should confess it to be the gratuitous gift of God, if we are thinking of true grace, that is, grace without merits: because, as it is read in the same letter, *God apportions to each one a measure of faith*[^19]. Good works indeed are done by a person; but faith is brought about in a person, without which those works are done by no one. *For everything that is not of faith is sin*[^20].

**3. 10.** Wherefore, lest a man extol even the merit of his prayer itself—even if to one who prays there is given help for overcoming the desires of temporal things and for loving eternal goods and God himself, the fount of all goods—the faith which prays was given to one not praying, which surely, had it not been given, could not pray. *For how shall they call upon him in whom they have not believed? Or how shall they believe in him whom they have not heard? How shall they hear without one who preaches? Faith, therefore, is from hearing, and hearing through the word of Christ*[^21]. Accordingly the minister of Christ, the preacher of this faith, according to the grace that was given him, is a planter and a waterer: *and yet neither he who plants is anything, nor he who waters, but God who gives the increase*[^22], who *apportions to each one a measure of faith*[^23]. Hence too it is said elsewhere: *Peace to the brethren, and love with faith*; which, lest they should attribute it to themselves, he at once subjoined, *from God the Father and our Lord Jesus Christ*[^24]: because faith is not of all who hear the word, but of those to whom God apportions a measure of faith; just as neither do all things sprout that are planted and watered, but those to which God gives the increase. But why one believes and another does not believe, when both hear the same thing, and, if a miracle is done in their sight, both see the same thing—this is *the depth of the riches of the wisdom and knowledge of God*, whose judgments are inscrutable, and with whom there is no iniquity, while *he has mercy on whom he wills, and whom he wills he hardens*[^25]: for these things are not unjust because they are hidden.

**3. 11.** Then, after the remission of sins, unless the Holy Spirit inhabits the cleansed house, does not the unclean spirit return with seven others, and *the last state of that man becomes worse than the first*[^26]? But that the Holy Spirit may dwell there, does he not *breathe where he wills*[^27]; and is not *the love of God, without which no one lives well, poured out in our hearts*, not by us, *but through the Holy Spirit who has been given to us*[^28]? For the Apostle defined this faith, saying: *Neither circumcision is anything, nor uncircumcision, but faith, which works through love*[^29]. For this is the faith of Christians, not of demons: for *the demons also believe and tremble*[^30]; but do they also love? For if they did not believe, they would not say, *You are the Holy One of God*[^31], or, *You are the Son of God*[^32]. But if they loved, they would not say, *What have we to do with you?*[^33]

**3. 12.** Faith, then, draws us to Christ—faith which, unless it were given to us from above as a gratuitous gift, he himself would not say: *No one can come to me unless the Father, who sent me, draws him*[^34]. Hence too, a little later, he says: *The words that I have spoken to you are spirit and life. But there are some of you who do not believe*[^35]; then the Evangelist subjoins: *For Jesus knew from the beginning who they were that believed, and who it was that would betray him*[^36]. And lest anyone should suppose that believers belonged to his foreknowledge in the same way as those who do not believe—that is, that faith itself was not given them from above, but only their will was foreknown—he at once added and said: *And he said, Therefore I told you that no one can come to me unless it be given him by my Father*[^37]. Hence it was that, of those who had heard him speaking of his flesh and his blood, some were scandalized and withdrew, while some remained by believing: because no one can come to him unless it be given by the Father, and thereby also by the Son himself, and by the Holy Spirit. For the gifts and works of the inseparable Trinity are not separate; but the Son, in thus honoring the Father, offers no evidence of any disparity, but provides a great example of humility.

**3. 13.** Here again these defenders of free choice—rather, deceivers, because puffers-up, and puffers-up because presumptuous—speaking not against us but against the Gospel, what else do they say than what the Apostle put to himself as if it were said by such men? *You will say to me then, Why does he still find fault? For who resists his will?*[^38] This contradiction he set against himself as if from another, as it were from the voice of those who are unwilling to accept what he had said above: *Therefore he has mercy on whom he wills, and whom he wills he hardens*[^39]. To such men, then, let us say with the Apostle—for we cannot find anything better than he to say: *O man, who are you to answer back to God?*[^40]

**3. 14.** For we seek the merit of hardening, and we find it. For by the merit of sin the whole mass is damned; nor does God harden by imparting malice, but by not imparting mercy. For those to whom it is not imparted are neither worthy nor do they merit it: but rather they are worthy of this, they merit this, that it not be imparted. But we seek the merit of mercy, and we do not find it, because there is none—lest grace be made void, if it is not given freely but rendered for merits.

**3. 15.** For if we say that faith preceded, in which there was the merit of grace, what merit did a man have before faith, that he should receive faith? *For what does he have that he has not received? But if he has received, why does he boast as if he had not received?*[^41] For just as a man would not have wisdom, understanding, counsel, fortitude, knowledge, piety, the fear of God, unless, according to the prophetic word, he had received *the Spirit of wisdom and understanding, of counsel and fortitude, of knowledge and piety and the fear of God*[^42]; just as he would not have virtue, love, continence, unless he had received the Spirit of whom the Apostle says, *For you have not received the Spirit of fear, but of power, and of love, and of continence*[^43]: so he would not have faith unless he had received the Spirit of faith, of whom that same one says: *But having the same Spirit of faith, according to what is written, I believed, therefore I have spoken; we too believe, and therefore we speak*[^44]. And that he received it not by merit but by the mercy of him who *has mercy on whom he wills*[^45], he most plainly shows where he says of himself: *I obtained mercy, that I might be faithful*[^46].

**4. 16.** If we say that the merit of prayer precedes, so that the gift of grace may follow: prayer indeed, by obtaining whatever it obtains, plainly shows that it is the gift of God, lest a man suppose that what he has is from himself—which, if it were in his power, would surely not be asked for; nevertheless, lest even the merits of prayer be thought to precede, by which grace would be given not gratuitously, and would then not even be grace, because rendered as a debt, prayer itself too is found among the gifts of grace. *For what we should pray for as we ought, we do not know*, says the Teacher of the Gentiles, *but the Spirit himself intercedes for us with unutterable groanings*[^47]. But what is "intercedes," if not "makes us intercede"? For to intercede with groanings is the surest indication of one in need. But it is unlawful to believe that the Holy Spirit is in need of anything. Rather it was said "intercedes" because he makes us intercede, and inspires in us the disposition of interceding and groaning; as in the Gospel: *For it is not you who speak, but the Spirit of your Father, who speaks in you*[^48]. For neither does this take place in us as though we do nothing. The help of the Holy Spirit, therefore, is so expressed that he himself is said to do what he makes us do.

**4. 17.** For that it is to be understood not as our spirit, of which it was said *intercedes with unutterable groanings*, but as the Holy Spirit, by whom our weakness is helped, the Apostle himself sufficiently shows. For he began thus: *The Spirit*, he says, *helps our weakness*[^49]; then he subjoined thus: *For what we should pray for as we ought, we do not know*, and the rest. Of this Spirit indeed he says more openly elsewhere: *For you have not received a spirit of slavery again in fear, but you have received the Spirit of adoption of sons, in whom we cry, Abba, Father*[^50]. Behold, here he did not say that the Spirit himself cries in praying, but, *in whom we cry, Abba, Father*. Yet in another place he says: *Because you are sons of God, God has sent the Spirit of his Son into your hearts, crying, Abba, Father*[^51]. Here he does not say "in whom we cry," but he preferred to call the Spirit himself the one who cries, by whom it is brought about that we cry: as are those expressions, *The Spirit himself intercedes with unutterable groanings*, and *It is the Spirit of your Father who speaks in you*.

**4. 18.** As, therefore, no one is rightly wise, rightly understands, rightly prevails in counsel and fortitude, no one is knowingly pious or piously knowing, no one fears God with a chaste fear, unless he has received *the Spirit of wisdom and understanding, of counsel and fortitude, of knowledge and piety and the fear of God*[^52]; nor does anyone have true virtue, sincere love, religious continence, except through the Spirit of power and of love and of continence: so without the Spirit of faith no one will rightly believe, nor without the Spirit of prayer will anyone pray to good effect. Not that there are so many spirits, but *one and the same Spirit works all these things, distributing to each his own as he wills*[^53]; because *the Spirit breathes where he wills*[^54]: but—this must be confessed—he helps in one way when not yet dwelling within, in another when dwelling within. For when not yet dwelling within he helps that they may become faithful; dwelling within, he helps those already faithful.

**5. 19.** What, then, is a man's merit before grace, by which merit he might receive grace, since all our good merit is brought about in us only by grace; and since, *when God crowns our merits, he crowns nothing other than his own gifts*? For just as from the beginning of faith we obtained mercy, not because we were faithful, but that we might become so; so in the end, in which there will be eternal life, he will crown us, as it is written, *in compassion and mercy*[^55]. Not in vain, therefore, is it sung to God: *And his mercy will go before me*[^56]; and, *His mercy will follow me*[^57]. Hence even eternal life itself, which will surely be had in the end without end, and is therefore rendered for preceding merits—yet because those very merits by which it is rendered were not won by us through our own sufficiency, but brought about in us through grace, it too is called grace, for no other reason than that it is given freely; not indeed because it is not given for merits, but because even the very merits for which it is given were themselves given. But where do we find that even eternal life is called grace? We have, in the same magnificent defender of grace, the apostle Paul: *The wages of sin*, he says, *is death; but the grace of God is eternal life in Christ Jesus our Lord*[^58].

**5. 20.** See, I beg you, with what brevity and yet how watchfully he set the words, which, if diligently considered, will somewhat clear up the obscurity of this question. For when he had said, *The wages of sin is death*, who would not judge that he was adding most fittingly and consistently, if he said, "But the wages of righteousness is eternal life"? And it is true; because, as by the merit of sin death is rendered as wages, so by the merit of righteousness eternal life is rendered as wages. Or if he were unwilling to say "of righteousness," he might say "of faith"; since *the righteous lives by faith*[^59]. Hence too it is called a reward in very many places of the holy Scriptures: but nowhere is righteousness or faith called a reward, because a reward is rendered for righteousness or faith. And what a reward is to a worker, that wages are to a soldier.

**5. 21.** But the blessed Apostle, fighting most watchfully against that exaltation which tries to creep in even upon the great—so much so that, on its account, he says there was given to him an angel of Satan to buffet him, lest he raise the summit of presumption—against this plague of exaltation, then, he says, *The wages of sin is death*. Rightly "wages," because it is owed, because it is worthily repaid, because it is rendered by desert. Then, lest righteousness extol itself on its own good merit, just as human evil merit is undoubtedly sin, he did not state it from the contrary, saying, "The wages of righteousness is eternal life"; but, *The grace*, he says, *of God is eternal life*. And, lest this be sought by some other way apart from the Mediator, he added, *in Christ Jesus our Lord*[^60]: as if to say, "Having heard that the wages of sin is death, why do you prepare to extol yourself, O human—not righteousness, but, under the name of righteousness, plain pride? Why do you prepare to extol yourself and to demand eternal life, contrary to death, as owed wages? That to which eternal life is owed is true righteousness: but if it is true righteousness, it is not from you; it is from above, *coming down from the Father of lights*[^61]. That you might have it—if indeed you have it—you surely received it: *for what good do you have that you have not received?*[^62] Wherefore, O human, if you are to receive eternal life, it is indeed the wages of righteousness, but to you it is grace, to whom even righteousness itself is grace. For it would be rendered to you as something owed, if the righteousness to which it is owed were from yourself. But now *of his fullness we have received*[^63] not only the grace by which we now live justly in labors unto the end, but also grace in return for this grace, that afterward we may live in rest without end." Nothing does faith believe more wholesomely than this, because nothing more true does the understanding find: and we ought to hear the prophet saying, *Unless you believe, you shall not understand*[^64].

**6. 22.** "But," one says, "people who are unwilling to live rightly and faithfully will excuse themselves, saying: What have we done, we who live badly, since we have not received the grace by which we might live well?" They cannot truly say that they do nothing evil, since they live badly: for if they do nothing evil, they live well; but if they live badly, they live badly of their own—either what they drew originally, or what they have added on top. But if they are vessels of wrath fitted for destruction, to whom what is owed is rendered, let them impute this to themselves, because they are made from that mass which, on account of the sin of one, in whom all sinned, God deservedly and justly damned: but if they are vessels of mercy, to whom, made from the same mass, he was unwilling to render the owed punishment, let them not puff themselves up, but glorify him who showed them mercy that was not owed; and if they are minded otherwise in anything, this too he himself will reveal to them.

**6. 23.** Finally, in what way will these men excuse themselves? Surely in that way which, briefly, as if from their voice, the Apostle set against himself, so that they say: *Why does he still find fault? For who resists his will?*[^65] For this is to say: "Why is there a complaint about us, that we offend God by living badly, when no one can resist his will, who has hardened us by not granting mercy?" If, then, these men are not ashamed by this excuse to contradict not us but the Apostle, why should it irk us to say to them what the Apostle said, this same thing again and again: *O man, who are you to answer back to God? Does the thing formed say to him who formed it, Why have you made me thus? Or has the potter no power over the clay*[^66]—from the same mass, surely deservedly and rightly damned—to make one vessel for unowed honor on account of the grace of mercy, another for owed dishonor on account of the justice of wrath, and to make known the riches of his glory upon the vessels of mercy, thus showing what he bestows on them, when the vessels of wrath receive that punishment which was equally owed to all? Let it suffice meanwhile for a Christian still living by faith, and not yet seeing what is perfect, but knowing in part, to know or believe that God frees no one except by gratuitous mercy through our Lord Jesus Christ, and damns no one except by most equitable truth through the same our Lord Jesus Christ. But why he frees or does not free this one rather than that one, let him search who can the so great depth of his judgments—yet let him beware the precipice. *Is there then injustice with God? By no means!*[^67] But *his judgments are inscrutable, and his ways unsearchable*[^68].

**6. 24.** And at least in those of greater age it can deservedly be said: These were unwilling to understand, that they might act well. These—what is graver—understood, and did not obey: because, as it is written, *A hard servant will not be corrected by words; for even if he understands, he will not obey*[^69]. Whence will he not obey, except by his own most wicked will? To whom a graver damnation is owed by divine equity: *for to whom more is given, more is required of him*[^70]. For Scripture calls those inexcusable from whom the truth is not hidden and in whom iniquity persists. *For the wrath of God is revealed from heaven*, says the Apostle, *upon all the ungodliness and unrighteousness of those men who hold back the truth in unrighteousness: because what is known of God is manifest in them. For God has manifested it to them. For his invisible things, from the creation of the world, are clearly seen, being understood through the things that are made; his eternal power also and divinity: so that they are inexcusable*[^71].

**6. 25.** If, then, he calls those inexcusable who could see his invisible things, understood through the things that are made, and yet did not obey the truth but remained unrighteous and ungodly—for they did not fail to know him, but, *knowing*, he says, *God, they did not glorify him as God, nor give thanks*[^72]—how much more inexcusable are those who, instructed from his law, trust that they themselves are *guides of the blind*, and, teaching others, *do not teach themselves*; who preach that one must not steal, and steal; and the rest that the Apostle says of them! For to them he says: *Therefore you are inexcusable, O man, every one who judges: for wherein you judge another, you condemn yourself; for you do the same things that you judge*[^73].

**6. 26.** The Lord himself also says in the Gospel: *If I had not come and spoken to them, they would have no sin; but now they have no excuse for their sin*[^74]. Surely it is not that they would have no sin at all, who were full of other great and many sins; but he wishes this sin to be understood as one they would not have had if he had not come—namely, that, when they had heard him, they did not believe in him. He asserts that they have no excuse by which they could say: "We did not hear, therefore we did not believe." For human pride, presuming as it were on the powers of free choice, thinks itself excused when what it sins seems to be of ignorance, not of will.

**6. 27.** According to this excuse, divine Scripture calls inexcusable whomever it convicts of sinning knowingly. Yet the just judgment of God does not spare even those who have not heard: *For whoever have sinned without the Law, will perish without the Law*[^75]. And although they may seem to excuse themselves, he does not admit this excuse, who knows that he made man upright and gave him a precept of obedience, and that it was only by the free choice of will, which he wrongly used, that sin, which would pass into his descendants, flowed forth. For neither are those damned who have not sinned, since that sin *passed from one into all*, in whom, before their own sins in each individual, all sinned in common. And thereby every sinner is inexcusable, whether by the guilt of origin or by the addition of his own will as well; whether he knows or is ignorant, whether he judges or does not judge: because the very ignorance, in those who were unwilling to understand, is without doubt sin; but in those who could not, it is the penalty of sin. Therefore in both there is no just excuse, but just damnation.

**6. 28.** But the divine words call those inexcusable who sin not in ignorance but knowingly, for this reason: that, according to the judgment of their own pride, by which they trust greatly in the powers of their own will, they may see themselves to be inexcusable; because they no longer have their excuse from ignorance, and there is not yet the righteousness for which they presumed the will to suffice. But he to whom the Lord has granted the grace both of knowing and of obeying says: *Through the Law comes knowledge of sin*[^76]; and, *I did not know sin except through the Law; for I did not know concupiscence, unless the Law said, You shall not covet*[^77]. Nor does he wish a man to be understood as ignorant of the law that gives precepts, but as in need of the grace that sets free, where he says: *I delight in the law of God according to the inner man*[^78]: and with this not only knowledge but also delight in the law, he afterward says: *Wretched man that I am, who will free me from the body of this death? The grace of God through Jesus Christ our Lord*[^79]. No one, therefore, frees from the wounds of that brigand except the grace of the one Savior. No one frees those *sold under sin*[^80] from the chains of the one who takes captive, except the grace of the one who redeems.

**6. 29.** And thereby all who wish to be excused in their wickednesses and iniquities are for that reason most justly punished, since those who are freed are freed only by grace. For if the excuse there were just, it would no longer be grace, but justice, that freed them. But since only grace frees, it finds nothing just in the one whom it frees—not will, not action, not even the excuse itself: for if this is just, whoever uses it is freed by desert, not by grace. For we know that by the grace of Christ even some of those are freed who say, *Why does he still find fault? For who resists his will?*[^81] If this is a just excuse, then they are freed not by gratuitous grace, but on account of the justice of this excuse. But if it is grace by which they are freed, then surely this excuse is not just. For grace is then true grace, by which a man is freed, if it is not rendered according to the debt of justice. Nothing, therefore, comes about in those who say, *Why does he still find fault? For who resists his will?* except what is read in the book of Solomon: *The foolishness of a man violates his ways, but he blames God in his heart*[^82].

**6. 30.** Although, then, God makes the vessels of wrath unto destruction, that he may show his wrath and demonstrate his power, by which he uses even evils well; and that he may make known the riches of his glory upon the vessels of mercy, which he makes for honor—not owed to the damnable mass, but bestowed by the bounty of his grace—yet in those same vessels of wrath, made for owed dishonor on account of the merit of the mass, that is, in men created indeed on account of the goods of nature but destined for punishment on account of their vices, he himself knows how to damn, not to make, the iniquity which truth most rightly disapproves. For just as human nature, which all praise without doubt, is attributed to his will, so the fault of man, which all condemn without refusal, is attributed to man's will. And this will of man either transmitted a hereditary defect into the descendants whom it had in itself when it sinned, or acquired the other defects as well, when each one lived ruinously in himself. But neither from what is drawn originally, nor from those evils which each one, in his own life, by not understanding or by being unwilling to understand, gathers up, or even, instructed from the law, aggravates by the addition of transgression—from none of these is anyone freed and justified except by the grace of God through Jesus Christ our Lord; not only by the remission of sins, but first by the very inspiration of faith and the fear of God, the disposition and effect of prayer being wholesomely imparted, until he heal all our infirmities, and redeem our life from corruption, and crown us in compassion and mercy.

**7. 31.** But these men, who think that God becomes a respecter of persons if in one and the same cause his mercy comes upon some, while his wrath remains upon others, surely lose all the force of human argument in the case of infants. For—to keep silent meanwhile about the fact that there is upon infants too, however recently come from the maternal womb, that penalty of which the Apostle, speaking, says, *Through the offense of one, unto all men unto condemnation*[^83], from which only that one frees, of whom he likewise says, *Through the justification of one, unto all men unto justification of life*[^84]—to pass over this meanwhile, then, and say this only of infants, which they too, terrified by the evangelical authority, or rather broken by the most harmonious conspiracy of faith of Christian peoples, concede without any refusal, namely, that no infant, unless born again of water and the Spirit, enters the kingdom of heaven: what cause, I ask, are they going to allege for this, that one infant is so governed as to depart hence baptized, while another, handed over to the hands of unbelievers, or even of believers before he is offered by them to be baptized, breathes his last? Will they ascribe this to fate, or to fortune? I do not think they will burst out into such madness, desiring as they do to hold the Christian name in however small a degree.

**7. 32.** Why, then, will no infant, without having received the bath of regeneration, enter the kingdom of heaven? Did he himself perhaps choose the unbelieving or negligent parents of whom he was to be born? What shall I say of the unexpected and sudden innumerable deaths by which often even the infants of religious Christians are forestalled and snatched away before Baptism; while, on the contrary, the infants of the sacrilegious and of the enemies of Christ, coming in some way into the hands of Christians, depart from this life not without the sacrament of regeneration? What will those say here who contend that, in order for grace to be given, some human merits must precede, lest God be a respecter of persons? What merits, at last, have here preceded? If you think of the merits of these very infants, they have none of their own; the same mass is common to both. If you consider the merits of the parents, those are good whose children perished by sudden deaths without the baptism of Christ; but those are evil whose children, through some power of Christians, came to the sacraments of the Church. And yet the providence of God, by whom *our hairs are numbered*[^85], without whose will *a sparrow does not fall to the ground*[^86]—a providence which is neither pressed by fate, nor impeded by chance happenings, nor corrupted by any iniquity—does not, that they may be born again unto the heavenly inheritance, provide for all the infants of his own children, and yet provides even for some infants of the ungodly. This infant, sprung from faithful married parents, received with the joy of the parents, is suffocated by the drowsiness of the mother or the nurse, and is made bereft and devoid of the faith of his own; that infant, born of a sacrilegious defilement, exposed by the cruel fear of the mother, is gathered up by the merciful devotion of strangers, is baptized by their Christian solicitude, and is made a sharer and partaker of the eternal kingdom. Let them ponder these things, let them consider these things; let them dare here to call God either a respecter of persons in his grace, or a rewarder of preceding merits.

**7. 33.** For even if they try to suspect some merits of greater age, whether good or evil, what will they say about these infants—of whom the one could acquire by no evil merits of his own the violence of the one who suffocated him, nor the other by good merits the diligence of the one who baptized him? They are of excessive vanity and blindness, if, even after considering these things, they do not yet deign to cry out with us: *O the depth of the riches of the wisdom and knowledge of God! How inscrutable are his judgments, and how unsearchable his ways!*[^87] Let them not, then, oppose with most obstinate insanity the gratuitous mercy of God. Let them allow the Son of man, at whatever age, *to seek and to save what was lost*[^88]; and let them not dare to judge concerning his inscrutable judgments, why in one and the same cause his mercy comes upon one, while his wrath remains upon another.

**8. 34.** For who are these who would answer back to God, since he, when Rebecca had twins from one act of intercourse with Isaac our father, *when they were not yet born and had done nothing good or evil, that according to election his purpose might stand, the election, namely, of grace, not of debt—the election by which he himself makes those to be chosen, and does not find them—not from works, but from him who calls, said that the elder would serve the younger*[^89]? To this judgment the blessed Apostle took up also the testimony of a far later prophet: *Jacob I loved, but Esau I hated*[^90]; so that it might be understood that this, made plain afterward through the prophet, was, before they were born, in the predestination of God through grace. For what did he love in Jacob, before, once born, he had done anything good, except the gratuitous gift of his mercy? And what did he hate in Esau, before, once born, he had done anything evil, except original sin? For neither in the former would he love righteousness, which the former had done none of; nor in the latter would he hate the nature, which he himself had made good.

**8. 35.** But it is astonishing, when they are hemmed in by these straits, into what precipices they hurl themselves, fearing the nets of truth. "He hated the one and loved the other of the not-yet-born," they say, "for this reason: because he foresaw their future works." Who would not be astonished that this most acute insight was lacking to the Apostle? For he did not see it, when, the question being raised against him as by an adversary, he did not rather give that answer—so brief, so plain, so (as these men think) true and conclusive. For when he had proposed the astonishing matter, how it could rightly be said of those not yet born and doing nothing good or evil that God loved one and hated the other, expressing the hearer's emotion by the very question raised against himself: *What then shall we say?* he says; *is there injustice with God? By no means*[^91]. Here, then, was the place to say what these men hold: "For God foresaw their future works, when he said that the elder would serve the younger." But the Apostle does not say this; rather, lest anyone dare to boast of the merits of his works, he wished what he said to avail for the commending of God's grace and glory. For when he had said, *By no means is there injustice with God*—as though we said to him, "Whence do you show this, since you assert that it was said not from works but from him who calls, The elder shall serve the younger?"—*For he says to Moses*, he says, *I will have mercy on whom I will have mercy, and I will show mercy to whom I will be merciful. Therefore it is not of him who wills nor of him who runs, but of God who shows mercy*[^92]. Where now are merits, where works, whether past or future, as accomplished or to be accomplished by the powers of free choice? Has not the Apostle brought forth an open verdict on the commending of gratuitous grace, that is, of true grace? Has not God made foolish the wisdom of the heretics?

**8. 36.** But what was being done, that the Apostle should say this, that he should recall the example of those twins? What was he endeavoring to persuade? What did he desire to inculcate? Surely this, which their madness assails, which the proud do not grasp, which those are unwilling to be wise about who, *ignorant of God's righteousness and wishing to establish their own, are not subject to the righteousness of God*[^93]: for the Apostle was treating sufficiently of grace itself, and therefore commended the children of the promise. For what God promises, none but God does: for it has some measure of reason and truth that man should promise and God should do; but that man should say he himself does what God promised is the reprobate sense of proud impiety.

**8. 37.** Commending, then, the children of the promise, he shows this first signified through Isaac the son of Abraham. For the work of God appears more evidently in him whom the usual order of nature did not beget, from barren wombs and bodies worn out with old age, so that in the children of God, who were foretold as future, this might be a sign of a divine work, not a human one. *In Isaac*, he says, *shall your seed be called*[^94]: that is, *not those who are the children of the flesh are the children of God; but the children of the promise are reckoned as seed*[^95]. For this is the word of the promise: *At this time I will come, and Sarah shall have a son*[^96]. *And not only that*, he says, *but also Rebecca, having conceived from one act of intercourse with Isaac our father*[^97]. To what purpose did he add "from one act of intercourse," except that Jacob should not boast of his own merits, nor of the merits of others among his forebears, nor even of those of that single father—the father's will perhaps having been changed for the better—saying that he was loved by the Creator because his father, when he sowed him, was more praiseworthy in better morals? "From one act of intercourse," he says: there was then one merit of the father toward sowing them, one merit of the mother toward conceiving them: because, even if the mother carried them enclosed in her womb until she gave birth, and perhaps varied her wills and dispositions, she varied them not toward one but surely toward both, whom she carried alike in her womb.

**8. 38.** The intention of the Apostle, therefore, must be looked at: how, for the sake of commending grace, he is unwilling that the one of whom it was said, *Jacob I loved*, should glory except in the Lord; so that, when, from the same father, the same mother, one act of intercourse, before they had done anything good or evil, God loves one and hates the other, Jacob may understand that, from that mass of original iniquity in which he sees his brother—with whom he had a common cause—to have deserved by justice to be damned, he himself could be set apart only by grace. *For when they were not yet born*, he says, *nor had done anything good or evil, that according to election the purpose of God might stand, not from works but from him who calls, it was said to her, The elder shall serve the younger*[^98].

**8. 39.** But that the election of grace comes about with no preceding merits of works, the same Apostle most plainly shows in another place: *So then*, he says, *even at this present time a remnant according to the election of grace has been saved. But if by grace, then no longer from works; otherwise grace is no longer grace*[^99]. According to this grace, therefore, consequently taking up the prophetic testimony too: *As it is written*, he says, *Jacob I loved, but Esau I hated*[^100]; and immediately: *What then shall we say?* he says; *is there injustice with God? By no means*[^101]. But why "by no means"? Is it on account of the works of both that he foreknew as future? Nay, this too "by no means": *for he says to Moses, I will have mercy on whom I will have mercy, and I will show mercy to whom I will be merciful. Therefore it is not of him who wills nor of him who runs, but of God who shows mercy*[^102]. And so that, in the vessels which are fitted for destruction, which is owed to the damned mass, the vessels made for honor from the same mass might recognize what the divine mercy has bestowed on them: *For the Scripture says to Pharaoh*, he says, *For this I raised you up, that I might show in you my power, and that my name might be glorified in all the earth*[^103]. Finally, he concludes regarding both: *Therefore he has mercy on whom he wills, and whom he wills he hardens*[^104]: this he does with whom there is no iniquity. He has mercy, then, by a gratuitous gift; but he hardens by a most just desert.

**8. 40.** But let the arrogance of the proud unbeliever, or the excuse of the damnable man who is punished, still say: *Why does he still find fault? For who resists his will?*[^105] Let him say it, and let him hear what befits a human being: *O man, who are you to answer back to God?*[^106] and the rest, on which I have already discoursed enough and often, as far as I could. Let him hear these things and not despise them. But if he despises them, let him find that, even so that he might despise them, he was hardened; but if he does not despise them, let him believe that, even so that he might not despise them, he was helped—but hardened by what is owed, helped by grace.

**9. 41.** For even if—of the two twins from the patriarch Isaac, which we have already shown is said with how great blindness—God foresaw the future works of those who lived and grew old, and therefore loved Jacob but hated Esau, no one can on that account say of infants who are going to die, in such a way that he does not provide for this one to receive Baptism but provides for that one, that God foresees their future works: for how are those things called future which will be none at all?

**9. 42.** "But God," they say, "in those whom he takes away from here, foresees how each one would have lived if he lived; and therefore the one whom he knew was going to live wickedly he causes to die without Baptism; thus punishing in him evil works, not those he did, but those he was going to do." If, then, even works not committed are punished by God, let them first consider how falsely they promise that infants who die without Baptism will not go into damnation—infants who, if for that reason they are not baptized, because they were going to live badly if they lived: for on account of that very bad life they will doubtless be damned, if even the evils which were going to be are damned. Next, if the sacrament of Baptism is provided for them to receive whom God knew, if they lived, would have lived well, why are not all kept in the life which they would adorn with good works? Why even do some of those who are baptized, living long, live most wickedly, and sometimes arrive even at apostasy? Why did he not cast the very first pair of sinners, whom God surely knew were going to sin, out of paradise beforehand, lest they commit there what was unworthy of so holy a place, if even sins not yet committed are justly punished? What, finally, is bestowed on him who *is snatched away, lest malice change his understanding, or deceit beguile his soul*[^107], if even those things are justly punished which, though he did not do them, he was nevertheless going to do by living? Lastly, why is not the bath of regeneration rather provided for the one going to die who was going to live badly if he lived, so that the sins he was going to commit might be remitted to him in Baptism? For who is so senseless as to deny that those things can be remitted through Baptism which he says can be punished without Baptism?

**10. 43.** But in disputing against those who, convicted on every side, endeavor to convince men that God is an avenger even of sins not committed, we must beware lest we be thought to be fabricating these things against them; while they, on the other hand, may by no means be believed to be so dull as to either hold these views or try to persuade anyone of them; yet, had I not heard them say these things, I would not have thought they must be refuted. For they are hemmed in both by the authority of the divine readings and by the firm rite of the Church handed down and retained from antiquity in the baptism of infants, where it is most plainly shown that infants, both when they are exorcized and when they answer, through those who carry them, that they renounce the devil, are set free from the dominion of the devil; and, not finding a way out, they go on into headlong folly, while they are unwilling to change their opinion.

**10. 44.** This indeed they think they say most acutely: "How does sin pass into the children of the faithful, which we do not doubt was remitted in the parents through Baptism?" As though carnal generation could not have on that account what only spiritual regeneration takes away: or as though in Baptism the weakness of the concupiscence of the flesh were at once healed, just as its guilt is at once abolished—but it is by the grace of being reborn, not by the condition of being born. Hence whoever is born, even of one reborn, through this concupiscence, will without doubt harm the one born, unless he too is likewise reborn. But whatever difficulty there is in this question, it does not so hinder the workers in the field of Christ from baptizing infants unto the remission of sins, whether they are born of the faithful or of unbelievers, as it does not hinder farmers from grafting—by the care of grafting—wild olives into olive trees, whether they spring from wild olives or from olives. For if, in order that he might answer, it were proposed to a countryman what the cause is that, since an olive is one thing and a wild olive another, nevertheless nothing but a wild olive is born from either seed, he does not omit the work of grafting, even if he cannot resolve that question: otherwise, while he thinks that the shoots sprung from the seed of the olive are nothing but olives, the sloth of his idleness brings it about that the whole field runs wild with bitter sterility.

**10. 45.** For that which they devised, when they were pressed by the weight of truth—because *the Lord is faithful in his words*[^108], and therefore his Church in no way deceitfully baptizes infants unto the remission of sins, but, so that what is done may be done by faith, what is said surely comes about—what, then, they devised, when this most plain mass of truth pressed them, what Christian would not laugh at it, however very crafty he perceives it to be? For they say: "Infants do indeed answer truly, through the mouths of those who carry them, that they believe unto the remission of sins—not, however, because sins are remitted to themselves, but because they believe that in the Church or in Baptism sins are remitted in those in whom they are found, not in those in whom there are none." And thereby they are unwilling that "they should be so baptized unto the remission of sins as though the remission itself takes place in them—infants whom they contend have no sin—but because, although without sin, they are nevertheless baptized in that Baptism by which the remission of sins takes place in any sinners whatever."

**10. 46.** It may indeed be that this shifty cunning could be refuted more subtly and acutely at leisure. Nevertheless, according to this craftiness of theirs, they do not find what to answer to this: that infants are exorcized and breathed upon. For this is done deceitfully without doubt, if the devil does not have dominion over them; but if he does have dominion, and therefore they are not deceitfully exorcized and breathed upon, through what does he have dominion, if not through sin, being surely the prince of sinners? Accordingly, if they now blush and dare not say that these things are done in the Church mendaciously, let them confess that what was lost is sought even in infants: for it had been lost only on account of sin, and what cannot be sought except by grace cannot be found. But thanks be to God, that at least, when they argue against the remission of sins, lest it be believed to take place in infants, they nevertheless now confess—though through the hearts and mouths of their elders—that infants believe. As, therefore, they hear the Lord saying, *Whoever is not born again of water and the Holy Spirit shall not enter the kingdom of heaven*[^109], on account of which they concede that they must be baptized, so let them hear the same Lord saying, *He who does not believe shall be condemned*[^110]: because, just as they confess that infants are reborn through the ministry of those who baptize, so too that they believe through the hearts and mouths of those who answer for them. Let them dare, then, to say that the innocent will be condemned by a just God, if he is bound by no bond of original sin.

**10. 47.** If this discourse is lengthy and burdensome to your occupations, grant pardon; because, in order to write these things to you, and, provoked by your letter, the tokens of your benevolence toward us, to converse with you about these matters, I too forced myself to interrupt my own occupations. If you should learn that they devise anything else against the catholic faith, and whatever you yourselves, with faithful and plainly pastoral love, discourse against them, lest they lay waste the weak of the Lord's flock, make it known to us. For by the restlessness of heretics our diligence is roused, as from the sleep of sloth, that we may search the Scriptures more watchfully, whence they may be met, lest they harm the sheepfold of Christ: thus, through the manifold grace of the Savior, even what the enemy contrives unto ruin God turns into help; *since to those who love God all things work together for good*[^111]. May you ever live in God, mindful of us, most dear brother.
