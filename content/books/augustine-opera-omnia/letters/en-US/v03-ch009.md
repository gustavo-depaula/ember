# Letter 164

To the most blessed lord, brother and fellow bishop Evodius, from Augustine, greetings in the Lord.

**1. 1.** The question which you proposed to me from the letter of the apostle Peter is wont, as I do not think is hidden from you, to disturb us most vehemently—how those words are to be taken as spoken of hell. I therefore put the same question back to you, so that, whether you yourself are able, or you find someone who is able, you may take away the doubt from it and end my doubt. But if I am able first, when the Lord grants it, and am able to impart it to you, I will not defraud your charity: but now I will intimate what moves me there, so that, according to these things, you may either yourself think about those apostolic words, or consult whomever you find suitable.

**1. 2.** When he had said that Christ was *put to death in the flesh, made alive in the spirit*, he immediately added: *in which, coming, he preached also to those spirits who were shut up in prison, who once were unbelieving, when the patience of God waited in the days of Noah, while the ark was being built, in which a few, that is, eight souls, were saved through water*[^1]: then he added and said: *Which now also in a like form baptism saves you*[^2]. It moves me, then, that if the Lord, when he died, preached among the dead to the spirits shut up in prison, what good those alone deserved that the Lord should descend to hell, who at that time were unbelieving when the ark was being built. For after the times of Noah, many thousands of so many nations, up to the passion of Christ, died, whom he could find among the dead: not, surely, of those who had believed in God, like the prophets and patriarchs of the stock of Abraham, like Noah himself before, and his whole house, which was saved through water—except perhaps one son, who was afterward reprobated; and as, besides the progeny of Jacob, there were others believing in God, like Job, like the city of Nineveh, and any others there are who either appear in the Scriptures or lie hidden in the human race: but I mean of those many thousands of men who, ignorant of God and given over to the worship of demons or images, from the times of Noah up to the passion of Christ departed from this life—whom Christ, finding them among the dead, how did he not preach to them, but only to those who in the days of Noah were unbelieving when the ark was being built? Or if he preached to all, why did Peter mention those alone, the so innumerable multitude of the rest being passed over?

**2. 3.** And that the Lord indeed, put to death in the flesh, came into hell, is sufficiently established. For it cannot be contradicted, either of the prophecy which said, *For you will not leave my soul in hell*[^3]—which, lest anyone dare to think otherwise, the same Peter expounds in the Acts of the Apostles—or of those words of the same Peter by which he asserts that he loosed the pains of hell, by which it was impossible for him to be held. Who, then, but an unbeliever would deny that Christ was among the dead in hell? But if it moves anyone how it is to be taken that the pains of hell were loosed by him (for he had not begun to be in them as in bonds, and so loosed them as if he had loosed the chains by which he had been bound), it is easy to understand that they were loosed as the snares of hunters can be loosed, so that they hold not; not because they held. It can also be so, that we believe him to have loosed those pains by which he himself could not be held, but by which others were held, whom he knew were to be freed.

**2. 4.** But who these are, it is rash to define. For if we say that absolutely all who were found there were then freed, who would not rejoice, if we could show this? Especially on account of certain men who have become familiarly known to us by their literary labor, whose eloquence and genius we admire: not only poets and orators, who in many places of their little works showed those same false gods of the Gentiles to be despised and laughed at, and sometimes even confessed one and true God, although with the rest they worshiped those superstitious things; but even those who said these things not by singing or declaiming, but by philosophizing: many too whose writings we do not have, but in whose writings we have learned of lives in a certain manner praiseworthy, so that—except for the worship of God, in which they erred, worshiping vain things which had been publicly instituted to be worshiped, and serving the creation rather than the Creator—in the rest of their morals of frugality, continence, chastity, sobriety, contempt of death for the safety of their homeland, and faith kept not only to citizens but even to enemies, they may deservedly be set forth for imitation. All which things, indeed, when they are not referred to the end of right and true piety, but to the empty pride of human praise and glory, even they themselves become in a way empty, and are rendered barren: yet by a certain quality of mind they so delight us, that we would wish those in whom they were to be freed from the torments of hell, either especially or with the rest, if human sense did not stand otherwise, and the justice of the Creator otherwise.

**2. 5.** Since these things are so, if the Savior loosed all from there and, as you wrote in inquiring, emptied hell, so that thenceforth the final judgment is now awaited—these are the things which in this matter not without reason move me, and which are wont meanwhile to occur to me as I think. First, by what authority that opinion is confirmed. For what is written, that at the death of Christ the pains of hell were loosed, can be understood either to pertain to him—that he loosed them, that is, made them void, so far as that he himself should not be held by them, especially since there follows, *by which it was impossible for him to be held*—or, if the cause is sought why he willed to come into hell, where those pains were by which he could not be held at all, since he was, as it is written, *free among the dead*[^4], in whom the prince and ruler of death found nothing that was owed to punishment: this, namely, which is written, *the pains of hell being loosed*, can be taken not in all, but in certain ones, whom he judged worthy of that freeing; so that he be not thought to have descended there in vain, about to profit none of those who were there held shut up, nor yet that it should follow that what divine mercy and justice granted to certain ones should be thought to have been granted to all.

**3. 6.** And concerning that first man, the father of the human race, that he loosed him from there, almost the whole Church agrees; which it is to be believed she did not believe in vain, from whatever source this was handed down, even if from this no express authority of the canonical Scriptures be produced: although that which is written in the book of Wisdom—*She preserved him who was first formed, the father of the world, when he alone was created, and led him out of his own offense, and gave him the power to govern all things*[^5]—seems to make for this opinion rather than for any other understanding. Some add that this benefit was granted also to the ancient saints, Abel, Seth, Noah and his house, Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, and the other patriarchs and prophets, so that when the Lord came into hell, they were loosed from those pains.

**3. 7.** But in what way it is to be understood that Abraham, into whose bosom that pious poor man too was received, was in those pains, I for my part do not see: perhaps those explain it who can. But that two only, that is, Abraham and Lazarus, were in that bosom of memorable rest before the Lord descended into hell, and that of these two alone it was said to that rich man, *Between you and us a great chasm has been fixed, so that those who wish to pass from here to you cannot, nor cross over from there to here*[^6]—I do not know whether there is anyone to whom this does not seem absurd. Further, if there were more than two there, who would dare to say that the patriarchs and prophets were not there, to whom in the Scripture so distinguished a testimony of God's justice and piety is borne? What, then, he conferred on these by loosing the pains of hell, in which they were not, I do not yet understand; especially since I could nowhere in the Scriptures find hell itself called in a good sense. But if this is nowhere read in the divine authorities, surely that bosom of Abraham, that is, the dwelling of a certain hidden rest, is not to be believed to be any part of hell. Although in these very words of so great a Master, where he says that Abraham said, *Between you and us a great chasm has been fixed*, it appears sufficiently, as I think, that the bosom of so great a felicity is not a certain part, and as it were a member, of hell. For what is a great chasm but a certain gulf, separating much the things between which it not only is but even has been fixed? Wherefore, if holy Scripture had said that Christ when dead came into that bosom of Abraham, without naming hell and its pains, I wonder whether anyone would dare to assert that he descended to hell.

**3. 8.** But because evident testimonies mention both hell and its pains, no cause occurs why the Savior should be believed to have come there, except to save men from its pains; but whether all whom he found in them, or certain ones whom he judged worthy of that benefit, I still inquire: yet that he was among the dead in hell, and conferred this benefit on those placed in its pains, I do not doubt. Whence, what he conferred on those righteous who were in the bosom of Abraham, when he descended into hell, I have not found—those from whom, according to the beatifying presence of his divinity, I never see him to have withdrawn: just as also on that very day on which he died, he promised to the thief that he would be with him in paradise, when he was about to descend to loose the pains of hell. Surely, then, he was in paradise and in the bosom of Abraham, even before, by his beatifying wisdom, and in hell by his judging power: for where is his divinity not, being besieged by no place? Nevertheless, according to the creature which, by assuming it from a certain time, while remaining God he became man—that is, according to the soul—that he was in hell, the Scripture openly declares, both sent before through prophecy and sufficiently set forth through apostolic understanding, by which it was said: *You will not leave my soul in hell*[^7].

**3. 9.** I know that it seems to some that by the death of the Lord Christ such a resurrection was already granted to the righteous as is promised to us at the end; since it is written that, by that earthquake in which, at his passion, the rocks were split and the tombs opened, many bodies of the righteous arose, and were seen with him when he rose, in the holy city. If these, indeed, did not sleep again with their bodies laid down once more, we must see how Christ is understood to be the firstborn from the dead, if so many preceded him in that resurrection. But if it is answered that this was said by anticipation, so that the tombs are understood to have been opened by that earthquake while Christ hung on the cross, but the bodies of the righteous to have arisen not then, but when he had first arisen—although, as I said, it was joined by anticipation, so that both Christ may without ambiguity be believed the firstborn from the dead, and to those righteous it was immediately granted that, with him going before, they rose into eternal incorruption and immortality—there still remains what may move us: how it could be said by Peter, which surely was said most truly, when through that prophecy he asserted that not David but Christ had been foretold, that his flesh did not see corruption; and what he added about David, that his tomb is among them: whence surely he would not convince them, if his body were no longer there; because, even if he too had earlier risen in his recent death, and his flesh had not seen corruption, that tomb could nonetheless remain. But it seems hard that David was not in that resurrection of the righteous, if eternal life was already granted to them, from whose seed Christ is so frequently, and with such evidence, and with such honor, commended. That too will be endangered which is said to the Hebrews about the ancient righteous: *That they provided something better for us, that they should not be perfected without us*[^8], if they are already set in that incorruption of resurrection which is promised to us, who are to be perfected, at the end.

**4. 10.** Why, then, Peter wished to mention only those to whom, shut up in prison, the Gospel was preached, who in the days of Noah were unbelieving when the ark was being built, you see how full of hiding places it is, and what things move me lest I dare to affirm anything from this. To this is added that, when the Apostle had said: *Which now also in a like form baptism saves you—not the putting away of the filth of the flesh, but the appeal of a good conscience toward God through the resurrection of Jesus Christ, who is at the right hand of God, swallowing up death, that we might be made heirs of eternal life, having gone into the heavens, with the angels and powers and virtues subjected to him*[^9]—he immediately added: *Since Christ, then, suffered in the flesh, arm yourselves also with the same knowledge; for he who has suffered in the flesh has ceased from sins, so as now to live the remaining time in the flesh no longer by the desires of men, but by the will of God*[^10]: then he says: *For the time past suffices for fulfilling the will of men for those who walked in lusts, and concupiscences, and drunkenness, in revelings, drinkings, and unlawful services of idols, in which they are astonished that you do not run into the same confusion of luxury, blaspheming. They shall render an account to him who is ready to judge the living and the dead*[^11]: after these words he subjoins: *For this reason the Gospel was preached also to the dead, that they might be judged indeed according to men in the flesh, but live according to God in the spirit*[^12].

**4. 11.** Whom does this profundity not move? He says the Gospel was preached *to the dead*: by whom, if we understand those who have gone out of the body, those, I think, will be the ones of whom he said above, *Who were unbelieving in the days of Noah*; or surely all whom Christ found in hell. What, then, does he mean, *that they might be judged indeed according to men in the flesh, but live according to God in the spirit*? How are they judged in the flesh, which they do not have, if they are in hell; or which they have not yet received, if they are even loosed from the pains of hell? For neither, if, as you say in inquiring, hell was emptied, are all who were then there to be believed to have risen in the flesh; nor did those who, rising, appeared with the Lord receive flesh for this, that they should be judged in it according to man: which I do not see how it can be taken even of those who were unbelieving in the days of Noah; for it is not written that they lived in the flesh, nor can it be believed that the pains of hell were loosed for this reason, that those who were freed from there should receive flesh to pay the penalty. What, then, is it, *that they might be judged according to men in the flesh, but live according to God in the spirit*? Or perhaps this was granted to those whom Christ found in hell, that through the Gospel they should be made alive in the spirit, although in the future resurrection they are to be judged in the flesh, so that through some penalty of the flesh they may pass into the kingdom of God? But if it is so, why only of those who once did not believe in the days of Noah, and not also of the rest whom the visitation of Christ found there, did they revive in the spirit through the preaching of the Gospel, afterward to be judged in the flesh by a transitory penalty? But if we take it of all, the question remains why Peter mentioned only those who were then unbelieving when the ark was being built.

**4. 12.** This too moves me, that those who try to render a reason about this matter say that, when Christ descended to hell, for those who were found there those penal places, as it were prisons, were emptied—those who had not heard the Gospel, which while they lived was not yet preached throughout the whole world, and who had just causes why they had not believed what had not been announced to them; but that thenceforth those will not have an excuse who despise the preaching of the Gospel celebrated and diffused among all nations; and therefore, those prisons then being emptied, there now remains a just judgment by which the contumacious and unbelieving are punished even with eternal fire. Those who hold this do not consider that this excuse can be had by all who, even after the resurrection of Christ, before the Gospel reached them, departed from this life. For after the Lord returned from hell, no one was permitted to go again to hell, except after hearing the Gospel, when so many were dying throughout the whole world before this announcement reached them; all of whom will have the excuse which is said to have been offered to those to whom the Lord, when he had come, is said to have preached in hell, because before they had not heard.

**4. 13.** Unless perhaps it be said that even those who, after the Lord's resurrection, died, or are dying, before the Gospel had been announced to them, could or can there in hell hear it, so that there they may believe what is to be believed about the truth of Christ, and may have, even they, the remission and salvation which those deserved to whom Christ announced it there. For not because Christ again ascended from hell was his fame there extinguished; for from here too he ascended into heaven, and yet by his announcement those who believe in him will be saved. For he was exalted for this very reason, and there was given to him *the name which is above every name, that at the name of Jesus every knee should bow, not only of things in heaven and on earth, but even of things in hell*[^13]. But if we admit this opinion, by which it can be thought that men who, while they lived, in no way believed, can believe in Christ in hell—who could bear the absurdities that follow, and the things contrary to the faith? First, lest we seem to grieve in vain over those who have gone out of the body without that grace, and in vain to take care, and earnestly to exhort men to receive it before they die, lest they be punished with everlasting death. Or if only those believe uselessly and unfruitfully in hell who, when the Gospel was announced to them here, were unwilling to believe; but it will profit those to believe who did not here despise what they could in no way hear: another more absurd consequence follows—that the Gospel should not be preached here, since all surely are going to die, and ought to come to hell without any guilt of having despised the Gospel, so that it may be able to profit them when they have believed there: to hold which is of impious vanity.

**5. 14.** Wherefore let us hold most firmly what the faith, confirmed by the most well-founded authority, holds: that *Christ died according to the Scriptures, and that he was buried, and that he rose on the third day according to the Scriptures*[^14], and the rest that have been written about him by the most attested truth: among which is also this, that he was in hell, and, the pains being loosed by which it was impossible for him to be held—from which he is also rightly understood to have loosed and freed those he willed—he received again the body which he had left on the cross, placed in the tomb. But in that question which you proposed about the words of the apostle Peter, since you perceive the things which move me, and others perhaps, if they be discussed more diligently, can move us, let us inquire, either by thinking of them among ourselves, or by consulting those whom it is fitting and we are able to consult.

**5. 15.** Consider, however, whether perhaps the whole of what the apostle Peter says about the spirits shut up in prison, who in the days of Noah had not believed, does not pertain to hell at all, but rather to those times whose figure he transferred to these times. For that deed was a figure of things to come: that those who now do not believe the Gospel, while the Church is being built up among all nations, may be understood to be like those who then did not believe when the ark was being built; but those who believed, and are saved through baptism, may be compared to those who were then saved in that same ark through water; whence he says: *So also in a like form baptism saves you*[^15]. To this likeness of figure, then, let us fit the rest also concerning the unbelievers, and not suspect that in hell the Gospel was preached, or is even now still preached, to make and to free believers, as if there too a Church had been established.

**5. 16.** To that sense which moves you, then, those who believed that Peter meant this seem to have been drawn for this reason: because he said that it was preached to the spirits shut up in prison, as if souls cannot be understood as spirits—souls which were then in the flesh, and were shut up in the darkness of ignorance as in a prison; from which kind of prison he desires to be freed who says, *Bring my soul out of prison, that it may confess to your name*[^16]; which is elsewhere called the shadow of death: from which, surely not in hell but here, those were freed of whom it is written, *To those who sat in the shadow of death, a light has risen*[^17]. But to those in the days of Noah it was preached in vain, because they did not believe, when the patience of God waited for them through the time of so many years in which that ark was built (for the very building of it was in a way a preaching); just as now those like them do not believe, who under the same figure are shut up in the darkness of ignorance as in a prison, looking in vain upon the Church being built throughout the whole world, with judgment imminent, as it were a flood by which then all the unbelieving perished: for the Lord says, *As in the days of Noah, so will it be also in the days of the Son of man. They were eating, drinking, marrying, taking wives, until Noah entered the ark; the flood came, and destroyed them all*[^18]. But because that deed also signified a future thing, therefore there the flood signified both baptism to the faithful and punishment to the unbelieving: just as, in a figure not of a deed but only of a saying, that which is written of the stone by which Christ was signified prefigured two things, both a stumbling block to the unbelieving and a building to the faithful. But sometimes in the same figure, whether of deed or of saying, even two things signify some one thing; as the faithful were signified by the timbers fitted into the building of the ark, and the same also by the eight souls who were freed in that same ark: just as in that Gospel likeness of the sheepfold, the same Christ is both the shepherd and the door.  
That before the incarnation the Son of God spoke to men.

**6. 17.** Nor let it move anyone to hinder this understanding, that the apostle Peter said that this same Christ preached to those shut up in prison who once had not believed in the days of Noah, so that we should therefore think this is to be understood because at that time Christ had not yet come. For he had not yet come, namely in the flesh, as he came when after these things he was seen on earth and conversed with men: yet from the beginning of the human race—whether to convict the wicked, as Cain, and earlier Adam himself and his wife; or to console the good; or to admonish both, so that some might believe to their salvation, others not believe to their punishment—he himself surely came, not in the flesh, but in the spirit, addressing by suitable visions whom he willed, as he willed. But as to what I said, He came in the spirit—even the Son himself, in the substance of the Deity, since he is not a body, is surely spirit: but what does the Son do without the Holy Spirit, or without the Father, since all the works of the Trinity are inseparable?

**6. 18.** The very words of the Scripture in question also sufficiently indicate this, as I think, to those who attend diligently: *For Christ*, he says, *died once for our sins, the just for the unjust, that he might bring us to God, being put to death indeed in the flesh, but made alive in the spirit: in which, coming, he preached also to those spirits who were shut up in prison, who once had been unbelieving, when the patience of God waited in the days of Noah, while the ark was being built*[^19]. Now, as I judge, the order of the words is attended to: Christ put to death in the flesh, but made alive in the spirit. In which spirit, coming, he preached also to those spirits who once had been unbelieving in the days of Noah: since, before he came in the flesh to die for us—which he did once—he often came before in the spirit to whom he willed, admonishing them by visions as he willed, surely in the spirit, by which spirit he was also made alive, when in his passion he had been put to death in the flesh. For what is it that he was made alive in the spirit, but that the same flesh, in which alone he had been put to death, rose by the life-giving spirit?

**7. 19.** For that Jesus was put to death in the soul, that is, in that spirit which is man's, who would dare to say, since the death of the soul is nothing but sin, from which he was wholly free, when he was put to death in the flesh for us? For if the souls of all men are from that one which was breathed into the first man, through whom *sin entered into the world, and through sin death, and so it passed to all men*[^20]: either the soul of Christ is not from there, since he had no sin at all, neither original nor his own, on account of which death might seem owed to him—for he paid for us the death he did not owe, in whom the prince of the world and ruler of death found nothing; for it is not absurd that he who created the soul for the first man should create one also for himself—or, if it too is from there, by assuming it he cleansed it, so that, without any sin at all, neither committed nor transmitted, coming to us he was born of a virgin. But if souls are not propagated from that one, and the flesh alone draws original sin from Adam, then the Son of God created a soul for himself as he creates for others, which he nevertheless did not mix with the flesh of sin, but with the likeness of the flesh of sin. For he took from the Virgin the true substance indeed of flesh, yet not the flesh of sin, since it was neither sown nor conceived from carnal concupiscence; mortal indeed, and changeable through the ages, as most like to the flesh of sin, without sin.

**7. 20.** And through this, whatever opinion about the soul be true—of which I dare not yet rashly affirm any, except only to repudiate that by which souls are believed to be thrust, according to the merits of I know not what prior acts of their own, each into its own body as into a prison—certainly the soul of Christ was not only immortal according to the nature of the others, but was also put to death by no sin nor punished by any damnation, by which two causes the death of the soul can be understood; and therefore it could not be said that Christ was made alive in the spirit according to it. For he was made alive in that thing in which he had been put to death: therefore it was said of the flesh. For the soul itself revived by the soul returning, since it was itself dead by the soul departing. He was therefore said to be put to death in the flesh, because he died according to the flesh alone: but made alive in the spirit, because, by the working of that spirit in which he came to whom he willed and preached, even the flesh itself was made alive and rose, in which he now comes to men.

**7. 21.** Accordingly, that too which was afterward said of the unbelievers—*who will render an account to him who is ready to judge the living and the dead*[^21]—does not follow that we should here understand as dead those who have gone out of the body. For it can be that he called the unbelievers dead; that is, dead in soul, of whom it is said, *Let the dead bury their dead*[^22]; but the living, those who believe in him, not in vain hearing, *Rise, you who sleep, and arise from the dead, and Christ will enlighten you*[^23]; of whom the Lord himself also says, *The hour comes, and now is, when the dead will hear the voice of the Son of God; and those who hear will live*[^24]. Accordingly, even what follows, when Peter says, *For this reason the Gospel was preached also to the dead, that they might be judged indeed according to men in the flesh, but live according to God in the spirit*[^25], does not compel us to understand it of hell. For this reason, in this life, the Gospel was preached also to the dead, that is, to the unbelieving and the wicked, so that when they have believed they might be judged indeed according to men in the flesh, that is, in various tribulations and in the very death of the flesh; whence the same Apostle says in another place that *it is time that judgment begin at the house of the Lord*[^26]: but live according to God in the spirit, because in it too they had been put to death, while they were held by the death of unbelief and impiety.

**7. 22.** Whoever is displeased by this exposition of Peter's words, or to whom, even if it does not displease, it nevertheless does not suffice, let him seek to understand them according to hell. And if he is able so to loose those things by which I said above I am moved as to take away their doubt, let him impart it to me also: and if this should be done, those words could be understood in both ways; but this opinion is convicted of no falsehood. As for those questions which you sent before, except for the vision of God through the body—on which a greater work must be undertaken—I have answered as I could, and sent it through the deacon Asellus, which I think you have already received. But in your recent memorandum, to which I have now replied, you had asked two things, both of which have been treated, one more broadly, the other more briefly: namely, about the words of the apostle Peter, and about the soul of the Lord. As for the copy of your letter which contains the question whether the substance of God can be seen corporeally as it were in a place, and which somehow went astray among us, and could not be found though long sought, I again ask that you not be reluctant to send it.
