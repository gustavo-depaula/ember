# Letter 83

TO THE MOST BLESSED LORD, AND VENERABLY MOST BELOVED AND MOST LONGED-FOR BROTHER AND FELLOW-BISHOP ALYPIUS, AND TO THE BROTHERS WHO ARE WITH YOU, AUGUSTINE AND THE BROTHERS WHO ARE WITH ME SEND GREETING IN THE LORD.

**1.** The sorrow of the Church of Thiava does not permit my heart to rest, until I hear from you that they have been restored to their former spirit; which must be done quickly. For if the Apostle busied himself so much about one man only, saying: *Lest such a one be swallowed up by greater sorrow;* where he also says: *So that we may not be taken possession of by Satan; for we are not ignorant of his minds*[^1]—how much more does it behoove us to act vigilantly, lest we lament this in the whole flock, and especially in those who have now come to Catholic peace, and whom I can in no way abandon. But because the narrowness of time did not allow us to refine together there an opinion carefully deliberated, let your Holiness receive what has pleased me, after our parting, on long reflection; and if it pleases you too, let the letter which I wrote to them in our common name now be sent without delay.

**2.** You said that they should have half, and that the other half should be provided for them by me from somewhere. But I think that, if the whole were taken from them, there would be something for which we might be said to have labored so greatly not about money, but about justice. But when we grant them half, and in that way compose peace with them at some point, it will sufficiently appear that our concern was nothing other than financial; and you see what ruin follows. For both we will seem to them to have taken away half of another's property; and they will seem to us to have suffered dishonorably and unfairly, so as to be helped from the half, when the whole had been the poor's. For as for what you said: "Care must be taken, lest, when we wish a doubtful matter to be corrected, we make greater wounds," it will be worth just as much, if half is granted them. For on account of that very half, those whose conversion we wish to provide for—so that by this example it be done with them—will put off the sale of their goods through those excusatory delays. Then it would be a wonder if a doubtful matter caused so great a scandal of the whole people, when they think their bishops, whom they hold in high regard, stained with sordid avarice, while the malign appearance is not avoided.

**3.** For when anyone is converted to the monastery, if he is converted with a truthful heart, he does not think of that, especially when admonished how great an evil it is. But if he is false, and seeks his own, not the things of Jesus Christ[^2], he surely does not have love. And what does it profit him, if he distributes all his goods to the poor, and hands over his body to be burned[^3]? To this is added that that thing, as we have already discussed, can henceforth be avoided, and dealt with regarding him who is converted, if he cannot be admitted to the fellowship of the brothers before he has stripped himself of all those impediments, and is drawn out of leisure once his property has ceased to be his. But this death of the weak, and so great an impediment to the salvation of those for whom we labor so greatly to gain them for Catholic peace, cannot otherwise be avoided, except that they understand most openly that we in no way busy ourselves about money in such cases. Which they will in no way understand, unless we leave for their uses that thing which they always thought belonged to the priests; for even if it did not belong to him, they ought to have known this from the beginning.

**4.** It seems to me, therefore, that this rule is to be retained in matters of this kind: that whatever, by the law by which such things are possessed, has belonged to one who is ordained a cleric somewhere, should pertain to the Church in which he is ordained. But the matter in question is so much the priest Honoratus's by the same law that, not only had he been ordained elsewhere, but were he still established in the monastery of Thagaste to die without his property having been sold, or transferred to anyone by a manifest donation, none but his heirs would succeed to it—just as the brother Aemilianus succeeded the brother Privatus in those thirty solidi. These things, then, are to be guarded against beforehand; but if they have not been guarded against, those rights must be preserved for them which, according to civil society, are established for having or not having such things; so that we may abstain, as much as we can, from every malign thing—indeed, even appearance[^4]—and may keep a good reputation, very necessary to our stewardship. But how malign the appearance is, let your holy prudence observe. Apart from their sorrow, which we have experienced, lest perhaps I myself be deceived, as is wont to happen while I err, more prone toward my own opinion, I told the case to our brother and colleague Samsucius, not yet saying what now seems to me, but rather adding what had seemed to both of us when we were resisting them: he was vehemently horrified, and marveled that this had seemed so to us; moved by nothing else than the foul appearance itself, most unworthy not of our life, but of anyone's life and morals.

**5.** Accordingly, I beg you not to delay sending, signed, the letter which I wrote to them in our common name. And if perhaps you most acutely perceive that to be just, let the weak not be compelled now to learn what I do not yet understand, so that in their regard, in this case, that may be observed which the Lord says: *I have many things to say to you, but you cannot bear them now*[^5]. For sparing such weakness, he also said, concerning the paying of tribute: *Therefore the sons are free; but lest we scandalize them,* and the rest, when he sent Peter to pay the didrachmas which were then exacted[^6]. For he knew another law, by which he owed nothing of the kind; but by that law he paid him tribute, by which law we said the heir of the priest Honoratus would have succeeded, if he had died before he either gave away or sold his property. Although even in the very law of the Church the apostle Paul spares the weak, and does not exact the stipend due to him, certain in conscience that he would most rightly exact it; but avoiding nothing other than the suspicion that disturbs the good odor of Christ, and abstaining from that malign appearance, in those regions where he knew this behooved[^7], and perhaps before he had experienced the sorrow of men. But let us, slower, or having experienced it, correct what we ought to have foreseen.

**6.** Finally, because I fear everything, and I remember what you proposed at our parting—that the brothers of Thagaste hold me debtor for half of that price—if you clearly perceive this to be just, I do not refuse, on this condition only: that I repay when I have it, that is, when something so great accrues to the monastery of Hippo that this can be done without straitness; so that, with so great a sum withdrawn there, no less than an equal part, according to the number of those dwelling together, may come to ours.
