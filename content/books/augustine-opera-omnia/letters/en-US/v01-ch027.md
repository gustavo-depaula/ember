# Letter 27

To the truly holy and venerable lord, and to be proclaimed with outstanding praise in Christ, his brother Paulinus, Augustine sends greeting in the Lord

**1.** O good man and good brother, you were hidden from my soul: and I tell it to bear that you are still hidden from my eyes, and it scarcely obeys me; indeed, it does not obey. Or does it bear it? Since the longing for you tortures me within the soul itself? For if I were suffering bodily troubles, and if they did not disturb the equanimity of my mind, I would rightly be said to bear them; but since I do not bear it with an even mind that I do not see you, it is intolerable to call this toleration. But since you are such as you are, to be without you would perhaps be more intolerably borne. It is well, then, that I cannot bear it with an even mind, since, if I bore it with an even mind, I would not be one to be borne with an even mind. It is a strange thing, but yet true, that has happened to me: I grieve that I do not see you, and grief itself consoles me. So displeasing to me is the fortitude by which the absence of good men such as you are is patiently borne. For we surely long for the future Jerusalem, and the more impatiently we long for it, the more patiently we endure all things for its sake. Who, then, can fail to rejoice at seeing you, so that he can fail to grieve so long as he does not see you! Therefore I can do neither; and since, if I could, I would do it monstrously, I am delighted that I cannot, and in the fact that I am delighted there is some consolation. And so, as I grieve, a grief not soothed but pondered consoles me. Do not reproach me, I beg, with the holier gravity in which you excel, and say that I do not rightly grieve that I do not yet know you, since you have laid open your mind to me—that is, your inner self—to be looked upon. For what? If I had learned somewhere, even in your earthly city, that you were my brother and lover, and so great and such a man in the Lord, would you think I would feel no grief if I were not allowed to know your home? How, then, should I not grieve that I do not yet know your face—that is, the home of your soul, which I know as my own?

**2.** For I read your letters flowing with milk and honey[^1], displaying the simplicity of your heart, in which you seek the Lord, thinking of him in goodness[^2], and bringing to him brightness and honor[^3]. The brothers have read them, and they rejoice untiringly and ineffably at such abundant and such excellent gifts of God, your good things. As many as have read them snatch at them, because they are snatched away when they read. How sweet the odor of Christ[^4], and how it is fragrant from them, cannot be told. While those letters offer you to be seen, how they rouse us to seek you! For they make you both clearly perceptible and desirable. For the more they in some way display your presence to us, the more they do not allow us to bear your absence. All love you in them, and desire to be loved by you. God is praised and blessed, by whose grace you are such. There Christ is roused, that he may deign to calm the winds and seas for you as you head toward his stability. There the reader sees a wife, not a leader to softness for her husband, but a leader back to fortitude into the bones of her husband, whom, brought back and restored to your unity, and joined to you by spiritual bonds the firmer for being the more chaste, with the services due to your Holiness, we greet again in you alone. There the cedars of Lebanon, laid low to the ground and raised up in the framing of the ark by the joinery of love, cleave the waves of this world without decay. There glory is despised in order to be acquired, and the world is left in order to be obtained. There the little ones—or even the somewhat grown-up sons of Babylon—are dashed against the rock[^5], that is, the vices of confusion and worldly pride.

**3.** These and such most sweet and most sacred spectacles your letters offer to those who read them; those letters, letters of unfeigned faith, letters of good hope, letters of pure love. How they breathe upon us your thirst, and the longing and the fainting of your soul for the courts of the Lord[^6]! What riches of a sincere heart do they boil up, breathing what most holy love! What thanks they give to God! what they obtain from God! Are they more soothing or more ardent; more luminous or more fruitful? For what is it that so caresses us, so kindles us, so rains upon us, and is so serene? What is it, I ask you, or what shall I repay you for them, except that I am wholly yours in him whose you are wholly? If this is little, surely I have no more. But you have brought it about that it does not seem little to me, you who in that letter deigned to honor me with such great praises that, when I pour myself back to you, if I should think this little, I would be convicted of not having believed you. I am indeed ashamed to believe so much good about myself, but I am more loath not to believe you. There is something I can do: I will not believe myself to be such as you suppose, since I do not recognize it; and I will believe that I am loved by you, since I feel it and plainly perceive it: thus I shall prove neither rash toward myself nor ungrateful toward you. And when I offer myself wholly to you, it is not little: for I offer one whom you most vehemently love; and I offer, if not such as you judge me to be, yet him for whom you pray that I may deserve to be such. For this I now rather ask you to do: that you not wish less to be added to me, toward what I am, while you esteem me already to be what I am not.

**4.** Behold, he is my most dear friend, and from early youth most familiarly dearest to me, who brings this letter to your Excellence and most outstanding Charity. His name is in the book On Religion, which your Holiness, as far as you indicate in your letter, read most gladly: for he has become more pleasant to you by the commendation of so great a man, who sent him to you. Yet I would not have you believe what this so familiar friend of mine may perhaps have said in praise of me. For I have often perceived even him to judge falsely, not from zeal for lying, but from a proneness to love, and to suppose that I had already received certain things, for the receiving of which I was gaping toward the Lord with the open mouth of my heart. And if this is to my face, who would not guess how much, when I am absent, he joyfully pours out about me—things better than true? But he will provide a supply of our books for your venerable zeal: for I do not know that I have written anything, whether for the ears of those who are outside the Church of God, or for those of the brothers, which he himself does not have. But when you read, my holy Paulinus, do not let the things which truth speaks through our weakness so snatch you away that you attend less diligently to the things which I myself speak; lest, while you eagerly drink in the good and right things which are given to the minister, you not pray for the sins and errors which I myself commit. For in those things which, if you attend, will rightly displease you, I myself am seen; but in those things which, through the gift of the Spirit which you have received, rightly please you in my books, he is to be loved, he is to be proclaimed, with whom is the fountain of life, and in whose light we shall see light[^7], not in a riddle, but face to face; but now we see in a riddle[^8]. In those things, then, which I myself have belched up from the old leaven, when, reading them, I recognize them, I judge myself with grief; but in those which I have said from the unleavened bread of sincerity and truth, the gift of God, I exult with trembling. For what have we that we have not received[^9]? But he is better who is rich in greater and more numerous gifts of God than he who is rich in fewer and smaller ones. Who denies it? But again, it is better to give thanks to God himself even for a small gift of God than to wish thanks to be given to oneself for a great one. That I may always confess these things from the heart, and that my heart may not be out of tune with my tongue, pray for me, brother. Pray, I beseech you, that I may call upon the Lord not wishing to be praised, but praising, and I shall be saved from my enemies[^10].

**5.** There is also another reason for you to love this brother more: for he is a kinsman of the venerable and truly blessed bishop Alypius, whom you embrace with your whole heart, and rightly; for whoever thinks kindly of that man thinks of God's great mercy and of God's wonderful gifts. And so, when he had read your request, by which you indicated that you desired him to write his history for you, he both wished to do it, on account of your goodwill, and was unwilling, on account of his modesty: and when I saw him wavering between love and shame, I transferred the burden from him to my own shoulders; for this he even bade me do by letter. Quickly, then, if the Lord helps, I will set the whole of Alypius into your heart; for this is what I especially feared, lest he should be afraid to disclose all that the Lord has conferred upon him, lest to someone of less understanding (for they would not be read by you alone) he should seem to be proclaiming not God's divine gifts granted to men, but himself, and you, who know how to read these things, should, on account of guarding against the weakness of others, be cheated of the debt of brotherly acquaintance: which I would have done already, and you would already be reading him, had not the brother's sudden departure been resolved upon. Him I so commend to your heart and tongue that you may show yourself to him as courteously as if you had learned of him not now, but before, along with me. For if he does not hesitate to disclose himself to your heart, he will be healed, either wholly or in great part, through your tongue. For I want him to be pounded more often by the voices of those who love a friend not in a worldly way.

**6.** But his son, our son, whose name too you will find in some of our books, even if he were not himself going to your Charity's presence, I had resolved to hand over into your hand by letter, to be consoled, exhorted, instructed, not so much by the sound of your mouth as by the example of your strength. For I burn that, while his age is still in green grass, he may turn the tares into grain, and may believe those who have experienced what he dangerously desires to experience. Now, then, from his poem, and from the letter which I sent to him, your most kindly and most gentle prudence understands what I grieve about him, what I fear, what I desire. Nor do I despair that the Lord will be present, so that through you, his minister, I may be freed from such great surgings of cares. Indeed, since you will read many of our writings, your love will be much more pleasing to me, if from those things which displease you, you will correct me, a just man in mercy, and reprove me[^11]. For you are not such that I should fear to have my head made fat with your oil. The brothers—not only those who dwell with us, and those who, dwelling anywhere, serve God equally, but nearly all who have gladly come to know us in Christ—greet, venerate, and long for your Brotherliness, your Blessedness, your Kindness. I do not dare to ask; but if you have leisure from ecclesiastical duties, you see what Africa thirsts for along with me.
