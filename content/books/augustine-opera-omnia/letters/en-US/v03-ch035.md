# Letter 186

To the most blessed lord, and to be embraced in a brotherly way in the bowels of Christ, more than can be said longed-for brother and fellow bishop Paulinus, from Alypius and Augustine.

**1. 1.** At last, at some time, God has provided us a most faithful bearer of letters, our brother Januarius, deservedly most dear to us all; through whom, even if we did not write, your sincerity could know all that is about us, as it were through a living and understanding letter. Pelagius—whom we believe, that he might be distinguished from the one who is called Pelagius of Terentius, to have been surnamed the Briton—that you loved as a servant of God, we know: but now how you love him, we do not know. For we too not only loved, but even love him; but we love him now in one way, we loved him once in another: for then, because he seemed to us of right faith; but now, that by God's mercy he may be freed from the things which he is said to hold hostile and adverse to the grace of God. For when rumor for some time bandied this about him, it was surely not easily to be believed; for rumor is wont to lie: but to bring us nearer to believing, there came the fact that we read a certain book of his, striving to persuade utterly those things which efface from the hearts of the faithful the grace of God imparted to the human race through the one Mediator of God and men, the man Christ Jesus, believed by the hearts of the faithful—handed to us by servants of Christ, who had most zealously heard him teaching such things, and had followed him. To which book, at their request, because we saw that it ought to be done, the name of the author being kept silent, lest, offended, he be rendered more incurable, an answer was made by the disputation of one of us. In which book this is contained, and is in manifold and abundant ways asserted, which he treats also in a certain letter given to your veneration, where he says that he ought not to be thought to defend free choice without the grace of God, since he said that a possibility of willing and working, without which we could will and do no good, was implanted in us by the Creator: so that, namely, by this teacher himself, that grace of God is understood which is common to Pagans and Christians, to the impious and the pious, to the faithful and the unfaithful.

**1. 2.** These evils, by which the coming of the Savior would be made void—where we can say this which the Apostle says of the Law, *If righteousness is by nature, then Christ died in vain*[^1]—we refuted, as we could, in the hearts of those who held these things, so that, these being known, he too, if it could be done, might amend them unprovoked; and so both the ruin of the error might be destroyed, and the man's modesty spared. But after letters came to us from the East, most openly ventilating this same cause, we ought in no way now, by whatever episcopal authority, to be lacking to the Church. There were sent, therefore, about this matter, from two councils, the Carthaginian and the Milevitan, reports to the apostolic See, before the ecclesiastical proceedings, by which Pelagius is reported to have been cleared before the bishops of the province of Palestine, had reached either our hands, or Africa. We wrote also to pope Innocent of blessed memory, besides the reports of the councils, a familiar letter, where we dealt with this same cause somewhat longer. To all of this he wrote back to us in the way which it was right and fitting for the prelate of the apostolic See.

**1. 3.** All of which you will now be able to read, if perhaps either none or not all of them had reached you: where you will see that, the moderation due toward the man being kept—lest he be condemned if he condemned what was depraved—nevertheless that novel and pernicious error itself was so compressed by ecclesiastical authority, that we much marvel that there are still some who try, by any error whatever, to resist the grace of God; if, indeed, they have learned that these things were done, which the grace of God through Jesus Christ our Lord (which the true and catholic faith always holds, the Church) transfers the little ones with the great from the death of the first man to the life of the second man; not only by giving grief over sins, but also by so helping toward not sinning and living rightly those who can now use the choice of the will, that unless it helps, we can have nothing of piety and righteousness, whether in deed, or even in the very will: for God works in us both to will and to work, for his good will.

**2. 4.** For who distinguishes us from that mass and conglomeration of perdition, except he who came to seek and to save what had perished? Whence the Apostle asks, saying: *For who distinguishes you?* where, if a man should say, My faith, my will, my good work; it is answered him: *For what have you that you did not receive? And if you received it, why do you boast as if you had not received it?*[^2] All this, surely, is for this reason, not that a man should not boast, but that *he who boasts should boast in the Lord*[^3]; not of works, lest perhaps anyone be exalted: not because good works are made void, since God renders to each according to his works, and there is *glory and honor and peace to everyone who works good*[^4]; but because works are from grace, not grace from works; since the faith which works through love would work nothing, unless the love of God itself were poured out in our hearts by the Holy Spirit who has been given to us. Nor would faith itself be in us, unless God divided to each a measure of faith.

**2. 5.** It is good, then, for a man that, with all the strength of his free choice, he say truly: *My strength, I will keep it for you*[^5]: for he who thought he could, without his help, keep what he had given, having set out into a far country and living prodigally, consumed all, and worn down by the misery of hard servitude, and returned into himself, said: *I will arise, and go to my father*[^6]: which good thought when would he have had, unless the most merciful father had inspired it too in him in secret? Understanding which, that minister of the New Testament says: *Not that we are sufficient to think anything of ourselves as from ourselves; but our sufficiency is from God*[^7]. Whence he too, when he had said, *My strength, I will keep it for you*; lest even this very thing, that he keeps it, he should arrogate to his own strength, as though it had come to his mind that, *unless the Lord guards the city, in vain do those who guard it keep watch; and he who keeps Israel neither sleeps nor slumbers*[^8]; added the cause whence he becomes fit to keep it, or rather by what keeper he is preserved: *Because you, O God*, he says, *are my upholder*[^9].

**2. 6.** Let this man, then, recollect, if he can, his own merits, whence it came about that God deigned to be his upholder, as though God were upheld. Let him recollect whether he sought, or was sought, namely by him who came to seek and to save what had perished. For if he should wish to seek what he merited before grace, that he should receive it, a man will be able to find his own evils, not goods, even if the grace of the Savior should find him having a life of one day upon the earth. Because if a man works some good that he may merit grace, the wages are not imputed to him according to grace, but according to debt. But if he believes in him who justifies the impious, that his faith may be reckoned unto righteousness (for the just lives by faith), surely before he is justified by grace, that is, made just, what is the impious man but impious? whom, if debt followed him, what would be rendered to his merit but punishment? If, then, by grace, it is no longer of works; otherwise grace is no longer grace. For to works debt is rendered, grace is given freely; whence too it is named.

**3. 7.** But if anyone should say that faith merits the grace of working well; we cannot deny it, nay rather we most gratefully confess it. For we wish that these our brothers, who boast much of their works, may have this faith, by which they may obtain the love which alone truly works well: but love is so far a gift of God, that it is even called God. Those, then, who have the faith by which they obtain justification, have come by the grace of God to the law of righteousness: whence it is said: *In an acceptable time I heard you, and in the day of salvation I helped you*[^10]. Accordingly, in those who by the election of grace are saved, God the helper works both to will and to work, for his good will; since to those who love God all things work together unto good: if all things, surely also that very love which we obtain by faith, that we may love, by his grace, him who first loved us that we might believe in him, and by loving may work well—which we did not work that we might be loved.

**3. 8.** But these people await rewards for their merits as though owed, nor do they attribute the merits themselves to the grace of God, but to the powers of their own will, just as it was said of carnal Israel, that, *pursuing the law of righteousness, they did not come to the law of righteousness: why? Because not from faith, but as it were from works*. For that is the righteousness from faith, which the Gentiles laid hold of, of whom it was said: *What then shall we say? That the Gentiles, who did not know righteousness, laid hold of righteousness; but the righteousness which is from faith. But Israel, pursuing the law of righteousness, did not come to the law of righteousness: why? Because not from faith, but as it were from works. For they stumbled at the stone of stumbling, as it is written: Behold, I place in Zion a stone of stumbling, and a rock of scandal; and whoever believes in him will not be confounded*[^11]. That is the righteousness from faith, by which we believe that we are justified, that is, made just, by the grace of God through Jesus Christ our Lord, that we may be found in him, *not having our own righteousness which is from the Law, but that which is through faith in Christ*[^12]. Which righteousness from God in faith is surely in the faith by which we believe that righteousness is given us divinely, not made by us in us by our own strength.

**3. 9.** For why did the Apostle call that righteousness which is from the Law his own, not from God? as though the Law were not from God? Who but an impious man would think this? But because the Law commands by the letter, does not help by the Spirit, whoever so hears the letter of the Law that it seems to him enough to have known what it commands or prohibits, so that he trusts to fulfill this by the strength of his own choice, and does not flee by faith to be helped to the life-giving Spirit, lest the letter, having made him guilty, kill him; he surely has zeal for God, but not according to knowledge. For, *being ignorant of God's righteousness*, that is, that which is given from God, *and wishing to establish his own*, that it be only from the Law, *he has not submitted to the righteousness of God: for Christ is the end of the law unto righteousness to everyone who believes*[^13], as the same Apostle says, that *we may be the righteousness of God in him*[^14]. Justified, therefore, from faith, *we have peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ*[^15]: but justified freely by his grace, lest faith itself be proud.

**3. 10.** Nor let him say to himself, If from faith, how freely? for what faith merits, why is it not rather rendered than given? Let not the faithful man say these things, because when he has said, That I may merit justification, I have faith; it is answered him: *For what have you that you did not receive?*[^16] Since, then, faith obtains justification, as to each one God has divided even the measure of faith itself, no human merit precedes the grace of God, but grace itself merits to be increased, that, increased, it may merit to be perfected, the will accompanying, not leading, following on foot, not going before. Whence he who said, *My strength, I will keep it for you*, and rendered the cause, saying, *Because you, O God, are my upholder*; as it were inquiring by what merits he had attained this, and finding nothing in himself before the grace of God: *My God*, he says, *his mercy will go before me*[^17]. However much, he says, I consider my preceding merits, his mercy will go before me. Accordingly, keeping for him the strength bestowed by him, by the same preserver he does not lose what by the same bestower he received. Nor does he merit greater things for any other reason than by piously and faithfully knowing from whom all his goods are, and by knowing this not from himself, lest even this be in him which is not from God. Whence the Apostle most excellently says: *We have not received the spirit of this world, but the Spirit which is from God; that we may know the things that have been given us by God*[^18]. And through this even man's merit itself is a gratuitous gift: nor does anyone merit to receive any good thing from the Father of lights, from whom comes down every best gift, except by receiving what he does not merit.

**4. 11.** But it is much more indulgent, and without any doubt more gratuitous, that which the grace of God through Jesus Christ our Lord bestows on infants—that generation from Adam may not harm them, and regeneration in Christ may profit them—in whom the mercy of God so long before goes before even the very sense of receiving: who certainly, if at this tiny age they go out of the body, knowingly receive eternal life and the kingdom of heaven, by the merit of that gift which here, although it surely profited, they did not know. Certainly in them, after the teaching of the former things, these are only the former gifts, in the giving of which the grace of God so works, that neither the will of the recipients is either sent before, or joined, or follows after: since so great a benefit is given not only to those not willing, but even to those resisting; which would be imputed to them as a great sacrilege, if the choice of the will already availed anything in them.

**4. 12.** This we have said on account of those who, not able in the cause of grace to search out the inscrutable judgments of God—why, from the mass of Adam, which surely from one wholly collapsed into condemnation, he makes that vessel for honor, that for dishonor—nevertheless dare to constitute little ones guilty of their own sins, so that those who cannot think good or evil things are supposed, through free choice, to be able to merit either punishment or grace: whereas rather the apostolic truth, by saying, *From one all unto condemnation*[^19], sufficiently shows that they are born in punishment, that, not by merit, but by mercy, they are reborn in grace. Otherwise grace is no longer grace, if it is given not freely by divine works, but rendered to human merits. Which grace alone so distinguishes from punishment, that, since punishment is owed from Adam to all, but grace through the one Jesus Christ is owed to none, but gratuitous, that it may truly be grace—the judgments of God can be inscrutable, as God, why he himself distinguishes the little ones, whom no merits distinguish; but cannot be iniquitous, because *all the ways of the Lord are mercy and truth*[^20]. Whence when to one the grace of mercy is shown, he has nothing to boast of human merit; since not from works, lest perhaps anyone be exalted. But when to another the truth of vengeance is rendered, he has no reason justly to complain: for that is rendered which is by right owed to sin; since the one in whom all sinned is surely not iniquitously punished even in each individual. In whose punishment it is more clearly shown what is conferred on the vessels of mercy by undeserved, but true grace, that is, gratuitous.

**5. 13.** But whence they argue against the Apostle, who most openly says: *Through one man sin entered into the world, and through sin death, and so it passed to all men, in whom all sinned*[^21], saying that even little ones have their own sins through free choice, it is tiresome to attend to, and irksome to bring forward, but we are rather compelled to say it. For what great and acute minds have been able to think, either it is of poverty to avoid by keeping silent, or of arrogance to pass over by despising. "Behold," they say, "Esau and Jacob struggle within the maternal womb, and while they are born, one is supplanted by the other, and, the hand of the one following and holding being found on the foot of the one preceding, a persevering struggle is in a way proved. How, then, in infants doing these things, is there no choice of one's own will, either to good or to evil, whence rewards or punishments may follow upon preceding merits?"

**5. 14.** To this we say that those movements and as it were quarrel of the little ones were a sign of great things, because it was not choice, but a portent. For neither are we going to give free choice of will even to asses, since a beast of burden of this kind, as it is written, a dumb beast of burden, answering with a man's voice, rebuked the madness of the prophet. But those who contend that such movements—not portentous, but voluntary acts, and made not about the little ones, but by the little ones—what will they answer to the Apostle, who, when he saw that these twins too were to be recalled as a proof of gratuitous grace: *For when they were not yet born, nor had done anything good or evil, that the purpose of God according to election might stand, not of works but of him who calls, it was said, The elder shall serve the younger*[^22]? then adding the testimony of the prophet, who indeed said these things long after, but yet declared the ancient counsel of God about this matter: *As it is written*, he says: *Jacob I loved, but Esau I hated*[^23].

**5. 15.** The Teacher of the Gentiles, in faith and truth, testifies that these twins, not yet born, had done nothing good or evil, that grace might be commended; so that what was said, *The elder shall serve the younger*, might be understood as said not of works, but of him who calls, that the purpose of God according to election might stand, not go before the merit of man. For he does not say the election of the human will or nature, since the condition of death and damnation was equal in each; but without doubt the election of grace, which does not find the ones to be chosen, but makes them—of which, speaking also in what follows in that same Epistle, he says: *So then even at this time a remnant has been saved by the election of grace. But if by grace, it is no longer of works; otherwise grace is no longer grace*[^24]. With which place this place sufficiently agrees, where it is recalled that it was said, not of works, but of him who calls: *The elder shall serve the younger*. Why, then, is so resistance made, so shamelessly, to the most splendid commender of grace, about the free choice of infants and the acts of the not-yet-born? why is grace held to be anticipated by merits—which would not be grace, if it were imputed according to merit? Why, against the salvation which was sent to the lost, which came to the unworthy, however acute, however copious and ornate, is it nevertheless—a wonder—contended by a Christian disputation?

**6. 16.** "But how," they say, "is there no iniquity with God, if by loving he distinguishes those whom no merits of works distinguish?" This is said to us as though the Apostle himself did not see, did not set forth, did not answer it. He surely saw what, these things being heard, human weakness or ignorance might think, and, setting the same question to himself, he says: *What then shall we say? Is there iniquity with God?* He immediately answered: *Far be it*. And rendering the reason why far be it, that is, why there is no iniquity with God, he does not say: "For he judges the merits or works even of little ones, even if they are still set in the maternal womb"—for how would he say this, who had already said about the not-yet-born, and about those who had as yet done nothing good or evil, that it was said not of works, but of him who calls, *The elder shall serve the younger*?—but, when he wished to show why there is no iniquity with God in them, he says: *For to Moses he says, I will have mercy on whom I have had mercy, and I will show mercy to whom I have been merciful*[^25]. What did he teach us here, except that from that mass of the first man, to whom death is by merit owed, it pertains not to the merits of men, but to the mercy of God, that anyone is freed: and so there is no iniquity with God; because neither by remitting, nor by exacting what is owed, is he unjust? For there is grace as indulgence, where there could be just vengeance. And hence it more evidently appears, to the one freed from owed punishment and freely justified, how great a benefit is conferred, that another equally guilty is punished without the iniquity of the one punishing.

**6. 17.** Finally he subjoins and says: *Therefore it is not of him who wills, nor of him who runs, but of God who has mercy*[^26]: this was said on account of those who are justified, freed by grace. But on account of those over whom the wrath of God remains—since God uses even these well, to teach others whom he deigns to free—he subjoined and said: *For the Scripture says to Pharaoh, For this I have raised you up, that I may show in you my power, and that my name may be announced in all the earth*[^27]. Then concluding to both: *Therefore*, he says, *he has mercy on whom he wills, and whom he wills he hardens*[^28]; surely neither by any iniquity, but both by mercy and truth. And yet still the bold weakness is moved, namely of those who, according to the conjectures of the human heart, try to think the inscrutable height of the judgments of God!

**6. 18.** Setting this question to himself from the adverse side, the Apostle says: *You will say to me then, Why does he still complain? for who resists his will?* Let us think this said to us. What else, then, than what the Apostle answered, ought we to answer? or if such things move us too, because we too are men, it behooves us all together to hear him saying: *O man, who are you that you should answer God? Does the thing formed say to him who formed it, Why have you made me thus? Or does not the potter of the clay have power, from the same mass, to make one vessel for honor, and another for dishonor?*[^29] If this mass were so in the middle that, as it merited nothing good, so it merited nothing evil either, it would not in vain seem iniquity, that from it vessels for dishonor should be made: but since through the free choice of the first man the whole flowed from one into condemnation, without doubt that vessels are made from it for honor is to be attributed not to its own righteousness, which no grace preceded, but to the mercy of God; but that they are made for dishonor, not to the iniquity of God—far be it that it be with God—but to judgment. Whoever is thus wise with the catholic Church does not dispute against grace for merits, but sings to the Lord mercy and judgment, so that he neither, ungrateful, refuse mercy, nor, unjust, accuse judgment.

**6. 19.** For there is another lump of which the same Apostle says: *But if the firstfruits are holy, so also is the lump; and if the root is holy, so also are the branches*[^30]. That is from Abraham, not from Adam, that is, from the communion of the sacrament and the likeness of faith, not from the mortal stock: but this lump, or, as it is read in very many manuscripts, mass, since the whole is of death, when through one man sin entered into the world, and through sin death, and so it passed to all men, in whom all sinned; by mercy one vessel is made for honor, but by judgment another for dishonor. Neither there do merits precede the grace of the liberator; nor here do sins escape the justice of the punisher. Which indeed, in the older ages, against the contentious, does not so evidently appear, where they fight as it were protected by some obscurity for the merits of men: but for this reason, against their contention, the Apostle found those in whom, not yet born, nor doing anything good or evil, it was said, not of works, but of him who calls, *The elder shall serve the younger*.

**6. 20.** In which matter, since the judgments of God are too deep and inscrutable, and his ways past finding out, let a man meanwhile hold that there is no iniquity with God; but by what equity he has mercy on whom he wills, and hardens whom he wills, let him confess himself, as a man, to be ignorant: yet in such a way that, on account of that which he holds unshaken—that there is no iniquity with God—although no one is justified by him by preceding merits, he nevertheless know that no one is hardened except by merit. For it is piously and truly believed that God, by justifying the guilty and impious, frees them from owed punishments: but if God is believed to damn anyone undeserving and liable to no sin, he is not believed to be alien from iniquity. Where, then, the unworthy is freed, so much the greater giving of thanks is owed, the more just a punishment was owed: but where the undeserving is damned, neither mercy nor truth is maintained.

**6. 21.** "How," they say, "is Esau not damned undeserving, if it was said, not of works, but of him who calls, *The elder shall serve the younger*? For as no good works of the one preceded, that he should pertain to grace, so neither evil works of the other, that he should pertain to punishment." Plainly no works in either, good or evil, but their own: but each was liable to that one in whom all sinned, that all should die in him: for whoever from that one were going to be many in themselves, were then one in him. Accordingly that sin would be of one alone, if none had gone out from him: but further, in whom there was a common nature, none is immune from its fault. If, then, both, who were not yet working their own things, good or evil, were nevertheless born guilty from their origin; let him praise mercy who is freed, let him not blame judgment who is punished.

**7. 22.** Here, if we should say, How much better that both were freed; nothing more fitting will be said to us than: *O man, who are you that you should answer God?*[^31] For he knows what he does, and how great a number there ought to be, first of all men, then of the saints, like the stars, like the angels; and, to speak of earthly things, like the cattle, fish, birds, like the trees and grasses, like, finally, our leaves and hairs. For we can, by human thought, still say, Since all these things which he made are good, how much better had he doubled and multiplied them, that they might be far more than they are: for if the world would not contain them, could he not also make it itself as much greater as he wished? And yet however much he would make either those things more, or this world more capacious and greater, nonetheless the same things could be said about multiplying them, and there would be no immoderate measure.

**7. 23.** For this too can be said—whether it be grace by which the unjust are justified, of which it is not lawful for us to doubt, or, as some wish, free choice always goes before, whose merit either punishment or reward follows—why were they created at all who, God foreknew without doubt, would so sin as to be damned by eternal fire? For although he did not make their sins, yet the natures themselves, which through themselves are without doubt good, in which nevertheless from the choice of the will the vices of sins were going to be, and in many such as eternal punishment was to be rendered—who but God created them? Why, except because he willed? but why he willed, O man, who are you that you should answer God? *Does the thing formed say to him who formed it, Why have you made me thus? Or does not the potter of the clay have power, from the same lump, to make one vessel for honor, and another for dishonor?*[^32]

**7. 24.** And, to say now what follows: *But if God, willing to show his wrath, and to demonstrate his power, endured in much patience the vessels of wrath, which have been perfected for perdition, that he might make known the riches of his glory upon the vessels of mercy*[^33]. Behold, a reason has now been rendered to man, as much as was owed to man; if, indeed, he grasp even this, who for the liberty of his choice contends in servitude to so great a weakness: behold, the causes have been told. You then, who are you that you should answer God; if God, willing to show his wrath, and to demonstrate his power—that he can even use evils best, evils, that is, not by divine condition, but by nature vitiated by the iniquity of the will, which by God the founder was founded good—endured in much patience the vessels of wrath fitted for destruction: not that they were necessary to him, whether angelic or human sins, to whom not even the righteousness of any creature is necessary; but that he might make known the riches of his glory upon the vessels of mercy, lest in good works they should extol themselves as if from their own strength, but humbly understand that, unless the grace of God, not owed but gratuitous, came to their aid, that would have to be rendered to their merits which they saw rendered to others in the same mass?

**7. 25.** A defined number, then, of the foreknowledge of God is certain, and a multitude of the saints, to whom, loving God—which he gave them by the Holy Spirit poured out in their hearts—all things work together unto good, to those who are called according to his purpose. Since *those whom he foreknew, he also predestined to be conformed to the image of his Son, that he himself might be the firstborn among many brothers. But those whom he predestined, them also he called*: here we ought to understand, according to his purpose. For there are others called, but not chosen; and through this not called according to his purpose. *But those whom he called* (that is, according to his purpose) *them also he justified: but those whom he justified, them also he glorified*[^34]. These are the sons of promise, these are the chosen who are saved by the election of grace, where it was said: *But if by grace, it is no longer of works; otherwise grace is no longer grace*[^35]. These are the vessels of mercy, in which God, even through the vessels of wrath, makes known the riches of his glory. Of these comes to be, through the Holy Spirit, one heart and one soul, which blesses God, and does not forget all his retributions: *who is propitious to all its iniquities; who heals all its weaknesses; who redeems its life from corruption; who crowns it in mercy*[^36], because it is not of him who wills, nor of him who runs, but of God who has mercy.

**7. 26.** But the other men, not belonging to that society, whose soul and body nevertheless the goodness of God wrought, and whatever the nature itself has besides the vice which the audacity of a proud will inflicted upon it, were for this reason created by the foreknowing God: that in them he might show what the free choice of the deserter, without his grace, was worth; and that in their just and owed punishments the vessels of mercy—which, not by the merits of their own works, but by the gratuitous grace of God, are distinguished from that conglomeration—might learn what had been conferred on them; so that every mouth may be stopped, and he who boasts may boast in the Lord.

**8. 27.** Whoever teaches otherwise, and does not acquiesce in the sound words of our Lord Jesus Christ, who said: *The Son of man came to seek and to save what had perished*[^37]—for he does not say, What was going to perish, but what had perished; showing what, except that by the sin of the first man the nature of the whole human race had perished?—whoever, then, teaches otherwise, and not acquiescing in the doctrine which is according to piety, against the grace of the Savior, and against the blood of the Redeemer, defends human nature as it were whole and free, and yet affects to be reckoned by the Christian name; what will he say about the distinguishing of little ones, why one is taken up into the life of the second man, another left in the death of the first man? If he should say that merits of free choice preceded; the Apostle answered the things we said above about the not-yet-born nor doing anything good or evil. But if he should say what is still defended in the books which Pelagius is most recently reported to have published; although he now seems to have anathematized, in the episcopal judgment of Palestine, those who say that Adam's sin harmed himself alone, and not the human race: if you say that both were born without judgment, and drew nothing from the damnation of the first man; surely, since he does not dare to deny that one who has been regenerated in Christ is adopted into the kingdom of heaven; let him answer what is going to be of him who, by no fault of his own not baptized, has been anticipated by this temporal death. If we do not think he is going to say that God will damn with eternal death an innocent one, not having original sin, before the years in which he could have his own; he is therefore compelled to answer what Pelagius, in the ecclesiastical judgment, that he might in some way be pronounced catholic, was compelled to anathematize—that infants, even if they are not baptized, have eternal life: for this being denied, what remains but eternal death?

**8. 28.** And through this he will dispute against the opinion of the Lord saying: *Your fathers ate manna in the desert, and died: this is the bread which came down from heaven, that if anyone eat of it he may not die*[^38]; for he was not speaking of this death, which even those who eat of that bread must necessarily suffer: and a little after: *Amen, amen I say to you, Unless you eat the flesh of the Son of man, and drink his blood, you will not have life in you*[^39]; surely that which is to be after this death: and against the authority of the apostolic See, where, when this very matter was being treated, this evangelical testimony was adduced, lest little ones not baptized be believed able to have life: and against the express words of Pelagius himself before the bishops by whom he was heard, where he anathematized those who said that infants not baptized have eternal life.

**8. 29.** Which we have recalled for this reason, because indeed among you, or rather in your city, if what we have heard is true, certain ones strive with so great obstinacy for this error, that they say it is easier even to desert and despise Pelagius, who, holding these things, anathematized them, than to depart from the truth of this opinion, as it seems to them. But if they yield to the apostolic See, or rather to the very master and Lord of the Apostles, who says that they will not have life in themselves, unless they eat the flesh of the Son of man, and drink his blood, which they surely cannot unless baptized; then surely they will at some time confess that little ones not baptized cannot have life, and through this, however more tolerably than all who commit even their own sins, yet are punished with eternal death.

**8. 30.** Since these things are so, let them dare to dispute, and let them strive to persuade whom they can, that God, the just, with whom there is no iniquity, will damn with perpetual death little ones innocent of their own sins, if they are not even bound and constrained by that one from Adam. But if this is most absurd, and utterly alien from the justice of God—and yet no one who remembers himself a Christian of the catholic faith denies or doubts that little ones, not having received the grace of regeneration in Christ, without the food of his flesh and the drinking of his blood, do not have life in themselves, and through this are liable to everlasting punishment—surely it remains that, because they themselves did nothing good or evil, therefore the punishment of their death is just, because they die in him, in whom all sinned; whence in him alone are they made alive, from whom neither could original sin be drawn, nor one's own perpetrated.

**8. 31.** He himself called us not only from the Jews, but even from the Gentiles; since even of that Jerusalem which killed the Prophets, and stoned those sent to her, although she was unwilling, he himself gathered those whom he willed, both before his incarnation, as the very Prophets, and after the Word was made flesh, as the Apostles and the thousands of men who placed the prices of their goods before the feet of the Apostles. For all those are sons of Jerusalem, unwilling that they be gathered, who nevertheless, she being unwilling, were gathered; of whom he says: *If I cast out demons by Beelzebub, by whom do your sons cast them out? Therefore they shall be your judges*[^40]. Of these it had been foretold: *If the sons of Israel are as the sand of the sea, a remnant shall be saved*[^41]. The word of God cannot fail, nor has he rejected his people whom he foreknew, who nevertheless were the remnant saved by the election of grace. But if by grace—which must often be said—it is no longer of works; otherwise grace is no longer grace: these are surely not our words, but the Apostle's. What, then, he cried out to Jerusalem unwilling that her sons be gathered, this we cry out against those who are unwilling that the sons of the Church, willing, be gathered: nor are they corrected even after the judgment which was made about Pelagius himself in Palestine; from which he would have gone out condemned, had he not himself condemned the things charged against him contrary to the grace of God—sayings which he could not obscure.

**9. 32.** For besides those things which, as he could, he dared to defend by some reasoning or other, certain things were charged against him which, unless, all evasion being removed, he had anathematized, he himself would have gone out anathematized. For it was charged that he says: "That Adam was made mortal, who, whether he sinned or did not sin, would have died: and that his sin harmed himself alone, and not the human race: and that infants newly born are in that state in which Adam was before the transgression: and that neither through the death or the transgression of Adam does the whole human race die, nor through the resurrection of Christ does the whole human race rise: and that infants, even if they are not baptized, have eternal life: and that rich men baptized, unless they renounce all things, even if they seem to do any good, it is not reckoned to them, nor can they have the kingdom of God: and that the grace of God and help is given not for individual acts, but is in free choice, or in the law and doctrine: and that the grace of God is given according to our merits: and that the sons of God cannot be called such, unless they have been made altogether without sin: and that there is no free choice, if it needs the help of God; since each one has in his own will to do something, or not to do it: and that our victory is not from the help of God, but from free choice: and that pardon is not given to the penitent according to the grace and mercy of God, but according to the merit and labor of those who through penance have been worthy of mercy."

**9. 33.** All these things Pelagius so anathematized—which the proceedings themselves sufficiently testify—that he brought no disputation toward defending them in any way; whence it follows that whoever follows the authority of that episcopal judgment, and the confession of Pelagius himself, ought to hold these things, which the catholic Church has always held: that Adam, unless he had sinned, would not have died: that his sin harmed not himself alone, but the human race too: and that infants newly born are not in that state in which Adam was before the transgression, so that to them too pertains even what the Apostle briefly says: *Through one man death, and through one man the resurrection of the dead. For as in Adam all die, so also in Christ all will be made alive*[^42]. Whence it comes about that infants not baptized cannot have not only the kingdom of heaven, but even eternal life. Let him confess also that rich men baptized, even if they do not lack their riches, and are such as the Apostle describes to Timothy, saying: *Charge the rich of this world not to be high-minded, nor to hope in the uncertainty of riches, but in the living God, who furnishes all things abundantly to enjoy; let them be rich in good works, give readily, share, lay up for themselves a good foundation for the future, that they may lay hold of the true life*[^43], cannot be deprived of the kingdom of God. Let him confess that the grace of God and help is given even for individual acts; and that it is not given according to our merits, that grace may be true, that is, given freely through the mercy of him who said: *I will have mercy on whom I have had mercy, and I will show mercy to whom I have been merciful*[^44]. Let him confess that those can be called sons of God who daily say, *Forgive us our debts*: which surely they would not truly say, if they were altogether without sin. Let him confess that there is free choice, even if it needs divine help. Let him confess that when we fight against temptations and unlawful concupiscences, although even there we have our own will, yet our victory comes not from it, but from the help of God. For not otherwise will be true what the Apostle says: *It is not of him who wills, nor of him who runs, but of God who has mercy*[^45]. Let him confess that pardon is given to the penitent according to the grace and mercy of God, not according to their merits; since the Apostle called even repentance itself a gift of God, where he says of certain ones: *Lest perhaps God give them repentance*[^46]. Let him confess all these things simply, without any evasions, if anyone consents to catholic authority and to the express words of Pelagius himself in the ecclesiastical proceedings. For neither is it to be believed that the things contrary to these were truly anathematized, unless these, to which they are contrary, are held with a faithful heart, and brought forth by an open confession.

**10. 34.** Nor in those more recent books, which that same Pelagius is said to have published after that judgment, although he seems to consent to the help of divine grace, does it sufficiently evidently appear what he thinks about this matter. For sometimes he so weighs the power of the will in equal measures on an even scale, that he defines it to avail as much for not sinning as for sinning: which, if it is so, no place is reserved for the help of grace, without which we say that the choice of the will avails nothing for not sinning. But sometimes he confesses that we are fortified by the daily help of the grace of God, although we have a strong and firm free choice for not sinning: which surely he ought to have confessed weak and infirm, until all the weaknesses of our soul be healed. For he was not praying for weakness of body who said: *Have mercy on me, Lord, for I am weak; heal me, Lord, for my bones are troubled*: for, to show that he was asking for his soul, he went on and added, *and my soul is troubled exceedingly*[^47].

**10. 35.** He seems, then, to think the help of grace granted as it were superfluously, that is, so that, even if it be not granted, we nevertheless have a strong and firm free choice for not sinning. But lest we be thought to suspect this of him rashly, and perhaps someone should say that he so thinks free choice strong and firm for not sinning (although without the grace of God it cannot effect and accomplish this), as we say that sound eyes are firm for seeing, which nevertheless they can in no way do if the help of light be lacking; in another place he shows what he would say, or what he thought, where he says: "That the grace of God is given to men for this, that what they are commanded to do by free choice they may be able more easily to fulfill through grace." Surely when he says, more easily, what does he wish to be understood, except that, even if grace be lacking, the things divinely commanded can be fulfilled through free choice, either easily, or even with difficulty?

**10. 36.** Where, then, is: *What is man, that you are mindful of him?*[^48] Where, finally, those testimonies which the prelate of the Church of Jerusalem, as is read in those same proceedings, recalls that he said to Pelagius himself, when it had been brought to him that he said a man could be without sin without the grace of God? For these three very great testimonies were spoken by him against an impious presumption of this kind: that which the Apostle says: *I labored more than all of them; yet not I, but the grace of God with me*[^49]; and: *It is not of him who wills, nor of him who runs, but of God who has mercy*[^50]; and: *Unless the Lord build the house, they have labored in vain who build it*[^51]. How, then, is what God commands fulfilled, without his help, even with difficulty, when, if the Lord does not build, the builder is said to have labored in vain; and it is not written: "It is indeed of him who wills, and of him who runs, but more easily of God who has mercy," but it is written: *It is not of him who wills, nor of him who runs, but of God who has mercy*; not because there is no will and running of man, but because he can do nothing unless he have mercy: nor did the Apostle say: "And I," but he said: *Yet not I, but the grace of God with me*: not because he himself did nothing good, but because he would do nothing, if it did not help? Although that possibility of free choice, as much to good as to evil, by an even weighing of the scale, leaves no place even for this ease, which at least he seems to have confessed by saying: "They can more easily fulfill through grace"; for if good is more easily fulfilled through grace, but evil is done most easily without grace: surely that possibility is not weighed by an even scale.

**11. 37.** But why more? Not only ought we to be cautious to avoid these people, but also not to grow sluggish in teaching or admonishing them, if they suffer it. We bestow more on them, however, without doubt, if we pray that they be corrected, lest with such great talents they either perish, or destroy others by a damnable presumption: because they have zeal for God, but not according to knowledge; and, *being ignorant of God's righteousness*, that is, the righteousness which is from God, *and wishing to establish their own, they have not submitted to the righteousness of God*[^52]. For whom, surely, since they are called Christians, it must be more observed than for the Jews, of whom the Apostle said this, lest they stumble at the stone of stumbling, by as it were acutely defending nature and free choice, like the philosophers of this world, who vehemently acted that they might be thought, or might think, that they could make for themselves a blessed life by the virtue of their own will. Let these, then, beware lest by the wisdom of word they make void the cross of Christ, and this be for them to stumble at the stone of stumbling. For human nature, even if it remained in that integrity in which it was created, would in no way preserve itself, its Creator not helping: since, then, without the grace of God it cannot keep the salvation which it received; how, without the grace of God, can it repair what it has lost?

**11. 38.** But not for this reason ought we not to pray for these people, because, if they are not corrected, it is to be imputed to their will, who are unwilling to believe that the grace of the Savior is necessary to them even for this very thing which they think consists in the powers of the will alone. For those too to whom these are in this cause altogether most similar, of whom the Apostle said this, that, being ignorant of God's righteousness, and wishing to establish their own, they have not submitted to the righteousness of God, surely did not believe by the fault of their own will. For they were not compelled, unwilling, to be unbelievers; but, by being unwilling to believe, they did not lack the crime of unbelief. And yet, because the will, that it may be moved to believe true things, does not suffice to itself, unless God helps by grace—the Lord himself saying, when he spoke of unbelievers: *No one comes to me, unless it be given him by the Father*[^53]; on account of this the Apostle too, although he insistently preached the Gospel to them, yet saw it was too little, unless he also prayed for them that they might believe: for he says: *Brothers, the good will indeed of my heart, and my prayer to God is made for them unto salvation*; and then he added those things which we said: *For I bear them witness that they have zeal for God, but not according to knowledge*[^54], and so on. Let us pray, then, for them, holy brother.

**12. 39.** For you perceive with us by what evil error they are held. For your letters are fragrant with the most sincere odor of Christ, where you appear a most genuine lover and confessor of his grace. But that we thought we should speak with you for a long time about this matter, first, because it is most delightful, we have done: for what ought to be more delightful than, to the weak, the grace by which they are healed, or, to the sluggish, the grace by which they are roused, or, to the willing, the grace by which they are helped? then, that, if we could by our disputation, with God's help, do anything, your assertion—not your faith, but of faith—against such people might be supported, just as we too are helped to this capacity by the letters of your brotherhood.

**12. 40.** For what is more abundant and fuller of most truthful confession than that which is in a certain letter of yours, where you humbly bewailed that our nature did not remain as it was created, but was vitiated through that father of the human race, saying: "Poor and grieving I, who am still concreted with the squalor of the earthly image, and refer more, by the senses of the flesh and earthly acts, to the first than to the second Adam, how shall I dare to depict myself to you, when I am proved to deny the image of the heavenly one by earthly corruption? On both sides shame shuts me in. I blush to depict what I am; I do not dare to depict what I am not: I hate what I am; and I am not what I love. But what will it profit wretched me to hate iniquity, and to love virtue; when I rather do that which I hate, nor strive, sluggish, rather to do that which I love? Discordant with myself, I am torn by an internal war, while the spirit fights against the flesh, the flesh against the spirit, and the law of the body assails the law of the mind by the law of sin. Unhappy I, who have not even by the wood of the cross digested in myself the venomous taste of the hostile tree! For that paternal poison through Adam endures in me, by which the father, transgressing, infected the whole of his kind": and the rest which you connect, much about this misery, groaning, awaiting the redemption of your body, and knowing yourself to be saved not yet in fact, but in hope.

**12. 41.** But perhaps you too transfigured another into yourself, when you said these things, and suffer no importunate and odious troubles from the flesh lusting against the spirit, although you do not consent to it: nevertheless both you, and whoever suffers these things, and awaits the grace of Christ by which he may be freed from the body of this death, were there—not yet, indeed, evidently in himself, but latently in that man—when the forbidden food was touched, and the perdition was conceived, going far and wide through all, unless by another path, through the Virgin, he had come who came to seek and to save what had perished. But about praying, and demanding with groans the help of advancing and living rightly, what part of your letter is not fervent? what is there of your discourse, however long, where there is not scattered, with groaning piety, what we say in the prayer: *Lead us not into temptation*? Let us, then, in turn both console ourselves in all these things, and exhort, and, as much as the Lord gives, help one another. But what things, and about whom, we have heard which we much grieve and are unwilling easily to believe, your holiness will hear from our common friend, by whose returning safe in the mercy of God we hope that we can be made most certain about all things.
