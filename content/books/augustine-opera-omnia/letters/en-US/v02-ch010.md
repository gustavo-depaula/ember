# Letter 109

To the venerable and longed-for bishop Augustine, to be embraced with the whole bosom of love, Severus.

**1.** Thanks be to God, brother Augustine, whose gift is whatever of good joys is in us. I confess, it is well with me when I am with you; I read you much: I will say something marvelous, but plainly true—as your presence is wont to be absent to me, so your absence has been made present. No turbulent dealings of temporal affairs interpose themselves between us. I act as much as I can, though I cannot as much as I will: what shall I say—"as much as I will"? You know very well how greedy I am for you; nor yet do I murmur because I do not act as much as I will, since again I act no less than I can. Thanks be to God, then, sweetest brother, it is well with me when I am with you; I rejoice to be more closely joined with you; and, so to say, clinging to you as one as much as I can, receiving the overflow of your fullness, I gather strength, in the hope that I may be made fit to shake and press out those things, so that whatever more secret and inward you keep enclosed, with the skins removed which you insert to be sucked by one still at the breast, your very inmost parts, if it is possible, may deign to pour out for me. Your inmost parts, I say, I desire to be poured back into me: your inmost parts, parts rich with heavenly fattening, and seasoned with all spiritual sweetness; your inmost parts, pure parts, simple parts, except that they are bound with the double bond of twin love; your inmost parts, parts suffused with the light of truth, and reflecting truth. To the flowing or rebounding of these I subject myself, that my night may fail in your light, so that we may be able to walk together in the brightness of day. O truly skillful bee of God, building combs full of divine nectar, dripping mercy and truth, running through which my soul takes delight, and strives to repair and prop up by vital pasture whatever it finds in itself lacking, or feels to be weak.

**2.** The Lord is blessed through the proclamation of your mouth and your faithful ministry: for you make it so to sound together and respond to the Lord who sings to you, that whatever of his fullness overflows down to us is made more pleasant and more welcome through your elegant service, and your trim cleanness, and your faithful and chaste and simple ministry: which you make so to shine forth through your acuteness and your vigilance that it dazzles the eyes and snatches them to itself; unless you yourself signal toward the Lord, so that whatever delightful thing shines in you, we, referring it to him, may recognize to be his, of whose goodness you are so good, and of whose purity and simplicity and beauty you are pure, simple, and beautiful; and, giving thanks to him for your good, may he by his gift deign to join us to you, or in whatever way to subjoin us, that we may be more fully subjected to him, under whose leading and moderation we rejoice that you are such, so that it may fall to you also to rejoice over us: which I do not despair of, if you aid me with your prayers—you by imitation of whom I have already profited somewhat, so as to desire to be such. You see what you do, that you are so good, how you snatch us into the love of our neighbor, which to us is the first and last step toward the love of God, and as it were the boundary by which each, of God and of neighbor, is annexed to the other; on which, as I said, standing as on a boundary, we are touched by the heat of both, and burn with the love of both. But the more this fire of our neighbor has burned and purified us, the more it compels us to go into that purer love of God. In which now no measure of loving is imposed on us, since there the very measure is to love without measure. It is not, then, to be feared lest we love our Lord more, but to be feared lest we love him less.

**3.** This foregoing letter, which—my sadness, as it were, wiped away by the happiness of my doings—offers you me more joyful, from the liberal leisure which it was permitted me to spend with you, set in this countryside (for so it could be permitted), was written, before the truly venerable bishop deigned to visit us, as a kind of goal of those joys; and what I truly wonder at, it came on the very day it was written. What is this, I beg, my soul, except perhaps that it delights us; yet, though it is honorable, it is not yet sufficiently useful, because it is in part? Meanwhile, though it be permitted to the whole, let us strive to fit this very part—that is, ourselves, as far as material yields to us for our sins, that is, as far as we ourselves yield—more polished and more compactible to ourselves, if indeed you admit this word. You have a letter not according to your greatness, but according to my smallness, somewhat longer, by which I may provoke you, so that now, not according to my smallness but according to your greatness, a letter may be sent to me. Which, however great it will be, will not be long to me, for whom all time is short for reading you. Write back to me when, or where, I ought to meet you, on account of that cause for which you bade me meet you. But if it is undisturbed, and perhaps nothing else better has pleased, then I will come; if not, I beg you, do not call me back from my course: for that alone has seemed something which I would prefer to myself. All the brothers who are our fellow servants in the Lord I both greatly long for and greet.
