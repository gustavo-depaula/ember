# Letter 177

To the most blessed and deservedly most honorable lord, brother, pope Innocent, from Aurelius, Alypius, Augustine, Evodius, and Possidius, greetings in the Lord.

**1.** From the two councils of the province of Carthage and of Numidia, we have sent to your holiness letters subscribed by no small number of bishops, against the enemies of the grace of Christ, who trust in their own strength, and in a way say to our Creator: You made us men; but we made ourselves righteous. They who say that human nature is free for this reason, that they may not seek a Liberator; for this reason whole, that they may judge a Savior superfluous. For they say it has such power that, by its own strength, once received in the origin of its creation, it can, through free choice, with nothing further helping from the grace of him who created it, tame and extinguish all desires and overcome temptations; and many of them rise up against us, and say to our soul: *There is no salvation for it in its God*[^1]. The household of Christ, then, which says, *When I am weak, then I am strong*[^2], and to which its Lord says, *I am your salvation*[^3], with anxious heart, with fear and trembling awaits the help of the Lord also through the charity of your veneration.

**2.** For we have heard that there are in the city of Rome, where he lived a long time, some who for various causes favor him; some, namely, because he is reported to have persuaded such things, but more who do not believe that he holds such things; especially because in the East, where he lives, ecclesiastical proceedings are boasted to have been drawn up by which he is thought to have been cleared: where indeed, if the bishops pronounced him catholic, it is to be believed to have been done for no other reason than that he said he confessed the grace of God, and that a man can so live righteously by his own labor and will, that he did not deny he was helped to this by the grace of God. For on hearing these words, the catholic prelates could understand no other grace of God than that which they are accustomed to read in the Books of God and to preach to the peoples of God: namely that of which the Apostle says: *I do not make void the grace of God. For if righteousness is through the Law, then Christ died in vain*[^4]; without doubt the grace by which we are justified from iniquity, and by which we are saved from weakness, not that by which we were created with our own will. For if those bishops had understood him to call that grace which we have even with the impious, with whom we are men, but to deny that by which we are Christians and sons of God; who of the catholic priests would—we do not say hear him patiently, but bear him before his eyes? Wherefore the judges are not to be blamed, because by ecclesiastical custom they heard the name of grace, not knowing what men of this kind are wont to scatter, either in the books of their doctrine, or in the ears of their followers.

**3.** It is not a matter of one Pelagius, who has perhaps already been corrected—which would that it were so; but of so many, by whose loquacious contention, and by their drawing the weak and unlearned souls as it were bound, but wearying by the very contention the firm and stable in faith, all places are now everywhere full. Either, then, he must be summoned to Rome by your veneration, and diligently asked what grace he means, by which he confesses—if indeed he now confesses—that men are helped to not sinning and to living righteously; or this very thing must be dealt with him by letters: and when he has been found to mean that grace which ecclesiastical and apostolic truth teaches, then without any scruple of the Church, without the hiding place of any ambiguity, he must be absolved, then truly there must be rejoicing over his cleansing.

**4.** For whether he says that grace is free choice, or that grace is the remission of sins, or that grace is the precept of the law; he says nothing of those things which, through the supplying of the Holy Spirit, pertain to conquering concupiscences and temptations—the Spirit whom he most richly poured out upon us who *ascended into heaven, and leading captivity captive gave gifts to men*[^5]. For from this we pray that we may be able to overcome the temptation of sins, that the Spirit of God, of whom we have received a pledge, may help our weakness. But he who prays and says, *Lead us not into temptation*, surely does not pray to be a man, which he is by nature; nor pray to have free choice, which he already received when the nature itself was created; nor pray for the remission of sins, because this is said above, *Forgive us our debts*; nor pray to receive a commandment: but plainly prays that he may do the commandment. For if he has been led into temptation, that is, has failed in temptation, he surely commits the sin which is against the commandment. He prays, then, that he may not sin, that is, that he do no evil, which the apostle Paul prays for the Corinthians, saying: *But we pray to the Lord that you do no evil*[^6]. Whence it sufficiently appears that, for not sinning, that is, for not doing evil, although there is no doubt that the choice of the will exists, yet its power does not suffice unless the weakness is helped. The prayer itself, therefore, is the clearest testimony of grace: let him confess this, and we will rejoice whether he is upright or corrected.

**5.** Law and grace must be distinguished. Law knows how to command, grace to help. Nor would the law command, unless there were a will; nor would grace help, if the will were enough. It is commanded that we have understanding, where it is said: *Do not be like the horse and mule, which have no understanding*[^7]; and yet we pray that we have understanding, where it is said: *Give me understanding, that I may learn your commandments*[^8]. It is commanded that we have wisdom, where it is said: *Be wise at last, O foolish ones*[^9]; and yet it is prayed that we have wisdom, where it is said: *But if any of you lacks wisdom, let him ask of God, who gives to all abundantly and does not reproach; and it will be given him*[^10]. It is commanded that we have continence, where it is said: *Restrain yourself*[^11]; and yet we pray that we have continence, where it is said: *When I knew that no one could be continent unless God granted it, and this very thing was wisdom, to know whose gift it was, I went to the Lord and besought him*[^12]. Finally, lest it be too long to run through all, it is commanded that we do no evil, where it is said: *Turn away from evil*[^13]; and yet it is prayed that we do no evil, where it is said: *But we pray to the Lord that you do no evil*[^14]. It is commanded that we do good, where it is said: *Turn away from evil, and do good*[^15]; and yet it is prayed that we do good, where it is said: *We do not cease praying and asking for you*: and among the other things which he prays for them, he says: *That you may walk worthily of God in every pleasing thing, in every good work and word*[^16]. As, therefore, we acknowledge the will, when these things are commanded; so let him too acknowledge grace, when they are asked for.

**6.** We have sent to your reverence a book which certain religious and honorable young men, servants of God, gave us—whose names too we do not keep silent; for they are called Timasius and James: who, as we have heard, and as you too deign to know, by the exhortation of Pelagius himself left the hope they had in the world, and now, continent, serve God. When at length, by some effort of ours, the Lord inspiring it, they were freed from this same error, they brought forward this same book, saying that it is Pelagius's, and earnestly asked that it be answered. It was done; the answer itself was written back to them: giving thanks, they wrote back. We have sent both, both the one to which answer was made, and what was answered: and lest we should be too burdensome, we have made marks at those places where we ask you not to be reluctant to inspect how, the question being set against him that he denied the grace of God, he so answered that he said it was nothing but the nature in which God created us.

**7.** But if he denies this book to be his, or those same places in the book, we do not contend; let him anathematize them, and most openly confess that grace which Christian doctrine shows and proclaims to be proper to Christians, which is not nature, but that by which nature is saved and helped: which fosters nature not by doctrine sounding in the ears or by any visible aid, like a planter and waterer as it were from without; but by the supplying of the Spirit, and a hidden mercy, as he does *who gives the increase, God*[^17]. For although by a not-to-be-disapproved reasoning it is called the grace of God by which we were created, so that we might be something, and might not be something such as a corpse which does not live, or a tree which does not feel, or a beast which does not understand, but men, who would both be, and live, and feel, and understand, and be able to give thanks for so great a benefit to our Creator; whence this too can deservedly be called grace, since it was given not by the merits of any preceding good works, but by the gratuitous goodness of God: yet other is that by which, predestined, we are called, justified, glorified, so that we can say: *If God is for us, who is against us? He who did not spare his own Son, but delivered him for us all*[^18].

**8.** About this grace the question was turning, when by those whom Pelagius gravely offended and disturbed it was said to him that he assailed it by his disputations, in which he asserted that, not only for doing but even for perfecting the divine commandments, human nature suffices itself through free choice. This grace the apostolic doctrine not undeservedly calls by that name, by which we are saved and justified by faith in Christ. Of this it is written: *I do not make void the grace of God. For if righteousness is through the Law, then Christ died in vain*[^19]: of this it is written: *You are emptied of Christ, you who are justified in the Law; you have fallen from grace*[^20]: of this it is written: *But if by grace, then no longer of works; otherwise grace is no longer grace*[^21]: of this it is written: *But to him who works the wages are not reckoned according to grace, but according to debt: but to him who does not work, but believes in him who justifies the ungodly, his faith is reckoned unto righteousness*[^22]; and many other things, which you can yourself better remember, and understand more prudently, and preach more illustriously. But that grace by which we were created men, even if we understand that it is not undeservedly so to be called, it is nevertheless a wonder if we read it so called in any lawful prophetic, evangelical, and apostolic Letters.

**9.** Since, then, concerning this grace most known to faithful and catholic Christians, the question was set against him, that he should cease to assail it; what is it that, when he had set this against himself in his book, as it were by an opposing person, so that by answering he might clear himself, he answered nothing else than that the nature of created man refers to the grace of the Creator; and so said that righteousness can be fulfilled without sin through free choice with the help of divine grace, because God gave this to man in the very possibility of nature? To whom it is deservedly answered: Then the scandal of the cross has been made void. Then Christ died in vain. For if he had not died for our offenses, and risen for our justification, nor ascended on high, and, leading captivity captive, given gifts to men, would that possibility of nature which he defends not be in men?

**10.** Or was perhaps the commandment of God lacking, and therefore Christ died? Nay, even this was already holy, and just, and good: it had already been said: *You shall not covet*: it had already been said: *You shall love your neighbor as yourself*, in which saying the Apostle says the whole law is fulfilled. And since, unless he loves God, no one loves himself, therefore the Lord says that on these two precepts hang the whole Law and the Prophets: which two precepts had already been given to men divinely. Or was the eternal reward promised to righteousness not yet there? This he himself does not say, who in his Letters set down that even in the Old Testament the kingdom of heaven was promised. If, then, for doing and perfecting righteousness there was already the possibility of nature through free choice, there was already the holy and just and good commandment of God's Law, there was already the everlasting reward promised; then Christ died in vain.

**11.** Therefore neither through the Law is there righteousness, nor through the possibility of nature, but from faith and the gift of God through Jesus Christ our Lord, the one mediator of God and men: who, unless in the fullness of time he had died for our offenses, and risen for our justification, surely both the faith of the ancients and ours would be made void. But faith being made void, what righteousness would remain to man, since the just man lives by faith? For from the time that, through one man, sin entered into the world, and through sin death, and so it passed to all men, in whom all sinned: without doubt from the body of this death, where another law fights against the law of the mind, his own possibility has freed and frees no one, which, lost, needs a Redeemer, wounded, a Savior; but the grace of God through faith in the one mediator of God and men, the man Christ Jesus: who, being also God, made man; and, remaining God, made man, himself remade what he had made.

**12.** But I think it escapes him that the faith of Christ, which afterward came into revelation, was in secret in the times of our fathers; through which faith nevertheless they too, by the grace of God, were freed, whoever in all times of the human race could be freed—by the hidden judgment of God, yet not a blameworthy one. Whence the Apostle says: *Having the same spirit of faith*, surely the same as they too, *according to what is written, I believed, therefore I have spoken; we also believe, therefore we also speak*[^23]; hence it is that the Mediator himself says: *Abraham your father desired to see my day; and he saw it, and rejoiced*[^24]: hence Melchizedek, having brought forth the sacrament of the Lord's table, knew how to prefigure his eternal priesthood.

**13.** But now, the Law being given in letters, which the Apostle says *came in that the offense might abound*[^25], and of which he says: *If, then, the inheritance is from the Law, it is no longer from the promise. But to Abraham God gave it through a promise. What then is the law? It was set forth for the sake of transgression, until the seed should come to whom the promise was made, ordained through angels in the hand of a mediator. But a mediator is not of one; but God is one. Is the law, then, against the promises of God? Far be it. For if a law had been given which could give life, righteousness would altogether be from the Law. But Scripture has shut up all things under sin, that the promise by faith in Jesus Christ might be given to those who believe*[^26]; is it not sufficiently shown that this was done through the Law, that sin might be acknowledged, and by transgression increased (*for where there is no law, there is no transgression*[^27]); and so, against the victory of sin, refuge might be taken to the divine grace which is in the promises: and thus the Law would not be against the promises of God; because through it there comes the knowledge of sin, and from the transgression of the Law the abundance of sin, so that for liberation the promises of God may be sought, which is the grace of God; and there may begin to be in man a righteousness, not his own, but God's, that is, given by the gift of God?

**14.** Which even now certain people, *being ignorant of God's righteousness*, as was then said of the Jews, *and wishing to establish their own, have not submitted to the righteousness of God*[^28]. For they too think themselves justified through the Law, free choice sufficing them for keeping it—that is, by their own righteousness, brought forth from human nature; not given from divine grace, on account of which it is called the righteousness of God. Whence likewise it is written: *For through the Law is the knowledge of sin. But now without the Law the righteousness of God has been manifested, attested by the Law and the Prophets*[^29]. When he says, has been manifested, he shows that it was there even then, but like that rain which Gideon obtained, then as it were hidden in the fleece, but now as it were on the threshing floor, manifest. Since, then, the Law without grace could be not the death of sin, but the strength of it—for so it was said: *The sting of death is sin, but the strength of sin is the Law*[^30]—just as many flee from the face of reigning sin to grace, now as it were open on the threshing floor, so few fled to it, then as it were hidden in the fleece. But this distribution of the times is referred to the depth of the riches of the wisdom and knowledge of God, of which it was said: *How inscrutable are his judgments, and his ways past finding out!*[^31]

**15.** Wherefore, if both before the time of the Law, and in the very time of the Law, the just fathers living by faith were justified not by the possibility of a weak and needy and vitiated nature, sold under sin, but by the grace of God through faith; and now the same grace, coming into the open already revealed, justifies: let Pelagius, then, anathematize his writings, where he disputes against it—even if not through contumacy, yet through ignorance—defending the possibility of nature for conquering sins and fulfilling the commandments; or, if he denies these to be his own, or says that the things which he denies to be his own were inserted into his writings by his enemies, let him nevertheless anathematize and condemn them, by the fatherly exhortation and authority of your holiness. If, then, he wishes, let him learn to take away the scandal burdensome to himself and pernicious to the Church; which scandal his hearers and perverse lovers do not cease to scatter everywhere. For if they learn that this same book, which they either think is his or know to be his, has been anathematized and condemned by him himself, by the authority of the catholic bishops, and especially of your holiness, which we in no way doubt is of greater weight with him, we suppose they will no longer dare, by speaking against the grace of God, which has been revealed through the passion and resurrection of Christ, to disturb faithful and simply Christian breasts; but rather, with the help of the Lord's mercy, your prayers burning with charity and piety contending together with us, they will trust, not in their own strength, but in that same grace, to be not only blessed forever, but also righteous and holy. Whence the letter too, written by one of us to him himself—to whom, through a certain eastern deacon, but a citizen of Hippo, he transmitted certain writings as it were of his own cleansing—we have rather thought should be directed to your blessedness, judging it better and asking that you yourself deign to send it to him: for so he will rather not disdain to read it, attending in it more to him who sent it than to him who wrote it.

**16.** But that which they say—that a man can be without sin, and easily keep the commandments of God if he wills—although when this is said with the help of grace, which nevertheless was revealed and given through the incarnation of his Only-begotten, it seems to be said more tolerably: yet, since it can not undeservedly move us, where and when through that same grace this is effected in us, that we should be without any sin at all; whether in this life, when the flesh lusts against the spirit, or rather in that life when there shall come to pass the saying which is written, *Where, O death, is your victory? Where, O death, is your sting? For the sting of death is sin*[^32]; it must be more diligently treated on account of certain others who held, and committed to memory in their writings, that even in this life a man can be without sin—not from the beginning of his nativity, but by conversion from sins to righteousness, and from a reprobate life to a good life. For so they understood what is written of Zechariah and Elizabeth, that they walked in all the justifications of the Lord without complaint. This which was said, without complaint, they took as said for without sin: not indeed denying, nay even—which is found in other places in their writings—piously confessing the help of the grace of our Lord, not through the natural spirit of man, but through the principal Spirit of God. They seem to have considered too little that Zechariah himself was surely a priest; but all priests then necessarily had, by the law of God, first to offer sacrifice for their own sins, then for the people's. As, therefore, now by the sacrifice of prayer we are convicted of not being without sin, since we are bidden to say, *Forgive us our debts*; so then too, by the sacrifices of animal victims, the priests were convicted of not being without sin, who were bidden to offer for their own sins.

**17.** But if the matter is so, that through the grace of the Savior we advance indeed in this life, desire failing, love growing; but are perfected in that life, desire extinguished, love completed: surely that which is written, *He who is born of God does not sin*[^33], was said according to that love which alone does not sin. For to the nativity which is of God, love, to be increased and perfected, pertains; not that desire which is to be diminished and consumed: which nevertheless, as long as it is in our members, by a certain law of its own fights against the law of the mind; but he who is born of God, neither obeying its desires, nor presenting his members as weapons of iniquity to sin, can say: *Now it is no longer I who do it, but the sin that dwells in me*[^34].

**18.** But however this question may stand—because, even if a man is not found in this life without sin, it is nevertheless said that it can come about through the help of grace and the Spirit of God, which, that it may come about, must be striven for and asked—one tolerably errs in this; nor is it a diabolical impiety, but a human error, to affirm that it must be worked at and wished for, even if what he affirms he cannot show: for he believes that to be possible which it is certainly praiseworthy to will. But for us it suffices that no one in the Church of God among the faithful is found, in whatever advancement and excellence of righteousness, who would dare to say to himself that the petition of the Lord's Prayer is not necessary, *Forgive us our debts*; and would say that he has no sin, lest he deceive himself, and the truth be not in him, although he now lives without complaint. For it is not whatever sin even of human temptation, but a grave sin, that comes into complaint.

**19.** The other things which are charged against him, as your blessedness shall see them defended in the proceedings, so will you without doubt judge about them. The most gentle sweetness of your heart will surely give us pardon, that we have sent your holiness a letter more lengthy perhaps than you would wish. For we do not pour back our little stream to increase your bounteous fountain; but in this not-small temptation of the time—from which may he free us to whom we say, *Lead us not into temptation*—we wish this to be proved by you, whether our stream too, though slight, flows from the same head of waters from which yours also abundantly flows, and to be consoled by your replies about the common participation in the one grace.
