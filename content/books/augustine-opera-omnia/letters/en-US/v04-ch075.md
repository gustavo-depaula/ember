# Letter 11\*

A MEMORANDUM TO MY LORD, THE HOLY FATHER AUGUSTINE, FROM CONSENTIUS

**1.** *My most blessed lord, the brother of Your Holiness, Bishop Patroclus, by a violent love compelled me to write certain things, inept and absurd perhaps, against the Priscillianists, by whom even the Gauls were now being laid waste; which indeed, lest perhaps I afflict your Paternity with a greater weariness of reading, I had determined to keep with me. But it happened that a certain servant of Christ, Fronto by name—to whom the Holy Spirit suggests the most flaming flames of faithful zeal—suddenly arrived; from whom, when I inquired what he had done about the things enjoined, he narrated many things to me, not only to be rejoiced at, but even to be astonished at. I had indeed enjoined him the year before, that, relying on a most innocent cunning, he should take up wars against the aforementioned Priscillianists, with whom the Spains so swarm that, concerning them, only the barbarians seem to have done nothing. I had also taught him, and instructed him with what art he ought to approach certain particular persons, sending to him the books which I was lately compelled to write at the command of my aforesaid lord, your brother, and especially this third book, which—after I had more fully learned for what reason I wrote in the person of a heretic—is marked by the discourse of a brief little preface. Hither, then, my venerable brother Fronto came most opportunely, while the inconvenience of brother Leonas's coming here detained him; who reported to me very many things, of which I will narrate a few, for the sake of admonishing.*

**2.** *"In the city of Tarragona," he says, "in which I built a monastery for myself, I received a packet (which you sent to me sealed, Bishop Agapius delivering it); within which I found the letters and memoranda and books you had transmitted; and soon, every plan and instruction having been received, I hastened to that heretic Severa, whose name you had clearly expressed to me, and, approaching her by that art which the text of your preface sets forth, I inquired after the names of the heretics. She, among other things, told me that a certain priest Severus—renowned for riches and power, and also for letters, the chief of this doctrine—had in vain cast the envy of his betrayal upon his dead mother. For when, the year before, the same Severus, supposing that the barbarians had withdrawn further, was making for the fortress in which he resided, after his mother's death, our Lord Jesus Christ, the inspector of all secrets, the dispenser of all chances, willed that his baggage be seized by the barbarians, that so great a crime might be disclosed. They, everything being turned to plunder, carried off three huge codices, which contained all kinds of sacrileges—believing them good, and perhaps to be bought by someone—to the neighboring city, whose name is Lleida; which, when they had recognized as execrable, they left with the bishop of that city, Sagittius. But he, running through everything—since he was proven evil in the sight of God—was given an occasion by which he should be made manifest even to men. For when those sweet poisons had madly pleased him, and the matter, made known to all, could no longer be covered by dissimulation, he fraudulently cut out the quires from those books which contained the shameful and sacrilegious science of magic spells, and, everything having been reviewed by him, he sent one codex—from which he had cut off whatever seemed most pernicious—to Titianus, the bishop of Tarragona, that is, the metropolitan, with letters; in which he professed that three codices had been seized among the baggage of the priest Severus and handed over to him by the enemy, among which this one displeased him most, but the rest he had stored in the archive of the Church."*

**3.** *"Bishop Titianus handed the received codex to Syagrius, bishop of Huesca, because in his church Severus had assumed the false name of the priesthood, admonishing him to examine the faith of his priest by a cautious investigation. But Bishop Syagrius, a man indeed holy and Catholic, but too credulous and incautiously kind, so believed Severus, covering his sacrilege with arguments, lies, and perjuries, that he even persuaded the others, that Severus, thinking the codices innocent—received from his maternal inheritance—had wished to carry them with him to his fortress, that there especially he might read or examine them with a leisured mind. When, therefore, these things were believed by all, that little woman Severa, who laid open to me—as to a heretic—all the secrets of her crimes, betrayed also the priest Severus: that, a bribe given, he had received back from Bishop Sagittius those codices (which he had pretended were his mother's), though already fraudulently halved."*

**4.** *"I therefore, after I learned everything and was confirmed by all the indications, signs, proofs, and witnesses, brought the matter to the rigor of an ecclesiastical examination, and instituted an action of accusation first against Severa herself, the betrayer of all, then against the priest Severus. But Severa, at first confused by the unexpected novelty of the matter, did not dare to deny whatever she remembered she had said to me; but afterward, when Severus relied against me on the powers of the illustrious and most lofty man, his kinsman, Count Asterius, she brought it about that the aforementioned woman fled to the aid of her niece, the daughter of Count Asterius, a most powerful woman, and, received into his praetorium—which was fortified by so great a guard of soldiers—denied all her confessions, perjuries being employed for her protection."*

**5.** *"All things, then, having been removed from the midst, when Severus and his supporters had noticed that I was a man both most vile by the abjection of my person and a beggar by the slenderness of my substance, utterly destitute of all proofs, barking with bare words against the force of so great a faction, they wished, even by the dread of death set before me, to make me cast off the accusation of him, saying: 'Set for yourself the peril that awaits you, if the priest proves himself innocent!' And at once I: 'What more,' I said, 'do you wish than that, if I am unable to prove what I intend, I be expelled from the Church for all the time of my life as a pagan?' Then the heretics, turned to an uproar: 'Do you see,' they say, 'O holy and venerable priests, and you peoples of every age and sex, that there has emerged a man needy in substance, rich in lies, armed with audacity, destitute of innocence? Beware lest a perilous example be given to all calumnious men! For if, in this one man—who did not fear to stain so holy and so noble a man with the blemish of a false charge—the ill-advised remission of your equity does not give a fearful warning to the rest, of necessity this impunity will, in rivalry, kindle all false accusers against each one of you."*

**6.** *"By such voices of the heretics the people of Tarragona were incited against me, and when they had begun to rage with huge seditions toward my destruction, I, crying out: 'Does it suffice you,' I said, 'that, if my accusation be vain and the faith of Severus right and approved, you slaughter me with stones?' 'It suffices,' they say, 'but this condition is to be corroborated by present proceedings.' So it came about that we pledged, in the ecclesiastical proceedings, by professions brought forth, I the peril of my blood, Severus the peril of his honor; where also, all the oaths being employed, he testified that, after his mother's codices had been seized by the barbarians, he had neither received them, nor had them, nor seen them."*

**7.** *"It followed that at once letters were sent to the bishops of Lleida and Huesca, namely that Sagittius, the two codices which he had affirmed by letters sent to the metropolitan bishop Titianus that he had long since retained, and Syagrius the one which he had received from the same Bishop Titianus in person, should deign at once to bring with them, since the case so demanded. But Severus at the same time, a charge of lying being woven together, sent letters to his kinsman, the illustrious man Count Asterius, and to all his other friends and relatives, most powerful men, in which he pretended that I, a most injurious and most deceitful sycophant, had assailed the count himself and his household and his daughter with intolerable charges and afflicted them with injuries and violences. Forthwith there came to Tarragona the illustrious man Count Asterius, to whom the care of so great an army and the command of so great a war had been committed, and with him a multitude of so many most powerful men, raging against me, one dead flea; but far otherwise than he believed, everything turned out contrary to Severus."*

**8.** *"For although the most evident truth of our side was pressed by the favor of all, even of the priests, yet so great was the power of our Lord Jesus Christ present to my weakness, that even the count himself—though innocent, though Catholic—was made afraid. For when, coming to Tarragona, he had learned that I alone was assailed by the most invidious accusation of his kinsman, his daughter, and all his friends and servants, since he is a just man and one fearing the Lord, and not much believing such things, he was unwilling to vindicate indiscriminately, in a most savage manner, the injuries especially of himself or of his own, and ordered me—whom, though poor, he yet knew to be a Christian—to be summoned, without injury, to his praetorium. But I, taking up the confidence which the help of our Lord Jesus Christ suggested to me, answered that I could not go to his house, which I knew to be filled with bands of heretics, lest perhaps I run into the snares of my enemies, who threatened me with public death; but that, nevertheless, if there were any matter or words which could neither be committed to messengers nor to letters, he himself ought to come to the church, if he bade me, so that there I might rather learn what that was."*

**9.** *"But he, without delay, at first dawn came to the church, and soon sought the chamber in which the bishops sat, he too with the rest about to bring forth complaints against me; and first they all, conversing long with the bishops Titianus and Agapius, placed great favor for themselves against me even with them, asserting that I was a most deceitful and most false informer, who had most craftily deceived an incautious and simple little woman and compelled her to speak a lying tale about the priest, and that now, on account of a most empty mist of suspicion, which she—who was said to be the betrayer—asserted to be false, the whole house of illustrious men was being assailed by my accusation, the count assailed by me with injuries, the count's daughter defamed."*

**10.** *"By these and such words they stirred up against me such hatreds of all, that not only the people but even certain priests threatened me with destruction. I was at once summoned to the chamber, alone, to be rebuked by the bishops, confounded by the clergy, torn apart by the heretics, accused by the count, spat upon by the soldiers, and even stoned by the peoples. When I had been led there, at once Bishop Agapius made me tremble with the terror of such words: 'Where,' he says, 'are Consentius's letters and those memoranda, I know not what, which you found in the packet that I myself brought to you?' To this I answered steadfastly that everything was with me; but he: 'Bring forth at once,' he says, 'everything and restore it to me, unless you prefer to incur the sentence of damnation now pronounced!' To this I, confidently mocking his most absurd threats, inquired for what reason he ordered everything to be brought forth to him. Then he: 'That we may read everything,' he says, 'and learn what that is which suddenly roused you to the persecution of most innocent men.' On the contrary, I myself said: 'You delivered everything to me, as you assert; how can it be that you do not know what you delivered?' Then he: 'Consentius handed everything to me sealed, to be carried to you; was it lawful that an unfaithful curiosity should goad me to unseal the seal?' To whom I: 'Why, then,' I said, 'do you now so greatly desire to know that which he who sent it wished you to be ignorant of? For surely, if he had chosen you as a suitable bearer, he ought not to have handed over to you sealed these things which you now at last anxiously inquire after; why, then, do you impute it to me, if I fear now to unseal to you those things whose fidelity even he attested by an excellent testimony who transmitted them, closed, through you?'"*

**11.** *"By these words the bishop was incited against me with such savagery of mind that, rising with fury even from his chair, he wished, in the sight of all, with his own hands to inflict slaughter on me. But—who would dare to believe it?—even with the count rebuking, the bishop's madness, repressed by the power of the Lord Jesus Christ, grew quiet. But the count himself, prudently and moderately, inquired for what reason I assailed his house and his kinsman the priest with certain hatreds. To whom I: 'Do you think this hatred, O noble and illustrious man,' I said, 'that I desire to purge your house, that I desire to free from the perils of eternal death those into whom the serpent Severus inserted a deadly poison, the antidote of a most clement strictness being poured in?' As I followed up these and such things, while, looking at the ground, he modestly held up his head in silence with his hand, the count heard not only patiently but even gladly, and at last answered me that the greatest thanks were to be rendered for so great a benefit—if indeed the assertion were true. Then I: 'Let Severus and the rest prove now,' I said, 'that I brought forth even in passing any word which pertained specially to your defamation; and yet, unless you were supported by a most approved testimony of the Catholic faith, the terror of your power would never have closed the free mouth of Fronto.' Then the count: 'I give you thanks,' he says, 'that you deign to bear testimony to my faith. But the faith of Severus and the rest, although they are joined to me by some kinship, yet it is certain cannot harm my faith."*

**12.** *"Rising, then, with this word, and, all things being interrupted, making for his praetorium, the count looked up to the confidence which the Lord suggested to me with such great admiration that he at once sent me such commands as no one could have believed: 'Pardon, I beseech you,' he says, 'servant of Christ, if perhaps we have injured you anything, and accompany me—who, as you see, am hastening to battle with my army—with the power of your prayers!' But Severus and the rest, confounded by the count's unexpected equity, were indeed quiet a little that day; but after a few days they stirred up against me such hatreds of all, that scarcely any man of so devout a mind and holy a purpose could be found in that city who did not judge me worthy of death, so that my mind, broken by despair, hoped nothing strong, sighed nothing not languid. But the Lord, who does not despise contrite and humbled hearts, the greater the zeal of men by which he foresaw I would be assailed, manifested the greater favor of his help toward me; for a certain most powerful slave—by whose discretion not only the whole household, but even the daughter of the aforesaid count, whose foster-father he had been, was governed, since he was most strong in body, most fierce in mind, most insolent in power, and had often laid futile snares for me (who, taking counsel for my life, by no means departed from the Church)—at last, turned to open fury, deserved to feel the most open vengeance of our defender."*

**13.** *"For, armed, he suddenly burst into the midst of the seditious bands, and, stretching out his hand toward me: 'Give me now the dog,' he said, 'and I will silence his barking with a death now well-deserved.' Then the peoples, and even those very ones who most seditiously threatened me with stoning, drove back the most bloody, frenzied man from the church with devout shouts. He, having set out that same day to a suburban estate, feasted pleasantly indeed; but my avenger, in the morning, struck him with the pain of so deadly a blow that he could by no means be carried to the city except dead on the seventh day. When this was done, all the faithful, terrified by the most evident power of this sign, ceased a little from assailing me; but my enemies and the whole household of the count, provoked to a greater madness of hatred, demanded me for punishment as a murderer—who by deadly curses had killed a man; but a few most faithless ones arose who said it had happened by chance."*

**14.** *"While these things were being done at Tarragona, at Lleida Bishop Sagittius was being compelled, by the letters of Bishop Titianus, to the restitution—nay, to the exhibition—of the codices which he had affirmed to be with him. For it was so done by a wonderful dispensation that, when Severus had at once sent to Sagittius letters surely secret, in which he had instructed him what had been done, the bearer first hastened with all speed to the fortress of Severus—which was distant by a longer space of journey—to compel a certain monk, Ursitio, a friend of Severus, to bring forth the codices which were demanded from Sagittius from the inner chambers of Severus, and to carry them secretly to Sagittius. But Sagittius, who—having secretly received bribes from Severus—remembered that he had returned the codices to him, when he was being burned by the inner fires of an evil conscience, and was astonished that he had received no letters of Severus about this matter, believed that Syagrius, bishop of Huesca—whom he had likewise learned to have returned his codex to Severus—had committed this with a like consciousness of avarice, and wrote secret letters to him, in which he says that he by no means doubts that he too is being compelled, by similar letters of the metropolitan bishop, to the restitution of one codex. Hence, since the two codices which he had had with him he had long ago sent to the same Syagrius—to whom the examination of his priest had been delegated—through a certain deacon Paulinus, but Paulinus, because he had by no means found Bishop Syagrius in the fortress, had handed them to the priest Severus, he therefore now earnestly entreated that, since this deed was utterly unknown to all—lest perhaps some suspicion arise—Syagrius would deign to unlock the inner chambers of his priest Severus and bring with him secretly the codices brought forth, so that he himself in the judgment might assert that he had by no means handed the codices to Severus."*

**15.** *"While these letters were being carried to Syagrius, bishop of Huesca, that Ursitio, the servant of Severus, secretly carried the books to Sagittius; which received, Sagittius was affected with as great joy as he had lately been vexed with sadness, and, now carrying a perjury presumed in his mind, at once set out for Tarragona. But Syagrius, when he had received both the summoning letters of Bishop Titianus and the secret letters of Sagittius, seeing that he—who by an ill-advised simplicity had returned to his priest Severus the codex he had received—was involved in the guilt of the greatest suspicion, when for some time he seethed anxiously, had rather determined to incline to this, that he too, holding to the assumed formula of lying, would extricate both the priest Severus and his fellow bishop Sagittius from the bonds of so great a suspicion; when suddenly, terrified by the same wonderful vision of our Lord Jesus Christ, he saw himself set before the tribunal of the dread judge, having received a sad sentence for so great a crime of conscience, and at once trembling rose, and was struck with such great consternation of mind that he not only published Sagittius's letters and compelled that Ursitio, the monk of Severus, who had been the carrier of the books, to confess everything before the ecclesiastical proceedings, but even, taking up on foot a very long and very difficult and most treacherous journey, followed in the footsteps Sagittius making for Tarragona."*

**16.** *"But Sagittius, who by the marvelous ordination of the Lord had gone far ahead, when he had sat in judgment with the bishops: 'Behold,' he says, 'O most blessed brothers, the codices which Your Holiness had ordered to be exhibited.' To this I—who, as I said before, had learned their secrets, that heretic Severa betraying them—began to assert that Sagittius had without doubt lately received in secret the codices long ago sold by Severus; for I knew both what bribe he had received and when, or through whom, he had sold to Severus the impious arms for destroying the souls of many. Then Sagittius—a man indeed powerful by right and learned in liberal letters—rising with great roaring against me, demanded that, unless I at once proved the objections, I should die by stones. But he himself, in the sight of the whole people, both by the Gospels and by all the oaths as witnesses, perjured himself not once but often, affirming that the codices had remained stored in the archive of his church from that time, which Severus had not even seen. Again Severus bound himself by like perjuries. All too, whomever I produced either as witnesses or informers, bringing forth a uniform lie, cried out that I was a sacrilegious rival of venerable priests."*

**17.** *"Severa herself first of all, denying all her information, accused me, perjuring, as the contriver of an unheard-of lie. To this I, when the intolerable roar of the whole people threatened me with most immediate punishment (which alone remedy my dismayed and confused mind found), demanded that the judgment be deferred to the next day; for I most confidently promised that Christ, the author of most incorrupt truth, would in whatever ways he willed make manifest on the morrow the perjuries of Sagittius and Severus and of all. This seemed human even to the heretics, that they should allow the punishment of my stoning to be prolonged by the very brief space which I asked. That same day, in the afternoon hours, Bishop Syagrius arrived, whom I—because I had learned that he was the chief supporter of his priest Severus and had secretly returned the codex to him—was likewise assailing; whom, after I learned that he had made on foot so long and so toilsome a journey, wondering what that was which had compelled a man both rich and weighed down with old age and hindered by infirmity of body to take up the difficulty of so great a labor, I at once ran down to the lodging to which he had turned aside, and, a greeting being premised: 'Does it please you,' I said, 'that you and Sagittius alike, by a uniform lie, drink my blood, denying that you secretly returned the codices to Severus, so that I, because I cannot prove this which—unless I had most manifestly learned it—I would surely not have objected, may perish innocent?"*

**18.** *"Then he: 'Who, my son,' he says, 'could disclose to you this which was done most secretly?' I, wondering at him—whom I had believed to have come to confirm the perjuries of all—at once first uttering a word of confession, unfolded to him the whole sequence of my information from the beginning; and when I had said that Sagittius and Severus had denied this very thing with so great an imploring of oaths, he, compelled by indignation at so wicked a perjury to cry out: 'Bring forth, boy,' he says, 'as quickly as possible, Sagittius's letters by which he confessed he had handed the codices to Severus; bring forth also the proceedings by which he was convicted of having lately received them through the monk Ursitio,' nor, dissembling that he, unwilling, was compelled by the terror of the Lord to make everything public, he reported also to me, in simple words, the whole sequence of his marvelous and terrible vision."*

**19.** *"Then I: 'I beg, venerable father,' I said, 'you who have been torn by so great a grace of Christ from the fellowship of the impious, that, the documents being now handed to me, you yourself be silent a little, until we learn whether Sagittius or Severus, his own conscience, on the morrow too, may compel to bear any reverence to the altars of Christ.' The morrow, then, came, and, with all the peoples standing in judgment, when I had seen the aforesaid bellows of lies breathing like or perhaps greater perjuries: 'What, Sagittius,' I said, 'why do you glory in the false name of bishop? Do you not fear to slaughter your soul with so great perjuries? Did you not lately direct such letters to Bishop Syagrius? Did you not receive the codices through the monk Ursitio?'"*

**20.** *"Hearing these things, he not only, denying all things, bound himself by multiplied perjuries, but even incited the people to my destruction—who fabricated so many incredible and unheard-of crimes—until, convicted by the letters brought forth by me, he at once fled judgment and his own disgrace. When this was done, when Bishop Titianus was urged by great voices of the people to his condemnation, he said that of the status of a bishop only many bishops could bear a sentence. But I, following in his footsteps Sagittius fleeing with a certain part of the people now outside the city, when I confronted him about the restitution of the quires which he had stolen from those very books, he swore indeed for a very long time that he had received the codices loosened and cut apart by the barbarians, and that it was doubtful to no one that all the things we said were lacking in those codices had perished among the barbarians. But afterward, terrified by fear, he was compelled to restore everything that was lacking; which, when I had demanded that they be reread in the people's hearing, such unheard-of crimes proceeded from that reading that no ears could bear the violence of that very poison."*

**21.** *"It is established that a council was thereupon entered upon about this matter, but the truth so suppressed—nay, so sold—by the incredible favor of all, that both communion was returned to the sacrilegious, and, lest the case could ever afterward be renewed by us, both the proceedings and the codices were burned in a fire. But when I freely stood by the bishops and most invidiously protested against the venal judgment, one of the number of seven bishops, that Agapius, fulfilled in deed the slaughter he had threatened me. For when, having seized me, he beat me with slaps and fists, and was restrained by the rest of his fellow bishops, who detested so great madness: 'Let him go now,' he says, 'if he prevails who boasts of having weighed down with his prayers a chief man from the count's house; let him now destroy me with similar curses."*

**22.** *"But I, rejoicing at the injury received: 'Let Christ hear these voices,' I said, 'and judge!' Now, not to recount what follows, an excessive fear of sin closes my mouth. For who would dare, intrepid, to estimate the deep abyss of the Lord's judgments*[^1]? We saw indeed the aforesaid bishop, about the seventh day, struck with a sudden pain of the throat, not give up his spirit before he begged pardon of me, a most unhappy man, for that deed or word of his. But can we, without sin, affirm that a bishop, who seemed Catholic, was struck by a blow of the Lord?"

**23.** *"During that same time, the hatreds of all growing strong against me, I was driven so far that, the labor of a very long journey being taken up, I fled to the help of the holy and most blessed Patroclus, bishop of Arles, whose most renowned constancy in the persecution of this heresy was praised. From whom indeed I obtained, not with difficulty, that all—both the accused and the judges who, by an unjust examination, perverted the weight of justice—should convene to a council; which, whether it is still to be held in the town of Béziers, as has been appointed, we do not know. Meanwhile, the imperial court granting us a most welcome short space of time, I made my way to this island, for this cause only, that I might enjoy your sight and might perhaps narrate these things to you in person, to be instructed by you, taking up even the labor of a most perilous voyage."*

**24.** *With these words, more or less, the narration of the holy and venerable man Fronto was ended, which I, most recently, as I received it from his mouth, have so brought to the ears of Your Beatitude. Now, then, although there is no hesitation about the truth of the history, yet, bringing my consultation to the heavenly senate of Your Beatitude, I first admonish this, that, even if no rumor, however uncertain, had sounded it, yet by most certain reasoning it is credible that the Spanish bishops will by no means come to the council which your holy and most blessed brother Patroclus, moved by the power not of authority but of devotion, has appointed. But the same holy and most blessed brother of yours and the other Gallican bishops—renowned and distinguished men, who do not allow so great a stain to flow into the Church of our Lord Jesus Christ—kindled, as is certain to me, with the fire of a greater zeal, will perhaps, referring these things even to the ears of the renowned prince, labor that—since through the whole world now the cancer of this dogma has spread by creeping secretly—it may be cut off through all the provinces by the cauteries of a uniform sentence.*

**25.** *But I have plainly learned that there are some who defend the most obscene and sacrilegious Priscillianists by the example of Your Beatitude; for they say: "The African bishops by no means thrust from the rank of the priesthood the Donatists who in any way were converted to them, and this the illustrious and noble teacher Augustine—nay, the very grace of the Holy Spirit which speaks through his mouth—believed should be sanctified, whereas among us there is such great cruelty that we thrust from the priesthood priests caught in the crime of this dogma, or establish so unruly a severity of judgment, that the doors of the Church are to be unlocked to none of those among whom these sacrileges have been caught, except through penance."*

**26.** *Accordingly, by an opportune suggestion, as I think, out of the confidence of your familiarity and love—if you graciously admit it—I dare to advise that your holy and venerable Paternity order some letter on this matter to be sent to your most blessed brother Bishop Patroclus—who, as I have learned, desires to be visited by your writings—through which the diversity of provinces and of persons and of dogmas, being declared, may teach that a diverse sentence ought to be brought. For there is a great difference, as it seems to my dullness, between Spain, which admits a hidden incest, and Africa, which publicly fornicates; so too between the Spaniards, who, caught in sacrilege, tremble, and the Africans, who even obstinately glory in schism, there is a great diversity. In the same way, the Priscillianists, most worthy of all execration and abomination, differ without doubt from the Donatists—however harsh and unpeaceful—by the quality of their crimes.*

**27.** *But if you weigh my inept and foolish little suggestion as of little worth, I beg that you would deign to grant pardon to a most bold love. Indeed, the book of which I made mention at the beginning, provoked by that very clean prosperity, I believed should be directed to your Paternity; which, if your Paternity orders to be handed to the most cunning and most select young men, and instructs them as is fitting, I think that many bands of Priscillianists, which lurk especially in this city, will be made public. May you flourish forever, mindful of me, holy and most blessed Lord.*

[^1]: See Ps 36:6 (35:7)
