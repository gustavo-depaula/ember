# Letter 205

TO HIS MOST BELOVED BROTHER CONSENTIUS, FROM AUGUSTINE.

**1. 1.** As far as concerns the bodily eyes, there are some whom we see and do not know, whose pursuits and life are unknown to us; some we know and do not see, whose love and affection have become known to us, among whom we number you too; and therefore we desire the more to see you, that you may be among those whom we both see and know. For those who, unknown, are thrust upon our eyes are not only not desirable, but scarcely tolerable, unless by some signs the beauty of the inner man appears in them. But those whose mind, like yours, has appeared to us by understanding before the body by seeing—we now indeed know them; but we desire also to see them, that through that which is beheld by the eyes we may enjoy, much more pleasantly and familiarly, the inner friend whom we already know. But perhaps God will grant us this too concerning you, with human affairs quieter and more tranquil, as we wish, so that it may be of honorable charity rather than of troublesome necessity. Now let me answer, as I shall be able with the Lord's help, the things which, besides the letter, you sent on another sheet to be inquired of me.

**1. 2.** You ask "whether the body of the Lord now has bones and blood, or the remaining features of flesh." What if you added, whether also garments? Would not the question be increased? For what cause, except that the things which in the use of this our life we know to be corruptible, we can scarcely think of without corruption, when certain proofs of the divine miracles have already been given, from which we may conjecture greater ones? For if the garment of the Israelites could be without wearing out for so many years in the desert, if the dead skin of their shoes endured so long without decay, God can everywhere extend, for as long a time as he wills, the incorrupt quality of any bodies whatever. I therefore believe that the Lord's body is in heaven such as it was on earth, when he ascended into heaven. But he had said to the disciples, as we read in the Gospel, doubting about his resurrection, and thinking that what they saw was not a body but a spirit: *See my hands and my feet; handle and see, for a spirit does not have bones and flesh, as you see me to have*[^1]. As he was handled with their hands when he was on earth, so he was led from their sight when he went into heaven. There the angelic voice sounded: *So shall he come, as you have seen him going into heaven*[^2]. Let faith be present, and no question will remain.

**1. 3.** Unless perhaps there is something to be inquired about the blood, because, when he had said, *Handle and see, for a spirit does not have flesh and bones*, he did not add "blood." Let us too, then, not add to inquire what he did not add to say: and, for brevity, if it please, let the question be ended. For perhaps, with the occasion of blood taken, a more troublesome searcher will press us, and will say: If blood, why not also phlegm, why not also yellow bile and black bile, by which four humors the medical discipline too testifies that the nature of the flesh is tempered? But whatever anyone may add, let him beware of adding corruption, lest he corrupt the soundness and chastity of his faith.

**1. 4.** From the custom of things experienced, human weakness measures the unexperienced works of God, and thinks it chatters somewhat acutely when it says: If it is flesh, it is also blood; if blood, also the other humors; if the other humors, then also corruption. In that way it would say: If it is flame, it also burns; if it burns, it also scorches; if it scorches, then the fire sent into the furnace by the impious king burned the bodies of the three men. But if whoever thinks rightly about the divine works does not doubt that this miracle was done in the three men, why should it not be believed that he who made those bodies not able to be corrupted by fire, made that body able to be corrupted neither by fire, nor by hunger, nor by disease, nor by old age, nor by any other force by which corruption is wont to ruin human bodies? But if anyone says that it was not to the flesh of those three men that incorruption against the fire was added, but rather from the fire itself the faculty of corrupting was taken away; why do we fear that he who made the fire unable to corrupt could not make the flesh unable to be corrupted? For if that is understood not of a change of the flesh, but of a change of the fire, it is much more marvelous: for at one and the same time it both did not burn the bodies of men, that it might not be able to harm, and burned the wood of the furnace, that it might be able to blaze. But those who do not even believe these things distrust too much the divine power, and with them, or to them, our discourse is now not directed: but those who believe these things, from these may also somehow conjecture those things which they faithfully inquire. The divine power, then, avails, from this visible and tangible nature of bodies, with certain things remaining, to take away what qualities it wills: and thereby it avails also to strengthen mortal members—with the features of form remaining, but the corruption of mortality being dead—with a stable vigor; so that decay be absent, the figure be present; motion be present, weariness be absent; the power of eating be present, the necessity of hungering be absent.

**2. 5.** As for what is read in the Apostle, *Flesh and blood shall not possess the kingdom of God*[^3], the question is indeed resolved even in that way which you have recalled, so that by the name of flesh and blood are understood the works of flesh and blood: but, because the Apostle was speaking not of works but of the manner of the resurrection, and was turning that question over in his disputation, it is better in that place understood that flesh and blood is put for the corruption of flesh and blood. For if the name of flesh signifies the operation, why should it not signify also the corruption, as it was said, *All flesh is grass*[^4]? For here too that very corruptibility was signified: for it follows, *And all the honor of flesh as the flower of grass; the grass has withered, the flower has fallen*[^5]. Does this too apply to that flesh of which it was said, *Handle and see, for a spirit does not have bones and flesh, as you see me to have*? For how could it wither or fall, when it is written that *Christ rising from the dead dies now no more, and death shall no longer have dominion over him*[^6]?

**2. 6.** Accordingly, inspect that apostolic sentence from above, and consider the whole. When he wished to persuade the resurrection of the dead to those who said that there is no resurrection of the dead, having set forth the resurrection of Christ as an example, among the other things he discussed there, he brought against himself a question and said: *But someone will say, How do the dead rise? And with what body do they come?*[^7] that is, with what kind of body? Then, applying proofs of seeds: *You fool*, he says, *what you sow is not made alive unless it first dies; and what you sow, you do not sow the body that shall be, but a bare grain, as it may be of wheat, or of some of the rest; but God gives it a body as he has willed, and to each of the seeds its own body*[^8]. According to this, then, he had said, *You do not sow the body that shall be*. For it is not that wheat will not be from wheat; but because no one sows the herb, or the stalk, and the manifold coverings of the grains in the chaff, with which the seeds nevertheless rise, therefore he says, *But a bare grain*: wishing hence to show that if God can add what was not in the bare seed, much more can he restore what was in the body of man.

**2. 7.** Now what he adjoins pertains to the difference of those who rise again, on account of the diverse glories of the faithful and the saints. *Not all flesh*, he says, *is the same flesh: one indeed of men, another the flesh of cattle, another of birds, another of fishes. And there are heavenly bodies, and earthly bodies; but one is the glory of the heavenly, and another of the earthly. And one is the glory of the sun, another the glory of the moon, and another the glory of the stars. For star differs from star in glory: so also is the resurrection of the dead*[^9]. In all these the sense is this: If the kinds of flesh, although all are mortal, nevertheless differ among themselves according to the diversities of living things; and if bodies, although all are visible, nevertheless differ according to the diversities of places, whence one is the glory of the heavenly, another of the earthly; and if among things in the lofty places, although all are heavenly, they too nevertheless differ in the brightnesses of their lights; it is no wonder that in the resurrection of the dead the glory of merits will differ.

**2. 8.** Hence now he comes to that which all flesh that rises to eternal life has in common, and he says: *It is sown in corruption, it shall rise in incorruption; it is sown in dishonor, it shall rise in glory; it is sown in weakness, it shall rise in power; it is sown an animal body, it shall rise a spiritual body*[^10]. In these words of the Apostle, is it lawful to think that our bodies will rise better than Christ's, when the example is set forth about him, which we ought faithfully to heed and, through his grace, to hope for? And thereby in no way could the body of Christ rise with corruption, if ours is promised to rise in incorruption; nor could that one rise without glory, if ours will rise in glory. But what glory is there, where there is still corruption? It is too absurd, then, that we should believe that body to have been both sown in weakness—that is, put to death—and to have risen in weakness; when our body is sown in weakness, rises in power; and the same Apostle says of Christ, *Although he was crucified out of weakness, yet he lives out of the power of God*[^11]. But who would be so absurdly wise as to believe that body sown an animal body, raised an animal body, if ours is sown an animal body, shall rise a spiritual body?

**2. 9.** It is established, then, and in no way to be doubted, that the body of Christ—which, although it did not see the corruption of putrefaction in the sepulcher, whence it is written, *Nor will you give your holy one to see corruption*[^12], could nevertheless be pierced through with nails and a lance—now altogether consists in incorruption; and that what was sown in the dishonor of passion and death is now in the glory of eternal life; and that what could be crucified out of weakness now reigns in power; and that what was an animal body, since it was taken from Adam, is now spiritual, since it is now inseparably joined to the spirit. For when the Apostle wished to bring a testimony of the Scriptures about the animal body, he set down that which is read in Genesis: *If there is*, he says, *an animal body, there is also a spiritual; as it is written, The first Adam was made into a living soul*[^13]. You surely recall how it is written: *And God breathed into his face the breath of life; and man was made into a living soul*[^14]. But it was said also of the animals, *Let the earth bring forth a living soul*[^15]. The animal body, then, is understood to be called like the other animals on account of the dissolution and corruption of death, which is daily restored by food, and afterward, the structure of the living thing being separated, is dissolved. But the spiritual body is that which is now, with the spirit, immortal.

**2. 10.** Although some judge that a body then becomes spiritual when even the body itself is changed into spirit, and that what man was, out of body and spirit, will both, and the whole, be spirit, as if the Apostle had said, "It is sown a body, it shall rise a spirit." But he said, *It is sown an animal body, it shall rise a spiritual body*: accordingly, just as the animal body is not a soul, but a body, so also the spiritual body we ought to think not a spirit, but a body. Who, moreover, would dare to suppose either that Christ's body did not rise spiritual; or that, if it rose spiritual, it was then no longer a body, but a spirit—when this opinion of the disciples is refuted, where, when, seeing him, they thought they saw a spirit, he says, *Handle and see, for a spirit does not have bones and flesh, as you see me to have*? Now therefore that flesh was a spiritual body, and yet was not a spirit, but a body, to be loosed and separated from the soul by no further death: just as the animal body, such as was animated by the breath of God when man was made into a living soul, even it too, from animal, would have been going to be spiritual without the intervention of death, had not the transgression of the precept, sin having first been committed, inflicted the penalty, before God should give the crown of preserved righteousness.

**2. 11.** Whence the Lord Christ came to us through us, when, righteous, he found us sinners; through the couch, as it were, of our humility, but not with the disease of our iniquity. For he appeared to us through an animal—that is, a mortal—body; who surely, if he had willed, would have come at first with an immortal body. But because we had to be healed by the humility of the Son of God, he descended even to our weakness, and showed the merit and reward of our faith by the power of his resurrection. Therefore the Apostle follows and says, *The last Adam into a life-giving spirit*[^16]. Whether the first Adam be understood, who was formed of dust before, and the last Adam, who was procreated of the Virgin; or whether both are fulfilled in each man, so that the first Adam is man in a mortal body, and the last Adam the same man in an immortal body: he nevertheless wished this to be the difference between a living soul and a life-giving spirit, that there the body is animal, here spiritual. For the soul in an animal body does indeed live, but does not give life so far as to take away corruption: but in a spiritual body, since, perfectly cleaving to the Lord, it is one spirit, it so gives life as to make the body spiritual, consuming all corruption, fearing no separation.

**2. 12.** Accordingly it follows: *But not first what is spiritual, but what is animal; afterward what is spiritual. The first man of the earth, earthly; the second man from heaven, heavenly. As is the earthly, such also are the earthly; and as is the heavenly, such also are the heavenly. As we have borne the image of the earthly, let us bear also the image of him who is from heaven*[^17]. What is *As is the earthly, such also are the earthly*, except, mortal from the mortal? And what is *As is the heavenly, such also are the heavenly*, except, immortal through the immortal? The former through Adam, the latter through Christ. For the Lord became earthly for this, although he was heavenly, that he might make those who were earthly heavenly; that is, he became, from immortal, mortal—by taking the form of a servant, not by changing the nature of the Lord—that he might make those who were mortal immortal, by imparting the grace of the Lord, not by retaining the injury of the servant.

**2. 13.** When, therefore, the Apostle, disputing about the resurrection of the body, taught that our bodies would be, from corruptible, incorruptible, from contemptible, glorious, from weak, strong, from animal, spiritual—that is, from mortal, immortal—he added that which is now in question, and said: *But this I say, brethren, that flesh and blood cannot possess the kingdom of God*[^18]. And lest anyone should think that the Apostle had defined this according to the substance of the flesh, he made clear what he meant by subjoining, *Nor shall corruption possess incorruption*[^19]. As though he said, "What I said, Flesh and blood shall not possess the kingdom of God, I said for this reason, because corruption shall not possess incorruption." In this place, then, by the name of flesh and blood he wished the corruption of mortality to be understood.

**2. 14.** Finally, as though it were said to him, "How will it be flesh, and not be flesh? For it will be flesh, since the Lord after the resurrection says, *Handle and see, for a spirit does not have bones and flesh, as you see me to have*; but it will not be flesh, since flesh and blood shall not possess the kingdom of God": he expounds what he meant, adding: *Behold, I tell you a mystery: we shall all indeed rise again*—or, as the Greek manuscripts have it, *we shall all indeed sleep*—*but we shall not all be changed*[^20]. Whether he wished this change to be understood for the worse or for the better, the following words show. *In an atom*, he says—that is, in a point of time which cannot be divided—*in the twinkling of an eye*—that is, in the utmost swiftness—*at the last trumpet*—that is, at the last sign that will be given, so that these things be fulfilled: *for the trumpet shall sound*, he says, *and the dead shall rise incorrupt, and we shall be changed*[^21]. This change, then, must without doubt be understood for the better, because all, both the righteous and the unrighteous, are going to rise: but, as the Lord says in the Gospel, *those who have done well, unto the resurrection of life; but those who have done evil, unto the resurrection of judgment*[^22]—calling "judgment" the everlasting penalty, as in another place: *He who does not believe*, he says, *is already judged*[^23]. Accordingly those who are going to rise unto judgment will not be changed into that incorruption which cannot even suffer the corruption of pain. For that belongs to the faithful and the saints: but these will be tortured with perpetual corruption; because *their fire shall not be extinguished, and their worm shall not die*[^24].

**2. 15.** What, then, does this distinction mean, *And the dead shall rise incorrupt, and we shall be changed*, except that all will rise incorrupt, but of these the righteous too will be changed into that incorruption which no corruption can at all harm? And thereby those who will not be changed into it will indeed rise incorrupt, in the integrity of their members, but yet to be corrupted by the pain of punishments, when they shall have heard, *Go, you cursed, into the eternal fire which is prepared for the devil and his angels*[^25]. From which evil hearing the righteous man will not fear. But when he had said of that change of the righteous, *we shall be changed*, as though we asked how this comes about, or what that change will be like, he adds and says: *For this corruptible must put on incorruption, and this mortal must put on immortality*[^26]. It must not, as I suppose, be doubted that, according to this saying, *Flesh and blood shall not possess the kingdom of God*, there will not be there the corruption and mortality of flesh and blood: for according to these qualities he named flesh and blood in this place.

**2. 16.** Wherefore, to set down something by way of example which for now occurs: as it is written, *Lest perhaps he who tempts have tempted you, and our labor be in vain*[^27]—and here the devil is understood, as though God does not tempt at all, of whom in another place Scripture says, *But he himself tempts no one*[^28]; nor is this sentence contrary to that by which it is said, *The Lord your God tempts you*[^29]; but the question is resolved, since the word "temptation" has diverse meanings, in that one is the temptation of deception, another the temptation of testing; according to the former, no one is understood to tempt except the devil, but according to the latter, God tempts: so when it is said, "Flesh shall possess the kingdom of God," and, "Flesh shall not possess the kingdom of God," let the meaning of this name too be distinguished, and there will be no question: since flesh according to the substance, according to which it was said, *A spirit does not have bones and flesh, as you see me to have*, will possess the kingdom of God; but flesh, when it is understood according to corruption, will not possess it. For this was expounded when it was said, *Flesh and blood shall not possess the kingdom of God*, in what was immediately added, *nor shall corruption possess incorruption*; as we have, I think, sufficiently discussed.

**3. 17.** But as for what you ask, whether the features of bodies are formed singly by God the Creator, it will not move you, if, as far as the human mind can, you understand the power of the divine operation. For how shall we be able to deny that God even now works all things that are created, when the Lord says, *My Father works until now*[^30]? Whence that cessation of the seventh day must be understood to have been made from founding the natures themselves, not from the administration of those that are reported as founded. Since, then, the nature of things is administered by the Creator, and through ordered ranks, in their predetermined places and times, all things are born, God works until now. For if God does not now form these things, how is it read, *Before I formed you in the womb, I knew you*[^31]? How also can it be taken, *But if God so clothes the grass of the field, which today is, and tomorrow is cast into the oven*[^32]? Unless perhaps it is to be believed that the grass is clothed by God, and bodies are not formed by God! For when he said *clothes*, he sufficiently indicates not a past ordination but a present operation. Hence too is that saying of the Apostle about seeds, which I recalled above: *You do not sow the body that shall be*, he says, *but a bare grain, as it may be of wheat, or of some of the rest; but God gives it a body as he has willed*[^33]. He did not say "gave," or "disposed"; but, *he disposes all things sweetly*[^34]. But it is much, even to understand slenderly, how changeable and temporal things are founded not by changeable motions of the Creator, but by his eternal and stable power.

**4. 18.** As for that which you thought should be inquired about—whether all the baptized, if they have departed from the body without penitence, entangled in diverse crimes, are going to obtain pardon at some time—I have already written a book of no small size; which, if you take care to have copied out, perhaps you will seek nothing further on this point.

**4. 19.** You also wish to know through me whether that breath of God into Adam is the very soul itself. Briefly I answer: either it is the soul itself, or by it the soul was made. But even if it is the soul itself, it was made. For about the soul God speaks through the prophet Isaiah, where he says, *Every breath I have made*[^35]; which the following words sufficiently indicate: for it follows, *On account of sin I grieved him a little*—that is, the breath itself; and the rest, which can be understood only of the human soul. For in this question it must especially be guarded against, lest the soul be believed to be not a nature made by God, but the very substance of God, like the only-begotten Son, who is his Word, or some particle of him; as though that nature and substance, by which God is whatever he is, could be changeable: which no one who feels that he has a soul fails to feel the soul to be. As I was dictating these things, the bearer, now awaiting the wind, was urging me vehemently to set sail. And so if you read here anything said in a disordered and uncultivated way, or if you perceive the whole to be so, give your effort to the teaching, pardon to the tongue. (And in another hand): May you live for God, most beloved son.
