# Letter 28

To the most beloved lord, and a brother and fellow presbyter to be obeyed and embraced with the most sincere devotion of love, Jerome, from Augustine

**1. 1.** Never has anyone become so well known to another by his face as the quiet, glad, and truly liberal exercise of your studies in the Lord has become to me. Although, therefore, I very much desire to know you altogether, yet I lack some small part of you—namely, the presence of the body: and even this, after our brother Alypius, now a most blessed bishop but then already worthy of the episcopate, saw you, and on returning seemed to me to have been seen, I cannot deny was in great part impressed upon me by his report; and before his return, when he was seeing you there, I was seeing you, but with his eyes. For anyone who knows us would say that we are, not in mind, but only in body, two—that is, by concord and a most faithful familiarity, though not by the merits in which he excels. Since, then, you love me first by the communion of spirit by which we strive toward one, and then from his mouth; I by no means impudently, as one unknown, commend to your Brotherliness our brother Profuturus, who, we hope, will truly profit us by our efforts and then by your help; unless perhaps he is such that I myself am made more commended to you through him than he through me. Thus far perhaps I ought to write, if I wished to be content with the manner of customary letters; but my mind teems with talk to be shared with you about our studies, which we have in Christ Jesus our Lord, who deigns, even through your love, to minister to us in no small measure many useful things, and certain provisions for the journey he has shown us.

**2. 2.** We ask, then—and the whole zealous society of the African Churches asks with us—that you not be reluctant to spend care and labor on translating the books of those who have treated our Scriptures most excellently in Greek. For you can bring it about that we too have such men, and one most of all, whom you sound more gladly in your letters. But about translating the holy canonical Scriptures into the Latin tongue, I would not want you to labor, except in the way in which you translated Job: namely, with signs added, so that it may appear what difference there is between this translation of yours and the Septuagint, whose authority is most weighty. But I cannot sufficiently marvel if anything is now found in the Hebrew copies which escaped so many translators most skilled in that tongue. For I pass over the Septuagint, about whose greater concord, whether of counsel or of spirit, than if it were one man, I do not dare to give any fixed opinion on either side, except that I think the preeminent authority must be attributed to them in this task without controversy. Those move me more who, although they translated later, and held tightly, as is said, to the way and rules of the Hebrew words and expressions, not only did not agree among themselves, but even left many things to be dug out and brought to light so long afterward. And either these things are obscure or they are manifest: for if they are obscure, you too are believed able to be deceived in them; if manifest, those translators are not believed to have been able to be deceived in them. Therefore, about this matter, by your love, I beseech you to make me certain, the causes set forth.

**3. 3.** I have also read certain writings said to be yours on the Epistles of the apostle Paul; of which, when you wished to unravel the one to the Galatians, that passage came into my hands where the apostle Peter is recalled from a ruinous pretense. There, that a defense of lying was undertaken, whether by you, such a man, or by anyone, if another wrote those things, I confess I grieve in no small measure, until those things which move me are refuted, if perhaps they can be refuted. For it seems to me most destructive to believe that any lie is contained in the holy Books; that is, that those men through whom that Scripture was ministered and written down for us told any lie in their books. For it is one question whether it is ever right for a good man to lie; and another question whether it was right for a writer of the holy Scriptures to lie: nay rather, it is not another question, but no question at all. For once an officious lie of any kind is admitted into so high a summit of authority, no particle of those books will remain which, as it seems difficult to anyone in morals or incredible in faith, may not, by that same most destructive rule, be referred to the design and office of a lying author.

**3. 4.** For if the apostle Paul was lying when, rebuking the apostle Peter, he said: *If you, being a Jew, live like a gentile and not like a Jew, how do you compel the gentiles to live like Jews?*[^1]—and if it seemed to him that Peter had acted rightly, whom he both said and wrote had not acted rightly, in order, as it were, to soothe the minds of those who were in an uproar; what shall we answer when perverse men rise up, forbidding marriage, whose coming he himself foretold[^2], and say that all that the same Apostle spoke for the confirming of the right of marriages was a lie spoken on account of men who could be roused to an uproar by love of their spouses—namely, not because he thought this, but in order that their hostility might be appeased? There is no need to recall many things. For even the praises of God can seem to be officious lies, so that the love of him may glow more among the more sluggish men; and thus nowhere in the holy Books will there be a sure authority of chaste truth. Do we not notice that the same Apostle, with great care for commending the truth, says: *But if Christ has not risen, vain is our preaching, vain too is your faith; and we are found even false witnesses of God, because we have given testimony against God, that he raised up Christ, whom he did not raise up*[^3]? If anyone should say to him: Why do you shudder at this lie, since you said that which, even if it were false, pertains most of all to the praise of God? Would he not, detesting the man's madness, in whatever words and meanings he could, lay open the inner chambers of his heart, crying out that it is no less—or perhaps even a greater—crime to praise falsehood in God than to find fault with the truth? It must therefore be brought about that such a man approach the knowledge of the divine Scriptures as thinks so holily and truly about the holy Books that he is unwilling to be delighted by any part of them through officious lies, and would rather pass over what he does not understand than prefer his own heart to that truth. For surely, when he says this, he asks to be believed himself, and acts so that we may not believe the authorities of the divine Scriptures.

**3. 5.** And I indeed, with whatever strength the Lord supplies, would show that all those testimonies which have been brought forward to establish the usefulness of lying must be understood otherwise, so that everywhere their firm truth may be taught. For as testimonies ought not to be lies, so they ought not to favor a lie. But this I leave to your understanding. For when more diligent consideration is brought to the reading, you will perhaps see it much more easily than I. But to this consideration the piety will compel you by which you recognize that the authority of the divine Scriptures becomes unsteady, so that each believes in them what he wants and does not believe what he does not want, if it is once persuaded that those men, through whom these things were ministered to us, could have lied officiously in their writings. Unless perhaps you are going to give certain rules by which we may know where it is right to lie and where it is not right. If this can be done, I beg, by no means explain it by lying and doubtful reasonings; and do not judge me burdensome or impudent, by the most truthful humanity of our Lord. For—not to say it is no fault—surely it is no great fault that my error favors the truth, if rightly in you the truth can favor a lie.

**4. 6.** I would desire to speak many other things with your most sincere heart, and to confer about Christian study; but no letter is sufficient for this desire of mine. I can do the same thing more abundantly through the brother, whom I rejoice to have sent to be mingled with and nourished by your sweet and useful conversations. And yet, much as I would wish, perhaps not even he (let me say it with his leave) holds it all; although I would in no way prefer myself to him. For I confess myself more capacious than he: but I see that he is being made fuller, in which he without doubt excels me: and after he returns, which with the Lord's help I hope will prosper, when I have become a sharer in his breast heaped full by you, he will not fill up what in me will still be empty and greedy of your thoughts. So it will come about that even then I shall be the more needy, and he the more abundant. Indeed, the same brother carries some of our writings with him; to the reading of which if you grant the favor, I beg you also to apply a sincere and brotherly severity. For I do not understand otherwise what is written: *The just man shall correct me in mercy, and shall reprove me; but let not the oil of the sinner make fat my head*[^4]; except that a healing reprover loves more than a flattering anointer of the head. But I, a most poor judge, read with the greatest difficulty what I have written, being either more timid than is right or more eager. I sometimes even see my own faults; but these I prefer to hear from better men, lest, when perhaps I have rightly reproved myself, I again flatter myself, and seem to myself to have passed a fearful rather than a just sentence upon myself.

[^1]: Gal 2:14
[^2]: See 1 Tim 4:1–3
[^3]: 1 Cor 15:14–15
[^4]: Ps 141:5 (140:5)
