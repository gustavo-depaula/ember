# Letter 147

*THE BOOK ON SEEING GOD*

FROM AUGUSTINE TO PAULINA, GREETINGS.

**1. 1.** Mindful of the debt that arose from your request and my promise, Paulina, religious handmaid of God, I ought not to have been negligent also in paying it. For when you had asked me to write something to you at length and copiously about the invisible God, whether he can be seen through the eyes of the body, I could not refuse, lest I offend your holy zeal; but I put off what I had promised, whether on account of my other occupations, or because the thing demanded of me was such that it had to be pondered somewhat longer. But since it was so great a matter that, in pondering it, it became more difficult not so much in deciding what should be felt or said about it, as in how it should be made convincing to those who felt otherwise, I thought that an end must at last be put to the delay, hoping that divine help would be more present to me as I wrote than as I deferred. For this reason it seems to me that in this inquiry the manner of living counts for more than the manner of speaking. For those who have learned from the Lord Jesus Christ to be meek and humble of heart make more progress by thinking and praying than by reading and hearing. Nor for all that should speech cease to play its part, but when the planter and the waterer have performed the office of their service, they leave the rest to him who gives the growth; for it is he who has even formed the planter and the waterer.

**1. 2.** Take in, then, the words of understanding according to the inner man. For it is renewed from day to day, even while this outer man is corrupted, whether by the chastisement of abstinence, or by some sickly state of health, or by any other chance, or certainly—what is necessary even for those who live long with a sound body—by the very advance of age. Lift up, therefore, the spirit of your mind, which is renewed in the knowledge of God according to the image of him who created it, where Christ dwells in you through faith, where there is *neither Jew nor Greek, slave nor free, male nor female*[^1], where you will not die when you begin to be loosed from the body, since neither did you wither there, though you are heavy with years. Intent through this inner self of yours, raised up, attend and see what I say. I do not wish you to follow my authority, so as to think you must believe something merely because it is said by me; but either believe the canonical Scriptures, if you do not yet see how something is true, or believe the truth that demonstrates inwardly, so that you may plainly see this.

**1. 3.** By way of example I will say something by which you may be more surely prepared for these matters, and that from the very point on which in this very work the labor of arguing is set before us. We believe that God is seen, not because we see him either through the eyes of the body, as we see this sun, or through the gaze of the mind, as each one sees himself within—sees himself living, sees himself willing, sees himself seeking, sees himself knowing, sees himself not knowing. You, therefore, having read this very letter, recall that you have seen the sun through the eyes of the body, and you can even see it at once, if it is the time of day for it, and you are in a place where, on that side, the sky lies open to your eyes all the way to the contemplation of the sun. But for seeing those things which I said are perceived by the mind—that is, that you are alive, that you will to see God, that you seek this, that you know that you live and will and seek, but do not know how God is seen—for seeing all these things, I say, you neither apply the eyes of the body, nor do you feel or seek the interval of space through which the gaze must be directed to reach the things it is to perceive: thus you see your life, your will, your inquiry, your knowledge, your ignorance, since this too is no vision to be despised, to see what you do not know. Thus, I say, you see all these things in such a way that you see them within yourself, you have them present to you, and without any outlines of figures or brightness of colors, all the more clearly and certainly the more simply and inwardly you behold them. Since, then, we now see God neither with the eyes of the body, as we see bodies whether heavenly or earthly, nor with the gaze of the mind, as we see those things, some of which I have mentioned, which you most certainly behold within yourself, why do we believe that he is seen, except because we lend faith to the Scriptures, where it is read: *Blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God*[^2], and whatever other things are written to this same sense by the same divine authority, which we count it impious not to believe, but which we do not doubt at all that it is the mark of devotion to believe?

**1. 4.** Hold, therefore, to this distinction: that if I point out something to you in my discussion that you see with the eyes of the flesh in such a way, or perceive by any other bodily sense, or recall that you have so perceived—as colors, crashing sounds, smells, tastes, degrees of heat are perceived, and whatever else we perceive through the body by seeing, hearing, smelling, tasting, touching—or that you see it with the gaze of the mind in such a way as you see your life, will, thought, memory, understanding, knowledge, your faith, and whatever else you behold with the mind, and which you do not doubt to be so, not only by believing but plainly by living—then you may judge that I have shown it to you. But what I shall have shown in such a way that it is not held as seen and perceived by the sense either of the body or of the mind, and yet I shall have said something that must be either true or false but is seen by neither of those two kinds—then it remains only to be believed or not believed. But if it is established by the clear authority of the divine Scriptures, namely those which are named canonical in the Church, it must be believed without any doubt. As for other witnesses or testimonies by which we are urged to believe something, you may be permitted to believe them or not, in proportion as you have weighed how much or how little weight they have toward producing faith.

**1. 5.** For if we believed nothing at all of those things which we have not seen—that is, which are not present to us in appearance, which we have not perceived either by mind or by body, nor learned from the holy Scriptures by reading or hearing—how should we know that there are cities where we have never been? Or that Rome was founded by Romulus, or, to speak of nearer things, Constantinople by Constantine? How, finally, should we know which parents begot us, of what fathers, grandfathers, ancestors we were born? For of such things we know very many, yet not as present to any sense, like the sun, or like the will of our own mind, nor by the authority of the canonical words, like the fact that Adam was the first man, or that Christ was born in the flesh, suffered, and rose again; but we learned them from others reporting them, of whose testimony in this kind of matter at least we have thought we ought not to doubt at all. And if in these we are somewhere deceived, by believing either that something is so which is not so, or that something is not so which is so, we judge there is no danger, provided it is not against that faith by which devotion is formed. This preface of mine does not yet discuss the question proposed, but prepares you and others who are going to read these things as to what sort of judges you ought to be of my writings or of anyone's; lest you either suppose you know what you do not know, or rashly believe what you have neither perceived by the senses of the body or of the mind in the evidence of the very thing to be known, nor learned that it must be believed on the authority of the canonical Scriptures, even if it was not present to the senses of your mind or body.

**1. 6.** Have we now, then, come to the matter? Or must the reader still be instructed? For some think that what we call believing, when a true thing is believed, is just this same thing as beholding it with the mind. But if that is so, this earlier preface of ours is mistaken, in which we distinguished that it is one thing to perceive something through the body, like the sun in the sky, and on earth a mountain, a tree, or any body whatever; and another thing to perceive by the gaze of the mind a thing no less evident, as our will is seen within us by ourselves when we will something, or our thought when we think, or our memory while we remember, or anything of the kind in the mind without a body; but yet another thing to believe what is neither present to the sight of body or mind nor recalled to have been present—as that Adam was created without parents, and that Christ was born of the Virgin, suffered, and rose again. For these things too were done bodily, and could certainly have been seen through the body if we had then been present; but now they are not present, as is present this light which is discerned by the eyes, or the will by which we now will something, which is beheld by the mind. But since this distinction is not false, surely that earlier construction was the less adequate, in that it had distinguished by a less clear expression between believing and beholding something present with the mind, lest they be thought wholly one and the same.

**2. 7.** What, then, shall we say? Is it enough for us to say that between seeing and believing there is this difference, that present things are seen, absent things believed? Plainly it is perhaps enough, if we understand things called present in this place to be those which are at hand to the senses, whether of mind or of body, from which indeed the word "present" is derived. For thus I plainly see this light by the sense of the body, and so too my own will, because it is at hand to the senses of the mind and is present to me within. But if someone should disclose his will to me, whose mouth and voice are present to me—yet because the will itself which he discloses to me lies hidden from the sense of my body and mind, I believe, I do not see; or if I think he is lying, I do not believe, even if perhaps it is as he says. Those things, then, are believed which are absent from our senses, if what is testified concerning them seems suitable. But those things are seen which are at hand, whence they are also called "present" to the senses of mind or body. For although there are five senses of the body—seeing, hearing, smelling, tasting, touching—sight is indeed assigned especially to the eyes, yet we use this word also for the rest. For we do not only say, See what is shining, but also, See what is sounding, See what smells, See what tastes, See what is hot. Nor, because I have said that those things are believed which are absent from our senses, let it be so taken as if they should be reckoned among those things which we once saw and which we retain and are certain we saw, although they are not at hand when they are recalled by us. For these are reckoned not among things believed but among things seen; and therefore they are known, not because we put faith in other witnesses, but because we without doubt remember and know that we saw them.

**3. 8.** Our knowledge, then, consists of things seen and things believed; but in those which we have seen or do see, we ourselves are the witnesses; while in those which we believe, we are moved to faith by other witnesses, when of those things which we neither recall having seen nor do see, signs are given, whether in voices, or in letters, or in any documents, by which, when seen, things not seen may be believed. And not without reason do we say that we know not only those things which we have seen or do see, but also those which we believe, moved by suitable testimonies or witnesses for each matter. Further, if we are not incongruously said to know even that which we most certainly believe, hence it has come about that even things rightly believed, though not present to our senses, we are said to see with the mind. For knowledge is attributed to the mind, whether it retains something perceived and known through the senses of the body or through the mind itself; and faith itself is certainly seen by the mind, although what is not seen is believed by that faith. Hence the apostle Peter says: *in whom now, not seeing, you believe*[^3]; and the Lord himself: *Blessed are those who have not seen and have believed*[^4].

**3. 9.** When, therefore, it is said to a man, Believe that Christ rose from the dead, if he believes, mark what he sees, mark what he believes, and distinguish the two. He sees a man whose voice he hears; and the voice itself is reckoned among bodily things seen, according to what we said above. These are two things: a witness, and a testimony; of which the one belongs to the eyes, the other to the ears. But this witness is perhaps confirmed by the authority of other testimonies, namely of the divine Scriptures, or of any others by which he is moved to faith. The Scriptures belong to the things seen by the eyes of the body, if he reads them, or by the ears, if he heard them. But he sees with the mind whatever he understands to be signified by the figures of letters or by sounds. He sees his own faith, by which he answers that he believes without hesitation. He sees the thought by which he considers what good it can do him that he believes. He sees the will by which he came to take up religion. He sees also a certain image of the resurrection itself formed in his mind, without which whatever is said to have been done bodily cannot be understood, whether it is believed or not believed.

**4. 9.** But, as I suppose, you distinguish how he sees his own faith by which he believes, and how he sees that image of the resurrection formed in his mind, which, if he hears of it, even one who does not believe sees.

**4. 10.** All these things, then, he sees, partly with the body, partly with the mind. But the will of the one from whom he hears that he should believe, and Christ's resurrection itself, he does not see but believes; and yet he is said to see it by a certain gaze of the mind, more according to the faith of the testimonies than the presence of the things believed. For those things which he sees are at hand to the senses of mind or body; but those which he believes are absent from the senses of both mind and body. Although the will of the one from whom he hears that he should believe is not past, but remains in the one speaking—which will the very one who speaks sees in himself, but the one who hears does not see it but believes it. Christ's resurrection, however, is past, which not even those men saw who lived then. For those who saw Christ alive, whom they had seen dying, yet did not see the resurrection itself as it was happening, but most certainly believed it, by seeing and touching alive the one whom they had known dead. We believe the whole: both that he rose, and that he was then seen and handled by men, and that he now lives in heaven and dies no more, and death no longer has dominion over him[^5]. But the thing itself is present neither to the senses of our body, as this heaven and earth are present, nor to the gaze of our mind, as faith itself is present by which we believe this.

**4. 11.** But by now, as I suppose, you have recognized through this preface of mine what it is to see either with the mind or with the body, and how believing differs from these. Believing indeed is done with the mind and is seen with the mind, since our faith is plain to our mind. But yet what is believed by that same faith is absent both from the sight of our body—as the body in which Christ rose is absent—and from the sight of another's mind—as your faith is absent from the sight of my mind, although I believe it to be in you, since I do not see it with the body, which not even you can do, nor with the mind, which you can do, as I see my own, which you cannot. For *no one knows what goes on in a man except the spirit of the man which is in him*[^6], until the Lord comes and *illumines the hidden things of darkness and makes manifest the thoughts of the heart*[^7], so that each may see them, not only his own, but also those of others. For it was according to this that the Apostle said that no one knows what goes on in a man except the spirit of the man which is in him—according to what we see in ourselves; for according to that which we believe and do not see, we know that many are faithful, and to many we are known.

**5. 12.** If, then, these things are now distinguished, let us come to the matter. We know that God can be seen, since it is written: *Blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God*[^8]. Or ought I perhaps not to have said, We know, but, We believe—since we have never seen God either with the body, as we see this light, or with the mind, as we see the faith itself in us by which we believe this, but only because it is written in that Scripture of which we are faithful, and we do not doubt at all that it is true? Yet the apostle John, when he said something of this kind, says: *We know that when he appears we shall be like him, for we shall see him as he is*[^9]. Behold, he said he knew what had not yet come to be, and which he had come to know not by seeing but by believing. Rightly, therefore, did we say, We know that God can be seen; although we have not seen him, but have believed the divine authority contained in the holy books.

**5. 13.** What, then, is it that the same authority says: *No one has ever seen God*[^10]? Or is it perhaps answered that those testimonies are about seeing God, not about him having been seen? For it is said, *They* shall see God; not, They have seen; and not, We have seen, but, *We shall see him as he is*[^11]. Accordingly, to these sayings the words *No one has ever seen God* are not contrary. For they shall see whom they have not seen, who being pure in heart shall have willed to be sons of God. What, then, is: *I saw God face to face, and my soul was saved*[^12]? Is this too not contrary to what was said: *No one has ever seen God*? And that which is written of Moses, that *he spoke with God face to face, as one speaks to his friend*[^13]; and that which the prophet Isaiah, speaking of himself, says: *I saw the Lord of hosts seated on a throne*[^14]; and any other like testimonies that are wont to be brought forward from the same authority—how are they not contrary to the saying in which it is said: *No one has ever seen God*? And yet the Gospel itself can be thought to be contrary to itself. For how is it true, which is said in it: *Whoever has seen me has seen the Father also*[^15], if no one has ever seen God? How is it true: *Their angels always see the face of my Father*[^16], if no one has ever seen God?

**5. 14.** By what rule of understanding, then, shall we prove that these as it were contrary and conflicting statements are not contrary and do not conflict? For it can in no way come about that this authority of the Scriptures should lie in any part. If we say that in what is written, *No one has ever seen God*, only men are to be understood—just as that other was also said, *No one knows what goes on in a man except the spirit of the man which is in him*[^17]: no one, certainly, but of men; for this cannot be taken of God, since it is written of Christ that *there was no need for anyone to bear him witness about man, for he himself knew what was in man*[^18]. For the Apostle, explaining this more plainly, says: *whom no man has seen, nor can see*[^19]. If, then, it was so said, *No one has ever seen God*, as if it were said, No man, thus far that question will seem to be resolved, so that what the Lord says is not contrary to this statement: *Their angels always see the face of my Father*—namely, so that we may believe that the angels see God, whom no one—but of men—has ever seen. How, then, did Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, Job, Moses, Micaiah, Isaiah, and any others see God, of whom the most truthful Scripture testifies that they saw God, if no man has ever seen God nor can see him?

**5. 15.** Although some, wishing to prove also that the ungodly will see God, think that God was seen even by the devil himself, so taking what is written in the book of Job, that the devil too came with the angels into the sight of God[^20]—so that now this too comes into question: how was it said, *Blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God*[^21]; and that other, *Pursue peace with all, and sanctification, without which no one will be able to see God*[^22]? For I greatly wonder if those who think the ungodly will see God, and that God was seen by the devil, go so far as to assert that the latter are pure in heart, and pursue peace and sanctification with all.

**5. 16.** For as to what the Lord says, *Whoever has seen me has seen the Father also*[^23], this, considered a little more attentively, can seem not to be contrary to what he said: *No one has ever seen God*[^24]. For he did not say, Because you have seen me, you have seen the Father also; but by saying, Whoever has seen me has seen the Father also, he wished to show the unity of substance of the Father and the Son, lest they be thought to be in any way unlike each other. And through this, since it was truly said, Whoever has seen me has seen the Father also, surely, since no man has ever seen God, neither is anyone to be thought to have seen the Father, nor the Son according to that which is God and Son, and with the Father one God. For according to that which he is man, he was certainly seen on earth and lived among men.

**6. 17.** But it is a great question how it is not contrary that so many of the ancients saw God, if no one has ever seen God, whom no man has seen nor can see. You see how difficult a question you have set before me, from which you wished me to write something at length and copiously, on the occasion of a brief letter of mine, which seemed to you to need to be explained more carefully and fully. Are you willing, then, to attend to what I have meanwhile found among others, distinguished expounders of the divine Scriptures, as to what they thought about the vision of God, in case perhaps they may satisfy your desire, although you perhaps already know them? Attend, then, to these few things, if you please. When the blessed Ambrose, bishop of Milan, in expounding the Gospel, had come to that place where the angel appeared in the temple to the priest Zechariah, see, on this occasion, how many and what great things he discoursed even about the vision of God.

**6. 18.** "Not without reason," he says, "is an angel seen in the temple, since the coming of the true priest was now being announced, and the heavenly sacrifice was being prepared, in which the angels would minister. And he is well said to have appeared to one who suddenly caught sight of him. This in particular divine Scripture is wont to hold, whether of angels or of God: that what cannot be foreseen is said to appear. For thus you have it: *God appeared to Abraham at the oak of Mamre*[^25]. For because he is not perceived beforehand, but is seen by a sudden sight, he is recorded as appearing. For sensible things are not seen in the same way as he in whose will it lies to be seen, and whose nature it is not to be seen but to be seen by will. For if he wills not, he is not seen; if he wills, he is seen. For God appeared to Abraham because he willed it; to others he did not appear, because he willed not. He was also seen by Stephen, when he was being stoned by the people, the heaven being opened; Jesus too was seen standing at the right hand of God, and was not seen by the people. Isaiah saw the God of hosts, but another could not see, since he appeared to whom it pleased him. And why do we speak of men, when even of the heavenly Virtues and Powers themselves we have read that *no one has ever seen God*[^26]? And he adds that he who is beyond the heavenly Powers, *the only-begotten Son, who is in the bosom of the Father, he himself has made him known*[^27]. Either, then, it must of necessity be granted, if no one has ever seen God the Father, that the Son was seen in the Old Testament; and let the heretics cease to give a beginning, from the Virgin, to him who was seen before he was born of the Virgin. Or certainly it cannot be refuted that the Father, or the Son, or indeed the Holy Spirit—if there is, however, a vision of the Holy Spirit—is seen in that form which the will has chosen, not which nature has fashioned; since we have received that the Spirit too was seen in a dove. And therefore no one has ever seen God, because the fullness of divinity that dwells in God no one has beheld, no one has comprehended with mind or eyes. For *he has seen* is to be referred to both. Finally, when it is added, *The only-begotten Son has himself made him known*, a vision of minds rather than of eyes is declared. For a form is seen, but a power is made known: the one is comprehended by the eyes, the other by the mind. But what shall I say of the Trinity? The Seraphim appeared when he willed it; and Isaiah alone heard his voice. The angel appeared, and is now at hand, but is not seen. For it is not in our power to see, but in his power to appear. Yet, although there is not the power of seeing, there is the grace of meriting that we may be able to see. And therefore he who had the grace merited the means: we do not merit the means, because we do not have the grace of seeing God. And what wonder if in the present age the Lord is not seen except when he wills? In the resurrection itself, too, it is not easy to see God, except for those who are pure in heart; and therefore *blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God*[^28]. How many blessed he had already numbered, and yet to these he had not promised the power of seeing God! If, then, those who are pure in heart shall see God, surely others shall not see him. For neither shall the unworthy see God; nor can he who has not willed to see God see God. Nor is God seen in a place, but in a pure heart; nor is God sought with bodily eyes, nor circumscribed by sight, nor held by touch, nor heard by address, nor perceived by his coming. And when he is thought absent, he is seen; and when he is present, he is not seen. Finally, not even all the apostles saw Christ; and therefore he says: *Have I been with you so long a time, and you still have not known me?*[^29] For he who has known what is *the breadth, and length, and height, and depth, and the love of Christ which surpasses knowledge*[^30] has seen Christ also, has seen the Father also. For we now no longer know Christ according to the flesh, but according to the spirit[^31]. For Christ the Lord is a Spirit before our face[^32], who in his mercy may deign to fill us unto all the fullness of God, that he may be able to be seen by us."

**7. 19.** If you understand these words, what remains that should be further required of me, since that question which seemed difficult is now resolved? For it has been distinguished how it was said, *No one has ever seen God*, and how the righteous men of old saw God. But the former was said because God is by nature invisible. The latter, however, saw—whoever saw God—because he appears to whom he wills, as he wills, in that form which the will has chosen, his nature still remaining hidden. For if, when the fathers saw God, his very nature appeared to them—although, had he not willed it, it certainly would not have appeared—how is it that no one has ever seen God, when, at his willing, his very nature was beheld by so many fathers? But if it is said that the Son was seen by the fathers, so that what is said—that no one has ever seen him—may be taken of God the Father, Ambrose did not indeed lose the occasion to refute thereby certain heretics, namely the Photinians, who attribute to the Son of God a beginning from the womb of the Virgin and will not believe that he existed beforehand. But because he saw others, namely the Arians, plotting more perniciously, whose error is without doubt buttressed if the Father's nature is believed to be invisible but the Son's visible, he asserted that the nature of both is one and alike invisible, adding also the Holy Spirit. This he intimated briefly indeed, but admirably, where he goes on to say: "Or certainly it cannot be refuted that the Father, or the Son, or indeed the Holy Spirit—if there is, however, a vision of the Holy Spirit—is seen in that form which the will has chosen, not which nature has fashioned." He could have said, Not which nature has shown; but he preferred to say, has fashioned, lest the very form in which God chose to appear should be thought to be fashioned out of his own nature, and from this it should certainly be concluded that he is of a convertible and changeable substance—which God himself, merciful and kind, may turn away from the faith of the devout!

**8. 20.** God, therefore, is by nature invisible, not only the Father, but the Trinity itself, one God. And because he is not only invisible but also unchangeable, he so appears to whom he wills, in whatever form he wills, that with him his invisible and unchangeable nature remains intact. But the desire of the truly devout, by which they long to see God and pantingly burn for it, is not, I think, aflame to behold that form in which he appears as he wills, which he himself is not, but rather that substance by which he is what he is. For holy Moses, his faithful servant, showed the flame of this desire of his where he said to God, with whom he spoke as a friend face to face: *If I have found grace before you, show me yourself*[^33]. What then? Was he not himself there? If it were not himself, Moses would not say to him, Show me yourself, but, Show me God. And yet, if he were beholding his nature and substance, much less would he say, Show me yourself. He himself, then, was in that form in which he had willed to appear; but he himself was not appearing in his own nature, which Moses longed to see. For that is promised to the saints in another life. Hence what was answered to Moses is true: that *no one can see the face of God and live*[^34]; that is, no one living can see him in this life as he is. For many saw, but what the will chose, not what the nature fashioned. And that which John says, if it is rightly understood: *Beloved, now we are sons of God, and it has not yet appeared what we shall be. We know that when he appears we shall be like him, for we shall see him as he is*[^35]—not as men saw him when he willed, in the form in which he willed, not in the nature in which, in himself, even when he was seen, he lay hidden; but *as he is*, which was asked of him when it was said to him, Show me yourself, by the one who spoke with him face to face.

**8. 21.** Not that anyone has ever comprehended the fullness of God, not only with the eyes of the body, but even with the mind itself.

**9. 21.** For it is one thing to see, and another to comprehend the whole by seeing. For that is seen which is in some way perceived as present; but the whole is comprehended by seeing which is seen in such a way that nothing of it lies hidden from the one seeing, or whose limits can be surveyed all around: as nothing of your present will lies hidden from you, but you can survey all around the limits of your ring. By way of example I have set two things, of which the one belongs to the gaze of the mind, the other to the bodily eyes; for sight, as he says, is to be referred to both, that is, both to the eyes and to the mind.

**9. 22.** Further, if no one has ever seen God for the reason that, as the disputant whose words we are considering says, "no one has beheld the fullness of his divinity, no one has comprehended it with mind or eyes; for *he has seen* is to be referred to both"—it remains to inquire how the angels see God, on account of what I recalled from the Gospel: *Their angels always see the face of my Father*[^36]. For if even to them he appears not as he is, but with his nature hidden, in the form in which he has willed, all the more must it be asked how we shall see him as he is, and as Moses desired when he asked that God, who was in his sight, would show himself to him. For this is promised to us as the highest reward in the resurrection: that *we shall be equal to the angels of God*[^37]; and through this, if not even they themselves see him as he is, how shall we so see him, when in the resurrection we have been made equal to them? But see what our Ambrose says consequently: "Finally," he says, "when it is added, *The only-begotten Son has himself made him known*, a vision of minds rather than of eyes is declared. For a form is seen, but a power is made known: the one is comprehended by the eyes, the other by the mind." He who a little before had said that vision is to be referred to both, now gives it not to the mind but to the eyes—not, I think, regarding his own words carelessly, but because more customarily in speaking we are wont to attribute vision to the eyes, as form to the body. For this manner of speaking too is more frequently employed in things that are contained by places and are varied by colors. But if there were no form to be beheld by the mind, that one would not be called *beautiful in form beyond the sons of men*[^38]. For this was not said according to the flesh and not according to the spiritual form. Form, then, is also spoken of as belonging to the gaze of the mind; but because it is more customarily spoken of in bodies, or in likenesses of bodies, therefore he said: "Form is seen, but power is made known: the one is comprehended by the eyes, the other by the mind." Accordingly, the Only-begotten who is in the bosom of the Father making him known by an ineffable making-known, the rational creature, pure and holy, is filled with an ineffable vision of God, which we shall then attain when we have been made equal to the angels. For just as these visible things, known by the senses of the body, are seen, *no one has ever seen God*; since if he has at any time been seen in that manner, he is not seen, like these things, by nature, but was seen by will, appearing in the form in which he willed, his nature lying hidden and remaining unchangeably in itself. But in the manner in which he is seen as he is, he is now perhaps seen by certain angels; by us, however, he will then be so seen, when we have been made equal to them.

**10. 23.** Then, when he had added that not even the Powers of the heavens, like the Seraphim, are seen except when they will and as they will, so that from this too might be inferred how great is the invisibility of the Trinity, "Yet," he says, "although there is not the power of seeing, there is the grace of meriting that we may be able to see. And therefore he who had the grace merited the means: we do not merit the means, because we do not have the grace of seeing God." By these words, then, since he is teaching not his own things but expounding the Gospel, he did not wish it to be understood that some of them will see God, but some will not see him—those to whom, believing, he gave the power to become sons of God—since to all of them belongs what was said: *We shall see him as he is*[^39]; but by saying, "we do not merit the means, because we do not have the grace of seeing God," he signified that he was speaking of this age, where to some God has deigned to appear, though not in his own nature, but in the form in which he willed—as to Abraham, as to Isaiah, and others like them; but to innumerable others, though belonging to his people and to the eternal inheritance, he shows himself by no such form at all. In the age to come, however, those who are to receive the kingdom prepared for them from the beginning will all see him with a pure heart; nor in that kingdom will there be any but such.

**11. 24.** Attend, then, to what he adds, now beginning to speak of that age: "And what wonder," he says, "if in the present age the Lord is not seen except when he wills? In the resurrection itself, too, it is not easy to see God, except for those who are pure in heart; and therefore *blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God*[^40]. How many blessed he had already numbered, and yet to these he had not promised the power of seeing God! If, then, those who are pure in heart shall see God, surely others shall not see him. For neither shall the unworthy see God; nor can he who has not willed to see God see God." You discern how circumspectly he now speaks of those who in the age to come shall see God: not all, namely, but those who are worthy. For both the worthy and the unworthy shall rise into that kingdom where God shall be seen; since *all who are in the tombs shall hear his voice and shall come forth*[^41]—but with a great difference: for *those who have done good, unto the resurrection of life; but those who have done evil, unto the resurrection of judgment*[^42]. Here "judgment" signifies eternal punishment, as that too was said: *He who does not believe is already judged*[^43].

**11. 25.** As for what holy Ambrose says, "nor can he who has not willed to see God see God," what else did he wish to be understood, except that he who does not wish to spend on the cleansing of his heart the care worthy of so great a thing does not wish to see God? See, accordingly, what he adds: "Nor," he says, "is God seen in a place, but in a pure heart." What could be said more evidently, what more expressly? From this vision, therefore, the devil and his angels, and all the ungodly with them, are excluded without any cloud of doubt, since they are not pure in heart; and through this, what is written in the book of Job, that the angels came into the sight of God, and that the devil came with them[^44], does not for that reason mean that the devil is to be believed to have seen God. For they are said to have come into the sight of God, not God into their sight. And those things come into our sight which we see, not those by which we are seen. They came, therefore, as is read in many manuscripts, to stand before God, not that God might stand before them. Nor is there need to linger over this place, that we may try, according to our powers, to show how this too comes about in time, since in the sight of God all things are always present.

**11. 26.** For now it is asked how God is seen—not in that form in which, in this age too, he willed to appear to certain people, when he spoke not only with Abraham and the other righteous, but even with Cain the fratricide—but how he is seen in that kingdom where his sons shall see him as he is. For then *their desire shall be satisfied with good things*[^45]; with which desire Moses was aflame, for whom to speak to God face to face was not enough, and who said: *Show me yourself manifestly, that I may see you*[^46]; as though he said what is sung in the Psalm out of the same desire: *I shall be satisfied when your glory shall be made manifest*[^47]. With this desire Philip too burned, and so longed to be satisfied, saying: *Show us the Father, and it is enough for us*[^48]. Speaking of this vision, that lover and longer for it, Ambrose himself, says: "Not in a place," he says, "is God seen, as at the oak of Mamre, as on Mount Sinai, but in a pure heart." And he continues, knowing what he desires, and for what he is aflame, and for what he hopes: "Nor," he says, "is God sought with bodily eyes," with which he showed himself to Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, and others in this age; "nor is he circumscribed by sight," on account of what was said: *You shall see my back parts*[^49]; "nor is he held by touch," as he even wrestled with Jacob[^50]; "nor heard by address," as he was heard not only by so many saints but even by the devil; "nor perceived by his coming," as once when *he walked in paradise toward evening*[^51].

**11. 27.** You see how the holy man strives to call our minds away from all the senses of the flesh, so as to make them fit for seeing God. And yet what does such a planter and waterer accomplish outwardly, unless God, who gives the growth, works inwardly? For who, without the help of the Spirit of God, could conceive that there is something, and something that more truly is than all that is perceived through the body, which is neither seen in a place, nor to be sought with the eyes, nor heard by address, nor held by touch, nor perceived by coming, and yet is seen—but with a pure heart? For he was not speaking of this life when he said this, since from this age, in which God appeared not as he is but in the form in which he willed, to whom he willed, he distinguished plainly enough the life of the age to come, by a most open distinction, where he says: "And what wonder if in the present age the Lord is not seen except when he wills? In the resurrection itself, too, it is not easy to see God, except for those who are pure in heart; and therefore *blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God*[^52]." From here he has begun to speak of that age where they shall see God—not all who shall rise, but those who shall rise to eternal life: not the unworthy, of whom it is said, *Let the ungodly man be taken away, lest he see the glory of the Lord*[^53]; but the worthy, of such as the Lord himself spoke, when, present, he was not seen: *He who loves me keeps my commandments*[^54]; and: *He who loves me shall be loved by my Father, and I will love him, and will show myself to him*[^55]—not those to whom it will be said, *Go into the eternal fire, which has been prepared for the devil and his angels*[^56], but those to whom it will be said, *Come, blessed of my Father; receive the kingdom prepared for you from the foundation of the world*[^57]. For the former shall go into eternal burning; but the righteous into eternal life[^58]. And what is eternal life, except what Life itself says elsewhere: *This is eternal life, that they may know you, the one true God, and Jesus Christ whom you have sent*[^59]? But in such a way as he promised that he would show himself to those who love him, with the Father one God—not in the way he was seen in the body in this age by the good and the evil.

**11. 28.** For in the future judgment too, in which he is to come in the same way he was seen going into heaven, that is, in the same form of the Son of man, those to whom he is to say, *I was hungry, and you gave me nothing to eat*[^60], shall see that same form; since *the Jews too shall see him whom they pierced*[^61]—not that form of God in which *he did not think it robbery to be equal to God*[^62]. In that form of God they shall then see him, who shall see him as he is. Nor shall they see him because they were poor in spirit in this life, because meek, because mourning, because hungering and thirsting for righteousness, because merciful, because peacemakers, because suffering persecution for righteousness' sake—although these same ones are all these things too; but because they are pure in heart. For this reason indeed, among those beatitudes, although those who have a pure heart do all those things, yet *they shall see God* is placed nowhere except where it was said, *Blessed are the pure in heart*[^63]; since with a pure heart he shall be seen who is neither seen in a place, nor sought with bodily eyes, nor circumscribed by sight, nor held by touch, nor heard by address, nor perceived by his coming. For *no one has ever seen God*[^64], either in this life as he himself is, or even in the life of the angels as these visible things are discerned by bodily vision; since *the only-begotten Son, who is in the bosom of the Father, has himself made him known*[^65]. Hence what he makes known was said to belong not to the vision of bodily eyes, but of minds.

**12. 29.** But again, lest our desire should pass over from a bodily sense to another bodily sense, that is, from the eyes to the ears, therefore, when he had said, "Nor is God sought with bodily eyes, nor circumscribed by sight, nor held by touch," he added also, "nor heard by address": so that, if we can, we may understand the only-begotten Son, who is in the bosom of the Father, to make him known in the way he is also the Word—not a sound striking upon the ears, but an image becoming known to minds—so that there, by an inner and ineffable light, what was said may grow clear: *Whoever has seen me has seen the Father also*[^66]—which was here said to Philip, when he saw and yet did not see. For the outstanding desirer of this vision, Ambrose, continues, saying: "And when he is thought absent, he is seen; and when he is present, he is not seen." He did not say, When he is absent, but, "when he is thought absent"; for he is nowhere absent who fills heaven and earth, nor is he enclosed in small spaces or diffused over great ones, but everywhere he is whole, and is contained by no place. He who understands this with a mind that surpasses itself sees God, even when he is thought absent. But he who cannot do this, let him pray and act that he may merit to be able; nor let him knock at a man, a disputant, that he may read what he has not read, but at God the Savior, that he may be able what he is not able. But as to why he said, "and when he is present, he is not seen," he opened it up consequently, saying: "Finally, not even all the apostles saw Christ; and therefore he says: *Have I been with you so long a time, and you still have not known me?*[^67]" Behold how God was present and was not seen.

**12. 30.** But why did he not dare to say, Finally, not even the apostles saw Christ, but said, "not even all the apostles," as though some of them then saw him with that vision in which he and the Father are one? Or did he perhaps attend to this, that when Peter had said, *You are the Christ, the Son of the living God*[^68], he received the answer: *Blessed are you, Simon Bar-Jona, because flesh and blood has not revealed it to you, but my Father who is in heaven*[^69]? Although whether that revelation came about in his mind through faith in so great a thing believed, or through a vision of it beheld, does not seem clear to me; since Peter himself showed himself still so very small toward him that he feared lest he lose him as he was dying, whom a little before he had confessed to be the Son of the living God, that is, the fountain of life.

**13. 31.** Then it can raise a difficulty how the very substance of God could be seen by certain people placed in this life, on account of what was said to Moses: *No one can see my face and live*[^70]—except that the human mind can be divinely caught up from this life to the angelic life before it is loosed from the flesh by this common death. For thus was he caught up *who heard there ineffable words which it is not lawful for a man to utter*[^71]; in whom there came about so great a turning away of attention from the senses of this life that, whether he was in the body or out of the body—that is, whether, as is wont to happen in a more vehement ecstasy, his mind was estranged from this life into that life while the bond of the body remained, or whether the loosing had altogether taken place, such as happens in full death—he said he did not know[^72]. Thus it comes about both that what was said is true: *No one can see my face and live*—since the mind must necessarily be drawn away from this life when it is taken up into the ineffability of that vision—and that it is not incredible that to certain saints, not yet so dead that their corpses remained to be buried, this excellence of revelation too was granted. This I think the one had in mind who did not wish to say, Not even the apostles saw Christ, but said: "not even all the apostles saw Christ"—believing that to some of them the vision even of that divinity itself, of which he was speaking, could even then be granted; certainly on account of the blessed Paul, since he too, although last, was certainly an apostle, who did not keep silent about that ineffable revelation of his.

**13. 32.** And yet to that most faithful old servant of God, Moses—who, strange to say, was still to labor in this land and still to govern that people—was granted what he asked, that he might see the glory of the Lord, he who had said: *If I have found grace before you, show me yourself manifestly*[^73]. For he received for the present a fitting answer, that he could not see the face of God, which no one could see and live; God in this way signifying that this vision belongs to another, better life. Then in the words of God the mystery of the future Church of Christ was figured. For Moses bore the type of the Jewish people, who would afterward believe in Christ who suffered; therefore it was said to him: *When I pass by, you shall see my back parts*[^74]; and the other things said there foreshadow, by a marvelous sacrament, the Church that would come after, on which it would now be long to discourse. But what I had set out to say, that even what he had asked was granted to his desire, was afterward shown in the book of Numbers; where the Lord rebukes the obstinacy of his sister, and says that to other prophets he appears in a vision and in a dream, but to Moses through a form, not through riddles[^75]; where he also added, saying: *And he saw the glory of the Lord*[^76]. What, then, is it that made him so excepted, unless perhaps because he held even then such a ruler of his people, and a servant faithful in his whole house, worthy of that contemplation, so that, as he had longed, he might see God as he is—the contemplation that is promised to all the sons at the end?

**14. 33.** Considering these things, I believe, that holy man whose words we are treating said, "not even all the apostles saw Christ," because perhaps some of them had seen him even at that time, according to what I have said. But to prove what he said, that not all saw him, he immediately added: "And therefore he says: *Have I been with you so long a time, and you still have not known me?*[^77]" Then, adding by what sort of people God is seen with that contemplation as he is: "For he," he says, "who has known what is the breadth, and length, and height, and depth, and the love of Christ which surpasses knowledge, has seen Christ also, has seen the Father also."

**14. 34.** I am wont to understand these words of the apostle Paul thus: in the breadth, the good works of love; in the length, perseverance to the end; in the height, the hope of heavenly rewards; in the depth, the inscrutable judgments of God, from which this grace comes to men; and to fit this understanding also to the sacrament of the cross, so that in the breadth is taken the transverse beam by which the hands are stretched out, on account of the signification of works; in the length, from that beam down to the earth, where the whole crucified body is seen to stand, which signifies to persist, that is, to remain with long-suffering; in the height, from the transverse beam upward to what projects toward the head, on account of the expectation of things above—lest those good works and perseverance in them be thought to be done for the sake of God's earthly and temporal benefits, but rather for the sake of that everlasting thing which faith, which works through love, hopes for from above; in the depth, however, that part of the wood which lies hidden, fixed in the secret of the earth, but from which rises up all that projects above, just as from the hidden will of God a man is called to a share in so great a grace, one in this way, another in that. As for the love of Christ which surpasses knowledge, that surely is where that peace is which surpasses all understanding[^78]. But whether that evangelical disputant also meant this in those apostolic words, or perhaps something else more fitting, you see nonetheless that this too, unless I am mistaken, does not depart from the rule of faith.

**14. 35.** But to the matter we are now treating, on account of the spiritual gaze: when he had said, "He who has known what is the breadth, and length, and height, and depth, and the love of Christ which surpasses knowledge, has seen Christ also, has seen the Father also," lest to anyone most dull he seem to speak of bodily vision, he says: "For we now no longer know Christ according to the flesh, but according to the spirit. For Christ the Lord is a Spirit before our face." What he says in this place, *we know*, he said according to the faith which is now; not according to the contemplation which will then be: for we know also that thing, whatever we hold by faith unfeigned, even if not yet by beholding through a form, yet already by believing unshakenly. Finally, when he had said that he no longer knows Christ according to the flesh, as the Apostle says, and had added the testimony from the prophet, *For Christ the Lord is a Spirit before our face*, he immediately added: "who in his mercy may deign to fill us unto all the fullness of God, that he may be able to be seen by us." Certainly it is manifest that he set down that knowledge, where he had said "we know," from faith, by which the righteous man now lives; not from the contemplation by which we shall see God as he is. For he wished this consequently for himself and for us, and signified it to be future, by saying: "who in his mercy may deign to fill us unto all the fullness of God, that he may be able to be seen by us."

**15. 36.** This fullness of God certain people understood in the Apostle's words in such a way that they thought we would become altogether what God is. For these words, as you recognize, the Apostle set down, speaking thus: *To know also the love of Christ which surpasses knowledge, that you may be filled unto all the fullness of God*[^79]. Therefore, they say, if we shall have anything less than God, and shall be in anything less than he, how shall we be filled unto all the fullness of God? But since we shall be filled, surely we shall be equal. You certainly turn away from and detest this error of the human mind, I know, and you do rightly. But how that fullness is to be understood, by which it was said that we are to be filled unto all the fullness of God, we shall afterward discuss, if God will, according to the powers he gives.

**15. 37.** But now attend diligently, by recalling what has been said, to whether what you had set before me, and which seemed difficult to explain, has been explained. For if you ask whether God can be seen, I answer: he can. If you ask how I know, I answer: because it is read in the most truthful Scripture, *Blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God*[^80], and other such things. If you ask how he was called invisible, if he can be seen, I answer that he is invisible by nature, but is seen when he wills, as he wills; for he was seen by very many, not as he is, but in whatever form it pleased him to appear. If you ask how even Cain the criminal saw him, when he was questioned and judged by him about his crime, or even the devil himself, when he came with the angels to stand before him, if the pure in heart shall see God, I answer that it does not indeed follow that those also see God who at some time hear voices made by him; for they did not see him who heard when he said to the Son, *I have both glorified it, and will glorify it again*[^81]; nevertheless it is not to be wondered at if some even not pure in heart see God in a form which his will has made, his invisible and in itself unchangeable nature lying hidden. If you ask whether he can also at some time be seen as he is, I answer that this is promised to the sons, of whom it was said: *We know that when he appears we shall be like him, for we shall see him as he is*[^82]. If you ask whence we shall see him, I answer: whence the angels see him, to whom we shall then be equal. For just as these things which are called visible are seen, *no one has ever seen God*[^83], nor can see him, since *he dwells in unapproachable light*[^84], and is by nature invisible as he is incorruptible—which the Apostle set down together, saying: *to the King of the ages, invisible, incorruptible*[^85]; for as he is incorruptible now and not afterward corruptible, so he is invisible not only now but also always. For neither is he seen in a place, but in a pure heart; nor sought with bodily eyes, nor circumscribed by sight, nor held by touch, nor heard by address, nor perceived by his coming. But the only-begotten Son, who is in the bosom of the Father, soundlessly makes known the nature and substance of the deity, and therefore shows it, even invisibly, to eyes worthy and fit for so great a sight. For these are the eyes of which the Apostle says, *the eyes of your heart enlightened*[^86]; and of which it is said: *Enlighten my eyes, lest I ever fall asleep in death*[^87]. For *the Lord is a Spirit*[^88]; whence *he who cleaves to the Lord is one spirit*[^89]. Accordingly, he who can see God invisibly, he himself can cleave to God incorporeally.

**16. 38.** I think there is now nothing further in the question you proposed for you to require. But in this whole discussion of ours, consider what you have seen, what you have believed; what you still do not know, whether because I have not said it, or because you have not understood it, or because you have not judged it to be believed. And as for those things which you have seen to be true, still distinguish whence you have seen them: whether by recalling that you saw them through the body, like heavenly or earthly bodies; or whether you have never touched them with bodily sights, but, beholding them by the mind alone, have perceived them to be true and certain, like your own will, about which I can believe you when you speak, but which I myself cannot see as it is seen by you. And when you have distinguished both these, attend also to whence you distinguish them. For although we see some things by the body, others by the mind, yet the very distinction of these two kinds is seen by the mind, not by the body; and the things which are beheld by the mind do not need any sense of the body in order that we may know them to be true. But the things which are seen through the body, unless the mind is present to take up such things reported, can be held by no knowledge; and what it is said as it were to take up when reported, it leaves outside. But it incorporeally commends their images, that is, the incorporeal likenesses of bodies, to the memory; whence, when it has willed and been able, it may judge them, as if brought forth from custody and presented to the gaze of thought. And when it is strong enough, it distinguishes even these two: what it has left outside in the bodily form, and what likeness of it it beholds within; and it discerns the former to be absent, the latter present: just as, in my absence, you think of the face of my body, and that image is present to you, but the face of which it is the image is absent; and the latter is a body, but the former an incorporeal likeness of a body.

**16. 39.** Having, then, diligently and confidently perceived and distinguished the things you see, attend to what you believe in this whole discourse, from the point at which I began to speak to you by this letter; and in those very things to which, unseen, you lend faith, weigh by distinguishing the very weights of the witnesses. For you do not believe me in the same way as Ambrose, from whose books I have set down those great testimonies. Or if you think both of us are to be believed with an equal balance, will you in any way compare us to the Gospel, or make our writings equal to the canonical Scriptures? Surely, if you are rightly wise in judging, you see that we stand far below that authority. And me indeed further below; but however much you believe either of us, you in no way compare either to that excellence. Wherefore, what is written: *No one has ever seen God*[^90]; and: *He dwells in unapproachable light, whom no man has seen, nor can see*[^91]; and: *Blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God*[^92]; and whatever else I have recalled from those holy books, you believe more firmly than what Ambrose said: "Nor is God seen in a place, nor sought with bodily eyes, nor circumscribed by sight, nor held by touch, nor heard by address, nor perceived by his coming." For such he either understood or believed God to be, who is beheld with a pure heart; and I confess that this is also my own view.

**16. 40.** You receive in faith, then, those divine words in one way, these of ours in another. For about us there is perhaps some scruple in you, lest in these divine utterances we understand something less clearly, and they be expounded by us not as they were said, but as we surmise. For perhaps you say within yourself: What if God is both seen with a pure heart and yet seen in a place? Or, What if those who are pure in heart shall see God even with bodily eyes, when this corruptible shall have put on incorruption, when we shall be equal to the angels of God? How much, then, you ought to believe us, or ought not, you perhaps do not know; and by believing us either more or less than you ought, you take heed lest you be deceived: but you do not doubt that the divine Scriptures, even when not yet clearly understood, are to be believed. But this consideration of believing or not believing, and the difficulty of knowledge, and the surges of doubt, and the devout faith owed to divine utterances—these you attend to and see in your mind, just as they are; nor do you at all hesitate about these, that they are so within you, whether as I have said, or rather as you yourself know them. You see, then, your faith, you see your doubt, you see your zeal and will to learn; and although you are moved by divine authority to believe what you do not see, you nonetheless see that you believe these things without hesitation: you distribute and distinguish all these.

**17. 41.** Will you, then, in any way compare bodily eyes to those eyes of your heart, by which you behold all these things to be true and certain, and by which you discern and distinguish things present to you invisibly? Since even concerning visible things themselves, which are as it were irradiated by the keenness of the bodily eyes, and concerning the bodily eyes themselves and their keenness, of whatever sort and however great it be, how far it stands distant from invisible things—not the more excellent ones, to which, even unseen, you owe faith, but from those I have recalled, which you do not believe as absent but behold as present by the mind—you would judge not by those same eyes of the flesh, but by those inner ones. Since, then, the inner eyes are the judges of the outer eyes, while the latter serve the former by a certain office of reporting and ministry, and the former see many things which the latter do not see, but the latter see nothing over which the former do not preside as judges, who would not place the former before the latter by an incomparable estimation?

**17. 42.** Since this is so, I beseech you: when this so great business is transacted within yourself; when you distinguish inner things from outer, and ineffably set the former before the latter; and when, leaving the latter outside, you linger inwardly among the former, and judge them by measuring them by certain incorporeal bounds of their own—do you think you are engaged in no light, or in some light? For I judge that things so great and such, so true, so clear, so certain, cannot be seen by you without light. Behold, then, that very light in which you perceive all those things, and see whether any ray of bodily eyes can approach it: surely it cannot. Attend also, and answer whether in it you see any spaces or intervals of places. You find nothing such there, I think, if you vigilantly drive away from your inmost gaze whatever images of bodies the senses of the outer man have brought in—but perhaps this is difficult. For from the habit of carnal life there rushes in, upon the inner eyes themselves, a throng of phantasms in the likenesses of bodies; striving to resist which, I cried out, at least by divine authority, grieving in that brief letter, and said: "Let the flesh, drunk with carnal thoughts, hear: *God is a Spirit*[^93]." For I curbed no one's mind more than my very own from vanity of this kind by that rebuke. For we are more easily inclined to accustomed things, and it is friendly to the weakness of the human soul to send or admit bodily conversation within, not where it may stand sound, but where it may in a manner lean or lie languid.

**17. 43.** Accordingly, if you cannot clearly clear the keenness of your mind, as it were, from the cloud of bodily likenesses, attend to those very likenesses vigilantly within yourself: behold heaven and earth by thinking of them, as you are accustomed to discern them with bodily eyes; and see that those images of heaven and earth, brought before the eyes of thought, are likenesses of bodies, not bodies. Judge, then, in this way against yourself for yourself, if you cannot in every way drive the imaginary forms of bodily qualities from the keenness of your mind; and from the very thing by which you are conquered, conquer. For there is no one, I think, even so given over to such imaginations as to believe that there are in his memory, or in the very gaze of his thought, the sun, moon, stars, rivers, seas, mountains, hills, cities, the very walls of his house, or even of his bedroom, and whatever such thing he has come to know and holds through the eyes of the body, in such a way that they either stand or move in spaces and intervals of places. Further, if the things in our mind which are most like bodies and places are nonetheless not contained by spaces and bounds of places, nor stored in our memory by local intervals, how much more do those things which bear no likeness of bodies—love, joy, long-suffering, peace, kindness, goodness, faith, gentleness, continence[^94]—hold no spaces of places, are separated by no gap, nor require any intervals of the eye of the heart through which to send its rays and see them! Are they not all in one without crowding, and known by their own bounds, without a circuit of regions? Or tell me in what place you see love; which, however, is known to you in just so far as you can behold it with the keenness of the mind: which you do not know to be great because you have surveyed by beholding some huge mass; nor, when it speaks to you within, that you may live according to it, does it resound with any sounds; nor, that you may discern it, do you raise the light of bodily eyes; nor, that you may hold it firmly, do you prepare the strength of bodily arms; nor, when it comes into your mind, do you perceive its coming.

**17. 44.** Behold, even love, however small, resides in our will and is plain to us, yet is neither seen in a place, nor sought with bodily eyes, nor circumscribed by sight, nor held by touch, nor heard by address, nor perceived by its coming: how much more God himself, whose pledge this is in us! For if our inner man, however small an image of him—not begotten of him but created by him—although it is still renewed from day to day, yet already dwells in such a light as no sense of bodily eyes approaches; if the things which in that light we behold with the eyes of the heart are both distinguished among themselves and separated by no spaces of places: how much more God, who *dwells in unapproachable light*[^95] to the senses of the body, to which there can be no approach except of a pure heart! Since, then, we have set this light before all bodily light, not only by the judgment of reason but also by the longing of love, the more we shall be able to do this, the better we shall be able, until all the languors of our soul are healed by him *who is gracious to all our iniquities*[^96]. For in this more living life, having been made spiritual, *we shall be able to judge all things, but ourselves to be judged by no one*[^97]. But *the animal man does not perceive the things that are of the Spirit of God, for it is folly to him; and he cannot know it, since it is spiritually judged*[^98].

**18. 45.** But if we cannot yet prefer the judging light to the light about which judgment is made, prefer the understanding life to the life that merely senses, prefer the nature which has not this thing in one place and that in another, but has all that it has in one place at once—such as is our own understanding—to the nature which so consists of parts that the half is less than the whole, such as is every body: then we are arguing in vain about things so great and such. But if we can now do this, let us believe God to be something better than our understanding, so that *his peace, which surpasses all understanding, may guard our hearts and our understandings in Christ Jesus*[^99]. That peace which surpasses all understanding is surely not less than our understanding, so that, while the latter is invisible to bodily eyes, the former should be thought visible. Or is the peace of God one thing and the splendor of God another? Since the same only-begotten Son, whose also is that love which surpasses knowledge, by the knowledge of which we shall be filled unto all the fullness of God, is not inferior to the light of our mind, which is granted by his illumining. Further, if this is unapproachable to the eyes of the flesh, how much more is that, which incomparably surpasses it! And through this, since there is something of ours that is visible, like this body, but something invisible, like the inner man; and since our best, that is, mind and understanding, is invisible to the eyes of the body, how shall that which is better than our best be visible to our inferior part?

**19. 46.** I think you now grant, all these things considered, that it was rightly said that God is neither seen in a place, but with a pure heart; nor sought with bodily eyes, nor circumscribed by sight, nor held by touch, nor heard by address, nor perceived by his coming. And if in these things we understand something less, or are otherwise minded, this too God will reveal to us, if we walk in that to which we have come[^100]. And we have come to believe that God is not a body but a spirit; we have come also to believe that *no one has ever seen God*[^101]; and that *God is light, and in him there are no darknesses at all*[^102]; and that *with God there is no change, nor shadow of alteration*[^103]; and that *he dwells in unapproachable light, whom no man has seen, nor can see*[^104]; and that the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit are one God, without any diversity and separation of nature; and that the pure in heart shall see him; and that *we shall be like him, for we shall see him as he is*[^105]; and that *God is love, and he who abides in love abides in God, and God abides in him*[^106]; and that we ought to *pursue peace and sanctification, without which no one will be able to see God*[^107]; and that this corruptible and mortal body of ours shall in the resurrection be changed and shall put on incorruption and immortality; and that *it is sown an animal body, it shall rise a spiritual body*[^108], the Lord *transfiguring the body of our lowliness, that he may make it conformed to the body of his glory*[^109]; and that *God made man to his own image and likeness*[^110]; and that *we are renewed in the spirit of our mind unto the knowledge of God, according to the image of him who created us*[^111]. Walking by faith in these and such authorities of the holy Scriptures, those who, by an understanding given or divinely aided, have advanced spiritually, and have been able *to compare spiritual things with spiritual*[^112], have seen that it is better to see with the mind than with the body; and that those things are seen with the mind which are not contained by places, nor separated among themselves by intervals of places, nor less in a part than in the whole.

**19. 47.** Hence it is that he confidently says that God is neither seen in a place, but with a pure heart; nor sought with bodily eyes, nor circumscribed by sight, nor held by touch, nor heard by address, nor perceived by his coming. Whence, because both his substance is commended in the holy Scriptures as invisible, and he is found in those same authorities to have been seen by very many through the body and in bodily places, or in spirit, by which bodily images are discerned through some likeness of a body—incorporeal though it be—as in dreams or in ecstasy, that holy man set apart the nature of God from visions of this kind, and said that these were such as the will of God had chosen, not such as nature had fashioned. For God makes those by which, as he wills, to whom he wills, when he wills, he may appear, his own substance lying hidden and remaining unchangeably in itself. For if our will, abiding in itself and hidden without any change of itself, expresses through voices by which it somehow shows itself, how much more easily can God almighty, his own nature lying hidden and remaining unchangeable, appear in whatever form he has willed, to whomever he has willed—he who created all things out of nothing, and, abiding in himself, makes all things new!

**20. 48.** But for that vision by which we shall see God as he is, he admonished that hearts must be cleansed. For because bodies, by the custom of speech, are called visible, therefore God is said to be invisible, lest he be believed to be a body: not because he will defraud pure hearts of the contemplation of his substance; since this great and highest reward is promised to those who worship and love God, the Lord himself saying, when he was appearing visibly to bodily eyes and promising that he, invisible, would be beheld by pure hearts: *He who loves me shall be loved by my Father, and I will love him, and will show myself to him*[^113]. For this nature of his is equally with the Father invisible, just as it is equally incorruptible: which, as was said above, the Apostle set down continuously, commending the divine substance as far as he could to men by preaching. Whether bodily eyes will behold it in the resurrection, the quality of bodies being changed, let those see who can establish this; the view of that one moves me more, who attributes this, not even in the resurrection itself, to bodily eyes, but to pure hearts.

**21. 49.** And concerning the quality of the spiritual body, which is promised to those who will rise, I do not refuse either to learn something still, or to inquire: provided that, in disputing about this matter, we can be free of those faults which are often stirred up by human zeal and contention, while *one is puffed up, beyond what is written, for one against another*[^114]; lest, while by wrangling we seek to investigate how God can be seen, we lose that very peace and sanctification, without which no one will be able to see God—which may he turn away from our hearts, that he may render them pure for his contemplation and guard them. Yet because I do not doubt it, I do not inquire: that the nature of God is never seen in a place. Now, whether anything that is not seen in a place can be seen by the eyes of this body, I am prepared, with the peace of love, to hear from those who are able to show it by disputation, and to contribute to the common store what moves me. For there are some who presume that God himself is altogether a body, thinking that whatever is not a body is no substance at all: these I judge are to be turned away from in every way. But there are others who do not doubt at all that God himself is not a body, but who think that those who shall rise to eternal life will see God even through the body, since they hope for such a quality of the spiritual body that even that which had been flesh becomes spirit. And if this is so, how far it stands from the former opinion, and how much more tolerable this is, even if it is not true, I think is most easily judged. First, because it makes a great difference whether one is otherwise minded than the truth holds concerning the Creator or concerning the creature. Then, because the effort of a mind is somehow to be borne which wishes to turn body into spirit, not God into body. Finally, because what I said in that letter of mine about these eyes of our flesh, that they can neither now see God nor will be able then, is true even so: for it was said only of bodily eyes, that there will then be none such if the body itself shall be spirit; and through this bodily eyes will never see God, since when he is seen, spirit, not body, will see him.

**21. 50.** The whole question, therefore, has now remained about the spiritual body: in what way this corruptible and mortal puts on incorruption and immortality, and in what way it is changed from animal into spiritual. This must be treated more carefully and anxiously, especially on account of the body of the Lord himself, *who transfigures the body of our lowliness, conformed to the body of his glory, that he may be able to subject all things to himself*[^115]. For since both God the Father sees the Son, and the Son the Father, without doubt those are not to be heard who will not attribute vision except to bodies. For it is not lawful to say that the Father does not see the Son; or that, in order that he himself may see, he is clothed with a body, if sight belongs only to a body. What of the fact that at the very beginning of the world, before any form of a servant was taken up by the Son, *God saw the light, that it was good*[^116]; and the firmament, and the sea, and the dry land, and all the grass and every tree, the sun, moon, stars, the creeping things of living souls, the birds of heaven, the living soul; finally, *God saw all that he had made, and behold, it was very good*[^117]? Since Scripture repeated this so often through each of the creatures, I wonder whence that opinion was born by which it was thought that vision belongs only to bodies. From whatever manner of speaking that opinion arose, the holy Scriptures are not wont to speak so; for unless they attributed vision not only to the body but also to the spirit, and more to the spirit than to the body, they would not have called the prophets properly "Seers," who saw even future things not by the body but by the spirit.

**22. 51.** But we must take care that we dare nothing presumptuously, if we should say that the body loses, through the glory of the resurrection, not only mortality and corruptibility, but even this very thing, that it is a body, and becomes spirit. For in that way either the substance of the spirit will be doubled, if the body too will be spirit; or, if there will be one spirit of the man, in such a way that, the body being changed and turned into spirit, it is doubled neither by twinning nor by increase, nor at all augmented by the accession of any quantity, it is to be feared lest nothing else seem to be said than that bodies will not remain immortal by that change, but rather will be no bodies at all and will utterly perish. Wherefore, until by diligent inquiry, if the Lord help, it be found what, according to the Scriptures, is more probably to be thought about the spiritual body which is promised in the resurrection, let it meanwhile suffice us that the only-begotten Son, who is also the mediator of God and men, the man Christ Jesus, sees the Father just as he is seen by the Father. But let us not try to transfer to that vision of God which is promised us in the resurrection this *desire of the eyes* from this world, but let us strive with devout affection at cleansing our hearts; nor let us think of a bodily face, when the Apostle says: *We see now through a mirror in a riddle, but then face to face*[^118]; especially since the Apostle said more expressly: *Now I know in part, but then I shall know even as I am known*[^119]. If, then, we shall then know God with a bodily face, we have now been known by his bodily face: *for then I shall know*, he says, *even as I am known*. Hence who would not understand that in that place he wished to signify also that face of ours of which he speaks in another place: *But we all, with unveiled face beholding the glory of the Lord, are transformed into the same image from glory to glory, as by the Spirit of the Lord*[^120]—namely from the glory of faith to the glory of eternal contemplation? For this is what this transformation accomplishes, by which the inner man is renewed from day to day; of whom also the apostle Peter, when he admonished that it be adorned, says: *Let not that which is outward be adorned with the curling of the hair, or gold, or pearls, or costly clothing; but that hidden man of the heart, who is rich before God*[^121]. For in that face in which the Jews have a veil, by not passing over to Christ—since, when anyone passes over to Christ, the veil shall be taken away[^122]—with that face unveiled we are transformed into the same image. But he says most plainly: *A veil is placed upon their hearts*[^123]. There, then, is the face, which being unveiled, we now behold in faith, though through a mirror and in a riddle; but then face to face.

**23. 52.** If you approve these things, hold with me the view of the holy man Ambrose, now confirmed not by his authority but by truth itself. For it does not please me for the reason that the Lord freed me from error chiefly through his mouth, and through his ministry granted me the grace of saving baptism, as though I favored my planter and waterer overmuch; but because on this matter he too said this, which to one who thinks devoutly and understands rightly is spoken also by him who gives the growth, God. "In the resurrection itself, then, it is not easy to see God, except for those who are pure in heart; and therefore *blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God*[^124]. How many blessed he had already numbered, and yet to these he had not promised the power of seeing God! If, then, those who are pure in heart shall see God, surely others shall not see him. For neither shall the unworthy see God; nor can he who has not willed to see God see him. Nor is God seen in a place, but with a pure heart; nor is God sought with bodily eyes, nor circumscribed by sight, nor held by touch, nor heard by address, nor perceived by his coming. And when he is thought absent, he is seen; and when he is present, he is not seen. Finally, not even all the apostles saw Christ; and therefore he says: *Have I been with you so long a time, and you still have not known me?*[^125] For he who has known what is the breadth, and length, and height, and depth, and the love of Christ which surpasses knowledge, has seen Christ also, has seen the Father also. For we now no longer know Christ according to the flesh, but according to the spirit. For Christ the Lord is a Spirit before our face, who in his mercy may deign to fill us unto all the fullness of God, that he may be able to be seen by us."

**23. 53.** These words of the holy man, which are not carnal but spiritual—in so far as you understand them, and recognize them to be true, not because he said them, but because truth cries out without noise—in so far you understand whence you may cleave to the Lord, and prepare yourself within as an incorporeal place of dwelling for him, to hear the silence of his making-known, and to see his invisible form. For *blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God*[^126]. He will not appear to them, like a body, from some interval of place; but he will come to them and make his dwelling with them[^127]: for thus they will be filled unto all the fullness of God—not when they themselves shall have become full God, but when they shall have been perfectly filled with God. But if we think only of bodies, and cannot even worthily think of that whence we may think of the bodies themselves, let us not seek what to say against our very selves, but rather, by praying away from this carnal habit and *stretching ourselves forth to the things that are before*[^128], let us cleanse our hearts. For, to say not only what blessed Ambrose said, but also what holy Jerome said: "Not only the divinity of the Father, but not even that of the Son, nor of the Holy Spirit—since there is one nature in the Trinity—can the eyes of the flesh behold, but the eyes of the mind"; of which the Savior himself says: *Blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God*[^129]. For, as the same Jerome elsewhere briefly and truly defined, an incorporeal thing is not seen by bodily eyes.

**23. 54.** These views of such great men on so great a matter I have wished to insert, not that you should judge the opinion of any man to be followed as though it were the authority of canonical Scripture, but that those who are otherwise minded may try to see with the mind what is true, and to seek God in simplicity of heart, lest they rashly censure such learned expounders of the divine utterances. Nor let it move you that it is said by some, with too little consideration: What, then, will bodily eyes see, if they shall not see God? Or will they perhaps be blind, or be without a cause? For those who say these things do not attend to this, that if there shall be no bodies, then surely there shall be no bodily eyes; but if there shall be bodies, there will be what bodily eyes can see. But let these things now be sufficiently said; which, if you consider them, diligently read and reread from the beginning of this little work of mine, you will perceive, perhaps without any doubt, that for seeing God, through his help, you ought to prepare a pure heart. But concerning the spiritual body, if the Lord help, we shall try in another work what we are able to discuss.
