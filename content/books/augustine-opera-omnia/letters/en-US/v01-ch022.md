# Letter 22

To Bishop Aurelius, from Augustine the presbyter

**1. 1.** Since for a long time, hesitating, I could not find with what grace I should answer the letter of your Holiness (for the affection of my mind has overcome everything, which affection, already rising of its own accord, the reading of your letter has roused much more ardently), I have nevertheless committed myself to God, that he might work in me according to my strength, so that I might write back those things which would suit the zeal of both of us in the Lord and the ecclesiastical care, according to your excellence and my compliance. And that first point, that you believe yourself to be aided by my prayers, I not only do not flee, but even gladly embrace. For thus, even if not by mine, at least by yours, our Lord will hear me. That brother Alypius has remained in our union, so as to be an example to the brothers who desire to avoid the cares of this world, you have received most kindly: I give thanks which I could explain in no words: may the Lord repay this into your soul. And so the whole assembly of brothers, which has begun to grow together among us, is bound to you by so great a prerogative that, though separated by such far-distant places on earth, you have taken thought for us as though most present in spirit. Wherefore we apply ourselves to prayers as much as we are able, that the Lord may deign to sustain with you the flock committed to you, and nowhere to desert you, but to be present as a helper in your opportunities, showing mercy with his Church through your priesthood, such as spiritual men, with tears and groans, intercede that he show.

**1. 2.** Know, then, most blessed lord and venerable in fullest love, that we do not despair—indeed, we hope vehemently—that our Lord and God, through the authority of the person you bear, which we trust has been laid not upon your flesh but upon your spirit, may be able to heal, with the grave sword of councils and through you, many carnal foulnesses and sicknesses which the African Church suffers in many and groans over in a few. For when the Apostle briefly set down in one place three kinds of vices to be detested and avoided, from which arises a crop of innumerable vices, one of these, which he set in the second place, is most sharply punished in the Church; but the two remaining, that is, the first and the last, seem tolerable to men, and so little by little it can come about that they are no longer even thought to be vices. For the vessel of election says: *not in reveling and drunkenness, not in debauchery and licentiousness, not in quarreling and deceit; but put on the Lord Jesus Christ, and make no provision for the flesh in its desires*[^1].

**1. 3.** Of these three, then, debauchery and licentiousness are thought so great a crime that no one who has stained himself with that sin seems worthy not only of ecclesiastical ministry but even of the communion of the sacraments themselves: and rightly so, altogether. But why this alone? For reveling and drunkenness are thought so permitted and lawful that they are celebrated even in honor of the most blessed martyrs, not only on solemn days (which itself, who that looks at these things not with the eyes of the flesh would not see is to be lamented), but even daily. If this foulness were only shameful and not also sacrilegious, we would think it must be borne with whatever strength of toleration we have. And yet where is that point, where the same Apostle, having enumerated many vices, among which he placed drunkards, so concluded as to say that with such persons we are not even to eat bread[^2]? But let us bear these things in the luxury and stain of the home, and of those banquets that are contained within private walls, and let us receive the body of Christ with those with whom we are forbidden to eat bread; let so great a disgrace be kept away at least from the sepulchres of the holy bodies, at least from the places of the sacraments, from the houses of prayer. For who dares to forbid in private what, when it is frequented in holy places, is called the honor of the martyrs?

**1. 4.** If Africa were the first to try to remove these things, she would deserve to be imitated by other lands: but since, both through the greatest part of Italy and in all or nearly all other churches across the sea, these practices—partly because they were never done, partly because, whether newly arisen or grown old, they have been extinguished and abolished by the diligence and animadversion of holy bishops who truly think about the life to come—[. . .] how can we doubt that we may amend so great a corruption of morals, even by setting so wide an example? And we indeed have a bishop from those parts, for which we give great thanks to God: although such is his modesty and gentleness, such finally his prudence and solicitude in the Lord, that even if he were an African, he would quickly be persuaded from the Scriptures that the wound which a licentious and ill-free custom has inflicted must be cured. But so great is the pestilence of this evil that it cannot be healed at all, as it seems to me, except by the authority of a council. Or if the medicine must be begun from one church, then, as it seems audacious to try to change what the Church of Carthage holds, so it is great shamelessness to wish to keep what the Church of Carthage has corrected. And for this matter what other bishop should be wished for than one who, as a deacon, abhorred these things?

**1. 5.** But what was then to be lamented must now be removed; not harshly, but, as it is written, *in a spirit of gentleness and meekness*[^3]. For your letters, the tokens of a most genuine love, give me confidence to dare to converse with you as with myself. Therefore not harshly, as I judge, not hardly, not in an imperious manner are these things removed; rather by teaching than by commanding, rather by admonishing than by threatening. For thus one must act with the multitude: but severity is to be exercised against the sins of a few. And if we threaten anything, let it be done with grief, threatening from the Scriptures the punishment to come, so that not we ourselves in our own power, but God in our discourse, may be feared. Thus first the spiritual, or those nearest to the spiritual, will be moved, by whose authority, and by admonitions most gentle indeed but most insistent, the rest of the multitude may be broken in.

**1. 6.** But since these drunkennesses and luxurious banquets in the cemeteries are usually believed by the carnal and unskilled populace to be not only honors of the martyrs but also consolations of the dead, it seems to me that this foulness and baseness could be more easily dissuaded from them, if it both be prohibited from the Scriptures, and the offerings for the spirits of those who sleep—which we must truly believe to help them somewhat—be not lavish upon the memorials themselves, and be provided to all who ask, without pride and with cheerfulness: and not sold; but if anyone wishes to offer some money, out of religion, let him distribute it on the spot to the poor. Thus they will neither seem to desert the memorials of their own—which can beget no light grief of heart—and that will be celebrated in the Church which is celebrated piously and honorably. Let these things, for the present, be said about reveling and drunkenness.

**2. 7.** But about quarreling and deceit, what need is there for me to speak, since these vices are graver not in the populace but in our own number? The mother of these diseases is pride, and greed for human praise, which often even begets hypocrisy. This is not resisted unless, by frequent testimonies of the divine Books, the fear and love of God be struck into the heart: provided, however, that he who does this offer himself as an example of patience and humility, by taking to himself less than is offered; yet, from those who honor him, by accepting neither all nor nothing, and accepting that praise or honor which is accepted not for his own sake—who ought to be wholly before God and to despise human things—but for the sake of those for whom he cannot take thought if he becomes cheap through too great a self-abasement. For to this pertains what was said: *Let no one despise your youth*[^4]; since he said this who in another place says: *If I wished to please men, I would not be a servant of Christ*[^5].

**2. 8.** It is a great thing not to rejoice in the honors and praises of men, but also to cut off all empty pomp, and, if anything necessary is retained thereof, to confer it all to the benefit and salvation of those who honor. For it was not said in vain: *God will break the bones of those who wish to please men*[^6]. For what is more languid, what so without stability and strength—which bones signify—than a man whom the tongue of evil-speakers weakens, when he knows that the things said are false? The grief of which thing would in no way tear apart the inmost depths of the soul, if love of praise did not break his bones. I presume upon the strength of your mind: and so these things which I share with you, I say to myself; yet you deign, I believe, to consider with me how grave, how difficult they are. For no one feels the forces of this enemy except one who has declared war on him; because, though it is easy for anyone to be without praise while it is denied, it is difficult not to be delighted by it when it is offered; and yet so great must be the mind's suspension toward God that, if we are praised undeservedly, we correct those whom we can, lest they think either that there is in us what is not, or that what is God's is ours, or that they praise things which, though they are not lacking in us, or even abound, are nevertheless by no means praiseworthy—such as are all the goods which we have in common either with cattle or with impious men. But if we are praised deservedly on God's account, let us congratulate those who are pleased by the true good; yet not ourselves, because we please men, but if before God we are such as they believe us to be, and it is attributed not to us, but to God, whose gifts are all things that are truly and deservedly praised. These things I sing to myself daily, or rather he sings them whose salutary precepts they are, whatever are found either in the divine Readings or are inwardly suggested to the mind; and yet, vehemently struggling with the adversary, I often receive wounds from him, when I cannot take from myself the delight of praise offered.

**2. 9.** I have written these things for this reason: that, if they are no longer necessary to your Holiness—whether because you yourself think of more things of this kind, and more useful, or because your Holiness has no need of this medicine—my evils may nevertheless be known to you, and you may know whence you should deign to ask God on behalf of my weakness: which that you may do most earnestly, I beseech you by the humanity of him who gave the precept that we bear one another's burdens[^7]. There are many things which I would bewail concerning our life and manner of living, which I would not wish to come to you through letters, if between my heart and your heart there were any means of communication other than my mouth and your ears. But if the old man Saturninus—venerable to us and most dear to us all in all sincerity, whose truly brotherly kindness and zeal toward you I perceived when I was present—should deign, when it seems opportune, to come to us, whatever we are able to converse about with his Holiness and spiritual affection will differ either not at all or not much from doing it with your Dignity. That you may deign to ask and obtain this from him along with us, I beg with such prayers as no words suffice for. For the people of Hippo vehemently and exceedingly dread my absence even at so great a distance, and in no way are willing to trust me so far as that I too should see the field which we have learned was given to the brothers by your provision and liberality, before your letter, through our holy brother and fellow servant Parthenius, from whom we have heard many other things which we desired to hear. The Lord will grant that the rest also, which we still desire, be fulfilled.
