# Letter 68

To the truly holy and most blessed pope Augustine, Jerome, greeting in Christ

**1.** *At the very moment of the departure of our holy son Asterius the subdeacon, my kinsman, the letter of your Blessedness arrived, in which you make satisfaction that you did not send a book against my littleness to Rome. This I had not even heard was done; but copies of a certain letter, as though written to me, came here through our brother Sisinnius the deacon. In which you exhort me to sing a* palinode *about a certain chapter of the Apostle, and to imitate Stesichorus, wavering between the vituperations and the praises of Helen; so that he who, by detracting, had lost his eyes, by praising recovered them*[^1]. I confess simply to your Dignity, that although the style and your arguments *seemed to me to be yours, yet I thought I ought not rashly to trust copies of a letter; lest perhaps, being wounded by my answering, you should justly complain, that I ought first to have proved the discourse to be yours, and so to write back. There was added to the delay the long sickness of the holy and venerable Paula. For while we sit by her, long languishing, we have almost forgotten your letter, or of him who had written under your name, mindful of that little verse:* Music in mourning is an untimely tale[^2]. *And so, if the letter is yours, write openly, or send truer copies, that without any rancor of stomach we may engage in the disputation of the Scriptures, and either amend our own error, or teach that another reproved in vain.*

**2.** *But far be it from me, that I should dare to touch anything of the books of your Blessedness. For it is enough for me to prove my own, and not to carp at another's. But your prudence knows very well, that each one abounds in his own sense*[^3], and that it is of boyish boasting—which formerly young men were wont to do—to seek fame for one's own name by accusing illustrious men. Nor am I so foolish as to think myself wounded by the diversity of your explanations; because neither are you wounded, if we have felt contrary things. But this is the true reproach among friends, if, not seeing our own wallet, we consider, according to Persius, the pack of others[^4]. It remains that you love one loving you, and, a young man, do not provoke an old man in the field of the Scriptures. We have had our times, and we have run as much as we could. Now, while you run, and traverse the long spaces, leisure is owed to us: and at the same time (that I may say it with your leave and honor), lest you seem to me to have proposed something about the poets to me alone, remember Dares and Entellus[^5], and the common proverb, that a tired ox plants its foot more firmly. These things we have dictated sadly. Would that we deserved your embraces, and by mutual conference might either teach something or learn!

**3.** *Calphurnius, surnamed Lanarius, has sent me, with his accustomed rashness, his slanders, which I have learned, by his zeal, have reached Africa too. To which I have briefly answered in part; and I have sent you copies of his little book, intending to send a fuller work, when it shall be opportune, at the first opportunity. In which I took care of this, that I should wound no one's Christian reputation, but only that I might confute the lie and madness of a raving and unskilled man. Remember me, holy and venerable pope. See how much I love you, that I have been unwilling to answer even when provoked, nor do I believe that to be yours which I would perhaps reprove in another. Our common brother humbly greets you.*

[^1]: See Plato, Phaedrus 243a–b
[^2]: See Sir 22:6
[^3]: See Rom 14:5
[^4]: See Persius, Satires 4.24; cf. Matt 7:3
[^5]: See Virgil, Aeneid 5.368–484
