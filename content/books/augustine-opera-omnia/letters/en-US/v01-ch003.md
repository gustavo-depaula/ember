# Letter 3

Augustine to Nebridius

**1.** Whether I should think it the work of some flattering speech of yours, so to say, or whether the matter truly stands so, is uncertain to me. For it happened suddenly and was not sufficiently weighed how far it ought to be entrusted to belief. You are waiting to hear what this is. What do you suppose? You have nearly persuaded me, not indeed that I am happy—for that is the possession of the wise man alone—but at least that I am as it were happy: as we say a man is a man, as it were, in comparison with that man whom Plato knew; or that the things we see are round, as it were, and square, as it were, when they are far removed from those things the mind of a few can see. For I read your letter by the lamp after I had already dined; bedtime was near, but not yet sleep-time. Indeed, lying long in bed I thought with myself and held this conversation, Augustine himself with Augustine: Is it not true, as it pleases Nebridius, that we are happy? Surely not; for that we are still foolish not even he himself dares to deny. What if the happy life befalls even the foolish? A hard saying—as if folly were some small misery, or any other than folly itself. How then did it seem so to him? Or did he, on reading those little books, dare to believe me even wise? No, a joy that exults is not so rash, especially in a man whose weight of judgment we know well. This, then, is it: he wrote what he thought would be most sweet to us, because whatever I set down in those writings became sweet to him too, and he wrote rejoicing and did not heed what a rejoicing pen should be entrusted with. What if he had read the *Soliloquies*? He would have rejoiced far more abundantly, yet he would have found nothing more to call me than happy. So he quickly poured out upon me the highest name and kept nothing in reserve to assert of me more gladly. See what joy does!

**2.** But where is this happy life? Where? Where in the world? Oh, if only it were to repel the atoms of Epicurus! Oh, if only it were to know that there is nothing below except the world! Oh, if only it were to know that the extremities of the sphere rotate more slowly than its middle! And other such things that we likewise know. But as things are, how am I happy, even in any qualified way, when I do not know why so great a world exists, since the principles of the figures by which it exists in no way prevent its being as much larger as anyone might wish? Or how would it not be said to me—indeed, how would we not be forced to confess—that bodies are divided to infinity, so that from a certain base, as it were, a certain number of little bodies might rise to a certain quantity? Therefore, since no body is allowed to be the smallest, in what way are we to allow there to be a largest, beyond which nothing larger could exist? Unless perhaps that point which I once told Alypius most secretly has great force: namely, that since that intelligible number increases infinitely yet is not infinitely diminished—for it cannot be resolved beyond the monad—the sensible number, by contrast (for what else is a sensible number than the quantity of corporeal things or bodies?), can indeed be diminished infinitely, but cannot increase infinitely. And so perhaps the philosophers rightly place riches in intelligible things and poverty in sensible ones. For what is more wretched than always to be able to become less and less? What is richer than to grow as much as you wish, to go where you wish, to return when you wish, as far as you wish, and to love greatly this thing that cannot be diminished? For whoever understands these numbers loves nothing so much as the monad—and no wonder, since it is through the monad that the rest come to be loved. But still, why is the world so large? For it could have been either larger or smaller. I do not know: it is simply such. And why in this place rather than that? Nor should there be a question on a matter where, wherever it might be, there would be a question. One thing greatly troubled me: that bodies are divided to infinity. To this perhaps an answer has been given, from the contrary force of intelligible number.

**3.** But wait; let us see what this something is, whatever it is, that is suggested to the mind. Surely the sensible world is said to be the image of some intelligible world. Now it is remarkable what we see in the images that mirrors reflect: for however vast the mirrors are, they do not return images larger than even the smallest bodies set before them. But in little glasses, as in the pupils of the eyes, even if a great face presents itself, a very small image is formed in proportion to the glass. Therefore the images of bodies may be diminished if the mirrors are diminished, but they may not be enlarged if the mirrors are enlarged. Surely something lies hidden here, but now I must sleep. For I seem to Nebridius to be happy not by seeking, but perhaps by finding something. But what is that something? Is it that reasoning which I am wont to flatter as my one and only, and which delights me too much?

**4.** Of what are we composed? Of mind and body. Which of these is better? The mind, of course. What is praised in the body? I see nothing other than its beauty. What is the beauty of the body? A fitting arrangement of parts together with a certain pleasantness of color. Is this form better where it is true or where it is false? Who could doubt that it is better where it is true? Where, then, is it true? In the mind, of course. Therefore the mind is more to be loved than the body. But in what part of the mind is this truth? In the understanding and the intelligence. What is opposed to this? Sense. Are we then to resist the senses with all the strength of the mind? Clearly. What if sensible things give too much delight? Let it be brought about that they do not delight. How is this brought about? By the habit of doing without them and of seeking better things. What if the mind dies? Then either truth dies, or the intelligence is not truth, or the intelligence is not in the mind, or something can die in which there is something immortal. But our *Soliloquies* already contain the proof that none of these can happen, and we are sufficiently persuaded; yet by some habit of evils we are terrified and waver. Finally, even if the mind dies—which I see can in no way happen—nevertheless that the happy life does not consist in delight in sensible things, this is sufficiently established at our leisure. By these considerations perhaps, and such as these, I seem to my Nebridius, if not happy, at least as it were happy: let me seem so to myself as well; what do I lose thereby, or why should I begrudge a good opinion? This I said to myself; then I prayed, as was my custom, and slept.

**5.** It pleased me to write this to you. For it delights me that you thank me if I hide from you nothing of what comes to my lips; and I rejoice that I thus please you. With whom, then, may I more freely play the fool than with one whom I cannot displease? But if it lies in fortune's power for one man to love another, see how happy I am, who rejoice so much in things that come by chance, and who—I confess—long to have such goods grow upon me abundantly. Yet the truest sages, who alone may rightly be called happy, willed that the goods of fortune be neither feared nor desired—or is it 'coveted'? You shall see. And this comes in nicely. For I want you to make me aware of this conjugation. For when I join similar words, I become more uncertain. For it is *cupio* like *fugio*, like *sapio*, like *iacio*, like *capio*; but whether the infinitive is *fugiri* or *fugi*, whether *sapiri* or *sapi*, I do not know. I could attend to *iaci* and *capi*, were I not afraid that the man who proved that *iactum* and *captum* are one thing and *fugitum*, *cupitum*, *sapitum* another might catch me and toss me about for sport wherever he pleased. And as for these same three, I likewise do not know whether they are to be pronounced with the penultimate syllable long and circumflexed or short with a grave accent. I have provoked you to a longer letter; I ask that I may read you a little while longer. For I cannot say as much as it is a pleasure to read you.
