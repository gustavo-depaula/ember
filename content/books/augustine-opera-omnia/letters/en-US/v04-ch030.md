# Letter 238

AUGUSTINE, TO PASCENTIUS

**1. 1.** I had indeed wished, at your asking and insisting—that you may deign to remember it, or rather at your commanding, by the merit of your age and dignity—to confer a discourse on the Christian faith even in person, with you present, as far as the Lord granted me the means. But because after the midday meal what had pleased between us in the morning displeased you—that our words should be taken down by notaries—lest you should further say, what I hear you do not keep silent about, that I did not dare to tell you my faith, receive in this letter what you may both read, and hand over to whomever you wish for reading, and what you wish in turn to answer by writing yourself. For it is unjust that anyone should wish to judge about another, and be unwilling to be judged about himself.

**1. 2.** And concerning our past agreement, which you were unwilling to fulfill at the midday appointment, it can easily be judged which of us did not have confidence in his own faith: whether the one who wished it to be said, and feared lest he be held to it; or the one who was so unwilling that it be withdrawn from the judgment of those debating that he wished it, committed to writing, to be even committed to the memory of readers, lest anyone, whether supposing by forgetfulness, or irritated by disagreement, should say that something was either not said by one of us which had been said, or said which had not been said. For in these things those are wont to seek the hiding places of their bad defense who are more desirous of contention than of truth. But this could be said neither by you nor by me, neither about you nor about me, if you had stood in the faith of our agreement, that our words should be taken down and written; especially since you yourself, in those very words by which you pronounced your faith, as often as you repeated them, so often varied them—which I believe was done by no fraud, but by forgetfulness.

**1. 3.** For first you said: "That you believe in God the Father almighty, invisible, unbegotten, incomprehensible: and in Jesus Christ his Son, God, born before the ages, through whom all things were made; and in the Holy Spirit." Hearing which, when I had answered that nothing had yet been said by you that conflicted with my faith, and therefore, if you had written these things, I could even subscribe to them; somehow the matter was brought to this, that, a sheet having been taken, you wished to express in writing too, by your own hand, those very things you had said. And when you had given this to me to read, I noticed that you had written too little: "the Father," when you had written "God almighty, invisible, unbegotten, unborn." When I mentioned this, after no little altercation you added "the Father": and "incomprehensible" indeed, which you had said in words, you had omitted in writing; but no mention was made of this by me.

**1. 4.** Then, when I had said that I was prepared to subscribe, that those words could still be mine too, I first asked, lest what had come to mind should slip away, whether anywhere in the divine Scriptures was read, "Father unbegotten." But I did this because, in the beginning of our discourse, when Arius and Eunomius had been mentioned—not by me, but by my brother Alypius, inquiring which of them Auxentius had followed, who had been praised by you with no small commendation—you, crying out, anathematized both Arius and Eunomius; then immediately you demanded that we too anathematize the ὁμοούσιον [consubstantial], as though there were any man who was called this, as Arius and Eunomius were. Then you vehemently insisted that we show you this word in the Scriptures, and at once communicate it to us. It was answered by us that, since we spoke Latin, and that was Greek, it must first be asked what ὁμοούσιον is, and then it must be demanded that it be shown in the holy Books. You, on the contrary, repeating that very word often, and bandying it about invidiously, and mentioning that it was written in the councils of our elders, vehemently urged that we show that very word which is ὁμοούσιον in the holy Books; though we again and again called back that, since our tongue was not Greek, it must first be interpreted and expounded what ὁμοούσιον is, and only then sought in the divine writings: because even if perhaps the name itself were not found, the thing itself nevertheless would be found. For what is more contentious than, when the thing is established, to contend about the name?

**1. 5.** These things, then, because we had already conversed about them between us, after it came to this, that you should write your faith, as I mentioned, although I saw nothing in those words contrary to our faith, and therefore said I was prepared to subscribe; I asked, as I said, whether the Scripture of God contained this word, that "the Father is unbegotten": and when you had answered that it was written, I asked more insistently that you show it. Then one of those who were present, as far as can be understood, an associate of your faith, said to me: "What then? do you call the Father begotten?" I answered: "I do not." And he: "If, then, he is not begotten," he says, "surely he is unbegotten." To whom I: "You see," I say, "that it can come about that even of a word which is not in the Scripture of God, an account may nevertheless be rendered whence it is shown to be rightly said. So, then, the ὁμοούσιον too, which we were being compelled to show in the authority of the divine Books, even if we do not find the word itself there, it can come about that we find that to which this word is judged to be rightly applied."

**1. 6.** These things having been said, I attended, that I might hear, what hence seemed to you, and you said: "That it was rightly done that the unbegotten Father should not be said in the holy Scriptures, lest an injury be done to him even by such a word." "Then," I say, "an injury has just now been done to God, and that by your hand." Hearing which, you now began to confess that you ought not to have said this. But when I admonished you that, if such a word seemed to you to pertain to an injury to God, you should delete it from where you had written it; you considered, I believe, that it could be rightly said, and could be defended; and again you said: "I altogether say this." Then I repeated what I had already said, that it can so come about that even the ὁμοούσιον is found neither written in the holy Pages, and yet, said in the assertion of faith, is defended; just as we nowhere read in those Books that the Father is *unbegotten,* and yet it is defended that it must be said. Then you took from me the sheet you had given, and tore it up. And we determined that, after the midday time, notaries should be present to take down our words, and that we should treat these matters between us as carefully as we could.

**1. 7.** We came, as you know, at the appointed hour; we brought notaries; that yours too might be present, we sat down. You said your faith again, and in your words I did not hear "the unbegotten Father." I believe that you considered what had been said about it in the morning, and wished to take precaution. Then you demanded that I too say my faith. When I requested, mentioning our forenoon agreement, that you would rather deign to dictate the things you had said; then you cried out that we were preparing a calumny for you, and therefore wished to keep your words written down. What I answered there it does not please me to recall, and would that you did not remember it either. Yet I preserved the honor due to your rank, and did not reckon as a curse what I had deserved to hear not from truth but from power. Nevertheless, because I repeated those very words, so as to say with a subdued voice, "Are we then preparing a calumny for you?" I ask that you pardon me.

**1. 8.** But you, hearing these things, again repeated your faith with a clearer sound, and in your words I did not hear "God the Son"; which you had never, as often as you said it, kept silent. Here I, as modestly as I could, would ask that what had pleased between us about taking down our words should be fulfilled, and I suggested the usefulness itself from the present experience, saying that you yourself could not retain in memory your own most familiar words, since we saw that you were never able to repeat them without passing over something most necessary; how much less could those who heard us so remember our words that, if perhaps either you about mine, or I about yours, should wish to reconsider and discuss something, they would be able clearly to recall what was said or not said; in which difficulty the recitation of the notaries would easily help us. Then you said indignantly: "It would have been better that you should always hear my reputation, since you had found me far inferior to what it had boasted to you." Then I mentioned that, when we greeted you before the meal, and you had proclaimed that reputation to us, I had said that it had lied about me: here indeed you said: "That I had spoken truly." Accordingly, since two things diverse about me have spoken to you—one my reputation, and another I myself—I ought surely to rejoice the more that I, rather than it, was found truthful. But because it is written: *God alone is truthful, but every man a liar*[^1]; I fear lest I have said this too about myself rashly: for we are not truthful in ourselves, or through ourselves, when we are; but when he speaks in his servants who alone is truthful.

**1. 9.** If you recall that these things were done as I have narrated, you see how you ought not to boast among men that I did not dare to assert my faith to you; since you were unwilling to stand in the faith of our agreement; and so great a man, who, for the faith you owe to the commonwealth, do not fear the curses of provincials, for the faith you owe to Christ, fear the calumnies of bishops. Then, since you desired that even honorable men be present at our disputation, I wonder how, in that very avoidance of calumny, you fear that your words be written by notaries, and do not fear that they be heard from your mouth by most illustrious witnesses. Do you not consider that it is difficult for men to suspect that you feared any calumny from us, so that you were unwilling that your words be taken down; but that, when you had considered that you had been held to the word written by your own hand before the meal, you considered at the same time that you could not so easily delete the tablets of the notaries as easily as you tore up that little sheet? But if you say that those things were not done as they have been narrated by me, either you are deceived by forgetfulness—for I do not dare to say "you lie"—or I am either likewise deceived, or I lie. You see, then, how rightly I say that the things which are done especially about these matters ought to be taken down and written, and how rightly this very thing had pleased you too, had not the afternoon fear broken the forenoon agreement.

**2. 10.** Hear, then, now my faith: the mercy of the Lord our God is powerful, which may grant that I so speak what I have believed that I offend neither his truth nor your humanity. I profess that I believe in the almighty God the Father, and I say that he is eternal with that eternity, that is, immortality, which God alone has; this I believe also of his only-begotten Son, in the form of God; this also of the Holy Spirit, who is the Spirit of God the Father and of his only-begotten Son. But because the only-begotten Son himself of God the Father, our Lord and God Jesus Christ, *after the fullness of time came*[^2], opportune for the day of our salvation, took the form of a servant[^3], many things are said of him in the Scriptures according to the form of God, many according to the form of a servant. Of which, for example, I mention two, that the single may be referred to the single. According to the form of God he himself said of himself: *I and the Father are one*[^4]: according to the form of a servant: *For the Father is greater than I*[^5].

**2. 11.** But what is written of God: *Who alone has immortality*[^6]; and: *To the invisible, the only God, be honor and glory*[^7]; and other things of this kind, we take not of the Father only, but also of the Son, as pertains to the form of God, and of the Holy Spirit. For the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit are one God, and the only true God, and alone immortal according to an altogether unchangeable substance. For if it is said of flesh of diverse sex, *He who clings to a harlot is one body*[^8]; and of the spirit of a man, who is not what the Lord is, it is consequently written: *But he who clings to the Lord is one spirit*[^9]; how much more is God the Father in the Son, and God the Son in the Father, and God the Spirit of the Father and the Son one God, where there is no diverse nature; when for diverse things in a certain way cohering to each other there is said to be either one spirit or one body?

**2. 12.** And since for soul and body cohering to each other there is said to be one man; why is there not much more, for the Father and the Son cohering to each other, said to be one God, since they cohere to each other inseparably, in a way that body and soul do not? And since body and soul are one man, although body and soul are not one thing; why is not the Father and the Son much more one God, since the Father and the Son are one, according to that voice of the Truth: *I and the Father are one*[^10]? Likewise, since the inner man and the outer man are not one thing—for the outer is not of the same nature as the inner, because the outer, with what is called the body, is called a man, but the inner is understood in the rational soul alone—yet both together are called not two men, but one: how much more is the Father and the Son one God, since the Father and the Son are one; because they are of the same nature or substance, or, if by any other word more fittingly is said that which God is, whence it was said, *I and the Father are one*? And so, there is one Spirit of the Lord, and one spirit of man, and they are not one thing; and yet that one, when it clings to the Lord, they are not two, but one spirit: and there is one inner man, and one outer man, and they are not one thing; and yet by the connection of a natural bond both together are not two, but one man. Much more, therefore, since the Son of God says, *I and the Father are one,* there is one God the Father, and one God the Son; and yet both together are not two, but one God.

**2. 13.** For that which, in many saints called *unto the adoption of sons*[^11], coheirs of Christ, one *faith* and one *hope* and one *love*[^12] wrought, that there should be in them *one soul and one heart*[^13] toward God; this especially compels us to understand the one and the same nature of the Father and the Son—if so it must be said—of deity, so that the Father and the Son, who are one, and inseparably are one, and eternally are one, are not two gods, but one God. For those men, by the consortium and communion of one and the same nature, by which they were all men, were one: and if at some time, according to the diversities of wills and opinions, and the dissimilarities of views and morals, they were not one; yet they will be fully and perfectly one, when it has been arrived at that end, *that God may be all in all*[^14]. But God the Father and his Son, his Word, God with God[^15], are always and ineffably one; whence the more they are not two gods, but one God.

**2. 14.** But men, understanding too little why a thing is said for what reason, wish to have headlong opinions: and, the Scriptures not having been diligently scrutinized, when they seize upon the defense of any opinion, they are either never or with difficulty turned from it, while they desire to be thought learned and wise rather than to be so; the things that are said on account of the form of a servant they wish to transfer to the form of God; and again, the things that are said so that the persons may be referred to one another, they wish to be names of nature and substance. But our faith is to believe and confess the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit one God; nor yet to call him who is the Son the Father; nor him who is the Father the Son; nor to name him who is the Spirit of the Father and the Son either the Father or the Son. For by these appellations is signified that by which they are referred to one another, not the substance itself by which they are one. For both a father, when he is called so, is called only the father of some son; and a son is understood only as of some father; and a spirit, according to that by which it is referred to something, is the spirit of someone breathing, and one breathing is surely breathing a spirit.

**2. 15.** But these things are not perceived corporeally, nor are they understood in God in the customary manner: *who is able to do,* as the Apostle says, *beyond what we ask and understand*[^16]: but if to do, how much more to be? For this name which is called "spirit," not according to that by which it is referred to something, but according to that by which some nature is signified, every incorporeal nature is called spirit in the Scriptures; whence this word befits not only the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit, but every rational creature and soul. Whence the Lord says: *God is a Spirit; and therefore those who adore God must adore in spirit and truth*[^17]: it is written also: *Who makes his angels spirits*[^18]: it is said of certain men: *For they are flesh, a spirit going and not returning*[^19]: and the Apostle says: *No one knows what is done in a man, except the spirit of the man, which is in him*[^20]: likewise it is written: *Who knows whether the spirit of the sons of man ascends upward, and the spirit of the beast descends downward to the earth?*[^21] Spirit is also called in the Scriptures, according to a certain distinction, in the one soul itself of one man: whence the Apostle says: *And may your whole spirit, and soul, and body be preserved unto the day of our Lord Jesus Christ*[^22]: likewise in another place: *If I pray in a tongue, my spirit prays; but my mind is unfruitful. What then is it? I will pray with the spirit, I will pray also with the mind*[^23]. But in a certain proper way the Holy Spirit is so called, according to that by which it is referred to the Father and the Son, that it is their Holy Spirit. For according to substance, since it has once been said, *God is a Spirit*, both the Father is spirit, and the Son, and the Holy Spirit itself, and yet not three spirits, but one spirit; just as not three gods, but one God.

**2. 16.** Why do you wonder? So great is the power of peace—not any sort whatever, as it is wont to be understood, nor such as is praised in this life through the concord and love of the faithful; but that peace of God which, as the Apostle says, *surpasses all understanding*[^24]: whose, but ours, that is, of every rational creature? Wherefore, considering our weakness, and hearing the Apostle confessing and saying: *Brothers, I do not consider myself to have laid hold*[^25]; and: *He who thinks he knows something does not yet know as he ought to know*[^26]; let us, as far as we can, converse with the divine Scriptures peaceably, without contention, not, with empty and childish animosity, striving to conquer one another, but that the peace of Christ may rather conquer in our hearts[^27], as much as he has granted us to be able to perceive it even in this life: so that, considering what the same peace has wrought among brothers, of whose so many minds and many hearts it made one soul and one heart toward God[^28], we may believe with due devotion much most of all that in that *peace of God which surpasses all understanding*, the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit are not three gods, but one God; the more excellently than the soul and heart of those men was one, the more excellent that peace which surpasses all understanding is than this peace by which the *one heart and one soul toward God* of all those held together.

**2. 17.** But we call the same one the Son of man whom we call the Son of God; not however on account of the form of God, in which he is equal to God the Father, but on account of the form of a servant which he took, by which he is less than the Father[^29]. And because we call the same one the Son of man, on this account we confess the Son of God crucified too, not from the power of the divinity, but from the weakness of the humanity; not from the permanence of his own nature, but from the assumption of ours.

**3. 18.** Now then behold for a little what eloquences of the Scriptures compel us to confess one Lord God, whether we are questioned only about the Father, or only about the Son, or only about the Holy Spirit, or at once about the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit. Certainly it is written: *Hear, O Israel, the Lord your God is one Lord*[^30]; of whom do you think it was said? If only of the Father, our Lord God Jesus Christ is not the Lord: and where is that voice of him who touched and cried out: *My Lord and my God*; which Christ himself did not reprehend but approved, saying: *Because you have seen me, you have believed?*[^31] Further, if both the Son is Lord God, and the Father is Lord God, and both now two lords and two gods, how will it be true: *The Lord your God is one Lord*? Or is the Father perhaps one Lord, but the Son not one Lord, but only Lord, as there are many gods and many lords, not as that one of whom it is written: *The Lord your God is one Lord?* What, then, shall we answer the Apostle saying: *For although there are those that are called gods, whether in heaven or on earth, as there are many gods and many lords; yet for us there is one God the Father, from whom are all things, and we in him; and one Lord our Jesus Christ, through whom are all things, and we through him*[^32]? Further, if what is said of the one God the Father compels us thence to separate the Son, let them say who dare, that the Father can no longer be understood as Lord, because he says, *one* Lord, *our Jesus Christ.* For if one, surely sole: if sole, how is the Father also; unless because both he and the Father are one God and sole God, the Holy Spirit not being separated? There is, then, one God the Father, and with him the Son one God, although not with him one Father. Likewise there is one Lord Jesus Christ, and with him one Lord the Father, although not with him one Jesus Christ, as though the Father too were Jesus Christ. For this name was assumed from the dispensation of mercy and the assumed humanity.

**3. 19.** Or do you perhaps not wish what the Apostle says—*one Lord our Jesus Christ, through whom are all things*—the "one" that was said to be joined to the "Lord," but to the "through whom are all things"; so that there is understood not one Lord, but one through whom are all things, so that the Father is not the one through whom are all things, but the Father alone from whom are all things, and the Son alone through whom are all things? If it is so, then at last confess that the Father and the Son are our one Lord and God. *For who has known the mind of the Lord? or who has been his counselor? Or who has first given to him, and it will be repaid to him? For from him, and through him, and in him are all things; to him be glory*[^33]. For he did not say, "From the Father all things, and through the Son all things"; but, *from him, and through him, and in him;* whom? but the Lord, of whom he says, *For who has known the mind of the Lord?* From the Lord, then, and through the Lord, and in the Lord are all things, not one being one and another being the other, but one Lord; because he did not say, "To them be glory," but, *to him be glory.*

**3. 20.** But if anyone says that what the Apostle says, *one Lord Jesus Christ through whom are all things*[^34], is understood neither as "one Lord," nor "one through whom are all things," but one Jesus Christ, who is also called the one Jesus Christ Lord, yet not so that he is one as one Lord, but as one Jesus Christ; what will he say when he hears the same Apostle crying out: *One Lord, one faith, one baptism, one God and Father of all*[^35]? For when he here mentions God the Father, where he says, *One God and Father of all*; without doubt that which he said there, *One Lord*, whom did he wish to be understood but Jesus Christ? If, then, it pleases them, let the Father cease to be Lord, because there is one Lord Jesus Christ. But if it is absurd and impious to suppose this, let us learn to understand the unity of the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit; so that what has been said of the one sole God we may not at once be prohibited from understanding of the Son or of the Holy Spirit: because the Father indeed is not the Son, and the Son is not the Father, and the Spirit of both is not the Father or the Son; but yet both the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit are the one sole and true Lord God.

**4. 21.** For the Holy Spirit, if he were not either God or true God, our bodies would not be his temple. *Do you not know,* he says, *that your bodies are a temple within you of the Holy Spirit, whom you have from God?*[^36] And lest anyone deny that the Spirit himself is God, he immediately went on and said: *And you are not your own: for you have been bought at a great price. Glorify, therefore, and bear God in your body*[^37]—him, namely, whose temple he had foretold our bodies to be. Now this is marvelous, if it is true what I hear you say, that the Holy Spirit is so less than the Son, as the Son is less than the Father. For since our bodies are members of Christ, as the Apostle says; and likewise our bodies are the temple of the Holy Spirit, as the same Apostle says[^38], I greatly wonder how they are members of the greater, the temple of the lesser. Or does it perhaps now please to say that the Holy Spirit is greater than the Lord Jesus Christ? For that sentence too seems to favor this opinion: *For whoever says a word against the Son of man, it will be forgiven him: but whoever says it against the Holy Spirit, it will not be forgiven him, neither in this age nor in the future*[^39]. For one sins more dangerously against the greater than against the lesser: nor is it right to separate the Son of man from the Son of God, because the Son of God himself was made the Son of man, not by changing what he was, but by assuming what he was not. But far be even such impiety, that the Holy Spirit be believed greater than the Son. Let not, therefore, the things that are said as though they showed one to be greater than the other easily move us into perversity.

**4. 22.** For certain things are even said in such a way that, to men understanding too little, the Son himself seems greater than the Father. For who, when he has been asked which is greater, the true or the truth, would not rather answer that the truth is greater? For from it are true whatever things are true. But it is not so in God. For we surely do not call the Son greater than the Father; and yet the Son is said to be the truth: *I am,* he says, *the way, and the truth, and the life*[^40]. But you wish to be understood of the Father only what he says: *That they may know you the one true God, and Jesus Christ whom you have sent*[^41]; where we supply that Jesus Christ too is the true God, so that this is the sense: "That they may know you, and Jesus Christ whom you have sent, the one true God"; lest that absurdity follow, that, if Jesus Christ is for this reason not true God, because it was said to the Father, *you the one true God*; for this reason the Father be not Lord, because it was said of Christ, *one Lord*. Nevertheless, according to the perverse understanding, or rather error, God the truth is greater than God the true; because the true is from the truth: the Son, therefore, greater than the Father, because the latter is the truth, the former the true. This perversity he drives from his mind who has learned that the Father is the true God by begetting truth, not by participating: but there is not one substance of the true one begetting, and another of the begotten truth.

**4. 23.** But since the eye of the human heart is weak for contemplating these things, it comes about that it is even turbulent from contention. And when will it behold these things? Scripture says that the Son of God, our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ, is the Word of God, and the truth, and the wisdom; and men say that before the incarnation which he received from the Virgin Mary, without any assumption at all of corporeal creation, through his very nature and substance by which he is the Word of God and the wisdom of God, he was visible and corruptible, while they wish to be consistent in what they think, that it was said of the Father only: *To the invisible, the incorruptible, the only God*[^42]. I ask you, the word of a man is not visible, much less the Word of God. And now that wisdom, of which it was said: *It reaches everywhere on account of its purity*[^43]; and: *Nothing defiled runs into it*[^44]; and: *Remaining in itself, it renews all things*[^45], and whatever like things which cannot be numbered; if it is corruptible, I know not what to say, except to grieve human presumption, and to wonder at the divine patience.

**4. 24.** But since it was said of it: *For it is the brightness of the eternal light*[^46]; not even yours, as I think, now say that the light of the Father (which what is it, but his substance?) was ever without the brightness begotten by it, as these things can be believed, and somehow understood, in divine and spiritual and incorporeal and unchangeable things: for I hear that they have now corrected themselves. Or is it perhaps false that they once said this, that the Father was once without the Son, as though there were eternal light without the brightness which it begot? What, then, do we say? If the Son of God was born of the Father, the Father has now ceased to beget; and if he ceased, he began: but if he began to beget, he was at some time without the Son; but he was never without the Son, because his Son is his wisdom, which is the brightness of the eternal light. Therefore the Father always begets, and the Son is always born. Here again it must be feared lest the generation be thought imperfect, if we say not that he was born, but that he is born. Suffer with me, I beseech you, in these straits of human thought and tongue, and let us alike flee to the Spirit of God saying through the prophet: *His generation who shall declare?*[^47]

**4. 25.** This one thing I ask in the meantime, that you diligently inquire whether anywhere the divine Scripture has said of diverse substances that they are one. For if it is not found said, except of those things which are established to be of one and the same substance; what need is there that we rebel against the true and Catholic faith? But if you find this written anywhere even of diverse substances, then I shall be compelled to inquire something else, whence I may show that the Father and the Son are rightly called ὁμοούσιον. For if those who either do not know our Scriptures, or do not laboriously scrutinize them, and yet believe the Son to be of the same substance and equal to the Father, say to those who are unwilling to believe this, while they nevertheless believe that God the Father has an only-begotten Son: "Did God not wish to have an equal Son, or could he not? if he did not wish, he is envious; if he could not, he is weak; but to think either of these of God is sacrilegious": I know not whether they can find what to say, if they are unwilling to say things most absurd and most foolish.

**5. 26.** Behold, as far as I could, I have set forth my faith to you. And more things indeed could be said, and more diligently disputed; but I fear lest these very things which I have said be a burden to your occupations: yet I wished them not only to be dictated and written, but also took care that they be subscribed by my hand; which indeed I had wished even before, if what had pleased between us had been kept. But now certainly I think it ought no longer to be said by you that I feared to tell you my faith, when I have not only said it, but, written, have subscribed it, lest anyone say of me either that I said what I did not say, or did not say what I said. Do this too, if you seek judges—not those who in your presence revere your person, but those who in your writings exercise their freedom. For if you fear calumny (which I would by no means dare to say, unless you yourself had said this), you may not subscribe: for I too for this reason did not wish your name to be written in this letter of mine, lest perhaps you would have been unwilling.

**5. 27.** It is easy for anyone to conquer Augustine; you will see whether by truth or by clamor. It is not for me to say, except that it is easy for anyone to conquer Augustine: how much more that he should seem to have conquered; or, even if he does not seem to have conquered, that it nevertheless be said? This is easy: I do not want you to think it great, I do not; I do not want you to desire it as a great thing. For when men notice in this matter so huge a flame of your heart, many will rejoice that they have found an occasion to make so powerful a man a friend with a few "Bravo, bravo." I do not want to say that, if they do not favor him, or if they bring forth a contrary opinion, they will even be able to dread him as an enemy; foolishly indeed and stupidly, but yet very many men are so.

**5. 28.** Do not, therefore, attend to how Augustine, whatever sort of single man he is, is conquered; but attend rather to whether the ὁμοούσιον can be conquered—not the Greek word itself, which it is easy for those who do not understand to mock, but that which is written: *I and the Father are one*[^48]; and: *Holy Father, keep them in your name, whom you have given me; that they may be one, as we also are*[^49]. Likewise a little after: *But I do not ask for these only,* he says, *but also for those who are going to believe in me through their word: that they all may be one, as you, Father, are in me, and I in you, that they too may be one in us; that the world may believe that you have sent me. And the glory which you have given me, I have given to them, that they may be one, as we also are one: I in them, and you in me, that they may be perfected into one*[^50]. See how often he said: *may they be one, as we also are one*; yet nowhere did he say, "They and we are one"; but, *as I and you are one, so may they too be one in us*: because, just as those were of one and the same substance, whom he also wished to make partakers of the one eternal life; so of the Father and the Son it was said for this reason, "We are one," because they are of one and the same substance, and not partakers of eternal life, but principally eternal life itself. And he could have said, according to the form of a servant: "I and they are one, or, may we be one"; yet he did not say this, because he wished to show one substance of the Father and his own, and one of theirs. But if he had said: "That you and they may be one, as I and you are one"; or: "That you and I and they may be one, as I and you are one"; none of us would refuse that it could be said, "They are one," even of diverse substances. But now you see how it is not so, because he did not say so, and by often saying it he vehemently commended what he said.

**5. 29.** You find, then, in the Scriptures something one of diverse natures, as we have shown above, but there is added or supplied what is one: just as from soul and body we say, one animal, and one person, and there is or are one man. But to be said without any addition, *they are one,* if you find in the Scriptures, except of those things which are of one substance, you will most justly demand that we bring forth something else, whence we may demonstrate the ὁμοούσιον. For there are also many other things; but in the meantime think of this one, the zeal for contention laid aside, that you may have God favorable. For it is not a man's good to conquer a man, but it is good for a man that the truth conquer him willing; because it is evil for a man that the truth conquer him unwilling. For the truth itself must necessarily conquer, whether one denying or confessing. Grant pardon, if I have said anything too freely, not to your contumely, but to my defense. For I presumed upon your gravity and prudence, because you can consider how great a necessity of answering you have imposed on me; or, if I have not done even this rightly, grant pardon for this too. *To this writing dictated and reread by me, I, Augustine, have subscribed.*
