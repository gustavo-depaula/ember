# Letter 4

Augustine to Nebridius

**1.** It is altogether remarkable how, beyond my expectation, it has turned out that, when I look for which of your letters still remain for me to answer, I have found only one that still holds me a debtor—the one in which you ask that, out of all the leisure which you suppose to be ours, or which you wish were ours together, we tell you what progress we have made in distinguishing the sensible from the intelligible nature. But I do not think it hidden from you that, if a person is the more entangled in false opinions the more often and familiarly he wallows in them, this happens much more easily to the mind in the case of true things. Yet it happens gradually, as we make progress with age. For although there is a great distance between a boy and a young man, no one who is questioned daily from boyhood will at any point say that he is now a young man.

**2.** I do not want you to take this in such a way as to think that in these matters we have arrived, as it were, at a certain maturity of mind, with the strength of a firmer understanding. For we are boys—but, as the saying goes, perhaps pretty ones, and not bad. For very often, when the eyes of the mind are troubled and laden with the cares of sensible blows, that little argument so well known to you lifts them up to take breath: that the mind and the intelligence are better than the eyes and this common kind of sight—which would not be so unless the things we understand had more being than these things we discern. Whether anything is strongly opposed to this reasoning, I ask you to consider with me. Meanwhile, refreshed by it, when, having prayed to God for help, I begin to be raised both into him and into those things that are most truly true, I am sometimes filled with so great an assurance of abiding things that I marvel now and then that I need that reasoning in order to believe these things exist, which are present to me with as great a presence as each person is present to himself. You too recall it; for I confess you are more careful in this matter, lest perhaps without knowing it I still owe you a reply. For so sudden a setting-down of so many burdens, which I had once counted up, does not win my belief—although I do not doubt that you received my letters, the answers to which I do not have.
