# Letter 103

To the rightly and deservedly to-be-received and in all ways to-be-honored lord and brother Augustine, Nectarius sends greetings in the Lord.

**1.** Upon receiving the letter of Your Eminence, in which you destroyed the worship of idols and the ceremonies of the temples, I seemed to myself to hear the voice of a philosopher—not of the one whom they remember in the Lyceum of the Academy, sitting in dark corners on the ground, sunk in some deep meditation, his head drawn down to his forehead and buried in his knees, so that some calumniator, destitute of learning, might attack the excellent discoveries of others, and accuse what has been excellently asserted, while he defends nothing of his own. Rather, plainly roused by your discourse, there stood before my eyes Marcus Tullius the consular, who, having preserved the lives of countless citizens, would bear the victorious standards of the public forum, crowned with laurel, into the schools of Greece as they looked on amazed; who, breathless, would turn about that trumpet of his sonorous voice and tongue, which with the breath of just indignation he had blown against those accused of crimes and against the parricides of the republic; and who would twist back even the toga itself, unrolling the pages of its folds, imitating the appearance of the philosophers' cloaks.

**2.** Therefore, when you urged us toward the worship and religion of the most surpassing God, I gladly listened; when you persuaded me that the heavenly fatherland is to be gazed upon, I received it with thanks. For you did not seem to me to be speaking of that city which some circuit of walls confines, nor of that which the discourse of the philosophers, calling it the cosmic city, professes to be common to all; but of that which the great God and the souls that have deserved well of him inhabit and dwell in, which all laws seek by diverse ways and paths, which we cannot express in speech, though perhaps by thinking we might find it. This city, then, although it ought principally to be sought and loved, yet that one in which we were born and begotten, I do not think should be deserted: the city which first poured into us the use of this light, which nourished us, which raised us, and—to say what properly pertains to the matter—of which the most learned men report that for men who have deserved well of it, a dwelling is prepared in heaven after the death of the body, so that a kind of promotion to the heights is granted to those men who have deserved well of the cities that bore them; and that they dwell the more with God who are shown to have given safety, by their counsels or works, to their fatherland. Now as for that which you deigned to say jestingly, that our city blazes not with arms but with flames and fires, and breeds thorns more than flowers, this is no great reproach, since we know that flowers are for the most part bred from thorns. For who doubts that roses too are born from thorns, and that the very grain is hedged with a palisade of awns, so that sweet things are for the most part mingled with rough?

**3.** Finally, it was stated in the letter of Your Eminence that the Church does not demand life or blood in vengeance, but that they are most afraid of being despoiled of those things by which they set most store. But I, unless my opinion deceives me, judge thus: that it is graver to be despoiled of one's goods than to be killed. For that which, as you know, is often repeated in letters—the death of the wicked takes away all sense, but a needy life begets eternal calamity: for it is graver to live badly than to end in a bad death. This too the principle of your work indicates, by which you support the poor, relieve the sick with care, apply medicine to afflicted bodies; you do everything, in short, so that the afflicted may not feel the long duration of calamity. But as for the measure of sins, it makes no difference of what sort the sin appears to be for which indulgence is asked. For first, if repentance both grants pardon and purges the offense, surely he repents who asks, who embraces the feet; and if, as some philosophers hold, all sins are equal, indulgence ought to be common to all. Someone spoke too insolently—he sinned; someone heaped up insults or accusations—he likewise sinned; someone seized what belonged to others—it is counted among offenses; someone violated profane or sacred places—he is not to be set apart from indulgence. Finally, there would be no room for pardon unless sins preceded it.

**4.** Now, since I have answered not as much as I ought, but as much as I could—more or less, as it is said—I beg and beseech you (would that I could do so in your presence, so that you might also see my tears) to consider again and again who you are, what you profess, what you do; to attend to what the appearance of that city is, from which men are dragged out to be led to punishment; what the lamentation is of mothers, of wives, of children, of parents; with what shame those who are set free, but tortured, can come back to their fatherland; what griefs or groans the consideration of wounds and scars renews. And when all these things have been pondered, consider first God, and think of the good repute of men, of goodness which is friendly and of familiar association, and seek to win praise by forgiving rather than by avenging. And let these things be said about those whom the true guilt of their own confession binds. To them indeed, in regard for the law—which I do not cease to praise—you have granted pardon. But how great a cruelty it is can scarcely be set forth, to attack the innocent and to call into a capital trial those whom it is agreed are distinct from the crime. If it happens that they are cleared, consider, I beg, from how great an odium their accusers will be freed, when, defeated, they have voluntarily dismissed the accused and left the innocent alone. May the most high God guard you, and preserve you as the safeguard of his law and our ornament.
