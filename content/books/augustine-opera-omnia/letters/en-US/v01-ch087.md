# Letter 87

TO THE DESIRABLE AND BELOVED BROTHER EMERITUS, AUGUSTINE.

**1.** When I hear that someone endowed with a good natural gift, and learned in the liberal disciplines—although the salvation of the soul is not placed there—nevertheless thinks otherwise than the truth demands in a most easy question, the more I marvel, the more I burn to know the man and to converse with him; or, if I cannot do that, at least by letters, which fly very far, to touch his mind, and to be touched by him in turn. As I hear that you are such a man, and I grieve that you are torn off and shut out from the Catholic Church, which, as was foretold by the Holy Spirit, is spread through the whole world—for what cause, I do not know. For it is certain that to a great part of the Roman world—not to say also to the barbarian nations, to whom too the Apostle said he was a debtor[^1], whose communion of Christian faith is woven together with ours—the party of Donatus is unknown; nor do they at all know either when or for what causes that dissension arose. All of whom, surely, all Christians, unless you confess them to be innocent of those crimes which you cast at the Africans, you will be compelled to say are liable to all evil deeds—since among you the ruined, to put it more mildly, lie hidden, so that you all are polluted. For you do not expel anyone from your communion; or you first expel him only when he has first done that for which he is to be expelled. Or do you not condemn one who lay hidden for some time, and was afterward betrayed and convicted? I ask, therefore, whether he contaminated you during the time when he lay hidden? You will answer: In no way. He would therefore contaminate you at no time, even if he should always lie hidden; for even of the dead, many things committed are often betrayed, nor is it a fraud to the Christians who communed with them while living. Why, then, have you cut yourselves off, by so rash and sacrilegious a sundering, from the communion of innumerable Eastern Churches, from which what was done—or which you teach or feign was done—in Africa always lay hidden, and still lies hidden?

**2.** For it is another question, whether you say true things—which indeed we convict to be false by much more probable documents, and which we declare those very things which you cast at us were then proved rather among your own. But this, as I said, is another question, to be taken up and discussed then, when there is need. Let now the vigilance of your mind attend to this: that no one can be contaminated by the unknown crimes of unknown men. Hence it is manifest that you have been separated, by a sacrilegious schism, from the communion of the world, to which the crimes—whether false or true—which you charge against the Africans are utterly unknown and always have been unknown; although this too must not be kept silent: that even known wicked men do not harm the good in the Church, if either the power to prohibit them from communion is lacking, or some reason of preserving peace hinders it. For who are those who, in the prophet Ezekiel, deserved both to be marked before the devastation of the lost, and to escape unharmed when those were devastated[^2], except—as is most plainly shown there—those who mourn and groan over the sins and iniquities of the people of God which are done in their midst? But who groans and mourns over what he does not know? From the same reasoning the apostle Paul too tolerates false brothers. For he does not say of unknown ones: *For all seek their own, not the things of Jesus Christ*[^3]; whom nevertheless he shows to have been with him. But of what kind are those who chose to offer incense to idols, or to hand over the divine books, rather than die, except of those who seek their own, not the things of Jesus Christ?

**3.** I pass over many testimonies of the Scriptures, lest I make a letter longer than is necessary, and I leave more to be considered by your erudition through yourself. But what is enough, see, I beg: if so many iniquitous men among one people of God did not make those who witnessed against them such as they themselves were—if that multitude of false brothers did not make the apostle Paul, set in one Church with them, one who sought his own, not the things of Jesus Christ—it is manifest that a man is not made this, that some wicked one is, with whom one approaches the altar of Christ, even if he be not unknown, provided only he be not approved, and be separated by a good conscience displeased with him. It is manifest, therefore, that to concur with a thief[^4] is nothing other than either to steal with him, or to receive his theft by the assent of the heart. This we say, in order to remove infinite and superfluous questions about the deeds of men, which in no way impede our reasoning.

**4.** But you too, unless you think this, will all be such as Optatus was in your communion, with you not ignorant of it: which far be it from the morals of Emeritus and of others such as he, of whom I do not doubt there are among you, far estranged from his deeds. For we cast at you nothing but the crime of schism, which you have made even a heresy by evilly persevering. But at how great a price this crime is reckoned by the divine judgment, read what I do not doubt you have read. You will find Dathan and Abiron devoured by the gaping of the earth, and all the rest, who had consented to them, consumed by a fire arising from their midst[^5]. That crime, then, the Lord God marked with a present punishment, as an example to be avoided, so that, while he most patiently spares such men, he might show what he reserves for the last judgment. For we do not reprehend your reasonings, if at that time when Optatus was boasting that he raged with insane power—when the accuser of him was the groaning of all Africa, with you groaning together, if indeed you are such as fame proclaims you, which God knows I both believe and wish—we do not, then, reprehend if at that time, lest he drag many excommunicated along with him, and cut off your communion by the fury of schism, you were unwilling to excommunicate him. But this very thing is what convicts you in the judgment of God, brother Emeritus, that, when you saw it was so great an evil for the party of Donatus to be divided that Optatus was thought rather to be tolerated in communion than that should be allowed, you remain in that evil which was perpetrated by your forebears in dividing the Church of Christ.

**5.** Here perhaps, in the narrowness of replying, you will try to defend Optatus. Do not, brother, do not, I beg; it does not become you; and if perhaps it becomes some other—if indeed anything becomes the wicked—it certainly does not become Emeritus to defend Optatus. But perhaps not to accuse him either. Be it so, then. Use a middle course of speech, and say: *Each one bears his own burden*[^6]. *Who are you to judge another's servant*[^7]? If, then, at the testimony of all Africa—indeed, of all lands wherever the fame of Gildo was seething—for he too was known—you never dared to judge about Optatus, lest you rashly judge about unknown things, can we, or ought we, at your testimony alone, to pass a rash sentence about unknown things concerning those who lived before us—so that it is too little that you charge us with unknown things, unless we too judge unknown things? For you defend not Optatus, even if perhaps he is imperiled by a false ill will, but yourself, when you say: I do not know what sort of man he was. How much more, then, does the Eastern world not know what sort of men the Africans were, whom you accuse though more unknown? From which Churches, nevertheless—whose names you have in the books and recite—you are sundered by a wicked dissension. If your most notorious and most ill-famed bishop of Thamugadi was unknown not to, I will not say, the colleague at Caesarea, but at Sitifis, of his own time, how could the Church of the Corinthians, Ephesians, Colossians, Philippians, Thessalonians, Antiochenes, of Pontus, Galatia, Cappadocia, and the other parts of the world built up in Christ by the Apostles, either know the African traditors, whoever they were, or deserve to be condemned by you, because it could not? And yet you do not commune with them, and you say they are not Christians, and you try to rebaptize them. What shall I say? What shall I lament? Or what shall I cry out? If I speak with a sensible man, I share with you the stings of this indignation. For you surely see what I would say, if I wished to say it.

**6.** Or did your forebears perhaps hold a council among themselves, and condemn the whole Christian world besides themselves? Has the estimation of things been brought to this: that the council of the Maximianists, who were cut off from your cutting-off, because compared with you they are very few, has no force against you; but your council has force against the nations, the inheritance of Christ, and his possession, the ends of the earth[^8]? I wonder whether he has blood in his body, who does not blush about this matter. Write back to these things, I beg: for from some, whom I could not but believe, I have heard that you would write back, if I wrote to you. I have indeed sent one letter even before; whether you received it or answered it—and perhaps I did not receive yours—I do not know. Now meanwhile I ask that it not be burdensome to you to answer these things, what seems good to you. But do not lift yourself into other questions; for here is the head of the most orderly inquiry: why the schism was made.

**7.** For even the earthly powers, when they persecute schismatics, defend themselves by that rule by which the Apostle says: *He who resists the power resists the ordinance of God; and those who resist acquire judgment for themselves. For rulers are not a terror to the good work, but to the evil. Do you wish not to fear the power? Do good, and you will have praise from it; for it is God's minister to you for good. But if you do evil, fear: for it does not bear the sword in vain; for it is God's minister, an avenger in wrath upon him who does evil*[^9]. The whole question, then, is whether schism is no evil, or whether you have not made a schism, so that you resist the powers for a good work, not for an evil one, whence you acquire judgment for yourselves. For this reason the Lord most providently did not say: *Blessed are those who suffer persecution* only; but he added, *for the sake of righteousness*[^10]. If, then, it is righteousness which you have worked in that dissension, in which you also remain, according to what I said above, I desire to know from you. But if it is iniquity to condemn the Christian world unheard—either because it did not hear what you heard, or because what you rashly believed was not proved to it, or you accused without certain documentation—and therefore to wish to rebaptize so many Churches of the Lord himself, founded by the preaching and toil of his Apostles while he was yet here in the flesh: because it is permitted you either not to know your African colleagues, wicked men living together, together handling the Sacraments, or even to know, but to tolerate them, lest the party of Donatus be divided; but to those set in a most remote world, it is not permitted not to know what you either know, or believe, or have heard, or feign about the Africans? What so great perversity is this, to embrace one's own iniquity, and to accuse the severity of the powers?

**8.** But indeed, it is not permitted to Christians to persecute even the wicked. Be it so, let it not be permitted; but is it lawful to cast this very thing at the powers ordained for this very purpose? Or shall we delete the Apostle? Or do your codices not have what I recalled a little before? But you, you will say, ought not to commune with such men. What then? Did you not commune with Flavianus, once vicar, a man of your party, because, serving the laws, he killed the guilty whom he had found? But, you will say, by you the Roman princes are roused against us. No, rather by you against yourselves, you who have dared both to mangle by cutting-off the Church—of which they, as was foretold so long before, are now members (for it was said of Christ: *And all the kings of the earth will adore him*[^11])—and persist in daring to rebaptize. But our people, against the unlawful and private violences of yours—which you too there, who do not do such things, grieve and groan over—seek protection from the ordained powers; not that they may persecute you, but that they may defend themselves; just as the apostle Paul, against the Jews conspiring to kill him[^12], before the Roman empire was Christian, acted so that protection even of armed men might be given him. But those princes, learning by whatever occasion of the wickedness of your schism, establish against you, by their solicitude and power, what they will. For they do not bear the sword in vain; for they are ministers of God, avengers in wrath upon those who do evil[^13]. Finally, even if some of our people do these things without Christian moderation, it displeases us; but yet we do not on their account abandon the Catholic Church, if we cannot purge it of the chaff before the final time of winnowing[^14]—since you too, on account of Optatus, when you did not dare to expel him, did not abandon the party of Donatus.

**9.** But indeed you say: Why do you wish us to be joined to you, if we are wicked? Because you still live, and can be corrected if you will. For when you are joined to us, that is, to the Church of God, the inheritance of Christ, whose possession are the ends of the earth[^15], you are corrected so as to live in the root. For of the broken branches the Apostle says thus: *For God is able to graft them in again*[^16]. You, then, change from that part in which you dissented; although the Sacraments which you had, since they are the same in all, are holy. Wherefore we wish you to be changed from your perversity, that is, that your cutting-off be rooted anew. For the Sacraments which you have not changed, as you have them, are approved by us; lest perhaps, when we wish to correct your depravity, we do a sacrilegious injury to those mysteries of Christ which were not depraved in your depravity. For neither had Saul depraved the anointing which he had received; to which anointing the king David, the pious servant of God, showed so great an honor[^17]. For this reason, therefore, we do not rebaptize you, because we desire to restore the root to you; yet the form of the cut-off shoot, if it has not been changed, we approve—which, however, though whole, is in no way fruitful without the root. It is another question about persecutions, which you say you suffer amid so great gentleness and mildness of ours, while yours truly do worse things unlawfully and privately; another about Baptism, which we do not ask where it is, but where it profits. For wherever it is, it is itself; but he who receives it is not also, wherever he is, himself. And so we detest the private impiety of men in schism; but the baptism of Christ we venerate everywhere: for if deserters carry over the emperor's standards with them, those men being either punished by condemnation or corrected by indulgence, the standards are received back safe, if they have remained safe. And if anything is to be inquired into more diligently about this matter, it is, as I said, another question. For this is to be observed in these matters, what the Church of God observes.

**10.** But it is asked whether yours or ours is the Church of God. Wherefore that must be sought from the head: why you made the schism. If you do not write back, I have, as far as I believe, an easy case before God; because to a man whom I have heard—apart from the schism—to be good and liberally instructed, I sent peacemaking letters. You will see what you answer to him, whose patience is now to be praised, but whose sentence at the end is to be feared. But if you write back with that care with which you see has been written to you, the mercy of God will be present, so that at some time the error which sunders us may perish both by the love of peace and by the reason of truth. Remember that I have said nothing about the Rogatists, who are said to call you Firmians, just as you call us Macarians. Nor about your bishop of Rusiccade, who is reported to have bargained with Firmus for the safety of his own people, so that the gates might be opened to him, and the Catholics given over to devastation, and other innumerable things. Cease, therefore, by commonplaces to exaggerate the deeds of men, whether heard or known. For you see what things of yours I keep silent, so that the matter may be dealt with concerning the origin of the schism, where the whole case lies. May the Lord God inspire in you a peaceful thought, desirable and beloved brother.
