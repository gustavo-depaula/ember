# Letter 9

Augustine to Nebridius

**1.** Although you know my mind, perhaps you do not know how much I wish to enjoy your presence. But God will at some time grant this so great a benefit. I read your most correct letter, in which you complained of solitude and of a certain abandonment by your friends, with whom life is most sweet. But what else am I to say to you here, except what I do not doubt you do? Betake yourself to your own mind, and lift it up into God as much as you can. For there you hold us too more surely—not through corporeal images, which we must now use in our recollection, but through that thought by which you understand that we are not together in a place.

**2.** When I considered your letters, in which I answered great questions for you who asked them—and there is no doubt of this—that question greatly terrified me in which you ask how it comes about that certain thoughts and dreams are introduced into us by the higher powers or by demons. For it is a great matter, which you too with your prudence perceive must be answered not by a letter, but either by a conversation in person or by some book. Nevertheless I shall try, knowing your talent, to sow beforehand certain glimmers of light upon this question, so that you may either weave the rest together yourself, or at least not despair that one can arrive at a probable investigation of so great a matter.

**3.** For I think that every motion of the mind produces something in the body. And this reaches even our senses, dull and slow as they are, when the motions of the mind are greater—as when we are angry, or sad, or joyful. From which one may conjecture that, even when we think something and it does not appear to us in our own body, it can nevertheless appear to airy or ethereal living beings, whose sense is most keen, and in comparison with which ours is not to be thought a sense at all. Therefore those traces, so to speak, of its own motion which the mind fixes in the body can both remain and produce a certain disposition, as it were; and these, when they have been secretly stirred and handled according to the will of the one stirring and handling them, bring upon us thoughts and dreams; and this happens with wonderful ease. For if it is manifest that the exercises of our earthly and most sluggish bodies—in playing musical instruments, or on the tightrope, and in countless other spectacles of this sort—have reached certain incredible feats, it is by no means absurd that those who do something in bodies with an airy or ethereal body, which by the natural order they penetrate, use far greater ease in moving whatever they wish, without our perceiving it, and yet our suffering something from it. For we do not perceive how an abundance of bile drives us to more frequent anger; and yet it does drive us, though this very abundance, which I mentioned, has been produced by our being angry.

**4.** But if you do not wish to accept this comparison from me in passing, turn it over in thought as much as you can. For if some difficulty in doing and fulfilling what it desires constantly arises in the mind, it is constantly angry. Now anger, as far as my opinion goes, is a turbulent appetite for removing the things that hinder ease of action. And so for the most part we are angry not only at men, but at the pen in writing, and we dash it down and break it; and dice-players at the dice, and painters at the brush, and anyone at any instrument by which he judges himself to suffer difficulty. By this constancy of being angry, even the physicians affirm that the bile grows. And by the increase of bile we are in turn easily angered, and with almost no causes present. Thus what the mind has produced in the body by its own motion will avail in turn to stir the mind itself.

**5.** These matters can be treated very fully, and brought by many testimonies of things to a surer and fuller understanding. But to this letter add that one which I recently sent you about images and memory, and treat it more carefully: for it appeared to me from your reply that it was understood by you less than fully. To this letter, then, which you are now reading, when you have added what was said there about a certain natural faculty of the mind that by thought diminishes and enlarges anything whatever, perhaps it will no longer move you how it comes about that even forms of bodies which we have never seen are figured among us, whether in thinking or in dreaming.
