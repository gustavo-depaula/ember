# Letter 115

To the most blessed and reverently most dear lord, brother and fellow priest Fortunatus, and to the brothers who are with you, Augustine sends greetings in the Lord.

**1.** Your Holiness knows well Faventius, who was the leaseholder of the Paratiana estate. He, fearing I know not what for himself from the owner of that same property, fled for refuge to the church of Hippo; and there he was, as those who flee for refuge are accustomed, waiting how, through our intercession, he might settle his affairs. As often happens, day by day less and less anxious, and as it were secure, his adversary having ceased, when he was coming out from a supper with his friend, he was suddenly carried off by a certain Florentinus, an official of the Count, as they say, by a band of armed men, as great as seemed to them sufficient for this deed. When this was announced to me, and it was still unknown by whom he had been carried off, yet there was suspicion about the man whom, fearing, he was protecting himself by means of the church, I at once sent to the tribune who is set over the guarding of the shore. He sent soldiers: no one could be found. But in the morning we learned both in what house he had been, and that after cockcrow he had departed with the man who had held him. I sent there too, where he was said to have been led away: where the aforesaid official, found, was unwilling to grant to the priest whom I had sent even that he might see him. The next day I sent a letter, asking that there be granted him what the Emperor commanded in such cases—that is, that those who have been ordered to be produced be questioned before the municipal records, whether they wish to spend thirty days under moderate custody in that city, to set their affairs in order or prepare their expenses: thinking surely that during those days we might perhaps be able to end his cause by friendly negotiation. But by now he has been led off, having set out with that official: and there is fear lest perhaps, brought to the office of the Consular, he suffer something bad. For he has a case with a most moneyed man, although the integrity of the judge is proclaimed by most illustrious fame. Lest, nevertheless, money should anywhere prevail in the office, I ask Your Holiness, most beloved lord and venerable brother, that you deign to hand my letter to the honorable and to us most dear Consular, and read it to him; for I judged it not necessary to set forth the same cause twice; and that he defer the hearing of his case, since I do not know whether he is guilty or innocent in it. And as to the fact that the laws were not kept concerning him, that he was carried off in this way, nor, as the Emperor commanded, brought to the municipal records to be questioned whether he wished to accept the benefit of the delay—let him not despise this; so that through it we may be able to end the matter with his adversary.
