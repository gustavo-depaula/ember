# Letter 75

To the truly holy and most blessed pope Augustine, Jerome sends greeting in Christ

**1. 1.** *Three letters at once, or rather brief little books, of your Dignity I have received through the deacon Cyprian, containing diverse, as you name them, questions, but, as I feel, reproaches of my little works: to which, if I should wish to answer, there will be need of the size of a book. Yet I will try, as much as I can, not to exceed the measure of a rather long letter, and not to make delay for the hastening brother, who, three days before he was about to set out, demanded letters from me; so that, as it were on the point of marching, I was compelled to blurt out these things, such as they are, and to answer with a tumultuary discourse, not with the maturity of a writer, but with the rashness of one dictating: which for the most part turns out not to teaching, but to chance; as sudden wars throw into confusion even the bravest soldiers, and they are compelled to flee before they can snatch up their arms.*

**1. 2.** *But our armor is Christ, and the instruction of the apostle Paul, who writes to the Ephesians*: Take up the arms of God, that you may be able to resist in the evil day[^1]. *And again*: Stand, your loins girt in truth, and clothed with the breastplate of righteousness, and your feet shod for the preparation of the Gospel of peace: above all taking up the shield of faith, with which you may be able to extinguish all the fiery darts of the evil one; and take the helmet of salvation, and the sword of the Spirit, which is the word of God[^2]. *Armed once with these weapons, king David went forth to battle; and taking five smooth stones from the torrent*[^3], he showed that there was nothing of roughness and filth in his senses amid the whirlwinds of this age, drinking from the torrent in the way; and therefore, his head exalted[^4], he cut off the most proud head of Goliath with that man's own blade, striking the blasphemer on the forehead[^5], and wounding him in that part of the body in which the presumer upon the priesthood, Uzziah, is struck with leprosy[^6]; and the holy man glories in the Lord saying: The light of your countenance is signed upon us, Lord[^7]. *Let us therefore say, we too,* My heart is ready, O God, my heart is ready; I will sing and make melody in my glory. Arise, psaltery and lyre; I will rise at dawn[^8]: *that in us may be fulfilled,* Open your mouth, and I will fill it[^9]; *and,* The Lord will give the word to those who preach the Gospel with much power[^10]. *You too I do not doubt to pray, that amid us contending the truth may conquer. For you do not seek your own glory*[^11], but Christ's; and when you conquer, I too conquer, if I understand my error; and on the contrary, when I conquer, you overcome, because not sons store up treasure for parents, but parents for sons[^12]. And in the book of Chronicles we read that the sons of Israel went forth to fight with a peaceful mind, even amid the very swords and sheddings of blood, and the corpses of the slain, thinking not of their own, but of peace's victory. Let us therefore answer all things, and resolve the manifold questions, if Christ bids, in a brief discourse. I pass over the offices of greeting, with which you stroke my head; I keep silent about the flatteries with which you strive to console your reproach of me: I will come to the very causes.

**2. 3.** You say that you received my book from a certain brother, which had no title, in which I had enumerated the ecclesiastical Writers, both Greek and Latin; and that when you asked him, to use your words, why the opening page was not inscribed, or by what name it was reckoned, he answered that it was called *Epitaphium*: and you argue that it would rightly be so called, if you had read there only the lives or writings of those who were already dead; but since the works of many, both at the time when it was written, and even now living, are recalled, you wonder why I imposed this title on it. I think your prudence understands that from the work itself you could understand the title. For you have read both the Greeks and the Latins who described the lives of illustrious men, that they never wrote an *Epitaphium* for this work, but "On Illustrious Men," for example, generals, philosophers, orators, historians, poets, epic, tragic, comic. But an *Epitaphium* is properly written for the dead: which indeed I know I once did on the falling-asleep of the presbyter Nepotian, of holy memory. Therefore this book is to be called either "On Illustrious Men," or properly "On Ecclesiastical Writers"; although by very many unskilled correctors it is said to be inscribed "On Authors."

**3. 4.** *In the second place you ask why I said, in the commentaries on the Epistle to the Galatians, that Paul could not reprove in Peter that which he himself had done*[^13], nor accuse in another the pretense of which he himself was guilty; and you assert that the apostolic reproof was not a matter of dispensation, but true, and that I ought not to teach a lie, but that all the things which are written sound as they are written. To which I first answer, that your prudence ought to have remembered the little preface of my commentaries, saying in my person: "What then? Am I foolish and rash, who promise that which he could not? By no means: nay rather, in this, as it seems to me, more cautious and more timid, that, feeling the weakness of my strength, I followed the commentaries of Origen. For that man wrote properly five volumes on the Epistle of Paul to the Galatians, and filled the tenth book of his Stromata with a concise discourse on the explanation of it; he also composed various treatises and excerpts, which alone could even suffice. I pass over my Didymus the Seeing, and the Laodicean lately gone out from the church, and Alexander, an old heretic, also Eusebius of Emesa, and Theodore of Heraclea, who too left some little commentaries on this matter. From which, if I should pick even a few things, there would be made something which would not be utterly despised. And so, to confess simply, I read all these, and heaping up very many things in my mind, a notary being summoned, I dictated either my own or another's things, mindful neither of order, nor of words sometimes, nor of meanings. It is now of the Lord's mercy, that through our inexperience things well said by others not perish, and not please among strangers, which please among their own." If, therefore, you thought anything worthy of reproof in our explanation, it would have been of your learning to inquire whether the things we wrote were held in the Greeks, so that, if they had not said them, then you might condemn my opinion properly; especially since I freely confessed in the preface that I had followed the commentaries of Origen, and dictated either my own or another's things, and at the end of that same chapter which you reprove, I wrote: "If this sense pleases anyone—by which neither is Peter shown to have sinned, nor Paul to have impudently accused a greater man—he ought to set forth by what consequence Paul reproves in another that which he himself committed." By which I showed that I do not defend definitely that which I had read in the Greeks, but expressed the things I had read, that I might leave it to the judgment of the reader, whether they were to be approved or disapproved.

**3. 5.** *You, then, lest you should do what I had asked, found a new argument, to assert that the Gentiles who had believed in Christ were free from the burden of the Law; but that those who believed from the Jews were subject to the Law: so that, through the person of each, Paul both rightly reproved those who kept the Law, as the teacher of the Gentiles; and Peter was rightly reproved, who, the prince of the circumcision*[^14], commanded the Gentiles that which only those who were of the Jews ought to have observed. If this pleases—nay rather, because it pleases, that whoever believe from the Jews are debtors to do the Law[^15]—you, as a bishop most well known in the whole world, ought to promulgate this opinion, and draw all your fellow bishops into agreement with you. I, in my little hut, with monks, that is, my fellow sinners, do not dare to determine about great matters, except to confess this ingenuously, that I read the writings of the elders, and in commentaries, according to the custom of all, set down various explanations, that out of many each one may follow what he wishes. Which indeed I think you too have read and approved, both in secular literature, and in the divine Books.

**3. 6.** *But this explanation, which first Origen, in the tenth book of his Stromata, where he interprets the Epistle of Paul to the Galatians, and the rest of the interpreters thereafter followed, they bring in for this cause especially, that they may answer Porphyry the blasphemer, who charges Paul with impudence, that he dared to reprove Peter, the prince of the Apostles, and to accuse him to his face, and to constrain him with reason, that he had done amiss—that is, that he was in that error in which was the very man who accused another of erring. What shall I say of John, who long ago in the pontifical rank ruled the Church of Constantinople, and properly wrote a very full book on this chapter, in which he followed the opinion of Origen and the ancients? If, therefore, you reprove me erring, permit me, I beg, to err with such men; and when you perceive that I have many companions of my error, you ought at least to produce one supporter of your truth. So much for the explanation of one chapter of the Epistle to the Galatians.*

**3. 7.** *But lest I seem to strive against your reasoning by the number of witnesses, and, on the occasion of illustrious men, to flee the truth, and not dare to join hands, I will briefly set forth examples from the Scriptures. In the Acts of the Apostles, a voice was made to Peter saying,* Rise, Peter, kill and eat*, that is, all the animals of the four-footed, and the serpents of the earth, and the birds of heaven*[^16]. By which saying it is shown that no man is by nature polluted, but all are equally called to the Gospel of Christ. To which Peter answered: Far be it, for I have never eaten anything common and unclean. And a voice came to him from heaven a second time, saying: What God has cleansed, do not you call common[^17]. *And so he went to Caesarea, and entering to Cornelius,* opening his mouth, he said: In truth I have learned that God is not a respecter of persons; but in every nation, he who fears him and works righteousness is acceptable to him[^18]. *Finally* the Holy Spirit fell upon them, and the faithful of the circumcision who had come with Peter were astonished, that on the Nations too the grace of the Holy Spirit had been poured out. Then Peter answered: Can anyone forbid water, that these should not be baptized? The Apostles and brothers who were in Judea heard that the Nations too had received the word of God. But when Peter had gone up to Jerusalem, those who were of the circumcision disputed against him, saying: Why did you go in to men having uncircumcision, and eat with them?[^19] *To whom, every reason being set forth, at last he concluded his discourse with this saying*: If, then, God gave them the same grace as to us who believed in the Lord Jesus Christ; who was I, that I could forbid God? Hearing these things, they were silent; and glorified God saying: Therefore to the Nations too God has given repentance unto life[^20]. *Again, when, a long time after, Paul and Barnabas had come to Antioch, and, the Church being gathered, had related* how great things God had done with them, and that God had opened to the Nations the door of faith[^21]; certain ones, coming down from Judea, taught the brothers and said: Unless you be circumcised according to the custom of Moses, you cannot be saved. A sedition therefore not small being stirred against Paul and Barnabas, they resolved to go up*, both those who were accused, and those who accused*, to the Apostles and presbyters at Jerusalem about this question. And when they had gone to Jerusalem, certain ones of the heresy of the Pharisees, who had believed in Christ, rose up, saying: It is right to circumcise them, and to charge them to keep the Law of Moses. And when a great question arose about this word, Peter*, with his accustomed freedom:* Men*, he says,* brothers, you know that from ancient days God chose among us, through my mouth, that the Nations should hear the word of the Gospel and believe; and God who knows hearts bore testimony, giving them the Holy Spirit, as to us also, and made no distinction between us and them, purifying their hearts by faith. But now why do you tempt God to impose a yoke upon the neck of the disciples, which neither our fathers nor we could bear? But through the grace of our Lord Jesus Christ we believe we are saved, as they too. And all the multitude was silent[^22], *and the apostle James, and all the presbyters together, went over to his opinion.*

**3. 8.** *These things ought not to be burdensome to the reader, but useful both to him and to me, that we may prove that, before the apostle Paul, Peter was not ignorant—nay rather, was the chief author of this decree, that the Law was not to be kept after the Gospel. Finally, Peter was of so great authority, that Paul wrote in his Epistle*: Then, after three years, I came to Jerusalem to see Peter, and remained with him fifteen days[^23]. *And again in what follows*: After fourteen years I went up again to Jerusalem with Barnabas, taking Titus also with me. But I went up according to revelation, and set forth to them the Gospel which I preach among the Nations[^24]: *showing that he did not have security of preaching the Gospel, unless it had been strengthened by the opinion of Peter and those who were with him. And immediately it follows*: But privately to those who seemed to be something, lest perhaps I should run, or had run, in vain[^25]. *Why privately, and not in public? Lest perhaps a scandal of faith should arise to the faithful of the number of the Jews, who thought the Law was to be kept, and so to believe in the Lord and Savior. Therefore at that time too, when Peter had come to Antioch (although the Acts of the Apostles do not write this, but Paul affirming it is to be believed), Paul writes that he withstood him to his face, because he was reprehensible. For before certain ones came from James, he ate with the Gentiles; but when they had come, he withdrew himself and separated, fearing those who were of the circumcision. And the other Jews consented with him, so that even Barnabas was led by them into that pretense.* But when I saw*, he says,* that they did not walk rightly toward the truth of the Gospel, I said to Peter before all: If you, being a Jew, live like a Gentile and not like a Jew, how do you compel the Gentiles to live like Jews?[^26] *and the rest. To no one, therefore, is it doubtful that the apostle Peter was the first author of this opinion, of which he is now charged as a transgressor. But the cause of the transgression is the fear of the Jews. For the Scripture says that first he ate with the Gentiles; but when certain ones had come from James, he withdrew himself and separated, fearing those who were of the circumcision*[^27]. But he fears the Jews, whose apostle he was, lest on the occasion of the Gentiles they should withdraw from the faith of Christ, and he, the imitator of the good shepherd, should lose the flock entrusted to him.

**3. 9.** *As, then, we have shown that Peter felt rightly indeed about the abolition of the Mosaic Law, but was compelled by fear to a pretense of observing it; let us see whether Paul himself, who accuses another, did any such thing. We read in the same book:* But Paul went through Syria and Cilicia, confirming the Churches: and he came to Derbe and Lystra; and behold, there was a certain disciple there, by name Timothy, the son of a believing Jewish woman, by a Gentile father. To him those who were at Lystra and Iconium, the brothers, bore testimony. Him Paul wished to go forth with him; and taking him, he circumcised him on account of the Jews who were in those places: for all knew that his father was a Gentile[^28]. *O blessed apostle Paul, who in Peter had reproved the pretense by which he withdrew himself from the Gentiles for fear of the Jews who had come from James; why are you compelled, against your own opinion, to circumcise Timothy, the son of a Gentile man, surely himself also a Gentile (for he was not a Jew, who had not been circumcised)? You will answer me: On account of the Jews who were in those places. Whoever, then, pardons yourself in the circumcision of a disciple coming from the Gentiles, pardon Peter too, your predecessor, that he did some things for fear of the believing Jews. Again it is written:* But Paul, when he had still endured many days, bidding the brothers farewell, sailed to Syria, and with him Priscilla and Aquila: and he shaved his head at Cenchreae; for he had a vow[^29]. *Be it so, that there he was compelled by fear of the Jews to do what he did not wish; why did he let his hair grow by a vow, and afterward shave it at Cenchreae according to the Law, which the Nazirites who vow themselves to God are wont to do, according to the precept of Moses*[^30]?

**3. 10.** *But these are small in comparison with the matter which follows. Luke, the writer of the sacred history, relates:* When we had come to Jerusalem, the brothers received us gladly[^31]; *and the following day James and all the elders who were with him,* his Gospel being approved, said to him: You see, brother, how many thousands there are in Judea who have believed in Christ, and all these are zealots for the Law. But they have heard about you that you teach a departure from Moses to those Jews who are among the Nations, saying that they ought not to circumcise their sons, nor to walk according to the custom. What then? Surely the multitude must come together; for they have heard that you have arrived. Do, therefore, this which we tell you. There are with us four men having a vow upon them; taking these, sanctify yourself with them, and spend on them that they may shave their heads; and all will know that the things they have heard about you are false, but that you yourself also walk keeping the Law. Then Paul, taking the men, the next day purified with them, entered into the temple, announcing the fulfilment of the days of purification, until an offering should be offered for each one of them[^32]. *O Paul, in this too I again ask you; why did you shave your head, why did you go barefoot according to the ceremonies of the Jews, why did you offer sacrifices, and victims be sacrificed for you according to the Law? Surely you will answer: Lest those who had believed from the Jews be scandalized. You pretended, then, to be a Jew, that you might gain the Jews; and this very pretense James and the other presbyters taught you: but yet you could not escape. For a sedition being stirred, when you were to be killed, you were snatched away by the tribune, and sent by him to Caesarea, under the careful guard of soldiers, lest the Jews should kill you as a pretender and destroyer of the Law; and whence, coming to Rome, in the lodging which you had rented for yourself, you preached Christ both to Jews and to Gentiles: and your opinion was confirmed by the sword of Nero*[^33].

**3. 11.** *We have learned that, on account of fear of the Jews, both Peter and Paul equally feigned that they kept the precepts of the Law. With what brow, then, with what audacity, does Paul reprove in another that which he himself committed? I—nay rather, others before me—set forth the cause which they thought, not defending an officious lie, as you write, but teaching an honorable dispensation; that they might both demonstrate the prudence of the Apostles, and check the impudence of the blaspheming Porphyry, who says that Peter and Paul fought between themselves in a boyish contest; nay, that Paul burned with envy of the virtues of Peter, and wrote boastfully the things which he either did not do, or, if he did, did impudently, reproving in another that which he himself had committed. They interpreted as they could; how do you unfold this passage? Surely you are about to say better things, who have reproved the opinion of the ancients.*

**4. 12.** *You write to me in your letter:* "*For you need not be taught by me how that is to be understood which the same Apostle says:* I became to the Jews as a Jew, that I might gain the Jews[^34], *and the other things which are said there, out of the compassion of mercy, not the pretense of deceit. For it is done as a sick man who ministers to a sick man; not when he lies that he has a fever, but when, with the mind of one suffering along, he thinks how he himself would wish to be served, if he were ill. For he was surely a Jew; but, made a Christian, he had not abandoned the Jewish sacraments, which that people had received fittingly, and at the lawful time when it was right: and therefore he undertook to celebrate them, when he was now an apostle of Christ, in order to teach that they were not ruinous to those who wished to keep them, as they had received them from their parents through the Law, even when they had believed in Christ; provided they did not now place in them the hope of salvation, since through the Lord Jesus salvation itself, which was signified by those sacraments, had arrived." The sense of your whole discourse, which you have drawn out in a most lengthy disputation, is this: that Peter did not err in thinking that the Law was to be kept by those who had believed from the Jews; but in this he deviated from the line of right, that he compelled the Gentiles to live like Jews. But he compelled them not by the command of one teaching, but by the example of his conduct. And that Paul did not speak contrary to the things which he himself had done; but rather why Peter should compel those who were of the Gentiles to live like Jews.*

**4. 13.** *This, then, is the sum of the question, nay rather of your opinion: that, after the Gospel of Christ, believing Jews do well if they keep the commandments of the Law; that is, if they offer the sacrifices which Paul offered, if they circumcise their sons, if they keep the Sabbath, as Paul did in Timothy, and all the Jews observed. If this is true, we fall into the heresy of Cerinthus and Ebion, who, believing in Christ, were anathematized by the fathers for this alone, that they mingled the ceremonies of the Law with the Gospel of Christ, and so confessed the new things that they did not let go the old. What shall I say of the Ebionites, who pretend to be Christians? Even today, through all the synagogues of the East among the Jews, there is a heresy which is called that of the Minei, and is even now condemned by the Pharisees, whom the common folk call Nazarenes, who believe in Christ the Son of God, born of the Virgin Mary; and they say that he is the one who suffered under Pontius Pilate, and rose again, in whom we too believe: but while they wish to be both Jews and Christians, they are neither Jews nor Christians. I beg you, then, you who think my little wound is to be healed—which was bored, nay rather, pricked, as it is said, with a needle—to heal the wound of this opinion, which has been struck with a lance, and, so to speak, with the mass of a heavy javelin. For it is not the same crime, in the explanation of the Scriptures to set down the diverse opinions of the elders, and again to introduce a most wicked heresy into the Church. But if this necessity weighs upon us, that we receive the Jews with their legal observances, and it shall be permitted them to observe in the churches of Christ that which they practiced in the synagogues of Satan, I will say what I feel: they will not become Christians, but they will make us Jews.*

**4. 14.** *For what Christian would patiently hear that which is contained in your letter: "Paul was a Jew; but, made a Christian, he had not abandoned the Jewish sacraments, which that people had received fittingly, and at the lawful time when it was right: and therefore he undertook to celebrate them, when he was now an apostle of Christ, in order to teach that they were not ruinous to those who wished, as they had received them from their parents through the Law, to keep them." Again I beseech you, that with your peace you hear my grief. Paul observed the ceremonies of the Jews, when he was now an apostle of Christ, and you say "they were not ruinous to those who wished to keep them, as they had received them from their parents." I, on the contrary, will speak, and, the world crying out against it, with free voice will pronounce, that the ceremonies of the Jews are both ruinous and deadly to Christians; and that whoever observes them, whether from the Jews or from the Gentiles, has been rolled down into the abyss of the devil*. For Christ is the end of the Law, unto righteousness to everyone who believes, namely Jew and Gentile[^35]: *for it will not be the end unto righteousness to everyone who believes, if the Jew is excepted. And in the Gospel we read:* The Law and the Prophets were until John the Baptist[^36]. *And in another place:* Therefore the Jews sought the more to kill him, because he not only broke the Sabbath, but also said that God was his Father, making himself equal to God[^37]. *And again:* Of his fullness we all received, and grace for grace, because the Law was given through Moses; but Grace and truth came through Jesus Christ[^38]. *For the grace of the Law which passed away, we received the grace of the Gospel which abides; and for the shadows and images of the old Instrument, the truth came through Jesus Christ. Jeremiah too prophesies in the person of God*: Behold, the days come, says the Lord, and I will accomplish for the house of Israel and the house of Judah a new testament; not according to the testament which I disposed for their fathers, in the day when I took their hand, to lead them out of the land of Egypt[^39]. *Observe what he says, that not to the people of the Gentiles, with whom he had not before made a Testament, but to the people of the Jews, to whom he had given the Law through Moses, he promises the new Testament of the Gospel; that they may by no means live in the oldness of the letter, but in the newness of the Spirit*[^40]. But Paul, over whose name the question is now ventilated, sets down frequent opinions of this kind; of which, for brevity's sake, I will subjoin a few. Behold, I Paul say to you that if you be circumcised, Christ will profit you nothing[^41]. *And again*: You are emptied of Christ, you who are justified in the Law; you have fallen from grace[^42]. *And below:* If you are led by the spirit, you are no longer under the Law[^43]. *From which it appears that he who is under the Law, not by way of dispensation, as our elders wished, but truly, as you understand, does not have the Holy Spirit. But what sort the legal precepts are, let us learn by God's teaching.* I*, he says,* gave them precepts not good, and ordinances in which they should not live by them[^44]. *These things we say, not because, after Manichaeus and Marcion, we destroy the Law, which we know to be both holy and spiritual according to the Apostle*[^45]; but because, after faith came and the fullness of times, God sent his Son, made of a woman, made under the Law, that he might redeem those who were under the Law, that we might receive the adoption of sons[^46]; and that we may by no means live under a tutor[^47], but under the adult and Lord, the heir.

**4. 15.** *It follows in your letter: "He did not on that account amend Peter, that he observed the ancestral traditions; which, if he wished to do, he would do neither mendaciously nor unfittingly." Again I say: you are a bishop, a teacher of the Churches of Christ, that you may prove what you assert to be true, receive some one of the Jews, who, made a Christian, circumcises the son born to him, who observes the Sabbath, who abstains from the foods which God created to be used with thanksgiving*[^48], who on the fourteenth day of the first month kills a lamb toward evening; and when you have done this—nay rather, when you have not done it (for I know that you are a Christian, and will not do a sacrilegious thing)—willing or unwilling, you will reprove your own opinion; and then you will know that it is harder by deed to confirm one's own than to reprove another's. And lest perhaps we should not believe you, or rather should not understand what you say (for often a discourse drawn out at length lacks understanding; and while it is not perceived, it is less reproved by the unskilled), you inculcate and repeat: "This, then, of the Jews Paul had abandoned, which they had as evil." What is the evil of the Jews which Paul had abandoned? Surely that which follows: because, being ignorant*, he says,* of the righteousness of God, and wishing to establish their own, they were not subject to the righteousness of God[^49]; *then, that after the passion and resurrection of Christ, the sacrament of grace being given and made manifest according to the order of Melchizedek*[^50], they still thought the old sacraments were to be celebrated not from the custom of solemnity, but from the necessity of salvation; which, however, if they had never been necessary, the Maccabees would have become martyrs for them fruitlessly and emptily: finally, that the Jews persecuted the preachers of grace, the Christians, as enemies of the Law. These and such errors and vices he says he reckoned loss and as dung, that he might gain Christ[^51]."

**4. 16.** *We have learned through you what evils of the Jews the apostle Paul abandoned; again, by your teaching, let us learn what good things of theirs he kept. "The observances of the Law," you will say, "which they celebrate after the ancestral manner, as they were celebrated by Paul himself, without any necessity of salvation." What you wish to say by "without any necessity of salvation" I do not sufficiently understand. For if they do not bring salvation, why are they observed? But if they are to be observed, surely they bring salvation; especially those which, observed, make martyrs. For they would not be observed, unless they brought salvation. For they are not indifferent between good and evil, as the philosophers dispute. Continence is good, luxury is evil; between the two, indifferent: to walk, to digest the dung of the belly, to cast forth from the nostrils the purgings of the head, to throw out rheum by spitting: this is neither good nor evil; for whether you do it or do not do it, you will have neither righteousness nor unrighteousness. But to observe the ceremonies of the Law cannot be indifferent; but it is either evil or good. You say good; I assert evil; and evil not only to those who believed from the Gentiles, but also to those who believed from the Jewish people. In this passage, unless I am mistaken, while you avoid one thing, you roll into another. For while you fear the blaspheming Porphyry, you run into the snares of Ebion, decreeing that the Law is to be observed by those who believe from the Jews. And you, who understand that what you say is perilous, again try to temper it with superfluous words: "Without any necessity of salvation, as the Jews thought they were to be celebrated, or with the deceitful pretense which Paul had reproved in Peter."*

**4. 17.** *Peter, then, feigned the keeping of the Law; but this reprover of Peter boldly observed the legal things. For it follows in your letter: "For if he celebrated those sacraments for this reason, that he pretended to be a Jew, that he might gain them, why did he not also sacrifice with the Gentiles, since to those too who were without the Law he became as one without the Law, that he might gain them too*[^52]; unless because the one thing he did as a Jew by nature, and all this he said, not that Paul might feign himself to be what he was not, but that he might feel that they must be mercifully so helped, as if he himself labored in the same error; not, of course, by the cunning of one lying, but by the affection of one suffering along." You defend Paul well, that he did not feign the error of the Jews, but truly was in error; nor wished to imitate Peter lying, so as, for fear of the Jews, to dissimulate what he was; but with all freedom said that he was a Jew. A new clemency of the Apostle! while he wishes to make the Jews Christians, he himself became a Jew. For he could not lead back the luxurious to frugality, unless he had proved himself luxurious, and mercifully, as you yourself say, help the wretched, unless he himself felt himself wretched. For truly wretched, and mercifully to be bewailed, are they who, by their contention and love of the abolished Law, made the apostle of Christ a Jew. Nor is there much difference between my opinion and yours, because I say that both Peter and Paul, for fear of the believing Jews, practiced—nay rather, feigned—the commandments of the Law; but you assert that they did this clemently, "not by the cunning of one lying, but by the affection of one suffering along": provided this be established, that either by fear or by mercy they feigned themselves to be what they were not. But that argument which you use against us, that he ought to have become a Gentile to the Gentiles too, if he became a Jew to the Jews, makes rather for us: for just as he was not truly a Jew, so neither was he truly a Gentile; and just as he was not truly a Gentile, so neither was he truly a Jew. But in this he is an imitator of the Gentiles, that he receives uncircumcision in the faith of Christ, and indifferently permits the eating of foods which the Jews condemn; not by the worship, as you think, of idols[^53]. For in Christ Jesus neither is circumcision anything, nor uncircumcision, but the observance of the commandments of God[^54].

**4. 18.** *I beg you, therefore, and again and again I implore you, to pardon my little disputation; and that, if I have exceeded my own measure, you charge it to yourself, who forced me to write back, and who, like Stesichorus, have deprived me of my eyes. Do not think me a teacher of falsehood, I who follow Christ when he says:* I am the way, and the truth, and the life[^55]. *Nor can it be that I, a worshiper of the truth, should bow my neck to a lie. And do not stir up against me the little crowd of the untaught, who venerate you as a bishop and, when you preach in the church, look up to you with the honor of the priesthood, while they make little of me, a man of advanced age, nearly decrepit, who keeps to the seclusion of the monastery and the countryside; and seek out for yourself those whom you may teach or rebuke. For to us, separated from you by such vast stretches of sea and land, the sound of your voice scarcely reaches. And if perhaps you write a letter, Italy and Rome will receive it before it is brought to me, to whom it is to be sent.*

**5. 19.** *As for what you ask in your other letters, why my earlier translation of the canonical books has asterisks and obeli marked in it, and why I afterward published another translation without these signs—with your leave I will say it—you seem to me not to understand what you asked. For that earlier translation is that of the Septuagint translators; and wherever there are obeli, that is, daggers, it is signified that the Septuagint said more than is contained in the Hebrew; but where there are asterisks, that is, shining stars, something has been added by Origen from the edition of Theodotion. And there we translated the Greek, here we expressed from the Hebrew itself what we understood, preserving the truth of the meanings rather than at times the order of the words. And I marvel how you read the books of the Septuagint translators not in their pure form, as they were published by them, but as emended—or rather corrupted—by Origen* *with obeli and asterisks, and do not follow the little translation of a Christian man; especially since he translated what was added from the edition of a Jewish and blasphemous man, made after the passion of Christ. Do you wish to be a true lover* *of the Septuagint translators? Do not read what is under the asterisks; rather, scrape it from the volumes, that you may prove yourself a partisan of the ancients. But if you do this, you will be forced to condemn all the libraries of the churches. For scarcely one book or another will be found that does not have these things.*

**6. 20.** *Further, as for your saying that I ought not to have translated after the ancients, and your use of a new syllogism: "Either the things the Septuagint translated were obscure, or they were plain. If obscure, then it must be believed that you too could be deceived in them; if plain, then it is clear that they could not be deceived in them"—I answer you with your own words. All the ancient commentators who preceded us in the Lord, and who translated the holy Scriptures, translated either obscure things or plain ones. If obscure, how have you dared to discuss after them what they were unable to explain? If plain, it is superfluous for you to have wished to discuss what could not have been hidden from them—especially in the explanation of the Psalms, which among the Greeks were translated in many volumes: first by Origen, second by Eusebius of Caesarea, third by Theodore of Heraclea, fourth by Asterius of Scythopolis, fifth by Apollinaris of Laodicea, sixth by Didymus of Alexandria. There are also passed down works by various authors on a few psalms; but now I speak of the whole body of the Psalms. Among the Latins, moreover, Hilary of Poitiers and Eusebius, bishop of Vercelli, translated Origen and Eusebius, of whom our own Ambrose also followed the former in certain places. Let your prudence answer me: why, after so many and such great translators, have you held different views in the explanation of the Psalms? For if the Psalms are obscure, then it must be believed that you too could be deceived in them; if plain, then it is not believed that they could be deceived in them; and so in either way your translation will be superfluous. And by this rule, after the earlier writers no one will dare to speak, and whatever one has taken up, another will have no license to write about it. Rather, it is the part of your humanity to grant to others the indulgence you grant to yourself. For I have not so much tried to abolish the old things, which I translated, corrected, from Greek into Latin for the men of my own tongue, as to bring into the open those testimonies that were passed over or corrupted by the Jews, so that our people might know what the Hebrew truth contains. If anyone does not care to read it, no one compels the unwilling. Let him drink the old wine with pleasure, and despise our new wines, which were published in explanation of the earlier writers, so that wherever those are not understood, they may be made clearer from ours. As for what kind of translation is to be followed in the holy Scriptures, the book I wrote On the Best Kind of Translating, and all the little prefaces that I set before the divine volumes in our edition, explain; and to these I think the prudent reader should be referred. And if, as you say, you accept me in the correction of the New Testament, and you set out the reason why you accept it—because very many who have knowledge of the Greek tongue can judge my work—you ought to have credited the same integrity also in the Old Testament, for there we did not fabricate things of our own, but translated the divine words as we found them among the Hebrews. Wherever you doubt, ask the Hebrews.*

**6. 21.** *But perhaps you will say: What if the Hebrews are unwilling to answer, or wish to lie? Will the whole throng of the Jews keep silent about my translation, and will no one be found who has knowledge of the Hebrew tongue? Or will they all imitate those Jews whom you say were found in a little town of Africa to have conspired in a slander against me? For you weave a tale of this sort in your letter:* *"A certain brother of ours, a bishop, when he had established that your translation be read in the church over which he presides, came upon something rendered by you far differently in the prophet Jonah from what had grown old in the ears and memory of all and had been chanted through the succession of so many ages. So great an uproar arose among the people, with the Greeks especially objecting and inflaming the charge of falsity, that the bishop was forced—for it was the city of Oea—to demand the testimony of the Jews. But they, whether through ignorance or malice, answered that this stood in the Hebrew codices, what both the Greeks and Latins had and said. What more? The man was compelled, as it were, to correct the error, unwilling, after great danger, to be left without his people. Hence it also seems to us that you too could sometimes have been deceived in some matters."*

**7. 22.** You say that I translated something badly in the prophet Jonah, and that, with the people clamoring in sedition over the discrepancy of a single word, the bishop nearly lost his priesthood. And you withdraw what it was that I supposedly translated badly, taking from me the occasion of my defense, lest whatever you said be dissolved by my reply—unless perhaps, as so many years ago, the gourd comes into the middle, with Cornelius and Asinius Pollio of that time asserting that I had translated "ivy" for "gourd." On this matter I have answered more fully in my commentary on the prophet Jonah. I am content now to have said only this: that in that passage, where the Septuagint translators rendered "gourd," and Aquila with the rest rendered "ivy," that is, *kissos*, in the Hebrew volume it is written *ciceion*, which the Syrians commonly call *ciceia*. It is a kind of shrub with broad leaves, like a vine-shoot; and when it has been planted, it quickly rises into a little tree, without any supports of reeds or stakes, which both gourds and ivy need, sustaining itself by its own trunk. Therefore, in expounding this word from the word itself, if I had wished to render it *ciceion*, no one would understand; if "gourd," I would be saying what is not in the Hebrew; I put "ivy," so as to agree with the other translators. But if your Jews, as you yourself assert, said through malice or ignorance that what is contained in the Greek and Latin codices stands in the volumes of the Hebrews, it is plain that they either do not know Hebrew letters, or wished to lie in order to mock the gourd-men. I ask, at the end of the letter, that you not force a resting old man and a long-since veteran to do military service and to risk his life again. You who are young, and placed on the pontifical summit, teach the peoples; and enrich the roofs of Rome with the new fruits of Africa. For me it is enough to whisper in a corner of the monastery with a poor little hearer and reader.
