# Letter 31

To the most beloved and most sincere lords, truly most blessed brothers, most excellent by the most abundant grace of God, Paulinus and Therasia, Augustine sends greeting in the Lord

**1.** Although I had wished that my letter, by which I answered your earlier one—if indeed your letter can in any way be answered by me—should come most swiftly into the hands of your Charity, so that in some way, though absent, I might quickly be with you, my own slowness brought me the gain of your letter. The Lord is good, who often does not grant what we want, in order to bestow what we would prefer. For it is one thing that you will write upon receiving my letter, another that you wrote without receiving it. Which, when we read it most joyfully, this joy would surely have been lacking to us, if, as we wished and most greatly wanted, our letter had quickly made its way to your Holiness. But now both to have this written and to hope for that as a reply delights us with a more heaped-up joy. Thus neither can our fault be accused, and the Lord's more bountiful kindness has done what he judged more conducive to our desire.

**2.** The holy brothers Romanus and Agilis, another letter of yours, hearing voices and giving them back, and a most sweet part of your presence—but one by which we gaped the more greedily to see you—we received with great pleasantness in the Lord. Whence, or when, or how could either you grant or we exact that you should teach us about yourselves by writing as much as we learned from their mouth? There was present also, what can be present to no paper, so great a joy in those narrating, that through the very face and eyes of those speaking we read you, written in their hearts, with ineffable gladness. This too was more: that any page whatsoever, however much good it contains written, profits itself nothing, although it is unfolded for the profit of others; but this letter of yours—that is, your brotherly soul—we so read in their conversation that it appeared to us the more blessed the more abundantly it had been written from you. And so we have, in imitation of the same blessedness, most zealously transcribed it into our hearts, inquiring everything about you.

**3.** Nor on that account did we let them go from us so quickly, though returning to you, without trouble. For see, I beg you, by what affections we were shaken. They were surely to be sent off the more swiftly the more earnestly they desired to obey you; but the more they desired this, the more they made you present to us: for thereby they showed how dear your inmost being was; therefore we wished the less to let them go, the more justly they urged to be let go. O thing not to be borne, did we not, by this parting, part from ourselves, were we not "members of one body, did we not have one head, were we not flooded by one grace, did we not live by one bread, walk on one road, dwell in the same house!" For why should we not use even the same words? For you recognize, I believe, that these are from your letter. But why are these your words rather than mine, which, true as they are, come to us from the communion of the same head? And if they have something proper, granted to you, the more for that did I so love them that they besieged the way of my breast, and would not allow words to pass from my heart to my tongue until those went forth first, the more they are yours. Holy brothers and beloved of God, and our members in turn, who could doubt that we are quickened by one spirit, except one who does not feel by what love we are bound?

**4.** Yet I would like to know whether you tolerate this bodily absence more patiently and more easily than we do? If it is so, I confess, I do not love that fortitude of yours—unless perhaps because we are such that we ought to be desired less by you than you ought to desire us. In me, at least, if there were a patience for bearing your absence, it would displease me; for I would act slothfully in seeing you: and what is more absurd than to become more slothful through fortitude? But by what care of the Church I am held, your Charity ought to attend to from this: that the most blessed father Valerius—how much he greets you along with us, and how he thirsts for you, you will hear from the brothers—did not allow me to be his presbyter, except by imposing on me the greater burden of a joint episcopate. Which, indeed, because I believed that the Lord willed it, by his so great love and the people's so great zeal, with some examples already preceding by which every excuse was shut off from me, I vehemently feared to refuse. But although the yoke of Christ is in itself easy, and his burden light[^1]; yet, on account of our roughness and weakness, if this bond bites me at all, and this burden presses me, it would be rendered, ineffably to me, somewhat more tolerable and bearable by the solace of your presence, you whom I hear to live more free and unencumbered of cares of this kind. Wherefore not impudently do I ask you, and beg, and demand, that you deign to come to Africa, which labors with a greater thirst for such men than with the renown of its drought.

**5.** God knows that not only on account of my own longing, nor only on account of those who learned of your purpose either through us or from fame proclaiming it anywhere, but also on account of the rest who partly do not hear, and partly, having heard, do not believe, yet can verify what they have ascertained, we desire you to be present in these lands even bodily. For although you do this diligently and mercifully, yet let your works shine even before the men of our regions, that they may see your good deeds, and glorify your Father who is in heaven[^2]. The fishermen, when the Lord called them, because they left their little boats and nets, rejoiced even in recalling that they had left all things and followed the Lord[^3]. And in truth he despises all things who despises not only as much as he was able, but even as much as he wished, to have. But in what was desired, the eyes of God are witnesses; in what was possessed, also those of men. But somehow, when one was seeking from the Lord superfluous and certain things, when he had heard that earthly things which are loved must all be sold, the things obtained bind more tightly than the things coveted. For why did that man depart sad, who, having taken counsel about eternal life, was advised both to distribute to the poor and to have treasure in heaven, if he wished to be perfect, except because, as the Gospel says, he had great riches[^4]? For it is one thing to be unwilling to incorporate what is lacking, another to tear away what is already incorporated: the former are rejected as food, the latter are cut off as members. With how great and how marvelous a joy, then, does Christian love behold, in our times, that done with gladness through the Lord's Gospel which the rich man heard from the mouth of the Lord with sadness?

**6.** Although I cannot explain in any words the conception and the bringing to birth of my heart; yet because you prudently and piously understand that this glory is not yours, that is, human, but in you the glory of the Lord—for you both most cautiously watch the enemy, and most devoutly act so that, as disciples of Christ, you may be humble of heart and meek[^5]; for earthly wealth is more usefully held humbly than relinquished proudly: because, then, you rightly understand that this is not your glory, but the Lord's, you see how small and slight a thing I have said. For I spoke of the praises of Christ, for which the tongues of angels are unequal. This glory of Christ, then, we desire to be brought also to the eyes of our men: in one marriage, set before both sexes, examples of pride to be trodden down, of perfection not to be despaired of. I do not know whether you do anything more merciful than this: that you are only as unwilling to be hidden, that you are such, as you were willing to be such.

**7.** Vetustinus, a boy pitiable even to the impious, I commend to your kindness and love: the causes of his calamity and his wandering you will hear from himself. For both his purpose, by which he promises that he will serve God, a longer time, a more robust age, and the passing of his fear will indicate more surely. Three books—and would that they were as good at unraveling so great a question as they are large—I have sent to your Holiness and Charity, fearing the labor of reading them in you the less, the more I perceive your ardor of loving; for their question is *On Free Will*. But I know that brother Romanianus does not have these, or does not have them all, through whom I gave nearly all the things I could write fitted to any ears whatever, by your zeal toward us, not to be brought to you, but I indicated them to be read. For he already had everything, and carried it with him: but through him I sent the first reply. I believe your Holiness has already perceived, by the spiritual sagacity which the Lord has granted you, what good that man bears in his mind, and in what weakness a part of him limps. Whence to your kindness and love I have, as I hope, with what solicitude commended both him and his son, of whom you have read, and by what great closeness they are joined to me. May the Lord build them up through you. Which is rather to be asked of him; for how much you would wish it, I know.

**8.** That you are writing against the Pagans I have learned from the brothers: if we deserve anything from your breast, send it without reserve for us to read. For your breast is such an oracle of the Lord that from it we presume that answers so pleasing, and so well unfolded against the most loquacious questions, are given to us. The books of the most blessed pope Ambrose I believe your Holiness has; but I greatly desire those which he wrote most diligently and most copiously against certain most unskilled and most proud men who contend that the Lord made progress from the books of Plato.

**9.** The most blessed brother Severus, my fellow student, prelate of the Church of Milevis, well known to the brothers in the same city, greets your Holiness with the duty owed along with us. All the brothers too, serving the Lord with us, do this as much as they long for you; they long for you as much as they love you, and they love you as much as you are good. The loaf which we have sent will become a more abundant blessing by the love of your kindness in receiving it. May the Lord keep you from this generation forever[^6], most beloved and most sincere lords, truly kind, and most excellent by the most abundant grace of the Lord, brothers.

[^4]: See Matt 19:16–22
