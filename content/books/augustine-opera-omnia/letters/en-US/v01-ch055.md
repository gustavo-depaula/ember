# Letter 55

To the Inquiries of Januarius, Book II

**1. 1.** When I had read your letter, in which you admonished me to render the debt of unraveling the remaining questions which you had asked long before, I could not endure that the most welcome and dearest desire of your zeal be longer deferred; and although in the midst of the heaps of my occupations, I made this my chief one, to answer the things you asked. But I do not wish to dispute longer about your letter, lest this very thing hinder me from at last rendering what I owe.

**1. 2.** You ask what cause there is "why the anniversary day of celebrating the Lord's passion does not return to the same day of the year, as the day on which it is handed down that he was born"; and then you subjoin: "if this happens on account of the Sabbath and the moon, what does the observance of the Sabbath and the moon mean in this matter." Here first you ought to know that the Birthday of the Lord is not celebrated in a sacrament, but only recalled to memory that he was born; and thereby there was no need but that the recurring day of the year, on which the thing itself was done, be marked by festal devotion. But there is a sacrament in some celebration, when the commemoration of a deed is so made that something is understood to be also signified, which is to be received in a holy way. And so we keep Easter in such a way that we not only recall to memory what was done—that is, that Christ died and rose again—but also do not omit the other things which are attested concerning him, for the signification of the sacraments. For because, as the Apostle says: *He died for our sins, and rose again for our justification*[^1]; a certain passing-over from death to life was consecrated in that passion and resurrection of the Lord. For even the very word which is called *Pascha* is not Greek, as it is commonly thought, but, those who know both tongues say, Hebrew. For not from suffering—since in Greek "to suffer" is said *paschein*—but from that which is passed over, as I said, from death to life, the thing was named by a Hebrew word: in which speech *Pascha* is said "a passing-over," as those who know this report. Which the Lord himself wished to touch when he says: *He who believes in me will pass from death to life*[^2]. And the same Evangelist is understood to have especially wished to express this, when, of the Lord about to celebrate the Passover with his disciples, where he gave them the mystical supper, *When Jesus had seen*, he says, *that his hour had come, that he should pass from this world to the Father*[^3]. Therefore the passing-over from this mortal life into another, immortal life—this is, namely, from death to life—is commended in the passion and resurrection of the Lord.

**2. 3.** This passing-over is now made by us through faith, which is to us for the remission of sins, in the hope of eternal life, for those who love God and neighbor; because *faith works through love*[^4], and *the just man lives by faith*[^5]. *But hope that is seen is not hope: for what anyone sees, why does he hope for it? But if we hope for what we do not see, we await it through patience*[^6]; according to this faith, and hope, and love, by which we have begun to be under grace, we have already died with Christ, and been buried with him through baptism into death[^7], as the Apostle says: *Because our old man too was crucified together with him*[^8]; and we have risen with him: *because he raised us up together, and made us sit together in the heavenly places*[^9]. Whence is also that exhortation: *savor the things that are above, not the things that are upon the earth*[^10]. But what follows and says: *For you are dead, and your life is hidden with Christ in God. When Christ shall appear, your life, then you too will appear with him in glory*[^11]; sufficiently indicates what he wishes to be understood: that now our passing-over from death to life, which is made through faith, is carried out in the hope of the future resurrection and glory at the end, *when this corruptible*, that is, this flesh in which we now groan, *shall put on incorruption, and this mortal shall put on immortality*[^12]. For now indeed we already have *the firstfruits of the Spirit* through faith, but still *we ourselves groan within ourselves, awaiting the adoption, the redemption of our body: for by hope we were saved*[^13]. When we are in this hope, *the body indeed is dead because of sin, but the spirit is life because of righteousness*. But see what follows: *But if the Spirit of him*, he says, *who raised Jesus from the dead dwells in you; he who raised Christ from the dead will give life also to your mortal bodies through his Spirit dwelling in you*[^14]. This, then, the whole Church, which is found in the pilgrimage of mortality, awaits at the end of the age, which was foreshown in the body of our Lord Jesus Christ, who is the firstborn from the dead[^15], because his body too, whose head he himself is, is nothing but the Church.

**3. 4.** For some, attending to the words which the Apostle assiduously says, that we have both died with Christ[^16], and risen with him, and not understanding in what sense they are said, have thought that the resurrection has already been made, and that none further is to be hoped for at the end of times: *Of whom*, he says, *are Hymenaeus and Philetus, who have erred concerning the truth, saying that the resurrection has already been made, and have subverted the faith of some*[^17]. The same Apostle, reproving them, detests them, who nevertheless says that we have risen with Christ. Whence, except because he says this has been made in us through faith, and hope, and love, according to the firstfruits of the Spirit? But because hope that is seen is not hope, and therefore if we hope for what we do not see, we await it through patience[^18]; there surely remains the redemption of our body, awaiting which we groan within ourselves. Whence is also that: *Rejoicing in hope, patient in tribulation*[^19].

**3. 5.** This renewal of our life, then, is a certain passing-over from death to life, which is first made through faith, that we may rejoice in hope, and be patient in tribulation, while still our outer man is corrupted, but the inner is renewed from day to day[^20]. On account of that very beginning of the new life, on account of the new man whom we are bidden to put on, and to put off the old[^21]; purging out the old leaven, that we may be a new lump, since *Christ our Passover has been sacrificed*[^22]: on account, then, of this newness of life, the first month among the months of the year has been assigned to this celebration. For it too is called *the month of the new things*[^23]. But because in the whole time of the age the third time has now appeared, therefore the resurrection of the Lord is on the third day. For the first time is before the Law, the second under the Law, the third under Grace, where now is the manifestation of the sacrament before hidden in a prophetic riddle. This, then, is also signified in the lunar number; for because the number seven is wont to appear mystically in the Scriptures with reference to a certain perfection, Easter is celebrated in the third week of the moon, the day of which falls from the fourteenth to the twenty-first.

**4. 6.** There is there also another sacrament, which, if it is obscure to you, because in such inquiries you are less learned, do not be saddened; nor on that account think me better, because I learned these things in boyish studies: for *He who glories, let him glory in this*, he says, *to know and understand that I am the Lord*[^24]. Some, then, zealous for such things, have inquired much about the numbers and motions of the stars. And those who have scrutinized these things more subtly have conjectured the increases and decreases of the moon from the turning of its globe—not that any substance either accrues to it when it is increased, or departs from it when it is diminished, which the Manichee, in his raving inexperience supposing, said that it is filled, as a ship is filled, from a fugitive part of God, which, mingled with the princes of darkness and defiled by their filth, they do not hesitate, with sacrilegious heart and mouth, both to believe and to speak. From this, then, they say the moon is filled, when that same part of God, with great labors purged from defilement, fleeing from the whole world and all its sewers, is restored to the mourning God until it return; and that it is filled through half a month, and through the other half is poured back into the sun, as into another ship. Nor yet, amid these blasphemies to be anathematized, could they ever invent anything, why, whether beginning to shine or ceasing, it gleams with a horned light, or why it begins to diminish from the half-month, and does not come to the full for pouring back.

**4. 7.** But those who have investigated these things by certain numbers, so as to foretell long before not only why the eclipses of the sun and moon happen, but also when they were going to happen, and to fix them by determined intervals of time by a canonical reckoning, and to commit them to writing—which those who now read and understand no less foretell, nor do they happen otherwise or at other times than they foretell. Such men, then (who are not to be pardoned, as the holy Scripture says; because *when they availed so much as to be able to estimate the age, its Lord*, whom by suppliant piety they could have, *they did not the more easily find*[^25]), have conjectured, from those very horns of the moon which are turned away from the sun, whether waxing or waning, that it is either illuminated by the sun—and the more it withdrew from it, the more from that part which appears to the lands it received its rays; but the more, after the half-month, from the other semicircle it approached it, the more, illuminated from the upper part, it could not, from that part which it turned toward the lands, receive the rays, and therefore seemed to decrease: or, if it had its own light, that it had it on one part in the hemisphere, which part, when, withdrawing from the sun, it gradually showed to the lands, until it showed the whole, it as it were displayed increases, while not what was lacking is added, but what was within is brought forth; and again gradually hid what lay open, and therefore seemed to decrease. But whichever of these two it be, this surely is manifest, and easily known to anyone attending, that the moon is not increased to our eyes, except by withdrawing from the sun, nor diminished, except by approaching the sun from the other part.

**5. 8.** Attend now to what is read in Proverbs: *The wise man abides like the sun; but the fool changes like the moon*[^26]. And who is the wise man who abides, except that sun of righteousness of whom it is said: *The sun of righteousness has risen for me*[^27], and whom the impious, lamenting on the last day that it had not risen for them, will say: *And the light of righteousness did not shine for us, and the sun did not rise for us*[^28]? For this sun visible to the eyes of the flesh, God makes to rise upon the good and the evil, who also rains upon the just and the unjust[^29]. But fitting likenesses are always drawn from visible things to the invisible. Who, then, is that fool who changes like the moon, except Adam in whom all sinned? For the human soul, withdrawing from the sun of righteousness—that is, from that inner contemplation of unchangeable truth—turns all its powers to earthly things, and is more and more darkened in its inner and higher parts: but when it begins to return to that unchangeable wisdom, the more it approaches it by the affection of piety, the more the outer man is corrupted, but the inner is renewed from day to day, and all that light of talent, which was sinking toward lower things, is turned to higher things, and is in a manner withdrawn from earthly things, so as to die more and more to this age, and its life be hidden with Christ in God[^30].

**5. 9.** It is changed, then, for the worse, advancing to outer things, and casting away its inmost things in its life[^31]; and this seems better to the earth, that is, to those *who savor earthly things*[^32], when the sinner is praised in the desires of his soul, and he who does iniquitous things is blessed[^33]. But it is changed for the better, when it gradually turns its intention and glory away from earthly things, which appear in this age, and turns to higher and inner things; and this seems worse to the earth, that is, to those who savor earthly things. Whence those impious ones, at last doing fruitless penance, will say even this among many things: *These are they whom at one time we held in derision and in the likeness of reproach: we, senseless, esteemed their life madness*[^34]. And thereby the Holy Spirit, drawing a likeness from visible things to invisible, and from corporeal to spiritual sacraments, willed that passing-over from one life into another life, which is named Pascha, to be observed from the fourteenth moon, so that not only on account of the third time, which I recalled above, because thence the third week begins; but also on account of that very turning from outer to inner things, a likeness should be taken from the moon: but even to the twenty-first, on account of that number seven, by which the signification of universality is often figured, which is also attributed to the Church herself, on account of the image of universality.

**6. 10.** Therefore the apostle John in the Apocalypse writes to seven Churches[^35]. But the Church, still established in this mortality of the flesh, is signified in the Scriptures by the name of the moon, on account of that very changeableness. Whence is that: *They have prepared their arrows in the quiver, to shoot in the dark moon the upright in heart*[^36]. For before that comes to pass which the Apostle says: *When Christ shall appear, your life, then you too will appear with him in glory*[^37], the Church seems dark in the time of her pilgrimage, groaning amid many iniquities; and then are to be feared the ambushes of deceitful seducers, which he wished to be understood by the name of arrows. Whence in another place, on account of the most faithful messengers of the truth, whom the Church everywhere bears, it is said: *The moon a faithful witness in heaven*[^38]. And when the Psalmist sang of the kingdom of the Lord: *There shall arise*, he says, *in his days righteousness and abundance of peace, until the moon be slain*[^39]: that is, the abundance of peace will so increase, until it consume all the changeableness of mortality. Then the last enemy, death, will be destroyed, and whatever resists us from the weakness of the flesh, whence there is for us not yet perfect peace, will be wholly consumed, when this corruptible shall have put on incorruption, and this mortal shall have put on immortality[^40]. Whence also the walls of that city which is called Jericho—which in the Hebrew speech is said to be interpreted "Moon"—fell down, the ark of the Testament being carried round in a seventh circuit[^41]. For what else does the announcement of the kingdom of heaven now do, than the carrying-round of the ark signified, except that all the fortifications of mortal life—that is, all the hope of this age, which resists the hope of the future age—be destroyed by the sevenfold gift of the Holy Spirit through free choice? For on this account, the ark being carried round, those walls fell not by a violent thrust, but of their own accord. There are also other testimonies of the Scriptures, which press upon us, through the mention of the moon, the signification of the Church, which in this mortality, away from that Jerusalem whose citizens are the holy angels, makes its pilgrimage in toils and labors.

**6. 11.** Yet on that account the foolish, who are unwilling to be changed for the better, ought not to think that those luminaries are to be adored, because from them a likeness is sometimes drawn to figure divine mysteries; for it is drawn from every creature. Nor on that account ought we to rush into the sentence of condemnation which is uttered by the apostolic mouth concerning certain men, who worshipped and served the creature rather than the Creator, who is blessed forever[^42]. For just as we do not adore the cattle, although Christ is called both a lamb and a calf[^43]; nor a wild beast, because he is called the lion of the tribe of Judah[^44]; nor a stone, because Christ was the rock[^45]; nor Mount Zion, because in it the Church is figured: so neither the sun nor the moon, although from that heavenly creature, as from many earthly ones, the figures of the sacraments are taken for mystical instructions.

**7. 12.** Wherefore the ravings of the Manichees are to be laughed at with detestation; to whom, when we object their empty fictions, by which they cast men headlong into the error into which they themselves were first cast headlong, they seem to themselves talkative, when they say to us: Why do you too celebrate Easter according to the computation of the sun and moon? as though we found fault with the orders of the stars, or the alternations of times, founded by the highest and best God, and not with their perversity, which abuses things most wisely founded for the most empty opinions of its folly. For if a mathematician is going to contradict us, that we draw likenesses from the stars and luminaries of heaven to figure the sacraments mystically, let the augurs also contradict, lest it be said to us: *Be simple as doves*; let the Marsi also contradict, lest it be said to us: *Crafty as serpents*[^46]; let the actors contradict, lest in the Psalms we name the lyre. Or because from these things signs of likenesses are taken for the mysteries of the word of God, let them say, if it please, that we either catch at auspices, or concoct poisons, or affect theatrical luxuries, which it is most insane to say.

**7. 13.** We therefore do not conjecture from the sun and moon, by yearly or monthly times, the outcomes of our actions, lest in the most perilous tempests of human life, dashed as it were upon the rocks of a wretched servitude, we make shipwreck of free choice: but for signifying a thing sacredly, we take up fitting likenesses with most religious devotion, as from the rest of creation—from the winds, from the sea, from the earth, from birds, from fishes, from cattle, from trees, from men—for discourse indeed in manifold ways, but for the celebration of the sacraments, now with Christian liberty, most sparingly; as from water, from grain, from wine, from oil. But in the servitude of the old people many things too were commanded to be celebrated, which are handed down to us only to be understood. We therefore do not observe days and years and months and times, lest we hear from the Apostle: *I fear for you, lest perhaps I have labored among you in vain*[^47]. For he blames those who say: I will not set out today, because it is the day after, or because the moon is so disposed; or: I will set out so that prosperous things may follow, because the position of the stars is thus: I will not transact business this month, because that star governs my month for me; or: I will, because it favors the month: I will not plant a vineyard this year, because it is a leap year. But let no wise man think that observers of times are to be reproved who say: I will not set out today, because a storm has arisen; or: I will not sail, because there are still the remnants of winter; or: It is the time of sowing, because the earth is satiated with the autumn rains; or if perhaps any other natural effects concerning the motion of the air and the humors for varying the qualities of times have been noted in the most orderly turning of the stars, of which it was said when they were founded: *And let them be for signs and for seasons and for days and for years*[^48]. But if any figures of likenesses are drawn, not only from heaven and the stars, but also from the lower creation, for the dispensation of the sacraments, it is a certain eloquence of wholesome teaching, accommodated to moving the affection of the learners, from visible to invisible things, from corporeal to spiritual, from temporal to eternal.

**8. 14.** Nor does any of us attend to the fact that at the time when we celebrate Easter, the sun is in Aries, as they call a certain place of the stars, where indeed in the month of the new things the sun is found: but whether they wish to call that place Aries, or anything else, we have learned this from the holy Scriptures, that God founded all the stars, and set them in order in the heavenly places where he willed; which, marked and ordered by stars, into whatever parts they divide, by whatever names they note them, wherever the sun should be in the month of the new things, this celebration would find it there, on account of the likeness of the sacrament of renewing life, of which we have disputed enough above. But if even that place of the stars could be called Aries, on account of some fittingness of figure, the divine discourse would not even fear to draw from such a thing some likeness of a sacrament, as from other creatures not only heavenly but also earthly; as from Orion and the Pleiades, as from Mount Sinai and Mount Zion, as from the rivers which are called Gihon, Pishon, Tigris, Euphrates, as from that river Jordan, so often ennobled in the holy mysteries, it has drawn mystical likenesses of things to be figuratively intimated.

**8. 15.** But how great is the difference between observations of the stars accommodated to the qualities of the air, as farmers or sailors observe; or for noting the parts of the world and directing a course somewhere and from somewhere, which the steersmen of ships do, and those who go through the sandy solitudes into the interior of the South by no certain or straight path; or when, for signifying figuratively something useful in teaching, some mention is made of certain stars: how great, then, is the difference between these uses, and the vanities of men observing the stars for this, that they may seek out neither the qualities of the air, nor the ways of the regions, nor merely the numbers of the times, nor the likenesses of spiritual things, but as it were the fatal outcomes of things already settled—who does not understand?

**9. 16.** But now let us next see why it is also observed, when Easter is celebrated, that the Sabbath fall: for this is proper to the Christian religion. For the Jews observe only the month of the new things and the moon from the fourteenth to the twenty-first. But because that Passover of theirs on which the Lord suffered so fell that between his death and resurrection the Sabbath day was in the middle, our fathers judged it should be added, that our festival too should be distinguished from the festival of the Jews; and that—which is to be believed not to have been done in vain by him who is before the times, and through whom the times were made, and who came in the fullness of the times[^49], and who had power to lay down his soul and again to take it up[^50], and therefore awaited an hour not fatal, but opportune for the sacrament which he had resolved to commend, when he said: *My hour has not yet come*[^51]—should be kept by posterity in the anniversary celebration of his passion.

**9. 17.** For that which now, as I said above, we carry out in faith and hope, and for attaining which we busy ourselves in love, is a certain rest from all the labor of all troubles, holy and perpetual: into it our passing-over is made from this life, which our Lord Jesus Christ deigned to foreshow and consecrate by his passion. But in that rest there is not slothful idleness, but a certain ineffable tranquillity of leisured action. For one rests at the end from the works of this life in such a way that one rejoices in the action of the other life. But because such action is carried out in the praise of God, without labor of the limbs, without anguish of cares; one does not so pass over to it through quiet that labor succeeds to it—that is, action does not so begin to be, that quiet ceases to be: for one does not return to labors and cares; but that which pertains to quiet remains in the action, neither to labor in work, nor to fluctuate in thought. Because, then, through rest one returns to the first life, whence the soul fell into sin, therefore by the Sabbath rest is signified. But that first life, which is restored to those returning from pilgrimage and receiving the first robe[^52], is figured by the first day of the week, which we call the Lord's day. Seek the seven days, read Genesis: you will find the seventh without an evening, because it signifies rest without end. The first life, then, was not everlasting for the sinner: but the last rest is everlasting, and thereby the eighth too will have everlasting blessedness, because that rest, which is everlasting, is taken up by the eighth, not extinguished; for otherwise it would not be everlasting. So, then, that will be the eighth which is the first, that the first life be not taken away, but restored eternal.

**10. 18.** Yet the Sabbath was commended to the former people to be celebrated corporeally in idleness, that it might be a figure of the sanctification in the rest of the Holy Spirit. For nowhere do we read in Genesis sanctification through all the former days; but of the Sabbath alone it is said: *And God sanctified the seventh day*[^53]. For souls love rest, whether pious or iniquitous; but by what they may attain to that which they love, very many do not know: nor do the bodies themselves seek anything by their weights, except what souls seek by their loves. For just as a body strives by its weight, whether downward or upward, until, coming to the place to which it strives, it comes to rest—for the weight of oil, if it be let go in the air, strives downward; but if under the waters, upward—so souls strive toward the things they love, for this reason, that, by attaining them, they may rest. And many things indeed delight through the body, but in them there is no eternal rest, nor even a lasting one; and therefore they rather soil the soul, and weigh it down rather, so as to hinder its sincere weight, by which it is borne to the heights. When, then, the soul is delighted by itself, it is not yet delighted by an unchangeable thing; and therefore it is still proud, because it holds itself for the highest, when God is higher. Nor is it left unpunished in such a sin, because *God resists the proud, but gives grace to the humble*[^54]. But when it is delighted by God, there it finds true, certain, eternal rest, which it sought in other things, and did not find. Accordingly it is admonished in the psalm: *Delight in the Lord, and he will give you the petitions of your heart*[^55].

**10. 19.** Because, then, *the love of God has been poured out in our hearts through the Holy Spirit who has been given to us*[^56], therefore the sanctification is recalled on the seventh day, where rest is commended. But because we cannot work well, except aided by his gift, as the Apostle says: *For it is God who works in you both to will and to work, according to good will*[^57], nor will we be able to rest after all our good works which we do in this life, except, by his gift, sanctified and perfected unto eternity; therefore of God himself it is said that, when he had made *all his works very good, on the seventh day he rested from all his works which he made*[^58]. For he was signifying the future rest, which he was about to give us men after good works. For just as, when we work well, he is said to work in us, by whose gift we work well: so when we rest, he is said to rest, by whose gift we rest.

**11. 20.** Hence it is that even in the three first precepts of the Decalogue which pertain to God (for the other seven pertain to the neighbor, that is, to man, because on two precepts the whole law hangs[^59]), the third is there placed concerning the observance of the Sabbath: so that in the first precept we understand the Father, where it is forbidden that any likeness of God be worshipped in the figments of men; not because God has no image, but because no image of his ought to be worshipped except that which is the same as he himself; nor it for him, but with him. And because the creature is changeable, and therefore it is said: *every creature is subject to vanity*[^60], since the nature of the universe is shown even in a part; lest anyone think the Son of God, the Word through whom all things were made[^61], to be a creature, there follows the other precept: *You shall not take in vain the name of the Lord your God*[^62]. But the Holy Spirit, in whom that rest is granted us which we love everywhere, but find not except by loving God—when his love is poured out in our hearts through the Holy Spirit who has been given to us[^63], because God sanctified the seventh day on which he rested—is intimated in the third precept of the law, which is written about the observance of the Sabbath; not that we should think we now rest in this life, but that all things which we do well should have no intention except in the future everlasting rest. For remember most of all, what I have already recalled above, that *by hope we were saved; but hope that is seen is not hope*[^64].

**11. 21.** But to that very fire of love, to be nourished and as it were fanned, by which, as by a weight, we may be borne upward or inward to rest, all these things pertain which are figuratively intimated to us; for they move and kindle love more than if they were set forth bare, without any likenesses of the sacraments. The cause of which thing it is difficult to tell. But yet it is so, that something intimated through an allegorical signification moves more, delights more, is honored more, than if it were said most openly in proper words. I believe that the very motion of the soul, so long as it is still entangled in earthly things, is more sluggishly inflamed: but if it is borne to corporeal likenesses, and thence referred to the spiritual things which are figured by those likenesses, by that very passing-over it is in a manner quickened, and, as fire stirred in a torch, is kindled, and is snatched, with a more ardent love, to rest.

**12. 22.** And therefore, among all those ten precepts, only that one which is set there concerning the Sabbath is commanded to be observed figuratively; which figure we have undertaken to understand, not also to celebrate by corporeal idleness. For since by the Sabbath is signified spiritual rest, of which it is said in the psalm: *Be at leisure and see, that I am the Lord*[^65], and to which men are called by the Lord himself saying: *Come to me, all you who labor and are heavy laden, and I will refresh you: take my yoke upon you, and learn of me, for I am meek and humble of heart, and you will find rest for your souls*[^66]; the other precepts there, however, we observe properly as precepts, without any figurative signification. For we have learned manifestly not to worship idols; and not to take in vain the name of the Lord our God, and to honor father and mother, and not to commit adultery, not to kill, not to steal, not to bear false witness, not to covet the neighbor's wife, not to covet any thing of the neighbor's[^67], do not figuratively hold forth one thing and mystically signify another; but they are observed as they sound. Yet we are not bidden to observe the Sabbath day to the letter, according to idleness from corporeal work, as the Jews observe it: and that observance of theirs which is so commanded, unless it signify some other spiritual rest, is judged to be laughed at. Whence not unfittingly we understand that, for kindling the love by which we tend to rest, all things avail which are figuratively said in the Scriptures; since that alone in the Decalogue is figuratively commanded, where rest is commended, which is everywhere loved, but is found certain and holy in God alone.

**13. 23.** Yet the Lord's day was declared, not to the Jews, but to Christians, by the resurrection of the Lord, and from then it began to have its festivity. For the souls of all the saints, before the resurrection of the body, are indeed in rest, but they are not in that action by which bodies, received again, are quickened. For such action the eighth day signifies, which is also the first, because it does not take away that rest, but glorifies it. For there does not return with the body the difficulty from the body, because neither corruption: *For this corruptible must put on incorruption, and this mortal must put on immortality*[^68]. Wherefore before the resurrection of the Lord, although the sacrament of the eighth, by which the resurrection is signified, was by no means hidden from the holy fathers full of the prophetic spirit (for a psalm is inscribed "for the eighth"[^69], and on the eighth day infants were circumcised[^70], and in Ecclesiastes, for the signification of the two Testaments, it is said: *Give to them seven, and to them eight*[^71]); yet it was reserved and hidden, and only the Sabbath was handed down to be celebrated: because there was before a rest of the dead; but the resurrection was of none, who, rising from the dead, would now not die, and death would no longer have dominion over him[^72]; so that, after such a resurrection had been made in the body of the Lord (that it might go before in the head of the Church, what the body of the Church would hope for at the end), now even the Lord's day, that is, the eighth, which is also the first, might begin to be celebrated. The very cause too is understood, why the observance of the Passover, where they are bidden to kill and eat the lamb, which most manifestly prefigures the passion of the Lord, was not so commanded them that they should attend to the Sabbath's falling, and to its concurring with the month of the new things at the third week of the moon, so that the Lord might rather mark that same day by his passion, who had also come to declare the Lord's day, that is, the eighth, which is also the first.

**14. 24.** Attend, therefore, to the most sacred three days of him crucified, buried, raised. Of these three, what the cross signifies, we carry out in the present life: but what the burial and resurrection signify, we carry out in faith and hope. For now it is said to a man: *Take up your cross and follow me*[^73]. But the flesh is crucified, when our members which are upon the earth are mortified—fornication, uncleanness, luxury, avarice[^74], and the rest of this kind, of which he likewise says: *If you live according to the flesh, you will die; but if by the spirit you mortify the deeds of the flesh, you will live*[^75]. Hence too he says of himself: *The world is crucified to me, and I to the world*[^76]. And in another place: *Knowing*, he says, *that our old man was crucified together with him, that the body of sin may be done away, that we may no longer serve sin*[^77]. So long, then, as our works are at this, that the body of sin be done away, so long as the outer man is corrupted, that the inner be renewed from day to day, it is the time of the cross.

**14. 25.** These are indeed good works, yet still laborious, whose reward is rest: but therefore it is said: *Rejoicing in hope*, that, thinking of the future rest, we may work with cheerfulness in our labors. This cheerfulness the breadth of the cross signifies, in the transverse wood, where the hands are fixed. For by the hands we understand works; by the breadth, the cheerfulness of the worker, because sadness makes straits; but by the height, to which the head is joined, the expectation of the recompense from the sublime righteousness of God, who will render to each according to his works, to those indeed who, according to the endurance of good work, seek glory, and honor, and incorruption, eternal life[^78]. And so the length, by which the whole body is extended, signifies endurance itself, whence they are called long-suffering who endure. But the depth which is fixed in the earth prefigures the secret of the sacrament. For you recall, unless I am mistaken, what words of the Apostle I unfold in this designation of the cross, where he says: *Rooted and founded in love, that you may be able to comprehend, with all the saints, what is the length, breadth, height, and depth*[^79]. But those things which we do not yet see, and do not yet hold, but carry out in faith and hope, are figured in the other two days. For these things which we now do, as it were fixed by the nails of the precepts in the fear of God, as it is written: *Pierce with nails my flesh from your fear*[^80], are reckoned among the necessary things, not among those which are to be sought and desired through themselves. Whence he says he desires that best thing, *to be dissolved, and to be with Christ: but to remain in the flesh*, he says, *is necessary for your sake*[^81]. What, then, he says, *to be dissolved and to be with Christ*, thence begins the rest, which is not interrupted by the resurrection, but glorified; which yet is now held by faith, *because the just man lives by faith*[^82]. *Or do you not know*, he says, *that as many of us as were baptized in Christ Jesus, were baptized in his death? We were buried together with him, then, through baptism into death*[^83]. Whence, except by faith? For it is not yet perfected in us, while we still groan within ourselves, and await the adoption, the redemption of our body: *For by hope we were saved; but hope that is seen is not hope: for what anyone sees, why does he hope for it? but if we hope for what we do not see, we await it through patience*[^84].

**14. 26.** Which—remember how often I recall it—lest we now think that in this life we ought to become blessed and free from all difficulties; and so, in the straits of temporal things, murmur against God with a sacrilegious mouth, as though he did not display what he promised. He promised indeed the necessities even of this life; but the consolations of the wretched are one thing, the joys of the blessed another. *Lord*, he says, *according to the multitude of my sorrows in my heart, your exhortations have gladdened my soul*[^85]. Let us not, then, murmur in difficulties, lest we lose the breadth of cheerfulness, of which it is said: *Rejoicing in hope*; because it follows: *patient in tribulation*[^86]. The new life, then, is now begun in faith, and carried out in hope: for then it will be perfect, when death shall be swallowed up in victory[^87], when that last enemy, death, shall be destroyed[^88], when we shall have been changed, and made equal to the angels: *For we shall all*, he says, *rise; but not all shall be changed*[^89]. And the Lord: *They will be*, he says, *equal to the angels of God*[^90]. For we have now been laid hold of, in fear, through faith, but then we shall lay hold, in love, through sight. *For so long as we are in the body, we are on pilgrimage away from the Lord: for we walk by faith, not by sight*[^91]. And so the Apostle himself, who says: *that I may lay hold, as I have been laid hold of*, openly confesses that he has not laid hold: *Brothers*, he says, *I do not reckon myself to have laid hold*[^92]. But yet, because that very hope, from the promise of the truth, is certain to us, when he had said: *We were buried together with him, then, through baptism into death*, he subjoined and said: *that as Christ rose from the dead through the glory of the Father, so we too may walk in newness of life*[^93]. We walk, then, in the matter of labor, but in the hope of quiet; in the flesh of the old, but in the faith of the new. For he says: *The body indeed is dead because of sin; but the spirit is life because of righteousness. But if the Spirit of him who raised Jesus Christ from the dead dwells in you; he who raised Jesus Christ from the dead will give life also to your mortal bodies through his Spirit dwelling in you*[^94].

**15. 27.** These things, both from the authority of the divine Scriptures and from the consent of the universal Church, which is spread throughout the whole world, are celebrated through the anniversary Easter. **15.** In a great sacrament, surely, as you now understand. And in the old Scriptures indeed no time is commanded for keeping the Passover, except from the month of the new things, from the fourteenth moon to the twenty-first: yet from the Gospel, because it is manifest on what day also the Lord was crucified, and was in the tomb, and rose again, there has been added too the observance of those very days through the councils of the fathers, and the whole Christian world has been persuaded that Easter ought to be celebrated in that manner.

**15. 28.** Lent indeed of the fasts has authority, both in the old Books from the fasting of Moses and Elijah[^95]; and from the Gospel, because for as many days the Lord fasted[^96], showing that the Gospel does not dissent from the Law and the Prophets. For in the person of Moses, the Law; in the person of Elijah, the Prophets are taken, among whom also he appeared glorious on the mountain[^97]; that it might more evidently stand out which the Apostle says of him: *Having testimony from the Law and the Prophets*[^98]. In what part of the year, then, could the observance of Lent be more fittingly established than next to and contiguous with the Lord's passion? Because in it is signified this laborious life, for which there is need of continence, that we may fast from the friendship of the world itself; which surely ceases not to flatter deceitfully, and to scatter about and toss the painted colors of its enticements. But I think this life is figured by the number forty for this reason: that the number ten, in which is the perfection of our blessedness—as in the number eight, because it returns to the first, so in this, because the creature, which is figured by the number seven, cleaves to the Creator, in whom the unity of the Trinity is declared—was announced temporally throughout the whole world, because the world is both delineated from the four winds, and raised up from the four elements, and varied by the turns of the four seasons of the year. But ten times four are consummated in forty; but the number forty, reckoned by its parts, adds the number ten itself, and they become fifty, as it were the reward of labor and continence. For not in vain did the Lord himself both abide forty days after the resurrection on this earth and in this life with the Disciples, and, after he ascended into heaven, ten days being interposed, sent the promised Holy Spirit, the day of Pentecost being completed: which fiftieth day has another sacrament, that seven times seven make forty-nine; and when one returns to the beginning, which is the eighth, which is also the first day, fifty are completed; which are celebrated after the Lord's resurrection, now in the figure not of labor, but of quiet and gladness. On this account both the fasts are relaxed, and we pray standing, which is a sign of the resurrection. Whence also on all the Lord's days this is observed at the altar, and Alleluia is sung, which signifies that our future action will be nothing but to praise God, as it is written: *Blessed are those who dwell in your house, Lord; forever and ever they will praise you*[^99].

**16. 29.** But the fifty days are commended also in the Scriptures; and not only in the Gospel, because then the Holy Spirit came, but also in the old Books. For there too, after they had celebrated the Passover, the lamb being killed, fifty days are numbered up to the day on which the Law was given on Mount Sinai to Moses the servant of God, written by the finger of God: but in the books of the Gospel it is most openly declared that the finger of God signifies the Holy Spirit. For when one evangelist had said: *By the finger of God I cast out demons*[^100]; another said this same thing thus: *By the Spirit of God I cast out demons*[^101]. Who would not prefer this gladness of the divine sacraments, when they grow bright with the light of sound doctrine, to all the empires of this world, even though pacified with an unusual felicity? Do not the two Seraphim, as it were, cry to one another, singing together the praises of the Most High: *Holy, holy, holy, Lord God of Sabaoth*[^102]; so do the two Testaments, faithfully agreeing, sing together the consecrated truth? The lamb is killed, the Passover is celebrated, and, fifty days being interposed, the Law is given, written for fear by the finger of God[^103]. Christ is killed, who like a lamb was led to be sacrificed[^104], as Isaiah testifies, the true Passover is celebrated, and, fifty days being interposed, the Holy Spirit is given for love, who is the finger of God, contrary to men seeking their own things, and therefore bearing a harsh yoke and a heavy burden, and not finding rest for their souls; because *love does not seek the things that are its own*[^105]. Therefore the animosity of the heretics is always unquiet, whom the Apostle declares to have the attempt of the magicians of Pharaoh, saying: *For as Jannes and Jambres resisted Moses, so these too resist the truth, men corrupt in mind, reprobate concerning the faith: but they will proceed no further; for their madness will be manifest to all, as theirs too was*[^106]. For because through that very corruption of mind they were most unquiet, they failed at the third sign, confessing that the Holy Spirit who was in Moses was adverse to them. For, failing, they said: *This is the finger of God*[^107]. But just as the Holy Spirit, reconciled and appeased, grants rest to the meek and humble of heart, so, contrary and adverse, he agitates the unmeek and the proud with unquiet. Which unquiet those most brief flies signified, under which the magicians of Pharaoh failed, saying: *This is the finger of God*.

**16. 30.** Read Exodus, and see where they celebrated the Passover, after how many days the Law was given. God speaks to Moses in the desert of Sinai on the third day of the third month. Note, then, one day from the entrance of that third month, and see what he says among the rest: *Go down*, he says, *testify to the people, and purify them today and tomorrow, and let them wash their garments, and be ready on the third day. For on the third day the Lord will come down on Mount Sinai before all the people*[^108]: then the Law was given, namely on the third day of the third month. Number, then, from the fourteenth day of the first month, on which the Passover was made, to the third day of the third month, and you will find seventeen days of the first month, thirty of the second, three of the third, which make fifty. The Law in the ark is the sanctification in the body of the Lord, through whose resurrection the future rest is promised to us, for receiving which love is inspired by the Holy Spirit. *But the Spirit was not yet given, because Jesus was not yet glorified*[^109]. Whence that prophecy was sung: *Arise, Lord, into your rest, you and the ark of your sanctification*[^110]. Where rest, there sanctification. Whence now, that we may love and desire, we have received a pledge. But all are called to the rest of the other life, to which one passes over from this life, which Pascha signifies, in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit[^111].

**17. 31.** Therefore the number fifty, multiplied by three, the number three itself being added for the eminence of the sacrament, is found also in those great fishes, which the Lord, after the resurrection demonstrating the new life, commanded to be lifted from the right side; nor were the nets broken[^112], because then there will be no unquiet of the heretics. Then the man, perfect and quiet, purged in soul and body through the chaste oracles of the Lord—silver tried by fire from the earth, purged sevenfold[^113]—will receive as reward the denarius[^114], so that there are seventeen. For in this number too, as in others exhibiting manifold figures, a wonderful sacrament is found. Nor undeservedly is the seventeenth psalm alone read entire in the books of Kingdoms[^115]; because it signifies that kingdom where we shall have no adversary. For its title is: *On the day on which the Lord delivered him from the hand of all his enemies, and from the hand of Saul*[^116]. For who is figured in David, except he who came according to the flesh from the seed of David[^117]? Who surely, in his body, which is the Church, still suffers enemies. Whence to that persecutor whom he struck down with his voice, and, transfixing into his own body, in a manner devoured, there sounded from heaven: *Saul, Saul, why do you persecute me?*[^118] But when will this body of his be delivered from the hand of all his enemies, except when that last enemy, death, shall be destroyed[^119]? To this time pertained that number of a hundred and fifty-three fishes. For the very number seventeen, rising into a triangle, completes the sum of a hundred and fifty-three. For rising from one to seventeen, add all the middle numbers, and you will find it: to one, namely, add two, they become three; add three, they become six; add four, they become ten; add five, they become fifteen; add six, they become twenty-one; add thus the rest, and the seventeenth itself, they become a hundred and fifty-three.

**17. 32.** These things are most firmly held from the Scriptures, that is, Easter and Pentecost. For that those forty days before Easter be observed, the custom of the Church has strengthened; so too that the eight days of the Neophytes be distinguished from the rest, that is, that the eighth sing together with the first. But that Alleluia be sung in the Church through those fifty days alone is not everywhere observed: for on other days too it is variously sung here and there; but on those days everywhere. But whether it be observed everywhere that we pray standing on those days and on all Lord's days, I do not know: yet what the Church follows in it, I have said as I could, and I judge it to be manifest.

**18. 33.** But about the washing of feet, since the Lord commended this on account of the form of humility, for the teaching of which he came, as he himself consequently set forth[^120], it has been asked at what time so great a thing should especially be taught even by deed, and that time occurred on which the very commendation might more religiously inhere. But lest it should seem to pertain to the sacrament of baptism itself, many were unwilling to receive this into custom. Some even did not hesitate to remove it from custom. But others, that they might both commend this at a more sacred time, and distinguish it from the sacrament of baptism, chose either the third day of the octave, because the number three too excels in many sacraments, or even the eighth itself, on which to do this.

**18. 34.** I wonder, indeed, why you so wished that I should write you something about those things which are variously observed through diverse places, when it is both not necessary, and one most wholesome rule in these is to be retained: that those things which are not against the faith, nor against good morals, and have something toward the exhortation of a better life, wherever we see them instituted, or learn that they have been instituted, we not only not disapprove, but even, by praising and imitating, follow—if the weakness of some does not so hinder that there be more detriment. For if it should so hinder that greater gains of the zealous are to be hoped for than the detriments of the calumniators are to be feared, it is without doubt to be done, especially that which can also be defended from the Scriptures; as about the singing of hymns and psalms, since we have both the examples and the precepts of the Lord himself and of the Apostles. About this matter, so useful for moving the mind piously, and kindling the affection of divine love, the custom is various, and most of the members of the Church in Africa are rather sluggish: so that the Donatists reproach us, because we soberly sing in church the divine canticles of the Prophets, while they inflame their drunkennesses to the singing of psalms composed by human talent, as if to the trumpets of exhortation. But when is it not the time, when brothers are gathered in church, of singing holy things, except when there is reading, or disputing, or the prelate prays with a clear voice, or common prayer is announced by the voice of the deacon?

**19. 35.** But at other little portions of the times, what better thing could be done by gathered Christians, what more useful, what holier, I do not see at all. But that which is instituted contrary to custom, as though it were the observance of a sacrament, I cannot approve, even if I do not dare more freely to disapprove many things of this kind, on account of avoiding the scandals of certain persons, whether holy or turbulent. But this I grieve over too much, that many things which are most wholesomely commanded in the divine books are less cared for; and all things are so full of so many presumptions, that one is more grievously rebuked who has touched the earth with a bare foot during his octave, than one who has buried his mind in wine-drinking. All such things, therefore, which are neither contained in the authorities of the holy Scriptures, nor found established in the councils of bishops, nor strengthened by the custom of the universal Church, but are innumerably varied according to the diverse motions of diverse places, so that the causes can scarcely or never at all be found which men followed in instituting them, I judge are, where the opportunity is given, to be cut away without any doubt. For although neither this can be found, in what way they are against the faith; yet they press down religion itself—which the mercy of God willed to be free with the very fewest and most manifest sacraments of celebrations—with servile burdens, so that the condition of the Jews is more tolerable, who, even if they did not recognize the time of freedom, are yet subjected to legal burdens, not to human presumptions. But the Church of God, established amid much chaff and many tares[^121], tolerates many things, and yet does not approve those which are against the faith or a good life, nor is silent about them, nor does them.

**20. 36.** And so that which you wrote, that certain brothers so abstain from eating meats that they think them unclean, is most openly against the faith and sound doctrine. From this, then, if I should wish to dispute longer, it could be thought by some that the Apostle commanded this obscurely; who even, among the many things he said about this matter, so detested the impious opinion of the heretics as to say: *But the Spirit manifestly says that in the last times some will depart from the faith, attending to spirits of seduction and to doctrines of demons, in the hypocrisy of liars, having their conscience cauterized, forbidding to marry, to abstain from foods which God created to be received with thanksgiving by the faithful, and by those who have known the truth: because every creature of God is good, and nothing to be rejected which is received with thanksgiving; for it is sanctified through the word of God and prayer*[^122]. And in another place he speaks of these matters: *All things are clean to the clean; but to the unclean and to unbelievers nothing is clean; but both their mind and their conscience are polluted*[^123]. You yourself, read the rest, and recite it to those to whom you can, that they may not make void in themselves the grace of God, because they were called to freedom; only let them not take up freedom as an occasion for the flesh[^124], and therefore now refuse, for the sake of restraining the desire of the flesh, to abstain from any foods, because it is not permitted them to do so superstitiously and unbelievingly.

**20. 37.** But those who read lots from the gospel pages, even though it is to be wished that they do this rather than run to consult demons; yet this custom too displeases me, to wish to turn the divine oracles, which speak about another life, to secular business and to the vanity of this life.

**21. 38.** If you do not think these things sufficient for what you have asked, you are too ignorant both of my strength and of my occupations. For I am so far from that which you supposed, that nothing escapes me, that I have read nothing more saddening in your letter, because it is also most openly false; and I marvel that this escapes you, that not only in countless other matters do many things escape me, but even in the holy Scriptures themselves I do not know far more than I know. But for this reason I bear a hope not unfruitful in the name of Christ, because I not only have believed my God that on those two precepts the whole Law and the Prophets hang[^125], but have also experienced it, and experience it daily; since no sacrament, or any discourse rather obscure of the sacred writings, is opened to me, where I do not find those same precepts: *For the end of the precept is love from a pure heart, and a good conscience, and faith unfeigned*[^126]; and *love is the fullness of the law*[^127].

**21. 39.** And so you too, most beloved, whether these things or others, so read, so learn, that you remember it was most truly said: *Knowledge puffs up, love builds up*[^128]. *But love is not jealous, is not puffed up*[^129]. So, then, let knowledge be applied as a certain scaffold, by which the structure of love may rise, which abides forever, even when knowledge will be destroyed[^130]; which, applied to the end of love, is much useful; but of itself, without such an end, is not only superfluous, but even proved harmful. But I know how a holy thought guards you under the shade of the wings of the Lord our God. Yet for this reason I have admonished these things, though briefly, because I know that that same love of yours, which is not jealous, will give and read this letter to many.
