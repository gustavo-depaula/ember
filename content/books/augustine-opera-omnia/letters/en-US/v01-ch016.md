# Letter 16

Maximus of Madaura to Augustine

**1.** *Eager to be gladdened often by your addresses, and prompted by your own discourse, with which a little while ago you very pleasantly struck me—our love safe—I have not failed to return like for like, lest you should call my silence a sign of regret. But I beg that, if you reckon these to be, as it were, the limbs of an old man, you will follow them with the indulgence of kindly ears. That Mount Olympus is the dwelling of the gods, Greece relates in tales, on uncertain faith. But the forum of our own city we see and prove to be possessed by a throng of salutary divinities. Indeed, that there is one supreme God, without beginning, without offspring of his nature, a great and magnificent father, as it were—who is so mad, so out of his mind, as to deny that this is most certain? His powers, diffused throughout the work of the world, we invoke under many names, since we are all of course ignorant of his proper name. For "God" is a name common to all religions. So it comes about that, while we follow his limbs, as it were, piecemeal with various supplications, we plainly seem to worship the whole.*

**2.** *But I cannot pretend that I am not impatient at so great an error. For who could bear that a Mygdon be preferred to Jupiter brandishing his thunderbolts; a Sanaes to Juno, Minerva, Venus, and Vesta; and to all the immortal gods—oh, the outrage!—the arch-martyr Namphamo? Among whom Lucitas too is looked up to with no less worship, and others without limit in number (names hateful to gods and men), who, conscious of unspeakable crimes, under the guise of a glorious death, heaping their crimes upon crimes, found an end, befouled, worthy of their characters and deeds. The tombs of these men—if it is worth mentioning—the foolish frequent, abandoning the temples and neglecting the shades of their own ancestors, so that the prophecy of that bard who took it ill stands out:* and Rome shall swear by shades in the temples of the gods*. But to me in this storm it seems almost as though the war of Actium has arisen again, in which Egyptian monsters dare to brandish their weapons against the gods of the Romans—weapons that will by no means last.*

**3.** *But this I ask, most wise of men: that, setting aside and casting off the strength of eloquence by which you are renowned among all, and omitting too the Chrysippean arguments with which you used to do battle, and putting aside for a little while dialectic—which, by the wrestling of its sinews, strives to leave nothing certain for anyone—you approve by the thing itself who this God is whom you Christians claim as if your own, and whom you compose yourselves to see present in hidden places. For we worship our gods in the light, openly, before the eyes and ears of all mortals, with pious prayers, and we make them propitious to us through pleasant sacrifices, and we contend that all these things are seen and approved by everyone.*

**4.** *But, an invalid old man, I withdraw myself from this further contest, and gladly proceed to the opinion of the Mantuan rhetorician:* each is drawn by his own pleasure[^1]*. After this I do not doubt, excellent man, who have strayed from my sect, that this letter, stolen away by the theft of some persons, will perish in the flames or in some other way. But if this should happen, the loss will be of the little paper, not of our discourse, a copy of which I shall keep forever in the hands of all the religious. May the gods preserve you, through whom we, all mortals whom the earth sustains, in a thousand ways, in harmonious discord, venerate and worship both them and the common father of them and of all mortals.*

[^1]: Virgil, Eclogues 2.65
