# Letter 108

To his beloved lord and brother Macrobius, Augustine

**1. 1.** My dearest sons, honorable men, when they had brought to your Benevolence my letter, in which I admonished and asked you not to rebaptize our subdeacon, wrote back to me that you had answered: "I cannot but receive those who come to me, and give them the faith they have asked for." And yet, if there comes to you one baptized in your communion, who was long separated from you, and through ignorance has thought himself to be baptized anew, and has demanded this—when you have inquired and learned where he was baptized, you do indeed receive him as he comes to you, yet you do not give him the faith he asks, but you teach the man that he has what he asks; and you do not heed the words of one in error, but apply the zeal of one who corrects. His own error, then, accuses one who badly gives what is now not to be given, and violates the Sacrament that has been given; it does not excuse the one who asks. Tell me therefore, I beseech you, how does he not have, who asked of you, what he had already received through me? If it is on account of "another's water" and "another's fountain"—which those who do not understand are accustomed to say, because it is written, *Abstain from another's water, and do not drink from another's fountain*[^1]—when Felician was separated from you in the party of Maximian, he was being dragged, an adulterer of truth, in the chain of sacrilege, as the words of your council indicate. If he had carried off your fountain with him, what fountain was there in which, he being separated, you baptized your own? But if he had baptized in another's fountain, why did you not rebaptize after him? For now your bishop sits at once with Primian, who was condemned by him and is his condemner.

**1. 2.** But as our people, who saw you in this matter, intimated to me by their letter, when they asked what you said about this, you answered that you, recently ordained, could not be the judge of your father's deeds, but remained in what you had received from your predecessors. Here indeed I grieved at your necessity, since I judge you, as I hear, a young man of good character. For what compels you to this answer, except the necessity of a bad cause? But if you attend, my brother, if you think rightly, if you fear God, no necessity compels you to persevere in a bad cause. For this answer of yours does not solve the question I put to you, but absolves our cause from all calumny of your accusation. For you say that you, recently ordained, cannot be the judge of your father's deeds, but remain in what you have received from your predecessors. Why, then, do we not rather remain in the Church which, as Scripture witnesses, beginning from Jerusalem and bearing fruit and growing through all nations, we received from the Lord Christ through the Apostles; and why are we now judged about the deeds of I know not what fathers, which are said to have been committed almost a hundred years ago? For if you do not dare to judge about your father, still placed in this life, whom you can question, why is it said to me that I should judge about a dead man long before I was born? And why is it said to the Christian nations that they should judge about the deeds of African traitors dead so many years ago, whom not even alive could so many Christians who then lived, placed in most remote lands, hear of or know? You who do not dare to judge Primian, who is living and known, why do you wish to lay upon me the judging of the ancient and unknown Caecilian? If you do not judge your fathers about their own deeds, why do you judge your brothers about the deeds of others?

**1. 3.** Or do you perhaps deny that we are brothers? But we hear better the Holy Spirit through the prophet commanding: *Hear, you who tremble at the word of the Lord: say, You are our brothers, to those who hate you and detest you, so that the name of the Lord may be honored, and may appear to them in gladness, but they themselves may be put to shame*[^2]. For truly, if the name of the Lord were more pleasant to men than the names of men, would Christ, crying out, *My peace I give you*[^3], be divided in his members by those who say, *I indeed am of Paul, but I of Apollos, and I of Cephas*[^4], and be torn apart by the names of men? Would Christ be blown away in derision in his own baptism, of which it is said, *This is he who baptizes*[^5]; of whom it is said, *Christ loved his Church and handed himself over for her, that he might sanctify her, cleansing her by the washing of water in the word*[^6]? Would he, then, in his own washing be blown away in derision, if the name of the Lord, whose own that baptism is, were more pleasant than the name of men, of whom you say, "Holy is what that one gives, not what this one gives"?

**2. 4.** And yet your colleagues, where they wished, heeded truth rather, and judged that not only the baptism which Primian gave in your communion, but also that which Felician gave in the sacrilegious schism of Maximian, was holy, on account of the gladness of the Lord's honor; and the mark, not only which Felician himself had received among you, but also which the deserter had stamped upon others outside us, they did not dare to violate when he was corrected, because they recognized it to be the King's. Of this so good deed of theirs you are unwilling to judge, where you ought praiseworthily to imitate them; and you follow their judgment in that for which they deserve to be detested by all. You fear to judge about Primian, lest you be compelled to hear what you must blame: no, rather, judge, and there you will be the more able to find what to praise. For we do not wish you to recall what Primian did badly, but what he did most excellently: who, in receiving those whom his condemner had baptized in a most criminal separation, corrected the error of men, but did not destroy the Sacraments of God. He recognized the good of Christ even in malignant men; but the evil of men, without violating the good of Christ, he amended. Or if this deed displeases you, attend at least to that, prudently mark it for your own good ability: that you do not judge the one Primian about the deeds of Primian himself, and you judge the Christian world about the deeds of Caecilian. You fear lest it pollute you, if you should know what you dare not avenge: absolve, then, the nations, which could not even know what you accuse.

**2. 5.** Nor yet was it the deed of the one Primian: you know too, in great part, I think, that nearly a hundred of your bishops, conspiring with Maximian in a damnable faction, dared to condemn Primian; and three hundred and ten, in the council of Bagai, as the words of its decree resound, "drove out with the thunderbolt of their sentence from the bosom of peace Maximian, the rival of the faith, the adulterer of truth, the enemy of mother Church, the minister of Dathan, Korah, and Abiram." Hence twelve others, who had been present at his ordination when he was raised up against Primian, were joined to him in condemnation not deferred: but to the rest, lest too great a cutting-off be made, with a day set, a delay for returning was granted, with their honors safe, if they should return within the limit of time. Nor did the three hundred and ten fear to recall to their college men accused of so great a sacrilege of Maximian, looking perhaps to what is written, *Love covers a multitude of sins*[^7]. But those to whom the time of delay was permitted baptized outside, apart from your communion, whomever they could baptize. For unless they had been outside, they could not have been invited, the delay being granted, to return. Then, both before the delay was ended and after it was ended, those twelve condemned with Maximian were accused before three or more proconsuls, so that they were driven from their seats by judicial force: among whom were Felician of Mustis, of whom I now treat, and Praetextatus of Assuras, recently dead, in whose place, when condemned, another had already been ordained. These two, not the one Primian but many of your fellow bishops—when they were celebrating with most frequent numbers the birthday of Optatus of Thamugadi—after their condemnation without delay, after the ending of the delay which had been given to the rest, after the accusation made public even with forensic din before so many consuls, received in their honors intact, and after them baptized no one. If this assertion is resisted, or if any of these things is denied, let me be required, at the peril of my episcopate, to prove what I say.

**2. 6.** The cause is ended, brother Macrobius: God did this, God willed this; it was of that hidden providence of his that in the cause of Maximian a mirror of correction should be set before you, so that all the criminal calumny against us—no, against the Church of Christ growing throughout the whole world—I do not say yours, lest I seem insulting to you, but at any rate that of your people, should be ended. For nothing at all remained of those things which are accustomed to be brought against us, as it were from the Scriptures, by men who do not understand them. For they have been accustomed to have these in their mouth: *Abstain from another's water*[^8]. But the answer is: It is not another's water, although it is in others; just as neither was that water of Maximian another's, from which you did not abstain. Likewise it is set before us: *They have become to me like deceitful water, having no faith*[^9]. But the answer is: This was said of feigned men, not pertaining to the Sacraments of God, which cannot be deceitful even in deceitful men. For surely those were deceitful who condemned Primian, as you yourselves declare, with false crimes; and yet the water was not deceitful in which, separated from you, they baptized whomever they could. For when you received it in those whom Felician and Praetextatus baptized outside, you perceived that it had been truthful even in deceitful men. It is set before us: *Whoever is baptized by a dead man, what does his washing profit him?*[^10] The answer is: If this is written of the baptism with which those baptize whom the Church has cast out as dead, he did not say that this washing does not exist, but that it profits nothing; which we too say. Yet when he comes with it to the Church, what outside was harmful will inside be profitable—not when the Baptism itself is repeated, but when the baptized one is corrected. As the council of Bagai speaks of Maximian and his associates, dead from your congregation, read into communion: "The truthful wave," it says, "has cast forth the shipwrecked members of some upon the harsh rocks. In the manner, as it were, of the Egyptians, the shores are full of the corpses of the perishing, for whom in death itself the punishment is greater, that, after the soul was wrenched out by the avenging waters, they do not find even burial itself." From this throng of the dead, as though coming to life again, you received Felician and Praetextatus in their honors, and those baptized by them in that death you did not rebaptize; because you recognized that the baptism of Christ given outside by dead men profits not the dead, yet that same baptism profits those coming to life again within. It is set before us: *Let not the oil of the sinner anoint my head*[^11]. The answer is that this is written to be understood of the smooth and deceptive flattery of a fawning sycophant, by which their head is anointed and grows, when sinners are praised in the desires of their soul, and those who have done unjust things are blessed. Which appears sufficiently from the previous verse; for the whole sentence is set down thus: *The just man will amend me in mercy, and will reprove me; but let not the oil of the sinner anoint my head*[^12]. He said that he preferred to be ground down by the truthful austerity of one who is merciful than to be lifted up by the deceptive praise of one who deceives. But however you understand it, certainly in those whom Felician and Praetextatus baptized in the sacrilege of Maximian, you either received the oil of sinners, or recognized that even with sinners ministering, the oil given is Christ's. For they were indeed sinners, when it was said of them in the council of Bagai: "Know that the guilty of a notorious crime, who by the deadly work of perdition glued together a filthy vessel with collected dregs, have been condemned."

**3. 7.** Let it suffice to have said these things about Baptism. But the cause of your separation is accustomed to be colored by these testimonies when they are not understood. It is written, *Do not share in the sins of others*[^13]. But the answer is that he shares in the sins of others who consents to evil deeds; not he who, while he himself is wheat, yet shares in the divine Sacraments together with the chaff so long as the threshing floor is being threshed. For it is written, *Go out from there, and do not touch the unclean thing*[^14]; and, *He who touches the polluted thing is polluted*[^15]; but by the consent of the will, by which the first man was deceived, not by the contact of the body, by which Judas too kissed Christ. For those fish of which the Lord speaks in the Gospel, good and bad, within the same nets, by which he fits together the unity of the congregation, swim alike until the end of the age, which is prefigured by the name of the shore—mixed in body, but separated in character. For it is written, *A little leaven corrupts the whole lump*[^16]; but the leaven of those who consent to those who do evil, not of those who, according to the prophet Ezekiel, *groan and mourn over the iniquities of the people of God which are done in their midst*[^17].

**3. 8.** Daniel too groans over this admixture of the bad; the three young men also groaned: the one attests this in prayer, the others in the furnace; and yet they did not separate themselves by bodily disjunction from the unity of the people whose sins they confessed. What and how great things all the prophets said against the very people among whom they were! And yet they did not seek for themselves, by bodily departure or segregation, another people among whom to be. The Apostles themselves tolerated the devil Judas mixed among them until the end, when he even destroyed himself by the noose, without any contamination of themselves; so that the Lord said to them, on account of his presence among them, *And you are clean, but not all*[^18]. Nor, on account of his uncleanness, as by leaven, was the lump of the unlike characters among them corrupted. Nor can it rightly be affirmed that his wickedness was hidden from them, except perhaps that by which he was even going to betray the Lord: for they themselves wrote of him that he was a thief, and carried off all that was put into the Lord's purse. No one calumniated them with that testimony, *You saw a thief, and you ran along with him*[^19]: for one runs along with the deeds of the bad not by the communion of the Sacraments, but by consent to those same deeds. How much the apostle Paul complains of false brethren! Yet he was not stained by bodily admixture with them, being separated by the diversity of a pure heart. For he rejoices that Christ is preached equally by certain men whom he knew to be envious; and surely envy is a diabolical vice.

**3. 9.** Finally bishop Cyprian, the Church now more copiously spread abroad, nearer to our times—on whose authority you sometimes try to confirm the repetition of Baptism—since that council, or those writings (if they are truly his, and not, as some suppose, composed and fabricated under his name), contain how greatly he loved unity, how by a most open exhortation he counseled that men were to be tolerated within it, even those against whom he himself held an opinion, lest the bond of peace be broken: attending above all to this, that if any human error has crept into one or the other of those to whom something seems other than the truth holds, brotherly concord being kept, love also *covers a multitude of sins*[^20]. This he so held, so loved, that if he had thought anything otherwise than the matter stands concerning the sacrament of Baptism, God would reveal even that to him, as the Apostle says to brethren walking in love: *As many of us, then, as are perfect, let us think this; and if you think anything otherwise, God will reveal this too to you. Nevertheless, in that which we have attained, let us walk*[^21]. Add to this that the fruitful branch, if it still had anything to be pruned, was pruned by the glorious sickle of martyrdom: not because he was killed for the name of Christ, but because he was killed for the name of Christ in the bosom of unity. For he himself wrote, and most confidently asserts, that those who are outside unity, even if they die for that name, can be killed but cannot be crowned. So much avails, whether for blotting out or for confirming sins, the love of unity, whether kept or violated.

**3. 10.** That same Cyprian, then, when, the Church laid waste by the persecution of the impious Gentiles, he lamented many who had lapsed, attributing this to the bad characters of those who lived in the Church itself in damnable conduct, groans over the characters of his own colleagues, and does not cover his groan with silence; but he says that they had advanced into such great covetousness that, while even brethren in the Church were hungry, they wished to have silver lavishly, seized estates by insidious frauds, increased their interest by multiplying usuries. I suppose that Cyprian was not stained by the avarice, estates, and interest of these men; nor yet did he separate himself from them by bodily segregation, but by the dissimilarity of his life. With them he touched the altar; but he did not touch their unclean life, when he so blamed and refuted it. For things that please are touched, things that displease are repelled. And therefore that most excellent bishop lacked neither the censure by which he might restrain sins, nor the caution by which he might keep the bond of unity. There is read in a certain letter of his which he wrote to the priest Maximus a clear and open opinion on this matter, by which he altogether commanded, holding the prophetic rule, that the unity of the Church must in no way be deserted on account of the admixture of the bad. "For although," he says, "tares seem to be in the Church, yet our faith or love ought not to be hindered, so that, because we perceive tares to be in the Church, we ourselves withdraw from the Church. We must only labor that we may be able to be wheat."

**3. 11.** This law of love was promulgated from the mouth of the Lord Christ; for these likenesses are his—both about the tares to be tolerated in the unity of the field throughout the world until the time of harvest, and about the bad fish to be tolerated within the same nets until the time of the shore. This law of love, then, if your forefathers had retained it in mind, if they had pondered it with the fear of God, they would not, on account of Caecilian and I know not what Africans—whether, as you suppose, truly criminal, or, what is rather to be believed, assailed by calumnies—have cut themselves off by a wicked separation from the Church which Cyprian himself described as stretching forth its rays through all nations, and extending its branches through all the earth in abundance of fruitfulness; they would not, I say, have torn themselves away from so many Christian nations, which altogether did not know who, or what, or against whom they objected. This is not done except either from private rancor rather than common benefit, or by that vice which Cyprian himself consequently numbers and mentions as to be avoided. For when he had commanded that, on account of the tares which are perceived in the Church, the Church is not to be deserted, he goes on and adds: "We must only labor that we may be able to be wheat, so that, when the wheat begins to be stored in the Lord's granaries, we may receive fruit for our work and labor. The Apostle in his Letter says: *But in a great house there are not only vessels of gold and silver, but also of wood and earthenware; and some indeed honored, some unhonored*[^22]. Let us give our effort, and labor as much as we can, that we may be a vessel of gold or silver. But to break the earthenware vessels is granted to the Lord alone, to whom also an iron rod has been given. A servant cannot be greater than his lord; nor let anyone claim for himself what the Father granted to the Son alone, so as to think himself able either to bear the winnowing-shovel and fan for winnowing and cleansing the threshing floor, or to separate all the tares from the wheat by human judgment. This is a proud presumption and a sacrilegious obstinacy, which a depraved fury assumes for itself; and while they always claim for themselves something more than gentle justice demands, they perish from the Church; and while they insolently exalt themselves, blinded by their very swelling, they lose the light of truth."

**3. 12.** What is clearer than this attestation of Cyprian; what more truthful? You see with how great an evangelical and apostolic light it shines forth; you see that those who, as though offended for their own justice by the iniquities of others, desert the unity of the Church, are themselves rather the most iniquitous. You see that they are the tares who are outside, those who were unwilling to tolerate tares in the unity of the Lord's field. You see that they are the chaff who are outside, those who were unwilling to tolerate such things in the unity of the great house. You see how truthfully it is written, *The evil son calls himself just; but he does not wash his going-out*[^23]: that is, the going-out by which he goes out from the Church he does not purify, does not excuse, does not defend, does not show pure and without crime—this is what "he does not wash" means; for if he did not call himself just, but were truly and legitimately just, he would not most impiously desert the good on account of the bad, but would most patiently endure the bad on account of the good, until the Lord himself, whether through himself or through his angels, should separate the tares from the wheat, the chaff from the grain, the vessels of wrath from the vessels of mercy, and the goats from the sheep, the bad fish from the good, at the end of the age.

**4. 13.** But if you try to take those testimonies of the Scriptures, which your forefathers believed should be understood or brought forward for dividing the people of God, otherwise than the sense of the divine words demands, now cease; attend to that mirror which God, by a most merciful provision—if you are wise—has set up for your admonition. I mean the cause of Felician: "the rival of the faith, the adulterer of truth, the enemy of mother Church," as was proclaimed in the council of Bagai, "the minister of Dathan, Korah, and Abiram"; about whom they added further that the open earth did not swallow him, but reserved him for the greater punishment of those above. "For carried off," they say, "he had gained his punishment by the shortcut of death; now he gathers heavier interest of death, since, alive, he is among the dead." For I ask whether those who, conspiring with him, condemned the innocence of Primian, then touched that unclean dead man. For if they touched him, surely by touching the polluted thing they were polluted. Why, then, in the communion of the same man, and to men separated from yours as though innocent, is "a delay for returning granted, so that, returning, they may have the foundations of unimpaired honor and faith," and why do those who, as though they were not present at the ordination of Maximian, deserve to hear that "the seedlings of the sacrilegious shoot did not pollute them"—men placed in the same party, bound together in the same schism, divided from you, allied to those men, set together in Africa, most well known, most friendly, most closely joined, who, though not present, ordained him, yet on his account condemned even the absent Primian? And the shoot of Caecilian is said to have defiled the most numerous, most remote, most unknown Christian peoples of the whole world, whose cause—I do not say cause, but many could not even know his name? They do not share in the sins of others, who not only knew the sin of Maximian, but even erected him by raising him up against Primian: and they share in the sins of others, who either, among far-off nations, did not know that Caecilian was made bishop, or, among nearer ones, only heard of it, or knew it done in Africa itself simply and peaceably, or in Carthage itself raised him up against no one! Nor did they run along with a thief, who communicated with him of whom the advocate Nummasius says, speaking on behalf of your bishop Restitutus then present, that "by the hidden, well-nigh theft of sacrilege, he invaded the primacy of the episcopal name"; nor did they set their portion with an adulterer, who communicated with the adulterer of truth; nor was their whole lump corrupted by a little leaven, when they favored him, when they remained in his party—not as though, unknowing, cut off by you, but taking care that he be cut off by you against yourselves and erected; and then you yourselves, who so invited them to return that you said those joined to Maximian by so great a fellowship were unpolluted by the sacrilegious shoot, because you received Praetextatus also and Felician with their honors safe, because you are at peace with them (since even today you see Felician sitting with you)—you are stained by no communion of the sins of others, defiled by no contact of uncleanness, corrupted by the leaven of no one's malignity: and to the Christian world, by these testimonies, the crime of another is objected, the division of unity is defended from a deadly separation, and by a cut-off branch, as an unclean branch, the one that has remained in the root of the true mother is accused!

**5. 14.** What of that in which you are accustomed to glory, the persecutions you have endured? If it is not the cause but the penalty that makes martyrs, then when it was said, *Blessed are they who suffer persecution*, it was added in vain, *for justice's sake*[^24]. Do not even the Maximianists most easily surpass you in the glory of this title, who endured persecution not only afterward with you, but earlier and from you? The words of the advocate accusing Maximian, in the presence of your colleague Restitutus—who had already been ordained in the place of Salvius of Membressa, condemned with those other eleven without delay, before the very day of the delay had passed—are the words I recalled a little before. Titianus too, on the very day the delay was over, accused Felician and Praetextatus with most grave words of the whole conspiracy against Primian. The council of Bagai too was more than once alleged in the proconsular records, and then in the municipal ones; tribunals were stirred up, most threatening commands obtained, it was demanded and decreed that those resisting be brought to coercion, the office was lent, the aids of the cities granted, by which what was judged might be fulfilled. Why, then, do you contend with us about commanded persecution, you who did not divide these things with us on equal terms? For since one who endures persecution does not always come to suffering, your clerics and Circumcellions so arranged between us that you should endure persecution, we suffering. But, as I said, contend about that glory with the Maximianists, who recite against you the forensic records, where you harried them with persecutions through the judges: but plainly, with certain of them corrected by such coercion, you afterward were reconciled; whence neither is our concord to be despaired of, if God deign to help, and to inspire in you a peaceable mind. For even that which is accustomed to be said against us by your party, more with a slanderous than a truthful mouth—*Their feet are swift to shed blood*[^25]—we rather have experienced in the so great brigandages of your Circumcellions and clerics, who, mangling human bodies with most atrocious slaughter, have stained so many of our places with blood: whose leaders, when at your entry into this country they led you with their bands, shouting "Praise to God!" amid their songs, had these, as it were, as voices, as the trumpet of battles in all their brigandages. Yet on another day, struck and goaded by the stings of your words, which you hurled against them through a Punic interpreter with an honorable and noble indignation of liberty, irritated rather than delighted by their compliances, they snatched themselves, as we could hear from those present who narrated it, with raging movements from the midst of the congregation; nor, after their feet swift to shed blood, did you salt the pavements with any water—which after our men your clerics thought should be done.

**5. 15.** But, as I had begun to say, this testimony from the Scriptures, which you are accustomed to fling at us more by reviling than by proving—*Their feet are swift to shed blood*[^26]—that grandiloquent sentence of the council of Vagai vomited out even against Felician and Praetextatus with a vehement rush. For when they had said of Maximian what seemed should be said: "Nor does a just death," they say, "condemn him alone for his crime; the chain of sacrilege drags very many also to fellowship in the crime, of whom it is written: *The venom of asps under their lips, whose mouth is full of cursing and bitterness. Their feet are swift to shed blood*[^27]," and so on. Which said, in order then to show who they were whom the chain of sacrilege dragged to fellowship in the crime, and to condemn them with Maximian by like severity: "Therefore the guilty," they say, "of a notorious crime, Victorianus of Carcabia"—to whom they add eleven others, among whom Felician of Mustis and Praetextatus of Assuras. After these things were said against them, so great a concord was made with them that nothing of their honors was diminished; no one washed by them, after the feet of the washers swift to shed blood, was judged to be washed again. Why, then, is our concord to be despaired of? May God turn aside the envy of the devil, and may the peace of Christ conquer in our hearts: and, as the same Apostle says, *let us forgive one another, if anyone has a complaint against another, just as God in Christ has forgiven us*[^28], so that, as I have already said and must often say, love may cover a multitude of sins.

**6. 16.** But you, brother, with whom I now treat, and over whom in Christ, as he himself knows, I desire to rejoice—if you wish to undertake the defense of the party of Donatus in this cause of Maximian by the faculty of your talent and eloquence, and not to act mendaciously, since the memory of it is recent, the men against whom these things were done being still placed in the body, with so many proconsular and municipal records too, where the Catholic Church has always been warned against you: so that you will confess that neither that about "another's water," and about "the water of falsehood," and "the washing of a dead man," and whatever else there is of this kind, is to be understood as you are accustomed; but rather in this way, that the baptism of Christ, given to the Church for participation in eternal salvation, is to be judged neither foreign outside the Church, nor reckoned alien among aliens, but avails to perdition for the foreign and alien, and to salvation for its own and proper; and in those men, when they are converted to the peace of the Church, the error is amended, the Sacrament not destroyed by punishing the error, and that what was harmful outside to the perverse begins to profit inside the corrected man: nor again will you take that about "not communicating in the sins of others," about "separation from the bad," about "not touching the unclean and polluted thing," about "guarding against the corruption of a little leaven," and other such things, as you are accustomed, lest you involve yourselves inextricably in the cause of Maximian. But, being wise, you will assert this, you will hold this, which sound doctrine commends, which the true rule proves by prophetic and apostolic examples: that the bad are rather to be tolerated, lest the good be deserted, than the good to be deserted, that the bad be separated. Only let the reprobate be sundered from imitation, from consent, from likeness of life and character, growing together, mixed together in tribulation, gathered together within the nets until the time of harvest, of winnowing, and of the shore. But about persecution—whatever was done by your people through the judges to expel the Maximianists and disturb them from their seats—how will you defend it, except that your more prudent men did this with the mind of correcting, not of harming, by a moderate terror; but if any exceeded the human measure, as in those things which the very city attests Salvius of Membressa suffered, that they do not prescribe to the rest, as chaff set with the wheat in one communion of the Sacraments, but separated by dissimilarity of life?

**6. 17.** Since these things are so, I embrace this defense of yours. For it will be this, if it is truthful; and it will be conquered by truth, if it is not this. I embrace, I say, your defense; but you perceive that this is mine too. Why, then, do we not labor to be wheat together in the unity of the Lord's threshing floor, and tolerate the chaff together? Why not, I beseech you—what cause, for whose good, for what benefit? Tell me. Unity is fled, so that peoples purchased by the blood of the one Lamb are inflamed against one another by contrary zeals, and the sheep are divided, as though ours among ourselves—the sheep of that householder who said to the servant, *Feed my sheep*[^29], not "Feed your sheep"; and of whom it is said, *That there may be one flock and one shepherd*[^30]; who cries out in the Gospel, *By this all will know that you are my disciples, if you have true love among you*[^31]; and, *Let both grow until the harvest, lest perhaps, when you wish to gather the tares, you uproot the wheat together with them*[^32]. Unity is fled, so that here the husband, there the wife resorts: let him say, "Hold unity with me, for I am your husband"; let her answer, "There I die, where my father is": so that in one bed they divide Christ, whom we detest if they divide the bed. Unity is fled, so that kinsmen, and fellow citizens, and friends, and guests, and whoever are bound to one another by human relationship, both Christians, may be in agreement in entering banquets, in giving and receiving in marriage, in buying and selling, in pacts and agreements, in greetings, in consents, in conversations, in all their affairs and dealings, and at the altar of God be at discord. For those who, however great a discord conceived elsewhere, ought there to end it, and, according to the precept of the Lord, first be reconciled to their brethren, and then offer the gift at the altar, are elsewhere in agreement, there at discord.

**6. 18.** Unity is fled, so that against the wickednesses of your people (for I do not wish to say "yours") we seek public laws, and against those very laws the Circumcellions are armed—laws which they despise with the very fury with which, raging, they stirred them up against you. Unity is fled, so that against their own owners rustic audacity is erected, and fugitive slaves, against apostolic discipline, are not only alienated from their masters, but even threaten their masters; nor only threaten, but also plunder by most violent assaults, with your "agonistic confessors" as authors and leaders, and in the crime itself as chiefs—who at "Praise to God!" adorn your honor, who at "Praise to God!" shed the blood of others, so that you, on account of the odium of men, when your people are gathered and dispersed, promise that you will restore the plunder to those from whom it was taken. And yet you do not even wish this, that you should be able to fulfill it, lest you be forced too much to offend the audacity of those whom your priests thought necessary to themselves. For they boast of their preceding merits toward you, demonstrating and enumerating, before that law by which you rejoice that liberty was restored to you, that your priests held, through them, the places and basilicas laid waste, our people put to flight; so that, if you should wish to be severe against them, you would appear ungrateful for their benefits.

**6. 19.** Unity is fled, so that whoever among us refuse to bear discipline flee to them to be defended, that they may be offered to you to be rebaptized. Like that subdeacon from the estate, Rusticianus, on whose account I have been compelled to write these things to you with great grief and fear: who, on account of his reprobate and perverse character, was excommunicated by his priest, and, having become also the debtor of many in that region, against the ecclesiastical authority and against his creditors sought no other refuge than that he might be flogged anew by you, and be loved by those men as though most clean. Already such a deacon of ours, himself too excommunicated by his priest, your predecessor rebaptized, and made your deacon, who not many days afterward, mixed, as he desired, with the audacity of those same lost men, was slain in a nocturnal assault, in the midst of his own brigandage and arson, by the rush of a relieving multitude. These are the fruits of this division which you are unwilling to heal, fleeing unity in the same way as the division itself ought to have been fled; which is in itself foul, and damnable to God, even if on its account these and other so horrible and wicked things were not done.

**7. 20.** Let us recognize, then, brother, the peace of Christ, and hold it together, and, as far as God grants, let us strive together to be good, and together amend the bad, unity being safe, by as much discipline as we can, and on account of that very unity tolerate them with as much patience as we can: lest, as Christ admonished, when we wish to gather before the time the tares which the blessed Cyprian attested are seen and perceived not outside but in the Church, we uproot the wheat together with them. For you do not in truth have certain privileges of holiness peculiar to yourselves, such that our bad men pollute us and your bad men do not pollute you; such that the ancient timidity of traitors, which we do not know, contaminates us, and the present audacity of lost men, which you see, does not contaminate you. Let us recognize that ark which prefigured the Church: let us be clean animals there together; and let us not refuse that even the unclean be carried in it with us until the end of the flood. They were in the ark together, but Noah did not offer them together to the Lord in the odor of sacrifice from among the unclean. Nor yet was the ark for that reason deserted before the time by any of the clean on account of the unclean. The raven alone deserted it, and separated itself before the time from the communion of that ark; but it was of the pairs of the unclean, not of the sevens of the clean: let us detest the uncleanness of this separation. For whoever are praiseworthy in such characters, this separation alone makes them damnable: because the evil son calls himself just, but does not wash his going-out; though, insolently puffed up and himself blinded by his swelling, he dares to say what the Prophet, foreseeing it, detested: *Do not touch me, for I am clean*[^33]. Whoever, then, before the time, as though on account of the uncleanness of certain men, deserts the congregation of this unity, like the ark in the flood carrying the clean and unclean, shows himself rather to be that which he flees.
