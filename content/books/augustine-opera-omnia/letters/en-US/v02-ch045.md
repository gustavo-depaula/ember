# Letter 144

TO THE HONORABLE AND DESERVEDLY ESTEEMED, MY DEAREST AND MOST LONGED-FOR BROTHERS OF EVERY RANK OF HONOR, THE PEOPLE OF CIRTA, FROM AUGUSTINE, BISHOP.

**1.** If what was grievously saddening us in your city has been done away with, if the hardness of the human heart that resisted a most manifest and, so to speak, public truth has been overcome by the force of that same truth, if the sweetness of peace is now relished and the love of unity no longer batters wounded eyes but enlightens and quickens sound ones, these are not our works but God's. I would not attribute these things to human works at all, not even if so great a conversion of the multitude had come about while we were present among you, speaking and exhorting. He brings this about and accomplishes it, who through his ministers admonishes outwardly by signs of things, but teaches inwardly by the things themselves through his own self. Nor for that reason should we be the more sluggish in coming to see you, since whatever praiseworthy thing has been done in you was done not by us but by him who alone does wondrous things. For we ought to hasten much more eagerly to behold the works of God than our own, since we too, if we are any good work, are his and not of men. Hence the Apostle said: *Neither he who plants is anything, nor he who waters, but God who gives the growth*[^1].

**2.** Xenocrates, as you write and as we too recall from those writings, by his discourse on the fruit of temperance suddenly converted Polemo to a different way of life—Polemo who was not only given to drink but was even drunk at the time. And yet, although he, as you have prudently and truly understood, was not won over to God but only freed from the dominion of self-indulgence, still I would not attribute even that, the better thing that was done in him, to human work, but to divine. For if there are any good things in the body itself, which is our lowest part—such as shape and strength and health, and anything of the kind—these come only from God, the creator and perfecter of nature; how much more, then, can no other than he bestow the goods of the mind! What can human folly think more proud, or more ungrateful, than to suppose that, while God makes a man beautiful along with his flesh, a man is made chaste in mind by himself? In the book of Christian Wisdom it is written thus: *When I knew that no one can be continent unless God grant it; and this very thing was wisdom, to know whose gift it was*[^2]. So if Polemo, having become continent from being self-indulgent, had thus known whose gift it was, so as to worship him devoutly with the superstitions of the gentiles cast aside, he would have come to be not only continent but truly wise and soundly religious—which would have availed him not only for the honorableness of the present life but also for the immortality of the life to come. How much less, then, ought I to claim for myself this conversion of yours, or of your people, of which you have just now informed us—a conversion that, neither with my speaking nor even with my being present, was without doubt brought about by God in those in whom it was truly brought about! This, therefore, you must above all recognize; this you must consider devoutly and humbly. To God, brothers, to God give thanks; fear God, lest you fall away; love him, that you may make progress.

**3.** But if love of some man still secretly keeps certain people apart, and fear of some man deceptively brings them together, let those who are such observe that they neither deceive as a witness nor escape as a judge the God to whom the human conscience lies bare. And if anything about the very question of unity disturbs them out of concern for their own salvation, let them, as I judge, wring from themselves this most just demand: that concerning the catholic Church, that is, the Church spread throughout the whole world, they believe rather what the divine Scriptures say than what human tongues slander. But as for the dissension itself that has arisen among men—who, whatever sort of men they were, certainly do not prejudge the promises of God, who said to Abraham, *In your seed shall all the nations be blessed*[^3], which was believed when it was heard foretold and is denied when it is seen fulfilled—let them for the present consider only this very brief and, unless I am mistaken, most unanswerable point: either this case was tried in the ecclesiastical court overseas, or it was not tried. If it was not tried there, the fellowship of Christ throughout all the peoples overseas is innocent, in whose communion we rejoice, and therefore from those innocent men they are cut off by a sacrilegious division; but if this case was indeed tried there, who does not understand, who does not perceive, who does not see that those whose communion is cut off from there were the ones defeated in it? Let them choose, then, which they would rather believe: what the ecclesiastical judges pronounced, or what the defeated litigants mutter. Against this combination of points, most brief to state and most easy to grasp, attend diligently, as befits your prudence, to how nothing sober can be answered to it; and yet our Polemo is overturned the more by the drunkenness of an inveterate error. Pardon a letter perhaps more long-winded than agreeable, but, as I think, more useful than charming, my honorable and deservedly esteemed lords, my dearest and most longed-for brothers. And as for our coming to you, may God fulfill the desire of us both. For we cannot put into words with what ardor of love we are kindled to see you; but we have no doubt at all that you kindly believe it.

[^1]: 1 Cor 3:7
[^2]: Wis 8:21
[^3]: Gen 22:18
