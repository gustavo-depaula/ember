# Letter 133

To the most excellent lord, and deservedly distinguished, and most dear son Marcellinus, Augustine sends greetings in the Lord.

**1.** Those Circumcellions and clerics of the party of Donatus, whom the care of public discipline had brought from Hippo to judgment for their deeds, I have learned were heard by your Nobility, and that very many of them confessed concerning the homicide which they committed against Restitutus, a Catholic priest, and concerning the beating of Innocentius, another Catholic priest, and his eye gouged out, and his finger cut off. Whence the greatest solicitude has been struck into me, lest perhaps your Sublimity should judge that they ought to be punished by so great a severity of the laws that they suffer such things as they did. And therefore by this letter I adjure your faith which you have in Christ, by the mercy of the Lord Christ himself, that you neither do this nor at all permit it to be done. For although we could dissemble about their destruction, who seem to have been presented to the examination not by our accusers, but by the official notification of those to whom the vigilance of guarding the public peace pertained; yet we do not wish the sufferings of the servants of God to be avenged, as if by way of retaliation, by like punishments. Not that we forbid the license of crimes to be taken away from criminal men; but we wish rather this to suffice, that, alive and maimed in no part of the body, they be either directed, by the coercion of the laws, from insane unquiet to the leisure of soundness, or, from malignant works, assigned to some useful work. This too is indeed called a condemnation; but who would not understand that it is to be named a benefit rather than a punishment, where neither is the audacity of raging relaxed, nor the medicine of repenting withdrawn?

**2.** Fulfill, O Christian judge, the office of a devout father; so be inflamed against iniquity that you remember to take counsel for humanity: and do not, at the atrocities of sinners, exercise the lust of avenging; but to the wounds of sinners apply the will of healing. Do not lose the paternal diligence which you preserved in the very inquiry, when you drew out the confession of such great crimes, not by stretching them on the rack, not by furrowing claws, not by burning flames, but by the beating of rods. Which manner of coercion is wont to be applied by masters of the liberal arts, and by parents themselves, and often even in the courts by bishops. Do not, then, avenge more atrociously what you found out more leniently. The necessity of inquiring is greater than that of punishing: for to this end even the gentlest men diligently and instantly examine a hidden crime, that they may find those whom to spare. Whence for the most part it is necessary that inquiry be exercised more sharply, that, the crime being manifested, there may be where mercy may appear. For all good works love to be set in the light; not for the sake of human glory, but, as the Lord says, *that they may see your good works, and glorify your Father who is in heaven*[^1]. And therefore it did not suffice the Apostle to admonish that we keep meekness, but that we even make it known to all: *Let your meekness,* he says, *be known to all men*[^2]; and in another place, *showing meekness to all men*[^3]. Whence neither would that most illustrious gentleness of holy David—when, his enemy being handed into his hands, he clemently spared him—stand out, unless his power equally appeared. Let not, then, the power of avenging exasperate you, from whom the necessity of examining did not shake out gentleness. Do not, the crime found, seek the striker, in finding whom you were unwilling to apply the torturer.

**3.** Finally, you have been sent for the benefit of the Church. This I attest profits the Catholic Church—or, that I may seem not to overstep the measure of my dispensation, this profits the Church pertaining to the diocese of Hippo-Regius—this is expedient. If you do not hear a friend asking, hear a bishop counseling. Although, since I speak to a Christian, especially in such a cause, I would not arrogantly say that it befits you to hear a bishop commanding, most excellent lord, and deservedly distinguished, and most dear son. Whence I indeed know that ecclesiastical causes are especially enjoined upon your Excellency, but because I believe this care pertains to the most illustrious and respectable proconsul, I have given a letter to him too; which I ask that you not find it burdensome to hand to him yourself and to allege, if there is need: and I beseech you both not to judge our intercession, or suggestion, or solicitude importunate; and not to discolor the sufferings of the Catholic servants of God—which ought to be useful to the weak for spiritual edification—by the reciprocal punishment of the enemies from whom they suffered, but rather, judicial severity being broken, do not at all neglect to commend both your faith, because you are sons of the Church, and the meekness of that same mother. May the omnipotent God increase your Eminence with all good things, most excellent lord, and deservedly distinguished, and most dear son.

[^1]: Matt 5:16
[^2]: Phil 4:5
[^3]: Titus 3:2
