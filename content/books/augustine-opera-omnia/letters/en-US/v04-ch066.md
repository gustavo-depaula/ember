# Letter 2\*

TO THE LORD DESERVEDLY DISTINGUISHED, MUCH TO BE HONORED BY ME WITH THE AFFECTION OF SINCERE LOVE, MY SON FIRMUS, AUGUSTINE SENDS GREETINGS IN THE LORD.

**1.** That I repay a reply rather late was caused by the care of my necessities, not by contempt of your will, which religiously desired it. Nor was this impediment ended, so that there should be leisure at some time to write back, but rather broken through, so that, amid the very things which most troublesomely stood in the way, I might snatch that which most gratefully pressed, lest I be too great a deferrer of this debt, which it has come about, to my own wish, that I should owe. But I did not undertake to discharge this except after first inspecting your letters: one which, after the books *The City of God* were sent by me, you most gratefully returned; another which the priest Lazarus brought me, where you deferred to send the declamation of our son which I had asked for; a third which you sent with that same declamation.

**2.** About these three letters of yours I have determined to speak with you by this one of mine. In that, then, which I first mentioned, of the twenty-two you said you had read the ten preceding books, and about these you wrote back in such a way that it appeared how well you had read them; but the twelve that follow I know you had not then yet read, but whether you have now read them I do not know; for it could be that, led by goodwill, you gave them all to friends to copy before you ran through them all by reading, and had not yet received those given back, or that, about those received and read, you now thought you should keep silent to me, judging it enough that about those ten you wrote back not cursorily, but disputed somehow diligently. You could also have wished to test whether I would direct my mind to demanding the part of your debt which I felt had not yet been rendered, or whether I would by no means care either to notice or to demand back the completions of this matter. Behold, then, what was done too little by you both I know and demand: about the twelve later books, render what you owe!

**3.** Among these is that eighteenth text of this work, which you attentively heard with us when it was read on three continuous afternoons; and from this you were kindled with a most flaming zeal to have them all, nor did you cease to press until you arrived at it. Let me not, I beg, be a troublesome exactor of fruit, who have made a most copious sowing. For as for the fact that in another of your letters you excuse yourself from receiving the Sacrament of regeneration, you refuse the whole fruit of so many books which you love; for their fruit is not this, that they delight the reader, nor this, that they make one not knowing to know many things, but this, that they persuade one either to enter the city of God without delay, or to dwell in it perseveringly; of which two the first is conferred by regeneration, the second by the love of justice. If these things do not work in those by whom they are read and praised, what do they work? Of these, then, as far as concerns you yourself, since they could not work in you even that which is first, however much you proclaim them, they have as yet worked nothing.

**4.** "But indeed a burden of so great a weight cannot be borne by powers still weak and not strengthened." For this is the first cause of your excuse; nor do you consider, O men, whoever you are who dread this burden, that you are most easily surpassed in bearing it by women, by whose religious multitude of faithful and chaste ones the Church is made fruitful. For if you considered it, you would by a necessary shame drive away superfluous fear. One of them (for such I ought to believe her, over whom I now rejoice) is your wife. For I do not fear to offend you, when by the example of a woman I exhort you to enter the city of God; for if the matter is difficult, the weaker sex is already there; but if it is easy, there is no cause why the stronger should not be there. It is not, then, to be ashamed of for a man to follow a woman entering into that thing which demands the powers of the mind, but rather it is to be ashamed of not even to follow her entering, and, with her placed within, where the strength of true salvation and devotion is received, to be detained outside, you the more capacious of strength. Nor indeed, when you have entered, will you follow her even there, but she you; for you will go before in strength what you follow in time. For I believe that, although she is faithful, you, a catechumen, nevertheless suggest to her some things pertaining to religion which you have read and she has not; but the things which she knows and you do not yet, she cannot lay open to you, for the mysteries of regeneration become known, rightly and orderly, only to those receiving them; so, while you are found more instructed in doctrine, she is more secure in the Sacrament.

**5.** But what does the knowledge even of the highest good itself profit, if that be not received by which alone whatever is evil is escaped? But evidently it is difficult both to bear the burden of new virtues, and easy to be pressed by the burden of old sins. These are rather the burdens to be dreaded, which are bound for the overwhelming and eternally sinking of men, nor are loosed in any other way except by regeneration in Christ, that a man may become a member of the head, the mediator, who, although he was distinct from us by divine majesty, deigned to become, by human weakness, near to us.

**6.** "And to religion," you say, "by this slowness progress is made. For he promises greater reverence for the faith who, about to come into the august secrets of the sacred mystery, approaches the more remote things hesitantly." For this you said was the other cause of your delay. But nowhere except within is it safe to make progress in religion, since now, on account of eternal life, the uncertainties of this life are not feared. For it is not to be inquired by vain and damnable curiosity, when each one may die, or whether this body be dissolved by disease, or whether the soul be snatched thence by some sudden and unexpected violence rushing in. Accordingly, just as that divine sentence which is written in the Gospel is thought of: *Unless one is reborn of water and the Spirit, he will not enter into the kingdom of God*[^1], so also, that, reborn, he may live justly, that other is to be thought of, which also proceeded from the mouth of Christ: *Unless your justice abounds more than that of the scribes and Pharisees, you will not enter into the kingdom of heaven*[^2]. But the scribes and Pharisees he himself shows in another place to have been wont to say good things by good doctrine, and yet not to do the good things they said[^3]; and thereby justice abounds more than theirs in those who both say good things on account of the truth of the doctrine and do good things on account of the love of justice, as the apostle James says: *Faith without works is dead in itself*[^4], because *even the demons believe and tremble*[^5], as he himself said. Yet they will surely not be saved, since they always work evil. On account of which the apostle Paul too defined that faith of the members of Christ *which works through love*[^6]. In it, then, within, progress is to be made; for the ill-persuading desire of this world is diminished as the love of God grows, and consumed when the love of God is perfect. One so making progress within, whom the last day of this vaporous life shall seize by whatever kind of death, whatever was lacking to his perfection out of justice is filled up out of grace. But for those who think that should be deferred which they do not deny to be good, there is that terribly thundered sentence of the divine Scriptures: *Do not delay to be converted to the Lord, nor put it off from day to day! For suddenly his wrath will come, and in the time of vengeance he will destroy you*[^7]. This, I beg you, that it be far from you: *break all delays*[^8] and seize the fortified camp, not which you may storm, but where, safe, you may bravely fight against the enemy, who, by accusing then, now even by craftily praising the Christian religion, deters from its reception; for he who then suggested to the minds of men that it was so great an evil [that ..., now that it is so great a good], that what is begun is most difficultly fulfilled—that is an insidious praise. Beware the wolf clothed in a sheep's skin! For attempting the things which you fear as harsh, let your hope be in God, and they will become gentle.

**7.** For that too, set down by you in the third place of your excuse, that in these matters his will is especially to be awaited, by whose will we are driven into all our appetites; where you added that an account of those things is not to be rendered by men which God suggests to us that we should will, since without him both that nothing has been done and that nothing can be done all, learned and unlearned, agree. This whole thing, then, you ought not so to think, as to seem to yourself to do the will of God, when you are unwilling to do his precepts, of which this too is one which I just now mentioned: *Do not delay to be converted to the Lord, nor put it off from day to day*[^9], but rather so consider these things, that you trust that, not by your own powers, but by his help, you will do what he commanded for your everlasting salvation, and therefore commit yourself, without delay, not to yourself, Firmus, the weak, but to him, the powerful, who can do all things, to change your life for the better and to receive the grace of regeneration; nor await when he may will, as though about to offend him if you will it first, since, with him helping and working, you will will it, whenever you have willed. His mercy indeed anticipates you, that you may will; but when you will, you surely will it. For if we do not will, when we will, then he does not confer anything on us, when he brings it about that we will. What, certainly, do I do, when I speak these things to you, but that you may will? But I in one way, he in another; I from without, he from within; I, when you hear or read, he, when you think and that you may think; I by speaking, he in an ineffable manner; I only by his giving, he through himself; I as his minister, and having this ministry from him, he as one not needing a minister, and the maker even of ministers, and using faithful ministers to this end, that he may bestow on them too the good of this work; finally, I as a man who for the most part cannot even persuade, he as God who has it in his power to be able [to persuade], when he wills.

**8.** For this the sacred Letters say of him, and any mind however little imbued with devotion. For who, unless too much turned away from the true, would dare to think or believe that God wishes to persuade anything and cannot? He persuades you, then, when he wills, whether through our ministry or in whatever other way he wills; that he may do this, he is to be prayed to by me, as you are to be exhorted. For if you obey the true and wholesome precepts, it is of his grace; if you do not obey, it is of your fault; from which fault, whomever he frees, he does it by mercy (among whom I want you to be, and now exhort you to will it, and ask him), but whomever he does not free, he does it by judgment. But his many hidden judgments we do not know; this, however, we know, that none of them can be unjust. He surely wished it to profit his own, who understand well, toward not deferring the day of regeneration, that he made the day of death uncertain. That we are driven into all our appetites by his will is so as you say. For I understand all the appetites of those things about which you were speaking when you said this. For you said that in these matters, to which I was surely exhorting you and which you think are to be deferred, his will is especially to be awaited, by whose will we are driven into all our appetites, and you added: "For an account of those things is not to be rendered by men which God suggests to us that we should will." If, then, you understand the appetites of those things, it is true; for that we are driven by his will into all appetites, even impious ones, is not true; or if you understand this for the reason that it does not seem absurd to say that that comes about by his will which would not come about except with him permitting, the sentence is not to be refuted. For nothing at all comes about except what either he himself does or he himself permits to be done, and since, willing, he does it, willing, he also permits, nothing at all comes about if he is unwilling. Yet truly it is said that whatever displeases him comes about against his will. He permits, however, that evils come about, because he is powerful even out of evils not his own to make his own goods. For everything that is just is good, and it is just to punish sins; it is good, therefore, to punish sins; it is good also to pardon sins; it is good to do things useful for freeing men; so it is good to restrain sins, that they go no further; it is good to free [people] from sins, even others' sins, and from the deserts of sins. And so, by punishing some, by forgiving others, by turning others to the advantages and helps of the devout, by all these he makes goods out of evils, he who permits evils to come about. Nor would God, the most powerful maker of every good, appear, if he did not allow evils to be, although what good he makes of them I cannot find. Yet he has removed them, by a vast separation, from the kingdom of the blessed, and thereby, even in the evils which he permitted to come about, he has not failed of all operation, and, where he willed that they not be, he made it so; which if he had done everywhere, not all goods would be in things, since the goods which he could make out of evils surely would not have existed. Nor yet do we therefore profit God by our evils, which descend from the will of either an evil angel or man, as though we had added a number to his good works; for what do we profit even when we are good? It would, then, have profited us, if we had never been evil, not God, who knows even out of evils to do good. Further, if our goodness had even always existed, it would have profited God nothing; how much less does our malice profit him, although out of it, through the omnipotence of his goodness, he could work goods; which goods, nevertheless, would not have existed, if our evils had not preceded, but, even if they had not existed, it would be neither better nor worse for him, to whom no evil can happen nor good accede, because his beatitude is neither diminished by any evil nor increased by any good; accordingly let us so say that all things come about by his will, that we distinguish the one permitting from the one doing, because we cannot deny him to be the judge. But does he, when judging and rendering to each according to his works[^10], condemn anyone on account of those works which he himself did in him? Far be it! But yet on account of those which he himself permitted to come about; for they would surely not come about without the omnipotent one, who could not be ignorant of them. Nothing can come about without God, whether doing or permitting.

**9.** Where, then, you said that all, learned and unlearned, agree both that nothing was done without him and that nothing can be done, no controversy is to be made about the agreement of all the unlearned, lest it implore of us a delay of disputation which there is none between us, since whether all agree or some dissent; you will nevertheless grant that it was said, if only, as I admonished, you distinguish the one doing from the one permitting, since it is most truly said that nothing was done nor can be done without the omnipotent God, whether permitting or doing. Accordingly he permits sins to come about, he does not do them; even if, for the deserts of other sins, he hands some over into the desires of their heart[^11], to do what is not fitting, either by deserting them, or by either sending or permitting malignant angels too for their deception, even every such sin pertains to those who, that they might so sin, deserved to be handed over by the divine and just judgment, but to God it does not there pertain except the very vengeance which is just for sin.

**10.** Therefore each one is driven to illicit appetites *drawn away and enticed by his own concupiscence*[^12], from which evil, when God frees, he has mercy, when he does not free, he judges, to whom the Church sings *mercy and judgment*[^13]; but when he does the one, when the other, the discretion and counsel is in his own power; to which counsel of his this too pertains, that we do not know these things, and indeed I think we shall always be ignorant of them. But whoever says that, in order for man to sin, it ought not to have been permitted by him, attends too little to the fact that that nature which ought not to sin and yet can already sin is without doubt better, if, permitted, it does not sin, than if, not permitted, it does not sin. Wherefore he, good and just, both made from the beginning such a nature which, permitted, would not sin if it had been unwilling to sin, and so punished it, sinning, that, serving the sins that dominate, it should also be made subject to evil angels—the nature which was unwilling to serve the justice of its own Lord—being [otherwise] to be made equal to the good angels. Hence are these errors and miseries of mortals which we see, with which this human life is filled, where a false happiness harms its lovers worse—desiring to enjoy which they heap sins upon sins—than the hard and harsh misery which all wish to bear but not all know how; for no one lacks it except after death, if he has led this life devoutly, since, as the divine eloquences have it, *a heavy yoke is upon the sons of Adam from the day of their going out from their mother's womb until the day of their burial into the mother of all*[^14], so far that [not even] baptized little children—for whom that which alone they drew from the damned stock, original sin, has been washed and taken away through the washing of regeneration—are found immune from this sentence. For that men so receive through the grace of Christ is not a gift of this age but a pledge of the future; for this generation from Adam is wholly damned; which damnation, that one may escape it, regeneration was instituted. But this damnation, as far as concerns the miseries of this age, no one even of the devout ends except after death, but the impious not even after death; nay, evils are even increased for the impious after the dissolution of this body and after the reception [of judgment], the goods being withdrawn from them which here they badly used. But why this regeneration is given not only to those greater in age, but also to some little children and not to others, the judgments of God are inscrutable[^15], but in them there is nothing hidden that is not also just.

**11.** When I exhort you to receive this regeneration, do not await that God may will it, but do what he commands; and hence let it rather become known to you that he willed it, because you could not have done it unless he himself had willed it. For the love by which you are about to do this is from God, but the concupiscence by which you are detained so as not yet to do it is not from God. Accordingly, as long as you do not do it, because by your own fault you are either, unknowing, deceived or, knowing, overcome, attribute it not to God but to yourself; but when you have done it, believe that God was present to you, since it is most true, and do not await this divine help by deferring, but rather experience it by doing.

**12.** Now then, about our "Greek," about whom the whole latter letter is, I will say to Your Excellence what I see ought to be said by me. For his great natural ability filled me with great joy, excelling in great talent and much advanced by liberal erudition. But to what these good things are referred, you know very well that it matters greatly. For if it appeared in such declamations as those in which he is now engaged by the necessity of exercise, you would have wished me to be the judge not so much of his talent as of his mind, so that, the considerations laid aside which the necessity of the material claimed for itself, I might bring a sentence on his ability alone. By this request of yours you yourself in a way judged, in that you did not want me now to judge. But I, the more I am delighted by his talent and his outstanding ability of speaking, the more anxious I am, loving him the more, as to with what mind these so great goods of his serve—which, as you know, men can use both well and badly; nor do I approve nor wish that he not wish to prove himself to the better rather than to the many, which condition you said belongs to the oratorical art. For this opinion, by an inveterate custom indeed, but not a right one of the human race, did not flow from the fountain of truth. For if you yourself did not know this, you would not say: "This seems to be the condition of the art itself," but you would say: "This is the condition of the art itself." It seems, then, to be, and is not, and it seems so to those who either do not read the better authors of that same art, or do not understand them well, or, having read and understood them, do not believe them. For that is altogether true which the most copious and most ornate teacher of that same ability said: *That eloquence with wisdom most profits states, but eloquence without wisdom for the most part too much harms, and never profits*[^16]. Whence the ancients judged that not the eloquent man (for there can be eloquence without wisdom), but the orator himself was to be so defined, that they said he was: *A good man skilled in speaking*[^17]. From which definition, if what is set in the first place be taken away, that which harms too much will be left; on account of which they thought and said that, when precepts of speaking are given to the foolish, they are not made orators, but certain arms are given to madmen.

**13.** Our Greek, therefore, I wish, I desire, I exhort, that, where there is no detriment to honesty, he be eager to please both the good and the many, but where, on account of the perversity of the many, he cannot please both, he choose rather to please the good than the many, and this not only in words and declamations, but also in life and acts; whence I most desire to know about what sort of morals of his you congratulate him, that you may make me too a partaker of your joys about him. For I do not doubt that you wish him to be better in all things than yourself; how many years too he carries in age, what letters he has read with the masters of both languages, what with you or even along with you, what perhaps by himself, and to what he now expends the effort of a studious mind, I desire to know by your reply—not curious, but bearing his care, as I think, not improperly.
