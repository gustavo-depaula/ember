# Letter 209

TO CAELESTINE, MOST BLESSED LORD, AND HOLY POPE TO BE VENERATED WITH DUE CHARITY, AUGUSTINE SENDS GREETING IN THE LORD.

**1.** First I render congratulation to your merits, that the Lord our God has established you in that See without any division, as we have heard, of his people. Then I intimate to your Holiness what concerns us, so that you may come to our aid not only by praying for us, but also by counseling and helping. For, set in great tribulation, I have directed these writings to your Blessedness: since, wishing to profit certain members of Christ in our vicinity, I, improvident and incautious, brought a great disaster upon them.

**2.** Fussala is the name of a fortified town bordering on the territory of Hippo: there was never before a bishop there, but, together with the region adjoining it, it belonged to the parish of the Church of Hippo. That land had few catholics; the other communities there, set among a great multitude of people, the error of the Donatists pitiably held, so that in that very town there was no catholic at all. It came about, in the mercy of God, that all those places cleaved to the unity of the Church; through how great labors and perils of ours, it would be long to explain—so much so that the presbyters who were first appointed by us for gathering them were stripped, beaten, maimed, blinded, killed: whose passions, however, were not useless and barren, the security of unity being there perfected. But because the said town is separated from Hippo by forty miles, when, in ruling them and in gathering their remnants, however small—which were straying in both sexes, no longer threatening but fleeing—I saw myself extended more widely than was fitting, and not sufficing for the diligence which I perceived ought, by most certain reasoning, to be applied, I took care that a bishop should be ordained and established there.

**3.** That this might be done, I was seeking a man fit and suited to that place, who was also instructed in the Punic tongue; and I had a presbyter ready, of whom I was thinking, for whose ordination I obtained by letter, by asking, that the holy old man who then held the primacy of Numidia should come from afar. With him now present, and the minds of all in suspense in so great a matter, on the spot that one who had seemed to me ready failed me, resisting in every way. But I—who surely, as the outcome taught, ought rather to have deferred than to hasten a perilous business—while I was unwilling that the most grave and most holy old man, wearied all the way to us, should return to his own without effect, on account of which he had come so far, offered to those not asking a certain youth, Antonius, who was then with me—nurtured indeed by us in the monastery from a tender age, but, except for the office of reading, known by no grades and labors of the clergy. But those wretched men, ignorant of what was going to be, most obediently trusted me as I offered him. What more? It was done; he began to be their bishop.

**4.** What shall I do? I do not wish, before your Veneration, to burden the one whom I gathered to be nurtured; I do not wish to desert those whom, to be gathered, I brought forth with fears and pains; and how I may do both I cannot find. For the matter came to such great scandal that there pleaded against him here among us those who had obeyed us about receiving his episcopate, as though we were counseling them well. In which cases, when the capital crimes of defilements—which had been charged not by those of whom he was the bishop, but by certain others—could by no means be proved, and he seemed to be cleared of those things which were most odiously bandied about, he became so pitiable both to us and to others, that whatever was charged by the townspeople and the men of that region concerning intolerable domination, concerning robberies and diverse oppressions and crushings, by no means seemed to us such that, on account of this, or on account of all things heaped together, we should think him to be deprived of the episcopate, but that the things which were proved to have been taken away must be restored.

**5.** Finally, we so tempered our sentences that, the episcopate being saved, yet there should not be left altogether unpunished the things which ought not to be set forth either for that same man henceforth and again to do, or for the rest to imitate. We preserved, then, his honor whole, for the youth to be corrected; but in correcting, we diminished his power, namely, that he should no longer preside over those with whom he had so acted that, by a just grief, they could by no means bear him to preside over them, and might show, with their own peril and his, the impatience of their grief, perhaps about to burst out into some crime. Whose mind of such a kind appeared evidently even then, when the bishops dealt with them about him; whereas now the notable man Celer, of whose over-powerful administration against himself he complained, bears no power, either in Africa or anywhere.

**6.** But why should I delay with many words? Labor together with us, I beg, by piety venerable lord, most blessed and to be venerated with due charity, holy Pope, and order all that has been directed to you to be read aloud. See how he conducted the episcopate; how far he consented to our judgment—deprived of communion, unless first all things were restored to the people of Fussala, and afterward, the matter being done, the things being valued, he set aside the solidi, that communion might be restored to him; with what crafty persuasion he led the holy old man, our primate, a most grave man, to trust him in all things, so as to commend him, as one in every way blameless, to the venerable Pope Boniface; and the rest, which it is needless to be recalled by me, since the said venerable old man has reported all things to your Sanctity.

**7.** But in those manifold Acts, in which our judgment about him is contained, I should rather fear lest we seem to you to have judged less severely than was fitting, did I not know you to be so inclined to mercy that you judge not only us, because we spared him, but even him himself, ought to be spared. But he tries to turn into prescription, and to usurp, what was done by us either kindly or leniently. He cries out: "I ought either to sit in my own chair, or not to be a bishop"; as though he now sits anywhere but in his own. For on account of this those places were left and committed to him, in which he was also formerly bishop, lest he be said to have been unlawfully translated to another's chair against the statutes of the fathers. Or should anyone be such an exactor, whether of severity or of leniency, that those who shall not have seemed to be deprived of the honor of the episcopate should in no way have anything punished in them; or that those in whom something shall have seemed to be punished should be deprived of the honor of the episcopate?

**8.** There exist examples, the Apostolic See itself judging, or confirming the judgments of others, that certain men, for certain faults, were neither stripped of episcopal honor nor left altogether unpunished. Which, that I may not seek them most remote from our times, I will recall recent ones. Let Priscus, bishop of the province of Caesarea, cry out: "Either the place to the primacy ought to have been open to me as to the rest, or the episcopate ought not to have remained to me." Let another bishop of the same province, Victor, cry out—he to whom, left in the same penalty in which Priscus too was, no bishop communicates anywhere except in his own diocese; let him cry out, I say: "Either I ought to have communicated everywhere, or I ought not even in my own places to have communicated." Let a third bishop of the same province, Laurentius, cry out, and cry out altogether with this man's words: "Either I ought to have sat in the chair to which I was ordained, or I ought not to have been a bishop." But who would blame these things, except one who attends too little that neither must all things be left unavenged, nor must all things be punished in one way?

**9.** Since, then, with pastoral and watchful caution the most blessed Pope Boniface set down in his letter, speaking of the bishop Antonius, and said, "If he has faithfully indicated to us the order of things," receive now the order of things which that man kept silent in his booklet, and thereafter what was done after the reading in Africa of the letters of that man of holy memory; and come to the aid of men who beg your help in the mercy of Christ much more eagerly than that man, from whose restlessness they desire to be freed. For trials, and public powers, and military attacks, as though about to execute the sentence of the Apostolic See, either he himself, or most frequent rumors, threaten, so that wretched men, catholic Christians, dread graver things from a catholic bishop than, when they were heretics, they dreaded from the laws of catholic emperors. Do not allow these things to be done, I beg you by the blood of Christ, by the memory of the apostle Peter, who admonished the overseers of Christian peoples not to lord it violently over the brethren. I commend to the benignity of the charity of your Holiness the catholic Fussalans, my sons in Christ, and the bishop Antonius, my son in Christ, because I love both, both I commend. Nor am I angry with the Fussalans, because they bring a just complaint about me to your ears, that I inflicted upon them a man not yet proved to me, not yet at least confirmed in age, by whom they were so afflicted: nor do I wish harm to be done to this man, against whose depraved desire I resist the more, the more sincere a charity I have toward him. May both deserve your mercy; those, that they may not suffer evils, he, that he may not do them: those, lest they hate the Catholic Church, if by catholic bishops, and especially by the Apostolic See itself, they are not helped against a catholic bishop; but he, lest he bind himself by so great a crime that those whom he strives to make his own against their will, he make estranged from Christ.

**10.** As for me indeed—what must be confessed to your Blessedness—in this peril of both parties so great a fear and grief torment me, that I think of withdrawing from the office of conducting the episcopate, and of giving myself over to laments befitting my error, if I shall behold the Church of God laid waste through the one to whose episcopate I gave my vote through imprudence, and—which God himself avert—perish even with the destruction of the one who lays it waste. For recalling what the Apostle says, *If we judged ourselves, we would not be judged by the Lord*[^1], I will judge myself, that he may spare me who is going to judge the living and the dead. But if you both refresh the members of Christ which are in that region from their deadly fear and sadness, and console my old age by this merciful justice, he will repay you, both in the present and in the future life, good things for good—he who through you comes to our aid in this tribulation, and who established you in that See.

[^1]: 1 Cor 11:31
