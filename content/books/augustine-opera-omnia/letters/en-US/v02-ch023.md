# Letter 122

To his most beloved brothers, his fellow clerics and the whole people, Augustine sends greetings in the Lord.

**1.** First of all I ask your love, and beseech you through Christ, that my bodily absence not sadden you. For in spirit and the affection of the heart I think you do not doubt that I can in no way depart from you; although it saddens me more, perhaps, than it does you yourselves, that my weakness cannot suffice for all the cares which the members of Christ demand of me, whom both his fear and his love compel me to serve. For let your Love know this: that I was never absent by licentious liberty, but by necessary servitude, which has often compelled my holy brothers and colleagues to endure even labors by sea and overseas; from which a not undevout mind, but a less fit health of body, has always excused me. Accordingly, dearest brothers, so act that, as the Apostle says, whether coming and seeing you, or absent, I may hear of you that you stand in one spirit, laboring together with one mind for the evangelical faith. If any temporal trouble harasses you, that itself ought rather to admonish you how you ought to think about that life where you may live without any labor, escaping not the troublesome straits of a small time, but the horrible penalties of eternal fire. For if now with so great care, so great intention, so great labor you act lest you fall into some transitory torments; how solicitous ought you to be to flee everlasting miseries! And if death is so feared, which ends temporal labor, how is that to be feared which sends into eternal grief! And if the delights of this world, brief and squalid, are so loved, how much more vehemently are the pure and infinite joys of the world to come to be sought! Thinking of these things, do not be slothful in good works, that you may come, in its time, to the harvest of your seed.

**2.** For it has been announced to me that you have forgotten your custom of clothing the poor; to which mercy, when I was present, I exhorted you, and now I exhort you, lest the affliction of this world conquer you and make you slothful—the world to which you see such things befalling as our Lord and Redeemer, who cannot lie, foretold would come. Not only, then, ought you not to do works of mercy less, but you ought to do them even more than you are wont. For just as those who see the ruin of a house threatening, its walls broken, migrate more hastily to safer places, so Christian hearts, the more they feel the ruin of this world drawing near with crowding tribulations, the more ought they to transfer to the heavenly treasure, with unwearied swiftness, the goods which they had arranged to store up on earth; so that, if some human mischance happen, he may rejoice who migrated from a ruinous place: but if nothing of the sort follow, he may not be saddened who, about to die at some time, has commended his own goods to the immortal Lord, to whom he is to come. And so, my most beloved brothers, from what each one has, according to his own powers which he himself knows, do what you are wont, with a more cheerful mind than you are wont, and amid all the troubles of this world keep in your heart the apostolic exhortation, where he says, *The Lord is near; be not solicitous about anything*[^1]. Let such things be announced to me about you, by which I may know that you are wont to do—not on account of my presence, but on account of the precept of God, who is never absent—what for many years in my presence, and sometimes even in my absence, you have done. May the Lord keep you in peace; and, most beloved brothers, pray for us.

[^1]: Phil 4:5-6
