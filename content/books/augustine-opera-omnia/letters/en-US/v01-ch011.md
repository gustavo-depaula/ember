# Letter 11

Augustine to Nebridius

**1.** When the question greatly agitated me which you proposed some time ago, even with a certain friendly reproach—namely, by what means we might live together—and I had determined to write back to you on this alone, and to demand answers, and to turn my pen to nothing else pertaining to our studies until this very matter was settled between us, the very brief and very true argument of your recent letter quickly set me at ease: namely, that we need not think about this, since either we, when we are able, must necessarily come to you, or you, when you are able, must come to us. Set at ease, then, by this, as I said, I considered all your letters, to see for which replies I was a debtor. In them I found so many questions that, even if they could be easily resolved, by their very heap they would overwhelm anyone's talent and leisure. But they are so difficult that, even if one of them had been laid upon me, I would not hesitate to confess myself most heavily burdened. This preface, however, is meant for this: that you cease for a while to seek new questions, until we are freed from the whole debt, and that you write back to me about your judgment alone. Although I know how much this is against me, who put off being a sharer, even for a while, in your divine reflections.

**2.** Receive, then, what seems to me concerning the mystical assumption of the man, which the religion in which we have been instructed commends as having been brought about for our salvation, and as something to be believed and known—a question I have chosen, not as the easiest of all to answer, but because it seemed to me worthier than the rest to have the effort of my reflection spent upon it. For those things that are asked about this world do not seem to me to pertain much to obtaining the happy life; and if they bring some pleasure when they are investigated, it is nonetheless to be feared that they occupy time that should be spent on better things. And so, as for what pertains to this matter undertaken at present, I first marvel that you are troubled why the Father and the Son are not said to have assumed the man, but also the Holy Spirit. For this Trinity is so commended and believed by the catholic faith to be inseparable, and so understood by a few holy and blessed persons, that whatever is done by it must be thought to be done at once by the Father, and by the Son, and by the Holy Spirit: nor does the Father do anything which the Son and the Holy Spirit do not also do, nor the Holy Spirit anything which the Father and the Son do not, nor the Son anything which the Father and the Holy Spirit do not do. From which it seems to follow that the whole Trinity assumed the man: for if the Son assumed it, but the Father and the Holy Spirit did not, they do something apart from one another. Why, then, is it celebrated in our mysteries and sacred rites that the assumption of the man is attributed to the Son? This is a most full question, so difficult, and about so great a matter, that here neither a view sufficiently ready, nor its proof sufficiently secure, can be had. Yet I dare, since I am writing to you, to indicate rather than to explain what my mind holds; so that you may, by your own talent and our friendship, by which it comes about that you know me very well, conjecture the rest yourself.

**3.** There is no nature, Nebridius, and absolutely no substance, which does not have in itself and display these three things: first, that it is; next, that it is this or that; third, that, as far as it can, it abides in what it is. The first shows the cause of nature itself, from which all things are; the second, the form through which all things are fashioned and in a way given shape; the third, a certain abiding, so to speak, in which all things are. But if it can come about that something exists which is not this or that, nor abides in its own kind; or which is either this or that, but does not exist, nor abides in its own kind as far as it can; or which abides indeed in its own kind, by the powers of that very kind of its own, but yet neither exists nor is this or that—then it can also come about that in that Trinity some person does something apart from the others. But if you discern that it is necessary that whatever exists immediately both is this or that and abides in its own kind as far as it can, then those three do nothing apart from one another. I see that I have so far dealt with the part of this question which makes its solution difficult. But I wanted to open up for you briefly how subtly, and with how great truth, the inseparability of this Trinity is understood in the Catholic faith.

**4.** Now receive how that which moves your mind may not move it. The form which is properly attributed to the Son pertains also to discipline, and to a certain art (if we rightly use this word in these matters), and to the intelligence by which the mind itself is formed by the thinking of things. And so, since through that assumption of the man it was brought about that a certain discipline of living and an example of precept might be instilled in us under the majesty and clarity of certain truths, not without reason is this whole thing attributed to the Son. For in many matters which I commit to your thought and prudence, although many things are present, yet something stands out, and therefore not absurdly claims for itself a certain propriety; as in those three kinds of questions, even if it is asked whether a thing is, there too is also what it is, for it surely cannot exist unless it is something; there too is also whether it should be approved or disapproved, for whatever exists is worthy of some estimation. So when it is asked what a thing is, it is necessary that it both exists and is weighed by some estimation. In this way too, when it is asked of what quality it is, it is also surely something, since all these are inseparably joined to one another; yet the question takes its name not from all of them, but according to the intention of the one asking. Therefore a discipline was necessary for men, by which they might be instructed and by which they might be shaped to the right measure. Yet that very thing which comes about in men through this discipline we cannot say either does not exist or is not to be sought; rather, we aim first to know, and that by which we may conjecture something, and that in which we may abide. Therefore a certain norm and rule of discipline had first to be shown; which was done through that dispensation of the assumed man which is properly to be attributed to the Son, so that there might follow also the knowledge of the Father himself—that is, of the one principle from which all things are—through the Son, and a certain inner and ineffable sweetness and pleasantness in abiding in this knowledge and in despising all mortal things, which gift and office is properly attributed to the Holy Spirit. Therefore, although all things are done with the highest communion and inseparability, they nevertheless had to be shown distinctly because of our weakness, who have fallen from unity into variety. For no one raises another to that in which he himself is, unless he descend somewhat to that in which the other is. You have a letter, not one that has ended your care about this matter, but one that has perhaps begun your reflections on some sure foundation; so that you may pursue the rest by the talent which is very well known to me, and attain it by the piety on which above all one must stand.
