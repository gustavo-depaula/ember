# Letter 118

**1. 1.** You thought that I should suddenly be hemmed in, or rather overwhelmed, by a throng of innumerable questions, even though you believed me free and at leisure: for when could I, in any leisure, untie so many knots of so many matters for one so hurried and, as you write, now almost setting out? For I would be hindered by the very number of the matters, even if the knots were easy to untie. But they are wrapped in such great perplexity, and bound with such tenacity, that even if they were few, occupying me though most at leisure, they would weary my attention by a great delay of time, and wear down my nails. But I would rather snatch you away from the midst of your delightful inquiries, and pack you in among my cares, so that you might either learn not to be emptily curious, or might not dare to lay the feeding and nourishing of your curiosity upon those among whose cares the greatest care is to repress and rein in the curious. For how much better, and how much more fruitful, if any time and effort is spent in writing you any letter, that it rather be spent on this, that your empty and deceptive desires be cut away—desires the more to be guarded against the more easily they deceive, veiled and cloaked by I know not what shadow of honorableness and the name of liberal studies—than that they be more vehemently roused by our ministry and, so to say, our attendance, into a dominion by which they may oppress your so good mind?

**1. 2.** For behold, so many dialogues read—if they have helped you nothing toward seeing the end and grasping the purpose of all your actions, what do they profit, tell me? For where you have set the end of all this most burning zeal of yours, both fruitless for you and troublesome for us, your letter sufficiently indicates. For when you were busying yourself with me by letter about solving the questions you sent, you wrote thus: "I could," you say, "entreat you more, and through many dear to you; but I know your mind, which does not desire to ask, but to grant to all, provided only that what is unseemly be absent—of which in this matter there is utterly nothing unseemly: yet, whatever it is, I ask that you grant it to one about to sail." In these words of your letter you think rightly of me, that I desire to grant to all, provided what is unseemly be absent; but that in this matter there is nothing unseemly does not seem so to me. For the comely face of things does not touch my sense, when I think of a bishop, distracted and stretched by the din of ecclesiastical cares, suddenly, as though growing deaf, holding himself off from all these things, and expounding the little questions of the Tullian dialogues to one schoolman. How unseemly this is—although, snatched by the ardor of your studies, you altogether refuse to attend—you yet feel nonetheless. For what else does it indicate that, when you had said there was in this matter utterly nothing unseemly, you at once added: "Yet, whatever it is, I ask that you grant it to one about to sail"? For this sounds as though it seems to you indeed that there is nothing unseemly in this matter, yet that, whatever of unseemliness there is, you ask that I grant it to one about to sail. But what is it, I ask you, that you added, "to one about to sail"? Or, unless you were about to sail, ought I not to grant you anything unseemly? Doubtless you think that the unseemliness is washed off by seawater. But if it were so, mine surely, who am not about to sail, would remain unexpiated.

**1. 3.** You write also that I know how most troublesome it is to you to be a burden to anyone, and you attest that God alone knows that, impelled by extreme necessity, you did this. Here indeed, when I read your letter, I noticed well enough that my mind recognized your necessity, when behold you bring to me and say: "You are not ignorant of the characters of men, who are prone to disparage, and how, if anyone questioned does not answer, he will be thought unlearned and dull." At this point indeed I was inflamed to write back to you: for by this languor of your soul you have penetrated my breast, and broken into my cares, so that I could not dissemble that I should—as much as God would help me—heal you; not so as to think about untangling and unfolding your questions, but so as to break your happiness, hanging from the tongues of men and tottering, away from so unhappy a moorline, and bind it to a seat altogether unshaken and stable. Then, O Dioscorus, you do not even regard your Persius, mocking you with his twisted little verse—nay, plainly pounding and curbing your boyish head, if sense be present, with a fitting cuff:  
Is your knowing nothing, unless another knows that you know it?  
So many dialogues, as I said above, you have read; into the disputes of so many philosophers you have thrust your heart. Tell me which of them set the end of his actions in the fame of the crowd, or in the tongue of men, even of the good and the wise. But you, and—what is more to be ashamed of—now about to sail, testify well enough that you have made splendid progress in Africa, when you assert that you are a burden to bishops most busy, and intent on far different matters, for no other cause than that they expound Cicero to you, except that you fear men prone to disparage, lest, questioned by them, if you do not answer, you be thought unlearned and dull! O matter worthy of the vigils and lucubrations of bishops!

**1. 4.** You do not seem to me to think, day and night, of anything else than that you may be praised by men for your studies and learning. Which, though I have always judged it dangerous for those tending toward fixed and right things, yet now I most of all experience in you. For from no other source than from this same ruin did you fail to see by what thing at last we could be moved to give you what you asked. For as perversely as you yourself are snatched to learn the things you ask for no other reason than that you may be praised, or not disparaged, by men, so perversely do you also suppose that we, in your petition, are moved by such causes alleged. And would that we could bring it about that you too should be least of all moved by so empty and deceptive a good as human praise, when we indicated to you that we are moved, not to grant you what you ask—since you write these things of yourself—but to correct you! "The characters of men," you say, "are prone to disparage." What next? "If anyone questioned does not answer," you say, "he will be thought unlearned and dull." Behold, I question you, not about something from Cicero's books, whose sense perhaps those reading cannot track down, but about your very letter, and about the meaning of your words. For I ask why you did not say, "He who does not answer will be shown to be unlearned and dull," but rather said, "He will be thought unlearned and dull"; except that you yourself understand well enough that he who does not answer such things is not unlearned and dull, but is thought so. But I admonish you that he who fears to be cut down by the tongues of such thinkers, as by sickles, is dry wood, and therefore not only is thought unlearned and dull, but truly is and is convicted to be so.

**1. 5.** Perhaps you will say: But since I am not dull in mind, and especially strive not to be, I do not wish to be even thought dull in mind. Rightly; but to what end you do not wish it, this I ask. For in opening up and unfolding those questions for you, which you did not hesitate to be a burden even to us, you said that this was the cause and this the end, and so necessary that you called it extreme necessity, namely lest, questioned about these things and not answering, you be thought dull and unlearned by men prone to disparage. But I ask whether this is the whole of why you desire these things from us, or whether you are unwilling to be thought unlearned and dull on account of something else. If this is the whole, you perceive, I think, that this is the end of your so vehement intention, by which you are a burden even to us, as you confess: to be thought neither dull nor unlearned by men prone to disparage. But what can be a burden to us from Dioscorus, except what burdens Dioscorus himself even unknowing? Which he will not feel, except when he wishes to rise; and would that these burdens were not so bound on that he tries even in vain to shake them off his shoulders! Which I do not say because these questions are learned, but because they are learned with such an end. For this end you now surely feel to be trifling, empty, windy. It has too a swelling, beneath which even a rot is engendered, and a pustule of the mind is spread over against not seeing the opulence of truth. Believe me, it is so, my Dioscorus; so may I enjoy you in your very will, and in that very dignity of truth, from whose shadow you turn away. For whence shall I make you trust me about this matter, except in this way, I do not find. For you do not see it, nor can you in any way see it, so long as you build ruinous joys on human tongues.

**1. 6.** But if there is not the end of these actions and of your intention, but you are unwilling to be thought unlearned and dull on account of something else, I ask what that is. If it is lest the approach be too narrow for you to acquire temporal riches, to obtain a wife, to grasp honors, and other such things, which rush past in a headlong flux and snatch into the depths those who have fallen into them—neither does it befit us to serve you toward this end; rather it befits us even to remove you from it. For we do not so forbid you to set your end in the uncertainty of fame that you should, as it were, migrate from the Mincius into the Eridanus; whither perhaps the Mincius itself would dash you even were you not migrating. For since the vanity of human praise does not satisfy the avid spirit—because it brings nothing for feeding but the empty and the inflated—that very avidity compels the spirit to refer it to something else, as though richer and more fruitful; which nonetheless, if it flows past in a temporal lapse, is just as if a river led into a river, so that there is no end of misery, as long as the end of our duties is placed in an unstable thing. We wish, therefore, to fix in some firm and unchangeable good the house of your most constant intention, and the most secure rest of all your good and honorable action. Or do you perhaps think to refer even that earthly happiness which I have mentioned, if by a breeze of prosperous rumor, or even by spreading sails to its blasts, you could reach it—to refer this too to something else, a fixed and true and full good? But it does not seem so to me, and truth itself altogether denies that it is reached either by so great windings, which is so very near, or by so great expenses, which is so freely given.

**1. 7.** Or do you think that human praise itself is to be used as an instrument, to prepare through it an approach to the minds of men for persuading them of true and wholesome things; and do you beware lest, when they have thought you unlearned and dull, they judge you unworthy to whom they should lend even the most intent or most patient ear, whether you exhort anyone to right deeds, or rebuke the malice and wickedness of one who sins? If you thought of this end of justice and beneficence in asking those questions, we have deserved ill of you, since in your letter you set forth, not rather that by which we might be moved, so as either gladly to give what you asked, or to refuse it because some other cause perhaps forbade, yet not because we were ashamed not only to serve, but even not to resist, your empty desire. For how much better and more wholesome, I ask you, do you receive the very rules of truth, much more certainly and briefly, through themselves, by which you may yourself refute all false things, lest you suppose yourself learned and intelligent in something false and shameful, if you have learned the year-laden and decrepit falsehoods of many men with a zeal more boastful than prudent—which I no longer think seems so to you. For not in vain have we said so long such true things to Dioscorus, from which point we have proceeded in this letter.

**2. 8.** Wherefore let us now see this: that you judge yourself by no means unlearned and dull through ignorance of these matters, but rather of truth itself, so that, whoever has written or shall write about these things, either they are things you now hold for certain, or, if they are false, you securely are ignorant of them, and are not worn down by empty anxiety about coming to know the diversity of others' opinions, lest you remain as it were unlearned and dull. Since, then, these things are so, let us also, if you please, see this: whether the false estimation of others, who are, as you write, prone to disparage—so that, if they perceive that you are ignorant of these things, they think you unlearned and dull, falsely indeed, yet think it—ought so to move you that you not incongruously ask that these things be expounded to you even by bishops; since we now believe that you desire these things to this end, that you may take thought for those men, to persuade them of truth and correct their life, who, if they think you unlearned and dull in those books of Cicero, will hold you unworthy from whom they should think any useful and wholesome knowledge is to be received by them. But it is not so, believe me.

**2. 9.** First, because that there are any in those lands, where you fear to seem unskilled and least acute, who would ask you anything about these questions, I do not at all see: since here, whither you came to learn these things, and at Rome, you have experienced how negligently they are held, and on this account are neither taught nor learned; and in Africa you so far suffer no one to question you about these things that you do not even find anyone to suffer you yourself, and for that lack you are compelled to send them to bishops to be expounded—as though, indeed, those bishops, even when young men, snatched by the same ardor of mind, or rather error, as you, took care to learn these things as though something great, and suffered them to endure in their memory right up to their episcopal gray hairs and ecclesiastical chairs; or, if they themselves wished, the greater and graver cares would not shut these things out even from the hearts of the unwilling; or, if any of them remained in their minds through too great habit, they would not rather wish even those things recollected to be buried in oblivion than to answer inept inquiries—since even in their very scholastic levity and rhetorical chairs they seem so to have grown dumb and torpid that they are thought fit to be sent from Carthage to Hippo, that they may be expounded; where they are so unusual and altogether foreign that, if I wished, for the sake of answering, to look into something, wishing to see how one comes from above to the sentence which was to be expounded to me, or how the discourse that follows is woven from it, I could not find the manuscript at all. But those Carthaginian rhetors, if they failed this zeal of yours, are not only not reprehended by me, but even approved, if perhaps they now recall that these used to be the contests, not of Roman forums, but of Greek gymnasia. But you, when you have cast your thought even into the gymnasia, and have found even these bare and cold of such matters, where to lay down these cares of yours, the Christian basilica of Hippo occurred to you, because in it now sits a bishop who once sold these things to boys. But I do not wish you to be still a boy; and it befits me, now, neither to be a seller of boyish things, nor a bestower of them. Since these things are so—that is, since two so great cities, craftsmen of Latin letters, Rome and Carthage, are neither a weariness to you, so as to seek these things from you, nor care for your wearinesses, so as to hear you out seeking these things—I marvel beyond what can be said that you, a young man of so good a talent, fear lest in Greek and Eastern cities you should bear anyone a troublesome questioner about these matters. For more easily would you hear little crows in Africa than this kind of voice in those parts.

**2. 10.** Then, if I am mistaken, and someone there perhaps arises as a questioner of questions of this kind—the more odious, the more inept a questioner he is in those places—do you not rather beware lest there much more easily arise those who, Greek men, would ask you, placed in Greece and first imbued with the Greek tongue, something from the very books of the philosophers which Cicero did not set down in his writings? And if this happen, what will you answer? That you preferred to know these things in the books of Latin authors rather than of Greek ones? By which answer you will first do injury to Greece, and you know how those men do not bear this. Then, now exasperated and angry, how quickly they will judge you—what you very much do not wish—both dull, who preferred to learn the doctrines of the Greek philosophers, or rather certain little particles of doctrines torn apart and scattered in Latin dialogues, rather than whole and woven in the Greek books of the authors themselves; and unlearned, who, when you are ignorant of so many things in your own tongue, sought in vain to gather such things in another. Or will you perhaps answer that you did not despise the Greek books about these matters, but first took care to learn the Latin, and now, wishing to be learned in Latin, seek the Greek? If a Greek man is not ashamed to have learned Latin as a boy, and now to wish to learn Greek when bearded, will he be ashamed, at last, to be ignorant of some things even in Latin itself—things which how many learned Latins are ignorant of along with you, you feel even by this very thing, that you say you are a burden to us by great necessity, placed amid so great a multitude of the learned at Carthage?

**2. 11.** Finally, grant that you were able to answer, questioned about all that you ask of us. Behold, now you are called most learned and most acute; behold, now a Greek breeze lifts you with praises to heaven; only remember your seriousness, and to what end you wished to deserve that praise, namely that those who lightly marveled at these light things, and now most benevolently and most avidly hang upon your lips, you might teach something most serious and most wholesome: that very thing, whatever it is, most serious and most wholesome—whether you hold it, and know how to hand it on rightly—I would wish to learn. For it is ridiculous, when you have learned much superfluous matter for this reason, that the ears of men be prepared for you for the necessary things, not to hold the necessary things themselves, for receiving which you prepared those ears through superfluous things; and, while you are occupied in learning whence to make men attentive, to be unwilling to learn what is to be poured into the attentive. But if you now say that you know this, and answer that this very thing is Christian doctrine, which we know you place before all things, and in which alone you presume to have the hope of eternal salvation, it has no need of the knowledge of Cicero's dialogues, and of a collection of begged, discordant opinions of others, to procure hearers. Let those who are about to receive something of the sort from you be made attentive by your character. I do not wish you first to teach something that must be unlearned, in order to teach true things.

**2. 12.** For if the knowledge of others' dissenting and conflicting opinions helps in any way one who introduces Christian truth, so that he may know how to destroy the opposing falsehoods—it helps only to this, lest one disputing against him fix his eye only on refuting your statements, while he diligently hides his own. For the knowledge of truth is fit both to discern and to overturn all false things, provided they be brought forward, even those that were before unheard. But so that not only open things be struck, but even hidden ones dug out, if there is need to know the errors of others, raise your eyes and ears, I pray you, and see and listen whether anyone brings forward against us anything about Anaximenes and Anaxagoras; when not even the ashes of the Stoics or Epicureans themselves—much more recent and much more talkative—are warm, from which any spark might be roused against the Christian faith: but circles and conventicles, partly fugitive, partly even boldly forward, of the Donatists, or the Maximianists, or the Manichees, or even of the Arians, Eunomians, Macedonians, Cataphrygians, and the other plagues to whose flocks and throng you are coming, resound innumerably; and if it irks you to know the errors of all these, what is it to us, for the defense of the Christian religion, to ask what Anaximenes thought, and to cook up again, with empty curiosity, quarrels long since put to sleep, when even the dissensions and questions of certain heretics who wished to glory in the Christian name—as the Marcionites and Sabellians, and many others besides—are now silenced? Yet if there is need, as I said, to foreknow some who are opposed to truth, and to have their opinions thoroughly handled, we ought rather to have thought about the heretics who call themselves Christians than about Anaxagoras and Democritus.

**3. 13.** But whoever shall ask of you what you ask of us, let him hear that you are more learnedly and prudently ignorant of these things. For if Themistocles did not care that he was held more unlearned, when he had refused to sing to the lyre at a banquet, where, when he had said that he did not know how to do that, and it was said to him, "What then do you know?" he answered, "To make a republic great from small": should you hesitate to say that you are ignorant of these things, when to him who asks what you know you can answer that you know how a man can be blessed even without these things? But if you do not yet hold this, you seek these things as perversely as if, weighed down by some dangerous disease of the body, you sought delicacies and the most delicate garments rather than medicines and physicians. For this knowledge must in no way be deferred, nor must any be preferred by you to it, especially now at this age, even in the order of learning. But see how easily you could know this, if you wished. For he who asks how he may come to the blessed life asks surely nothing else than where the end of good is, that is, where the highest good of man is established—not by a depraved and rash opinion, but by a sure and unshaken truth: which, wherever it be established by anyone, is not found except either in the body, or in the mind, or in God, or in some two of these, or at any rate in all. But if you learn that neither the highest good, nor any part of the highest good, is at all in the body, two will remain, the mind and God, in one or both of which it is. But if you add and learn this same thing about the mind as about the body, what else now will occur but God, in whom the highest good of man is established? Not that there are not other goods, but that is called highest to which all are referred. For each is blessed by enjoying that for whose sake he wishes to have the rest, when that is now loved not for the sake of another, but for its own sake. And therefore it is there called the end, because now whither it may run out, and to what it may be referred, is not found. There is the rest of seeking, there the security of enjoying, there the most tranquil joy of the best will.

**3. 14.** Grant, then, one who quickly sees that the body is not the good of the mind, but rather the mind is the good of the body; and one will now withdraw from inquiring whether that highest good, or some part of it, is in the body. For—what is most foolishly denied—the mind is better than the body. Likewise it is most foolishly denied that he who gives the blessed life, or some part of the blessed life, is better than he who receives it. The mind, therefore, does not receive from the body either the highest good or any part of the highest good. Those who do not see this are blinded by the sweetness of carnal pleasures, which they do not perceive to come from a lack of health. But the perfect health of the body will be that final immortality of the whole man. For God made the soul of so powerful a nature that, from its most full blessedness, which is promised to the holy at the end of time, there overflows even into the lower nature, which is the body, not the blessedness which is proper to one enjoying and understanding, but the fullness of health, that is, the vigor of incorruption. Those who do not see this, as I said, war in restless altercations, each variously according to his grasp, setting in the body the highest good of man, and stirring up throngs of carnal seditious men; among whom the Epicureans, with the unlearned multitude, flourished with more eminent authority.

**3. 15.** Grant likewise one who quickly sees that the mind itself too is not blessed by its own good, when it is blessed, otherwise it would never be wretched; and one will withdraw from asking whether in the mind is that highest and, so to say, beatific good, or some part of it. For when the mind rejoices in itself as in its own good, it is proud. But when it perceives that it is changeable—even by this one thing at least, that it is made wise from foolish—and discerns that wisdom is unchangeable, it ought at the same time to discern that wisdom is above its own nature, and that by participation in it and by its illumination it rejoices more richly and more surely than in itself. Thus, ceasing and subsiding from its own boasting and inflation, it strives to cleave to God, and to be remade and reformed by that unchangeable one, from whom it now takes its being—not only every form of all things, whether those reached by the sense of the body or those reached by the understanding of the mind, but even the very capacity for formation before formation, when something is called even formless which can be formed. And so it feels itself to be the less stable the less it cleaves to God, who supremely is: and therefore that he supremely is, because by no changeableness does he progress or fall away; but that for itself that change is expedient by which it progresses, so as perfectly to cleave to him, while that change which is in falling away is vicious; but that every falling away tends toward perishing, whither, although it does not appear whether any thing arrives, yet it appears to all that perishing leads to this, that what was should not be. Whence it gathers that things fall away, or can fall away, for no other reason than that they were made from nothing: so that what is in them that they are and abide, and that even for their defects they are ordered to the embrace of the universe, pertains to the goodness and omnipotence of him who supremely is and is the Founder, who is powerful even from nothing to make not only something, but even something great: but that the first sin, that is, the first voluntary falling-away, is to rejoice toward one's own power; for it rejoices in something less than if it rejoiced toward the power of God, which is surely greater. Not seeing this, and gazing at the powers of the human mind, and the great beauty of deeds and words, even though they were ashamed to set the highest good in the body, yet, setting it in the mind itself, they set it surely lower than where it must be set by the sincerest reason. Among those who think thus, among the Greek philosophers, the Stoics prevailed both in number and in subtlety of disputing; who yet, judging in natural matters all things to be corporeal, could turn the mind away from the flesh rather than from the body.

**3. 16.** But among those who say that the enjoyment of God, by whom both we and all things were made, is our one and highest good, the Platonists stood out, who not without merit judged that it pertained to their office to resist the Stoics and Epicureans especially, and almost them alone. For the same Academics are Platonists, as the very succession of hearers teaches. For Arcesilas, who, his own opinion hidden, first established to do nothing but refute these men—ask whom he succeeded, you will find Polemo; ask whom Polemo, Xenocrates; and to his disciple Xenocrates Plato left his school, the Academy. As far, then, as pertains to the question of the highest good of man, remove the persons of men, and set up the dispute itself; you will surely find two errors colliding with each other front to front: one setting the highest good in the body, the other setting it in the mind. But the reasoning of truth, by which our highest good is understood to be God, resists both, yet not teaching true things before unteaching depraved ones. Set this up again with the persons joined, you will find the Epicureans and Stoics fighting most fiercely among themselves; but the Platonists trying to adjudicate their quarrel, hiding the opinion of truth, and convicting and refuting the empty confidence of those men in falsehood.

**3. 17.** But the Platonists could not fulfill the person of true reasoning as those men fulfilled that of their errors. For all lacked the example of divine humility, which at the most opportune time was made manifest through our Lord Jesus Christ; to which one example, in the mind of anyone fiercely arrogant, all pride yields and is broken and dies. And therefore those men, not able by authority to lead the throngs, blinded by the love of earthly things, to faith in invisible things—since they saw them stirred up especially by Epicurean contentions, not only to drink in (whither they were carried of their own accord) but even to keep defending the pleasure of the body, so that in it the highest good of man might be established; but those who were moved against this pleasure by the praise of virtue to contemplate it somewhat less difficultly in the minds of men, whence the good deeds proceeded about which they could in some way judge—they saw at the same time that if they tried to introduce to these men any divine thing, and unchangeable above all things, which is touched by no sense of the body, but understood by the mind alone, which nevertheless exceeds even the nature of the mind itself, and that this is God, set before the human mind to enjoy, purified from every stain of human desires, in whom alone all longing for blessedness might rest, and in whom alone is for us the end of all goods—that they would not understand, and would, with the Epicureans or Stoics resisting, much more easily than to themselves give them the palm, so that the true and wholesome opinion would grow squalid by the mockery of unskilled peoples—a thing most pernicious for the human race: and this in moral matters.

**3. 18.** But in natural questions, if these men said that the maker of all natures is an incorporeal Wisdom, while those others nowhere departed from body—since some gave atoms, some the four elements, in which fire prevailed for making all things, as the principles of things—who would not see that an abundance of fools, given over to the body, since it was by no means able to gaze upon an incorporeal power as the founder of things, would rather be snatched into the support of those men?

**3. 19.** There remained the part of rational questions. For you know that whatever is inquired for acquiring wisdom has its question either about morals, or about nature, or about reasoning. Since, then, the Epicureans said that the senses of the body are never deceived, while the Stoics granted that they are sometimes deceived, yet both set the rule of comprehending truth in the senses; who, with these contradicting, would hear the Platonists? Who would think they should be held, not only in the number of the wise, but at all in the number of men, if it were promptly said by them that there is not only something which can be perceived neither by the touch of the body, nor by smell or taste, nor by these ears or eyes, nor at all be thought by any imagination of such things as are so sensed; but that this alone truly is, and this alone can be perceived, because it is unchangeable and everlasting; and that it is perceived by the understanding alone, by which alone truth, in the way it can be reached, is reached?

**3. 20.** Since, then, the Platonists held such things, which they could neither teach men given over to the flesh, nor were they of such great authority among peoples as to persuade them to be believed—until the mind should be brought to that disposition by which these things are grasped—they chose to hide their opinion, and to dispute against those who boasted that they had found the true, while they set the very finding of the true in the senses of the flesh. And of what sort their counsel was, what does it matter to ask? It certainly was not divine, nor endowed with any divine authority. Only attend to this: that Plato is shown by Cicero, in many ways most plainly, to have established the end of good, and the causes of things, and confidence in reasoning, in a wisdom not human but plainly divine, from which the human is in a manner kindled—in that wisdom, that is, altogether unchangeable, and in a truth always holding itself in the same way; but that those who set the end of good, and the causes of things, and confidence in reasoning in the nature of body or of mind were assailed under the name of Epicureans and Stoics by the Platonists; and that the matter rolled down by the succession of times to this, that now at the beginning of the Christian age, when faith in invisible and eternal things was wholesomely preached through visible miracles to men who could neither see nor think anything but bodies, the same Epicureans and Stoics are found, in the Acts of the Apostles, to have contradicted the blessed apostle Paul, who was sowing beforehand among the Gentiles this same end.

**3. 21.** In which matter it seems to me sufficiently demonstrated that the errors of the Gentiles, whether about morals, or about the nature of things, or about the method of investigating truth—which, though they were many and manifold, yet stood out especially in these two sects—although the learned assailed them, and overturned them with such great subtlety and copiousness of disputing, nevertheless endured until Christian times. Which we now certainly behold in our age so to have grown dumb that scarcely now in the schools of the rhetors is it even mentioned what their opinions were: yet the contests were rooted out and suppressed even from the most talkative gymnasia of the Greeks, so that if now any sect of error against the truth, that is, against the Church of Christ, has emerged, it does not dare to leap forth to fight unless covered with the Christian name. From which it is understood that even the philosophers themselves of the Platonic stock ought, with a few things changed which Christian teaching disapproves, to submit their devout necks to the one most invincible king Christ, and to understand that the Word of God clothed with a man—who commanded, and it was believed—is what they feared even to bring forth.

**3. 22.** To him, my Dioscorus, I would have you submit yourself with all devotion, and build for yourself no other way to grasp and obtain truth than that which was built by him who, as God, saw the weakness of our steps. And that way is, first, humility; second, humility; third, humility: and as often as you would ask, this I would say; not that there are not other precepts which may be said, but that unless humility both precede and accompany and follow all the good things we do—and be set before us that we may gaze on it, and set beside us that we may cleave to it, and laid upon us that we may be checked by it—when we are now rejoicing over some good deed, pride wrenches the whole of it out of our hand. For the other vices are to be feared in sins, but pride is to be feared even in right deeds, lest those things which were done praiseworthily be lost through desire of the praise itself. And so, just as that most noble rhetor, when he was asked what seemed to him should first be observed in the precepts of eloquence, is said to have answered "Delivery"; when it was asked what second, the same delivery; what third, he said nothing else than delivery: so if you would ask, and as often as you would ask, about the precepts of the Christian religion, it would please me to answer nothing else than humility, though perhaps necessity might compel me to say other things.

**4. 23.** To this most wholesome humility, which our Lord Jesus Christ, in order to teach it, was humbled—to this, I say, there is especially opposed a certain, so to say, most unskilled knowledge, while we rejoice to know what Anaximenes, what Anaxagoras, what Pythagoras, what Democritus thought, and other such things, that we may seem learned and erudite, when this is far from true doctrine and erudition. For he who has learned that God is not stretched out or diffused through places, neither finite nor infinite, as though in some part he were greater, in some smaller, but is wholly present everywhere—just as truth, of which no one soberly says that part is in this place and part in that, since truth surely is God—will in no way be moved by what whoever thought that the air itself was God thought about the infinite air. What is it to him if he does not know what those men call the form of a body? For they call that which is on all sides finite: and whether, for the sake of refuting, as an Academic, Cicero objected to Anaximenes that God ought to have form and beauty, thinking, as it were, of a corporeal appearance, because he had said that God is corporeal—for air is a body—or whether he himself held that there is an incorporeal form and beauty to truth, by which the mind itself is informed, and through which we judge all the deeds of the wise man to be beautiful, so that he said not only for the sake of refuting, but even most truly, that it befits God to be of a most beautiful appearance, because nothing is more beautiful than that intelligible and unchangeable truth itself? But as for what that man says, that the air, which nevertheless he held to be God, is begotten, it in no way moves the man who understands that the Word of God, God with God, was not begotten as the air is begotten—that is, brought about by some cause that it might be, when it is in no way God—but in a far other way, which no one understands except him to whom God himself has given inspiration. But who does not see that that man is foolish even in corporeal matters themselves, when he says that the air is begotten, and wills it to be God; but does not say that he by whom the air is begotten—for it cannot be begotten by nothing—is God? But when it is said that the air is always in motion, it will in no way disturb the man so that he therefore think it to be God, who knows that the motion of every body is lower than is the motion of the mind; but the motion of the mind far slower than is the motion of the highest and unchangeable Wisdom.

**4. 24.** Likewise Anaxagoras, or anyone, if he says that mind is truth itself and wisdom, what is it to me to contend with a man about a word? For it is manifest that the description and measure of all things is made by it, and that it is not incongruously called infinite—not through spaces of places, but through power, which cannot be comprehended by human thought; nor that wisdom itself is anything formless, for this is proper to bodies, that whatever things are infinite are also formless. But Cicero, in his zeal for refuting—as it seems, on account of adversaries who thought corporeally—denies that anything can be joined to the infinite, because on that part where anything is joined, there must necessarily be in bodies some end. Therefore he says that Anaxagoras did not see that there could be any motion joined and continent to sense—that is, adhering by a continuous coupling—to the infinite, that is, to an infinite thing; as though he were dealing with bodies, to which nothing can be joined except through the ends of places. And he so added: "Nor sense at all, by which the whole nature, struck, would feel"; as though Anaxagoras had said that that mind, the orderer and moderator of all things, has sense such as the soul has through the body. For it is manifest that the whole soul feels, when it feels something through the body: for from the whole, surely, nothing is hidden, whatever it is that is felt. But for this reason he said that the whole nature feels, in order, as it were, to take away from it what he said, that the mind is infinite. For how does the whole feel, if it is infinite? For the sense of the body begins from some place, nor does it run through the whole, except to whose end it has come; which cannot be said of the infinite. But neither had Anaxagoras said anything about the sense of the body. And in another way that is called whole which is incorporeal, because it is understood without the ends of places, so that it can be called both whole and infinite: whole on account of its integrity; infinite, because it is not encompassed by the ends of places.

**4. 25.** "Then," he says, "if he wished mind itself to be, as it were, some living being, there will be something more inward from which that living being is named"; so that this mind would be, as it were, a body, and have within a soul whence it would be called a living being. See how he speaks by corporeal habit, as living beings are wont to be seen, on account of the gross sense, I suppose, of those against whom he disputes: and yet he said a thing which, if they could awaken, would sufficiently admonish them, that everything which, like a living body, presents itself to the mind ought rather to be thought to have a soul and to be a living being, than to be a soul. For this is what he says: "There will be something more inward, from which that living being is named." But he adds: "But what is more inward than mind?" The mind, therefore, cannot have a more inward soul so as to be a living being, because it is itself the more inward. Therefore let it have a body outwardly, to which it is the more inward, so as to be a living being. For this is what he says: "It is girt, therefore, by an outer body"; as though Anaxagoras had said that, unless it were of some living being, mind could not be. If he held that mind itself is the highest wisdom, which is proper to no living being, as it were, because truth is in common present to all souls able to enjoy it. And therefore see how urbanely he concludes: "Since this does not please"—that is, does not please Anaxagoras, that that mind, which he calls God, be girt by an outer body, by which it might be able to be a living being—"an open and simple mind, with no thing joined by which it can feel"—that is, with no body joined through which it can feel—"seems to flee the force and notion of our understanding."

**4. 26.** Nothing is truer than that this flees the force and notion of the understanding of the Stoics and Epicureans, who can think nothing but corporeal things. But as for what he says, "our"—he wished it understood as human; and well he does not say "It flees," but "It seems to flee." For to them it seems that no one can understand this, and therefore they judge that there is nothing of the sort: but it does not flee the understanding of certain men, as far as is granted to man, that there is an open and simple wisdom and truth, which is proper to no living being, but by which in common every soul that can do so is made wise and truthful. Which if Anaxagoras perceived, and saw it to be God, and called it mind, not only does the name of Anaxagoras—which, on account of his lettered antiquity, all the petty letter-men, to speak soldier-fashion, gladly puff up—not make us learned and wise, but not even the very knowledge of him by which he knew this to be true. For truth ought not to be dear to me for the reason that it was not hidden from Anaxagoras, but because it is truth, even if none of those men had known it.

**4. 27.** If, then, neither the knowledge of that man who perhaps saw the truth ought to puff us up, so that we may seem learned by it, but neither the solid reality of the truth itself, by which we can be truly learned, how much less can the names and doctrines of those men who thought false things help our learning, and make hidden things known? Since, if we are men, it would befit us to be saddened by the errors of so many and so ennobled men, if it should fall to us to hear them, rather than studiously to seek these things for this reason, that among those who are ignorant of them we may be fanned about with most empty boasting. For how much better it would have been had I not even heard the name of Democritus, than that I should with grief think of one held great in his times—I know not who—who held that the gods are images which flow off from solid bodies, and are not themselves solid, and that these, going round here and there by their own motion and gliding into the minds of men, cause a divine power to be thought of; when surely that body, from which the image flows, is judged to be as much more excellent as it is more solid? And therefore his opinion wavered, as these men say, and tottered, so that sometimes he said that a certain nature, from which the images flow, is God—which nevertheless could not be thought of except through those images which it pours forth and emits, that is, which are borne from that nature (which he thinks to be I know not how corporeal and everlasting, and even by this divine), as it were by a continuous emanation like the likeness of a vapor, and come and enter into our minds, that we might be able to think of God or the gods. For these men suppose no other cause of any thought of ours than when, from those bodies which we think of, images come and enter into our minds: as though many and almost innumerable things were not thought incorporeally and intelligibly by those who have known how to think such things, like wisdom and truth themselves. Which if these men do not think of, I wonder how they dispute about it at all: but if they do think of it, I would wish them to tell me from what body, or of what sort, the image of truth comes into their minds.

**4. 28.** Although Democritus too is said in natural questions to differ from Epicurus in this, that the latter holds that there is in the concourse of atoms a certain animal and breathing force: by which force I believe he says that the images themselves too are endowed with divinity—not all images of all things, but of the gods—and that there are principles of mind in the universe, to which he attributes divinity, and animate images, which are wont either to profit us or to harm us. But Epicurus neither sets anything in the principles of things besides atoms—that is, certain little bodies so minute that they can neither be divided, nor be sensed by sight or touch—by the fortuitous concourse of which little bodies he says that both innumerable worlds, and living beings, and souls themselves come to be, and the gods, whom he sets in human form, not in any world, but outside the worlds and between the worlds: and he wills to think nothing at all besides bodies; which nevertheless, in order to think them, he says that images flow off from the very things which he supposes to be formed of atoms, and enter into the mind, subtler than are those images which come to the eyes. For he says that this too is the cause of seeing—certain huge images, such that they embrace the whole world from outside. But you understand now, I think, what images these men suppose.

**4. 29.** I wonder that it did not admonish Democritus, by this very thing, that the things he says are false, because such great images coming into our so small mind—if it is corporeal, as they will, and enclosed in so small a body—cannot wholly touch it. For when a small body is touched by a great body, it can in no way be touched at once by the whole: how, then, are they thought of wholly at once, if they are thought of insofar as, coming and entering, they touch the mind—images which can neither enter wholly through so small a body, nor wholly touch so small a mind? Remember that I say these things according to them; for I do not hold the mind to be such: or, if Democritus judges the mind to be incorporeal, Epicurus alone indeed can be pressed by this reasoning; but even he, why did he not see that there is no need, nor can it happen, that an incorporeal mind should think by the arrival and contact of corporeal images? About the sight of the eyes, certainly, both are alike refuted; for so small eyes can in no way wholly touch the so great bodies of the images.

**4. 30.** But when it is asked of them why one image of some body is seen, from which images flow innumerably, they answer that, by the very fact that the images flow and pass frequently, it happens, as it were by a certain crowding and density of them, that from many one is seen. Which vanity Cicero so refutes that by that very thing he denies that their eternal god can be thought of, since he is thought of by images flowing and gliding innumerably. And since, with the innumerability of atoms supplying, they say that the everlasting forms of the gods come to be, when certain little bodies so depart from the divine body that others succeed, and the same succession does not allow that nature to be dissolved: "All things, then," he says, "would be eternal, because to none is lacking that innumerability of atoms which from time to time supplies the perpetual ruins." Then, how does that god of theirs not fear lest he perish, when he is struck without any intermission, and agitated by the everlasting incursion of atoms? For he says that that body is struck which is hit by atoms rushing in, and is agitated which is penetrated: then, when from it images, about which enough has now been said, always flow, how can he be confident of immortality?

**4. 31.** In all which ravings of those who hold these opinions, this above all is to be lamented, that it does not suffice for them to be narrated, so that they may be rejected without any disputation of anyone opposing; but the talents of most acute men undertook even this task, copiously to refute these things, which, as soon as said, ought to have been derided and cast away even by the dullest. For if you grant that there are atoms, if you grant even that by a fortuitous concourse they strike and agitate one another; is it lawful to grant them even this, that atoms, fortuitously meeting among themselves, so make some thing that they modify it by form, determine it by figure, polish it by equality, illuminate it by color, enliven it by soul? All which whoever loves to see by the mind rather than by the eyes sees can in no way come to be except by the art of divine providence, and seeks it from him by whom he was made. For neither must it be granted in any way that atoms themselves exist; which—the subtlety being passed over which is handed down by the learned about the division of bodies—see how easily it can be shown according to their own opinion. For they themselves certainly say that all things that are natures are nothing else than bodies and the void, and the things that happen to these; which I believe means motion and impact and the resulting forms. Let them say, then, in what genus they set the images which they suppose to flow off from more solid bodies, themselves least solid, so that they cannot be sensed except by the touch of the eyes when we see, and of the mind when we think, if they too are bodies. For they so judge, that they can go out from a body and come to the eyes, or to the mind, which nonetheless they say is corporeal. I ask whether images flow off even from the atoms themselves. If they flow off, how are they now atoms, from which some bodies are separated? If they do not flow off, either something can be thought of without images—which they vehemently refuse—or whence do they know the atoms, which they could not even think of? But now I am ashamed to refute these things, since they were not ashamed to hold them. But since they have dared even to defend them, I am ashamed, no longer of them, but of the human race itself, whose ears could bear these things.

**5. 32.** Since, then, so great is the blindness of minds, through the filth of sins and the love of the flesh, that even these monstrosities of opinions could wear out the leisures of the learned by disputing, will you, Dioscorus, or anyone endowed with a wakeful talent, doubt that the human race could in no way be better counseled toward following truth than that a man, taken up ineffably and marvelously by Truth itself, and bearing his person on earth, should, by prescribing right things and doing divine things, wholesomely persuade men to believe what could not yet be prudently understood? We serve the glory of this one; we exhort you to believe immovably and constantly in this one, through whom it has come about that not a few, but even peoples, who cannot adjudicate these things by reason, believe by faith, until, propped up by saving precepts, they escape from these perplexities into the air of the purest and sincerest truth. To whose authority it befits us to be obedient the more devoutly the more we see that no error now dares to lift itself up, to gather to itself throngs of the unskilled, that does not seek the veils of the Christian name: but that of the ancients those alone, apart from the Christian name, endure somewhat more frequently in their little assemblies who hold those Scriptures through which the Lord Jesus Christ himself was announced—and pretend that they understand and see. Moreover those who, though they are not in Catholic unity and communion, yet glory in the Christian name, are compelled to oppose believers, and dare, as it were by reason, to lead the unskilled astray, when most of all the Lord came with this medicine, that he might command faith to peoples. But they are compelled to do this, as I said, because they feel that they lie most abjectly cast down, if their authority be compared with the Catholic authority. They try, therefore, to surpass the most stable authority of the most firmly founded Church, as it were under the name and promise of reason. For of all heretics this temerity is, as it were, the rule. But that most merciful commander of faith both fortified the Church, by the most thronged assemblies of peoples and nations, and the very seats of the Apostles, with the citadel of authority, and, through fewer devoutly learned and truly spiritual men, armed it with the most copious equipment even of most invincible reason. But the most right discipline is, that into the citadel of faith the weak be received as much as possible, so that, on their behalf, now most safely placed, the fight may be waged with the strongest reason.

**5. 33.** But the Platonists—who, with the errors of false philosophers barking around them at that time, having no divine person by which to command faith, preferred to seek how to cover their opinion rather than to bring it forth to be defiled—when now the name of Christ was growing frequent, the earthly kingdoms marveling and being disturbed, had begun to emerge, to bring forth and disclose what Plato had thought. Then the school of Plotinus flourished at Rome, and had as fellow disciples many most acute and skillful men. But some of them were depraved by curiosity for magic arts; some, recognizing that the Lord Jesus Christ bears the person of that very truth and unchangeable wisdom which they were trying to reach, crossed over into his service. And so the whole summit of authority and the light of reason was established in that one saving name and in his one Church, for recreating and reforming the human race.

**5. 34.** That I have spoken to you so very long in this letter—although perhaps you would prefer other things—I do not at all regret. For you will approve these things the more, the more you progress in truth; and then you will approve my counsel, which now you think has complied too little with the usefulness of your studies. Although even to those very questions of yours I have replied—not only to certain ones in this letter, but to nearly all the rest in the very pages on which you sent them—by annotating briefly, as I could. In which, if you judge that I have done too little, or otherwise than you wished, you do not think rightly, my Dioscorus, of whom you have now asked these things. But all the questions of the Orator and of the books On the Orator I have passed over. For I seemed to myself to be I know not what trifler, if I pursued them to be expounded. For about the rest I could even fittingly be questioned, if anyone should bring me the things themselves to be handled and solved, not from the books of Cicero, but through themselves. But in those, the things themselves now agree too little with our profession. But I would not do all these things, had I not, after the sickness in which I was when your man came, removed myself somewhat from Hippo. In which same days I was again attacked by a disturbance of health and fevers. Hence it came about that these things were sent to you later than they could have been. How you have received them, I demand to be written back.
