# Letter 262

TO ECDICIA, A MOST RELIGIOUS LADY AND DAUGHTER, AUGUSTINE SENDS GREETINGS IN THE LORD.

**1.** Having read the letter of your Reverence, and questioned its bearer about what remained to be asked, I grieved vehemently that you wished so to deal with your husband that the building of continence, which had already begun to be constructed in him, slipped miserably, perseverance being lost, into the ruin of adultery. For while he would be to be mourned, if, after continence vowed to God, and already taken up in act itself and in morals, he returned to the flesh of his wife; how much more now is he to be mourned, sunk into a deeper destruction, who, by so abrupt a dissolution, commits adultery, angry with you, ruinous to himself, as though he rage more bitterly against you, if he himself perish? But this so great an evil happened, while you treated his mind not with the moderation you ought: because, although you were now by consent no longer joined in carnal consortium, yet in the other things, as a woman you ought to have served your husband with conjugal compliance; especially since you were both members of the body of Christ. And surely, if, faithful, you had had an unbelieving husband, you ought to have acted in subject conduct, that you might gain him for the Lord, as the Apostles admonished[^1].

**2.** For I pass over that I have learned that you took up continence itself, while he was not yet willing, not according to sound doctrine. For he ought not to have been defrauded of the debt of your body, before his will too acceded to your will toward that good which surpasses conjugal chastity; unless perhaps you had not read or heard, or had not attended to, the Apostle saying: *It is good for a man not to touch a woman: but on account of fornications let each have his own wife, and let each woman have her own husband. Let the husband render the debt to the wife, and likewise the wife to the husband. The wife has not power over her own body, but the husband: and likewise the husband has not power over his own body, but the woman. Do not defraud one another, except by consent for a time, that you may have leisure for prayer; and come together again, lest Satan tempt you on account of your incontinence*[^2]. According to these apostolic words, even if he himself had wished to be continent, and you had been unwilling, he would be compelled to render the debt to you; and God would impute continence to him, if, yielding not to his own but to your weakness, lest you fall into the damnable crime of adultery, he did not deny you marital intercourse: how much more did it befit you—who ought rather to be subject—lest he too be drawn into adultery by diabolical temptation, to obey his will in rendering a debt of this kind, when God accepted from you the will of being continent, because you would not do it, lest your husband perish?

**3.** But this, as I said, I pass over, since afterward, you being unwilling to consent to him for rendering the conjugal debts, he himself consented to the same pacts of continence, and lived most continently with you for a long time, and by his own consent absolved you from that sin by which you denied him the debt of the flesh. This question, therefore, is no longer turned in your case, whether you ought to return to intercourse with your husband. For what you both had vowed to God by equal consent, you both ought to have rendered perseveringly to the end: from which purpose if he has lapsed, you at least most constantly persevere. Which I would not exhort you to, except because he himself had consented to you in this. For if you had never held his assent, no number of years would have defended you, but, after however long a time, had you consulted me, I would answer you nothing else than what the Apostle says: *The wife has not power over her own body, but the husband*[^3]: over which power he had already so permitted you continence, that he himself too took it up with you.

**4.** But it is this that I am saddened you observed too little, that you ought to have served and obeyed him in domestic conduct so much the more humbly and obediently, the more religiously he had conceded to you, even by imitating, so great a thing. For not because you alike abstained from carnal intercourse had he therefore ceased to be your husband; nay rather, you remained spouses among yourselves the more holily, the more you concordantly preserved holier pacts. Nothing, therefore, of your clothing, nothing of your gold or silver or any money, or any earthly things of yours, ought you to have done without his discretion, lest you scandalize a man who had vowed with you to God greater things, and had continently abstained from that which he could exact from your flesh by lawful power.

**5.** Finally, it came about that the bond of continence, with which that beloved man had bound himself, despised, he broke off, and, angry with you, did not spare himself. For, as the bearer of your letter reported to me, when he had learned that you had given all, or nearly all, that you had, to be distributed as to the poor to some two passing monks, I know not who; then he, detesting them along with you, and thinking them not servants of God, but penetrators of another's house, and your captivators and despoilers, indignantly cast away the so holy burden which he had undertaken with you. For he was weak, and therefore was not to be disturbed by you—who seemed stronger in the common purpose—by presumption, but to be borne by love: because, even if he was perhaps moved too sluggishly toward giving alms more generously, he could learn these things too, if he were not struck by your unexpected expenses, but invited by awaited compliances, so that even this which you rashly did alone, you might both do much more advisedly with concordant love, and much more orderly and honorably; nor would the servants of God be blasphemed (if indeed they were such) who, with the husband absent and unknowing, took so much from an unknown woman and another's wife; and God would be praised in your works, whose fellowship was so faithful that, by you in common, not only the highest chastity, but even a glorious poverty, was held.

**6.** But now, by inconsiderate haste, consider what you have done. For, even though I think well of those monks, by whom your husband complains that you were not edified but despoiled, and would not easily consent against the servants of God, perhaps, to a man having an eye troubled by anger; is it so great a good that you refreshed the flesh of the poor by more generous alms, as it is so great an evil that you undermined your husband's mind from so good a purpose? Or ought anyone's temporal safety to be dearer to you than his eternal? Would not God, if, thinking of a more ample mercy, you deferred to give your things to the poor for this reason, lest your husband, scandalized, perish to God, impute to you more abundant alms? Accordingly, if you recall what you had acquired, when you had gained your husband, to serve Christ with you with a holier chastity; understand, by those alms of yours, by which his heart was overturned, with how much heavier a loss you have been struck, than are those gains which you thought heavenly. For if bread broken to the hungry has a great place there[^4], how great a place there is the mercy to be believed to have, by which a man is snatched from the devil, like a roaring lion, and one seeking whom he may devour[^5]?

**7.** Nor do we say this in such a way that, if anyone be scandalized at our good works, we should think we must desist from them; but one cause is of strangers, another of necessary persons in some fellowship; one of the faithful, another of the unbeliever; one of parents toward children, another of children toward parents: another, finally, that which in these matters is most of all to be regarded, of husband and wife, where it is not lawful for a married woman to say, "I do what I will with my own"; since she herself is not her own, but her head's, that is, her husband's[^6]. *For so certain*, as the apostle Peter mentions, *holy women who hoped in God adorned themselves, subject to their husbands: as Sarah obeyed Abraham, calling him lord, whose daughters,* he says, *you have become*[^7]; since he was speaking to Christian, not to Jewish, women.

**8.** But what wonder if the father did not want his common son to be stripped by the mother of the supports of this life, not knowing what he would pursue, when he had begun to be of a somewhat greater age—whether the profession of a monk, or the ecclesiastical ministry, or the bond of conjugal relationship? For although the sons of the holy are to be roused and instructed toward better things, *yet each has his own gift from God; one so, another so*[^8]. Unless perhaps, foreseeing and taking precaution against such things, the father is to be reprehended, when the blessed Apostle says: *But whoever does not provide for his own, and especially for his household, denies the faith, and is worse than an unbeliever*[^9]. But when he was speaking about the giving of alms themselves, he says: *Not that there be refreshment to others, but straits to you*[^10]. Together, then, you would have taken counsel about all things, together you would have moderated what was to be treasured up in heaven, what to be left for the sufficiency of this life to you and yours and your son, lest there be refreshment to others, but straits to you. And in disposing and doing these things, if anything perhaps seemed better to you, you would suggest it reverently to your husband, and follow his authority, as that of your head, obediently; so that all who are soundly wise, to whom fame could carry this good of yours, would rejoice over the fruit and peace of your house, and the adversary would be in awe, having nothing depraved to say about you.

**9.** Further, if about giving alms and expending your things on the poor—about which good and great work there are such evident precepts of the Lord—you ought to share counsel with your faithful husband, who kept with you the holy pacts of continence, and not spurn his will; how much more, about your dress and clothing, ought nothing to have been changed or appropriated by you beyond his discretion, about which we read nothing commanded divinely? It is written indeed that women ought to be in an ornate habit; and the putting on of gold, and the twisting of hair, and the other things of this kind which are wont to be applied either for empty pomp or for the allurement of beauty, are deservedly reprehended[^11]. But there is, according to the measure of one's person, a certain matronly habit distinct from a widow's dress, which can befit faithful married women with the observance of religion preserved. If your husband did not want you to lay this aside, lest you boast yourself as a widow while he lived, I think that in this matter it was not to be carried to the scandal of dissension, rather toward the evil of disobedience than the good of any abstinence. For what is more absurd than for a woman to be proud over a husband about a humble garment, whom it would rather profit you to obey with bright morals, than to resist with blackish garments? For even if the dress of a nun delighted you, even this could be taken up more pleasingly with the husband observed and entreated, than presumed with him unconsulted and despised. But if he altogether did not permit it, what would perish from your purpose? Far be it that you should hence displease God, because, your husband not yet dead, you were clothed not like Anna, but like Susanna.

**10.** For not even he, who had already begun with you to keep so great a good of continence, even if he had wished that you take a conjugal, not a widow's, garment, would have compelled you also to an unbecoming adornment: by which, even if you were compelled by some hard condition, you could have, in a proud dress, a humble heart. Surely, among the fathers, that Esther, a queen fearing God, worshiping God, subject to God, yet served, subject, an alien king as husband, who did not worship the same God with her; who, when, in the extreme peril, not only her own but also of her people, who then was the people of God, she prostrated herself to the Lord in prayer, in her very prayer said that her royal adornment was to her like a menstrual rag[^12]; and him praying thus he immediately heard, who, the inspector of the heart[^13], knew she spoke truly. And surely she had a husband who was the man of many women, and a worshiper of alien and false gods. But you, if he too had endured in the purpose he had undertaken with you, and, offended by you, had not collapsed into crime, had a husband not only faithful, and worshiping the true God with you, but even continent; who without doubt, not unmindful of your purpose, even if he compelled you to conjugal garments, would yet not compel you to proud ornaments.

**11.** These things I have written to you, since you thought I should be consulted, not that I should break your right purpose by my discourse, but that I grieved your husband's deed, while you acted inordinately and incautiously. About whose recovery you ought most vehemently to think, if you truly wish to belong to Christ. Put on, therefore, humility of mind, and, that God may keep you persevering, do not despise your perishing husband. Pour out for him devout and assiduous prayers, sacrifice tears as the blood of a wounded heart. And write to him a satisfaction, asking pardon, because you sinned against him, in that, beyond his counsel and will, you did with your things what you thought ought to be done—not that you repent of having given to the poor, but of having been unwilling to have him as a partaker and moderator of your good work. Promise for the future, with the Lord's help, that, if he too repents of his shamefulness, and seeks again the continence he deserted, you will serve him, as is fitting, in all things; *lest perhaps,* as the Apostle says, *God give* him *repentance,* and he come to his senses *from the snares of the devil, by whom* he is held captive *according to his will*[^14]. But who does not know that your son, since you received him from legitimate and honorable marriage, is rather in the father's power than in yours? And therefore he cannot be denied to him, wherever he learns him to be, and lawfully demands him: and thereby, that he may, according to your will, be nourished and instructed in the wisdom of God, your concord too is necessary for him.
