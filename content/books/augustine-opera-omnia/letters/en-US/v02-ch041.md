# Letter 140

The book On the Grace of the New Testament

**1. 1.** You set forth to me, my most beloved brother Honoratus, five questions to be handled and solved, snatched from here and there, as either reading could move you, or could come into your mind as you thought, and poured out, as it were, into my sight. Of which, if I should wish, by discoursing, to order the solution—not so as to discourse about each piecemeal, as they were set forth, but to connect and weave them among themselves by one single tenor of discourse—it seems a difficult work; and yet I think they are more easily solved if I do this. For they will help one another in turn, if one will hang from another, until all come into the series of disputation, not separately in private places, as though each one dwelt in its own private intention, but gazing at some one thing, and laboring together at one thing by a social reason, and by an undivided truth.

**1. 2.** You wished, then, and admonished by writing, that it be expounded and opened to you what that voice of the Lord means: *My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?*[^1] and what it is that the Apostle says: *That, rooted and founded in love, you may prevail to comprehend with all the saints what is the breadth, length, height, and depth*[^2]: and which are the five foolish virgins, or which the wise: and what are the exterior darknesses: and how is to be understood, *The Word was made flesh*[^3]. These are certainly the five questions, recalled by me as briefly as they were set forth by you. Accordingly, if it please, let us add a sixth too, and let us especially inquire this: what is the grace of the New Testament. Let all those gaze at this, and to this, as each shall opportunely be able, let it confer its work for us; not, namely, in that order in which you set them forth and I recalled them, but let each, when it shall be necessary, as if called, answer, and fulfill the office of its work. So, then, let the exordium be born.

**2. 3.** There is a certain life of man entangled in carnal senses, given over to carnal joys, fleeing carnal offense, and pursuing pleasure. The happiness of this life is temporal: to begin from this life is of necessity, to persist in it is of will. For in this life the infant is poured forth from the mother's womb, flees, as much as it can, the offenses of this life, seeks its pleasures; it can do nothing more. But after it has come into the age in which the use of reason awakes in it, it will be able, the will being divinely aided, to choose another life, whose joy is in the mind, whose happiness is inner and eternal. For there is in man a rational soul, but it matters whither, by what will, he turns the use of that same reason; whether to the goods of the exterior and lower, or to the goods of the interior and higher nature; that is, whether to enjoy the body and time, or to enjoy divinity and eternity. For it is set in a certain middle, having below itself the corporeal creature, but above itself the Creator both of itself and of the body.

**2. 4.** The rational soul can, therefore, use even temporal and corporeal happiness well, if it does not give itself to the creature, the Creator being neglected, but rather makes that happiness serve the Creator, who bestowed it too by the most abundant largess of his goodness. For just as all the things which God created are good, from the rational creature itself to the lowest body: so the rational soul acts well in these things, if it keeps order, and, by distinguishing, choosing, and weighing, subjects the lesser to the greater, the corporeal to the spiritual, the lower to the higher, the temporal to the everlasting; lest, by neglect of the higher and appetite of the lower (since hence it becomes itself worse), it send both itself and its body into the worse, but rather, by ordered love, turn both itself and its body into the better. For since all substances are naturally good, order, praised in them, is honored, perversity, blamed, is condemned. Nor does the soul, by perversely using the creatures, bring it about that it escape the ordination of the Creator; since if it uses goods badly, he uses even bad things well: and through this, it, by perversely using goods, becomes bad, while he, by orderly using even bad things, remains good. For he who unjustly orders himself in sins is justly ordered in punishments.

**2. 5.** God, then, wishing to show that even earthly and temporal happiness is his gift, and ought to be hoped from no other source than from him, judged that in the earlier times of the age the Old Testament should be dispensed, which would pertain to the old man, from whom this life must begin. But those happinesses of the fathers are proclaimed as granted by the benefit of God, although pertaining to this transitory life. For those earthly gifts were promised and bestowed in the manifest; but in the hidden, by all those things the New Testament was figuratively foretold, and was grasped by the understanding of a few, whom the same grace had made worthy by a prophetic gift. Those holy ones, then, dispensed, according to the suitability of the time, the Old Testament, but pertained to the New Testament. For even when they carried on temporal happiness, they understood the eternal, true and to be preferred; and they ministered this in mystery, that they might attain that in reward. And if at any time they suffered adverse things, they suffered them for this, that, freed by the most evident divine help, they might glorify God, the bestower of all goods, not only of the everlasting which they devoutly hoped, but even of the temporal which they prophetically governed.

**3. 6.** But when the fullness of time came, that the grace which was hidden in the Old Testament might now be revealed in the New, God sent his Son, made of a woman; by which name, by the property of the Hebrew tongue, every female is named, whether untouched by a man, or already joined. But which Son he sent, and willed to be made of a woman, how great a God he is who deigned to undertake this humility for the salvation of the faithful, that you may recognize, now attend to the Gospel: *In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God: this was in the beginning with God. All things were made through him, and without him was made nothing. What was made in him was life, and the life was the light of men, and the light shines in the darkness, and the darkness did not comprehend it*[^4]. This God, then, the Word of God through whom all things were made, is the Son of God, abiding unchangeably, present everywhere, enclosed by no place, nor partly diffused through all things, as though he had a lesser part in a lesser place, and a greater in a greater, but everywhere whole, and so not absent even from the minds of the impious, although they do not see him, just as neither is this light seen by the eyes presented to the blind. He shines, then, even in such darkness as the Apostle signifies where he says, *You were once darkness, but now light in the Lord*[^5]: but such darkness did not comprehend it.

**3. 7.** He took up, then, a man whom men could see, that, healed by faith, they might afterward see what they then could not see. But lest the man Christ, by the very thing in which he visibly appeared, be not believed also God, and only so much be attributed to him as to a man of most excellent grace and wisdom, therefore *there was a man sent from God, whose name was John. He came in testimony, to bear testimony of the light, that all might believe through him. He was not the light, but that he might bear testimony of the light*[^6]. For so great a man ought to bear testimony about him who was not only a man, but even God, that of him it was said that *among those born of women none arose greater than John the Baptist*[^7]: for thus he to whom the greater John bore testimony was understood to be greater by this, that he was not only a man, but even God. A light, then, was John too, but such a light as the Lord himself bears testimony to, saying, *He was a burning and shining lamp*[^8]; which he said also to his disciples: *You are the light of the world*: and to show what sort of light, he went on and added, *No one lights a lamp and sets it under a bushel, but upon a candlestick, that it may shine for all who are in the house: so let your light shine before men*[^9]. These likenesses are given, that we may understand, as far as we can, or, if we cannot yet, may believe without any doubt, that the rational soul is not the nature of God; for that is unchangeable: but that it can be illuminated by participation; for lamps need to be kindled, and can be extinguished. Therefore that which is said of John, "He was not the light," looks to that light which is illuminated not by participating, but by whose participation those things which are illuminated by it are illuminated.

**3. 8.** Finally it follows: *He was the true light*: and as though we should ask how the true light is distinguished from the illuminated light—that is, Christ from John—*He was,* he says, *the true light, which illuminates every man coming into this world*[^10]. If every man, then John too. And to show the more, by a far more excellent distance, the divinity of this one: *He was in this world,* he says, *and the world was made through him, and the world did not know him*[^11]. Not the world which was made through him did not know him: for it does not have the power of knowing him, except the rational creature; although even this visible world was made through him, that is, heaven and earth: but by rebuking the world not knowing, he signified the unbelievers set in the world.

**3. 9.** Then he adds: *He came into his own, and his own did not receive him*[^12]: either because the unbelievers themselves too, insofar as they are men, are his through whom they were created; or he wished his own to be understood properly the Jews, from whom he took up the nature of the flesh; nor yet did all not receive him. For it follows and says: *But as many as received him, he gave them power to become sons of God, to those believing in his name, who were born not of bloods, nor of the will of the flesh, nor of the will of man, but of God*[^13]. This is the grace of the New Testament, which lay hidden in the Old, nor yet ceased to be prophesied and proclaimed by overshadowing figures, that the soul may understand its God, and by his grace be reborn to him. For this nativity is spiritual, therefore not of bloods, not of the will of man, nor of the will of the flesh, but of God.

**4. 10.** This too is called adoption. For we were something before we were sons of God, and we received the benefit that we might become what we were not; just as one who is adopted, before he was adopted, was not yet the son of him by whom he is adopted, yet was already one who might be adopted. And from this generation of grace is distinguished that Son who, when he was the Son of God, came to become the son of man, and to grant to us, who were sons of men, to become sons of God. For he became what he was not, but yet was something else; and this very something was the Word of God, through which all things were made, and the true light which illuminates every man coming into this world, and God with God. We too, by his grace, were made what we were not, that is, sons of God: but yet we were something, and this very something far lower, that is, sons of men. He descended, then, that we might ascend, and, abiding in his nature, was made a partaker of our nature, that we, abiding in our nature, might be made partakers of his nature. Yet not so: for the participation of our nature did not make him worse; but the participation of his nature makes us better.

**4. 11.** God, therefore, sent his Son, made of a woman, made under the Law. For he received the sacraments of the Law, *that he might redeem those who were under the Law*[^14]—that is, those whom the Law held guilty by the killing letter, while the precept was not fulfilled, before the spirit gave life; because the love of God, which fulfills the precept, is poured out in our hearts through the Holy Spirit who was given to us. Accordingly, when he had said, *That he might redeem those who were under the Law*, he at once subjoined, *That we might receive the adoption of sons*[^15]; that, namely, he might distinguish the grace of this benefit from that nature of the Son, who was sent as Son, not made by adoption, but always begotten Son, so that, the nature of the sons of men being shared, he might adopt the sons of men to share his own nature too. Wherefore, even when he had said, *He gave them power to become sons of God*, and had added the manner, lest a carnal nativity be understood—namely that he granted this to those who believe in his name, and through grace are reborn spiritually, not of bloods, nor of the will of man, nor of the will of the flesh, but of God—he soon commended, as it were, the sacrament of this exchange. For as though, marveling at so great a good, we would not dare to wish it, he at once subjoined: *And the Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us*[^16] (which is one of those five things which you wished to be expounded to you); as though he said: O men! do not despair that you can be made sons of God; because the Son of God himself, that is, the Word of God, was made flesh, and dwelt among us. Render the exchange; be made spirit, and dwell in him who was made flesh, and dwelt in you. For it is no longer to be despaired that men can be made sons of God by participation of the Word, when the Son of God was made son of man by participation of the flesh.

**4. 12.** We, then, changeable, being changed for the better, are made partakers of the Word: but the Word, unchangeable, in nothing changed for the worse, was made a partaker of the flesh, the rational soul mediating. For the man Christ neither, as the Apollinarist heretics supposed, had no soul, nor had a non-rational one; but Scripture, after its own manner—to show the more Christ's humility, lest it seem to have shrunk from the name of the flesh as from something unworthy—set "flesh" for "man": for not, because it is written, *All flesh will see the salvation of God*[^17], are souls there not to be understood. Accordingly, nothing else was said by "The Word was made flesh" than if it were said, "The Son of God was made son of man." Who, *when he was in the form of God,* as the Apostle says, *did not think it robbery to be equal to God*; for it was not of usurpation, that it should be called robbery, but it was inherent in his nature that he should be equal: *yet he emptied himself*—not losing the form of God, but taking the form of a servant; *he humbled himself, made obedient unto death, even the death of the cross*[^18]. You see how he commends the same one a man, whom God, so that there may be one person; lest, not a Trinity, but a quaternity be introduced. For just as the number of persons is not increased when the flesh is joined to the soul, that there may be one man; so the number of persons is not increased when the man is joined to the Word, that there may be one Christ. And so it is read, "The Word was made flesh," that we may understand the singularity of this person, not that we suspect the divinity changed into flesh.

**5. 13.** And so Christ, a man, that through him the grace of the New Testament might be revealed—which pertains not to temporal but to eternal life—surely was not to be commended by earthly happiness. Hence the subjection, hence the suffering, hence the scourgings, spittings, insults, the cross, the wounds, and, as though overcome and subjected, death itself, that his faithful might learn what reward of piety they ought to ask and hope from him whose sons they had been made; lest for this God should be served as a great thing, that those serving him should seek to attain earthly happiness, casting away and trampling their faith, esteeming it at the cheapest price: whence, by a most beneficent providence, the omnipotent God granted earthly happiness even to the impious, lest it be sought as a great thing by the good. Hence that seventy-third psalm introduces a man repenting that at some time, with a heart not right, he had served God for this reward, by which, when he saw the impious prevail and excel, disturbed, he had begun to think that God does not care for human affairs. And when the authority of the saints pertaining to God recalled him from that thought, he undertook and gave his mind to recognize so great a secret, which was not laid open to him laboring, until he entered the sanctuary of God, and understood concerning the last things; that is, until, the Holy Spirit being received, he learned to desire better things, and foresaw what punishment is to be for the impious, even those who had temporally flourished with a certain straw-like happiness. This seventy-third psalm—expounded by our ministry on the night on which the solemnity of most blessed Cyprian dawned—read and diligently attend to.

**5. 14.** Accordingly, Christ the man and the same Christ God—by whose most merciful humanity, and in whose servile form, we ought to learn what in this life is to be despised, and what in the other is to be hoped—in the very passion, in which his enemies seemed to themselves great and victors, took up the voice of our weakness, by which our old man was at the same time crucified, that the body of sin might be made void, and said, *My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?*[^19] And this is one of your five proposed. So begins the twenty-second psalm, which, about his passion, and the revelation of the grace which he brought for making and freeing the faithful, was sung in prophecy so long before.

**6. 15.** Accordingly, that same psalm—whose prophecy the Lord, showing it to pertain to himself, cried out its first verse when he hung on the wood—I will run through by recalling and expounding it, that from this you may understand how the grace of the New Testament was not kept silent even at that time when it was veiled in the Old. For it is said from the person of Christ, as far as pertains to the form of a servant, in which our weakness was borne. For Isaiah too said of him, *He bears our weaknesses, and is in sorrows for us*[^20]. From the voice, then, of this weakness—about which Paul, in that which he sought, was not heard, and therefore in a manner forsaken, yet hears from the Lord, *My grace is sufficient for you; for power is perfected in weakness*[^21]—from the voice, then, of this our weakness, which our head transfigured into himself, it is said in this psalm, *My God, my God, look upon me; why have you forsaken me?*[^22] For he is forsaken in his praying, in whom he is not heard. This voice Jesus transfigured into himself, namely the voice of his body, that is, of his Church, to be reformed from the old man into the new; namely the voice of his human weakness, to which the goods of the Old Testament had to be denied, that it might now learn to wish for and hope the goods of the New Testament.

**6. 16.** But among those goods of the Old Testament, pertaining to the old man, there is especially coveted the temporal prolongation of this life, that it be held somewhat longer, because it cannot be held always. Therefore all indeed know that the day of death will come, and yet all, or nearly all, try to defer it, even those who believe that they will live more blessedly after death: so great force has the sweet fellowship of flesh and soul. For no one ever hated his own flesh: and therefore the soul is unwilling even for a time to depart from even its weakness, although it is confident that it will, at the end, receive it back forever without weakness. Accordingly, serving the law of God with the mind, but with the flesh drawing the desires of sin, which the Apostle forbids to obey, the devout man indeed by the reason of the mind covets to be dissolved and to be with Christ; but by the sense of the flesh refuses and flees this, and, if it could be, does not wish to be unclothed, but clothed upon, that what is mortal may be absorbed by life—that is, that even the body itself be transferred from weakness to immortality without death intervening.

**6. 17.** But these words, by which the human day and the prolongation of this life are coveted, are the words of offenses, and are far from that salvation of which we do not yet bear the thing, but already the hope; of which it is written, *For by hope we have been saved: but hope that is seen is not hope*[^23]. And therefore in this psalm too, when he had said, *My God, my God, look upon me; why have you forsaken me?* he at once subjoins, *Far from my salvation are the words of my offenses*[^24]; that is, these are the words of my offenses, and they are far from that salvation of mine which not the grace of the Old, but of the New Testament promises me. Although it can also be distinguished thus: *My God, my God, look upon me; why have you forsaken me, far from my salvation?* as though he said, By forsaking me—that is, by not hearing me—you have been made far from my salvation, namely the present salvation of this life: so that another sense may be, "the words of my offenses," that is, these things which I have said are the words of my offenses, because they are the words of carnal desires.

**6. 18.** These things Christ says from the person of his body, which is the Church. These he says from the person of the weakness of the flesh of sin, which he transfigured into that likeness of the flesh of sin which he took up from the Virgin. These the Bridegroom speaks from the person of the bride, because he has in a manner united her to himself. And in Isaiah, where he says, *As a bridegroom he has bound a miter on me, and as a bride he has adorned me with an ornament*[^25]: "he has bound on me, and adorned me," is, as it were, the voice of one, and yet we understand both the bridegroom and the bride, Christ and the Church. But, *they will be two in one flesh; a great sacrament,* says the Apostle, *in Christ and in the Church*[^26]: therefore now not two, but one flesh. If, then, the flesh is one, surely fittingly the voice too is one. Why do you seek here, O human weakness, the voice of the Word through whom all things were made? Hear rather the voice of the flesh which was made among all things; since the Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us. Hear rather the voice of the medicine, by which you are healed so as to see God, whom he deferred to be seen by you; but a man he presented to be seen, offered to be killed, conferred to be imitated, transferred to be believed, that by this faith the eye of the mind might be healed for seeing God. Why, then, do we disdain to hear the voice of the body from the mouth of the head? The Church suffered in him, when he suffered for the Church: just as he too suffered in the Church, when the Church suffered for him. For just as we have heard the voice of the Church suffering in Christ, *God, my God, look,* and so on, so too we have heard the voice of Christ suffering in the Church, *Saul, Saul, why do you persecute me?*[^27]

**7. 19.** And so, when we are not heard for retaining or obtaining temporal goods, when for these we entreat God, in that he does not hear us, he forsakes us: but for the better things, which he wishes us to understand, and prefer, and desire, he does not forsake us. Whence the Psalm follows and says, *I cried to you by day, and you will not hear*; and by night: here too is understood, "and you will not hear." But see what he adds: *and not unto unwisdom for me*[^28]. He says this, then: "You will indeed not hear me crying by day"—that is, in prosperous things, that I not lose them; "and by night"—that is, in adverse things, that the prosperous things which were lost may return: but this you will do for me not unto unwisdom; but rather that I may be wise what I ought now, through the grace of the New Testament, to await from you, what to desire, what to ask. For I cry that temporal goods be not taken from me: *But you dwell in the holy place, the praise of Israel*[^29]. I do not wish you to forsake my concupiscence, by which I seek carnal happiness. But this is in the squalor of the old; you seek the cleanness of the new; by not hearing this desire you forsake it, because you seek the love in which you may dwell: but the love of God is poured out in our hearts, yet through the Holy Spirit who was given to us. Therefore you dwell in the holy place, the praise of Israel; the praise of those who see you, because they are praised not in themselves, but in you. For *what do they have that they have not received*[^30]? so that *he who glories, let him glory in the Lord*[^31].

**7. 20.** This is the grace of the New Testament: for in the Old Testament, when you commended that even earthly and temporal happiness itself ought to be sought and hoped from none but you: *In you our fathers hoped; they hoped, and you rescued them. To you they cried, and they were saved; in you they hoped, and were not put to confusion*[^32]. For those fathers, living among their enemies, you both filled with riches, and rescued from foes, and made to bring back glorious victories, and freed from various deaths. For one you substituted a ram, that he not be struck. Another you restored sound from rottenness, and rendered him double what he had lost. Another, among hungry lions, you kept unharmed and whole. By others, walking among flames, you were praised with a pleasing voice. Something of the sort the Jews expected to happen in Christ, whence they might prove whether he were truly the Son of God. From their person it is said even in the book of Wisdom: *Let us condemn him with a most shameful death: for there will be regard in his words. For if he is the true Son of God, he will take him up and free him from the hands of his adversaries*[^33]. *These things,* it says, *they thought, and erred: for their malice blinded them*[^34]. For attending to the time of the Old Testament, and that even temporal happiness of the fathers, in which God showed that such gifts too of his were to be shown them, they did not see that it was now the time in which it should be revealed in Christ that God grants eternal goods properly to the just, who grants temporal goods even to the impious.

**8. 21.** Finally, when he had said, *In you our fathers hoped; they hoped, and you rescued them. To you they cried, and they were saved; in you they hoped, and were not put to confusion*, see what he added: *But I am a worm and not a man*[^35]. This seems indeed to be said simply for commending humility, that he might show himself, in the eyes of his persecutors, something most abject and most contemned: but the height of the secret and the profundity of the mystery is not to be despised, especially in those words which are fitted to so great a Savior in the proclamation of prophecy. From this a most elegant sense was stated by earlier writers: that he willed himself, Christ, to be foretold by this name, because a worm is born of flesh without intercourse, just as he was born of a virgin. But in his book Job, when he was speaking of heavenly creatures, that in God's sight scarcely even they themselves are clean: *How much more,* he says, *is man rottenness, and the son of man a worm!*[^36] He set "rottenness" for mortality, which now bears, as it were conceived, the necessity of dying, into which man, by sinning, was thrust down; and "the son of man" a worm born of rottenness, as rotten—that is, mortal from mortality. And through this, in these words of the Psalm, he admonished us to seek another sense, that one indeed being safe and not rejected: so that we should search not only what he says, "But I am a worm," but also that which he added, "and not a man"; according to that which I recalled from the book of Job, as though he said, "But I am the son of man, and not a man": not that Christ is not a man, of whom the Apostle says, *There is one mediator of God and men, the man Christ Jesus*[^37]; for every son of man is a man; but this sense looks to a certain one who was so a man that he was not a son of man—that is, Adam. Perhaps, then, it is so said: "But I am a worm, and not a man," that is, "But I am the son of man, and not a man": as though it were said, "But I am Christ, in whom all are made alive; not Adam, in whom all die."

**8. 22.** And through this, O men, learn, through the grace of the New Testament, now to covet everlasting life. Why do you wish, as a great thing, to be so freed by the Lord from death as your fathers were freed, when God commended that of earthly happiness too there is no other bestower than himself? That happiness pertains to the old man, which oldness began from Adam: *But I am a worm, and not a man*; Christ, not Adam. From the old you were old; from the new be new: from Adam men, from Christ sons of men. For not without cause does the Lord in the Gospel most familiarly call himself son of man rather than man: nor without cause in another psalm: *Men and beasts you will save, O Lord; as your mercy is multiplied, O God*[^38]. For from you too is this salvation common to men and beasts. But new men have another, a certain proper salvation, separated from the fellowship of beasts, which pertains to the New Testament: they have it altogether; for of it there it follows and says, *But the sons of men will hope under the cover of your wings. They will be inebriated by the abundance of your house, and you will give them to drink of the torrent of your pleasure. For with you is the fount of life, and in your light we will see light*[^39]; he said afterward, "But the sons of men," as if distinguishing the sons of men from men. For in that happiness of the salvation which is common to men and beasts, he wished to call them by that name by which they might be shown to pertain to that first man, by whom oldness and death began, who was so a man that he was not a son of man. But on these latter, who hope for another happiness, and the ineffable pleasure of the fount of life, and the light of the everlasting light, he imposed this name, by which the Lord wished himself to be more familiarly called by those into whom such grace was revealed, and therefore called them sons of men rather than men.

**8. 23.** But do not think that, as it were, a rule of speech is fixed, to be kept by a perpetual law, so that wherever you read "men" or "sons of men," you always understand according to this distinction: but as the context of the reading is, whence the sense, if it lies open, is drawn; if it lies hidden, is dug out. As in this place of this psalm, whom would not the intention of the distinction move? When he who had said, *In you our fathers hoped, and you rescued them. To you they cried, and they were saved; in you they hoped, and were not put to confusion*, subjoins: "But I": for he does not say so, "And I," but "But I." What, then, is this one who so distinguishes himself? *But I am a worm,* he says, *and not a man*; as though to those whom you heard and rescued you commended that happiness which, pertaining to the Old Testament, was to be assigned to the old man, who began from Adam: "But I am a worm"—that is, son of man, not a man, like that man who was not a son of man.

**9. 24.** And through this, that too which follows: *The reproach of men, and the outcast of the people. All who saw me mocked me, and spoke with their lips, and shook their head. He hoped in God, let him rescue him, let him save him, since he wants him*[^40]. This they said with their lips, not in their heart, insulting that it was not done, not believing that it would be. But this too was done, yet as it behooved to be done in the son of man, in whom the hope of eternal life was to be revealed, pertaining to the New Testament, not as those expected; and because they did not see it done, they insulted him as though overcome, pertaining to the Old Testament and to the man in whom all die, not to the son of man in whom all will be made alive. For man brought death upon himself and upon the son of man: but life the Son of man brought to man, by dying and rising, the reproach of men and the outcast of the people unto death. For he willed to suffer this in the sight of his enemies, by which they might think him as it were forsaken, that the grace of the New Testament might be commended, by which we might learn to seek another happiness, which now is in faith, but afterward will be in sight. For *as long as we are in the body,* says the Apostle, *we journey away from the Lord; for we walk by faith, not by sight*[^41]: and therefore now in hope, then in the reality.

**9. 25.** Finally his resurrection—which, not like ours, ought not to be deferred long, that in the example of his flesh we might learn what we ought to hope in our own—he willed to demonstrate not to strangers, but to his own: strangers, I say, not by nature, but by vice, which is always against nature. He died, then, in the sight of men, but rose in the sight of sons of men; because death pertained to man, the resurrection to the son of man: for just as in Adam all die, so too in Christ all will be made alive. That, therefore, by the example of his own flesh he might exhort his faithful to despise temporal for eternal happiness, he endured unto death those persecuting and raging, and proudly mocking him as though conquered and overwhelmed. But that he raised up his own flesh, and rendered it to the sight and touch of the disciples, and ascended into heaven with them seeing, he built them up, and showed by most evident truth what they ought to expect, what to preach. But those from whom he had endured so great evils unto death, glorying as though over him overcome and extinguished, he left in that opinion, so that whoever of them wished to be saved with eternal salvation might believe this about the resurrection of him who was dead, which those who saw it, with signs attesting, preached, and for that preaching did not hesitate to endure like things.

**10. 26.** Wherefore James too, one of his Apostles, in his Letter, when he exhorted the faithful who, after the passion and resurrection of Christ, were still held in this life, distinguishing the dispensation of the Old Testament and the New: *You have heard,* he says, *of the endurance of Job, and you have seen the end of the Lord*[^42]: lest for this they should patiently endure temporal evils, that this be restored to them which we read he received. For both he was made well from that wound and rottenness, and all the things he had lost were restored to him double: where also the faith of the resurrection was commended. For the sons, restored not double, but the same in number, signified that those too whom he had lost would rise; so that thus they themselves too, joined to those former, might not be found foreign to the restoration of the double. That, therefore, we might not hope such a remuneration when we should suffer temporal evils, he did not say, "You have heard of the endurance and the end of Job"; but he says, "You have heard of the endurance of Job, and you have seen the end of the Lord": as though he said, "Endure temporal evils like Job; but for this endurance hope not for temporal goods, which to him returned increased, but rather for eternal ones, which in the Lord went before." Job, then, was of those fathers who cried out and were saved. For when he says, "But I," he sufficiently intimates by what salvation he wishes them to be understood to have been saved; in which he himself surely was forsaken: not that they were foreign to eternal salvation; but in the hidden then was that which in Christ was to be revealed. For in the Old Testament there is a veil which will then be taken away, when anyone passes thence to Christ: at whose crucifixion the veil of the temple too was rent, that there might be signified what the Apostle said of the veil of the Old Testament, that in Christ it is made void.

**10. 27.** For even in those fathers there were—though most rare—examples of patience even unto death, from the blood of Abel to the blood of Zechariah; whose blood the Lord Jesus says is to be exacted even from those who should persist in the iniquity of their fathers, by whom those men were killed: and in the New Testament there neither was lacking, nor is lacking, a multitude of good faithful, who are even preeminent in this temporal happiness too, and in it experience the goodness and mercy of the bestowing God, holding nevertheless that which the Apostle, dispenser of the New Testament, commanded the rich of this world: not to be proudly wise, nor to hope in the uncertainty of riches, but in the living God who furnishes us all things abundantly to enjoy; that they do well, be rich in good works, give easily, share, lay up for themselves a good foundation for the future, that they may lay hold of the true life: such as was manifested in Christ not only in spirit, but even in flesh, when he rose from the dead—not such as the Jews destroyed in him, when God did not rescue him from their hands, and he seemed to have forsaken him crying out, *My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?* that in that way he might transfigure into himself his martyrs, who, even if they were unwilling to die—which he said also to Peter, *Another will gird you, and carry you where you do not wish*[^43], signifying by what death he was to glorify God—and on this account would seem to be forsaken by their God for a time, when he was unwilling to grant them what they wished, might utter that voice of the heart, and apply that affection of piety which, the passion impending, the Lord expressed by his own mouth, transfiguring them again and again into himself: *Yet not what I will, but what you will, Father*[^44].

**11. 28.** For in whom first ought it to appear, except in our very head, on account of which life we are Christians? Therefore he did not say, "My God, my God, you have forsaken me"; but he reminded that a cause is to be sought, when he added, *why have you forsaken me?*—that is, why, for what, for what cause? For surely there was some cause, and that not a small one, that he should free Noah from the flood, Lot from the heavenly fire, Isaac from the imminent sword, Joseph from the calumny of the woman and the custody of the prison, Moses from the Egyptians, Rahab from the destruction of the city, Susanna from false witnesses, Daniel from the lions, the three men from the fires, and the other fathers who cried out and were saved; and should not free Christ from the hands of the Jews, and should forsake him in the power of those raging unto the end of death. Why this? For what was this, except for that which a little after in the same psalm he says, *not unto unwisdom for me*—that is, for my body, my Church, my least ones? For in the Gospel too: *When you did it to one of the least of mine,* he says, *you did it to me*[^45]. So, then, it was said, "not unto unwisdom for me," in the way it was said, "you did it to me," and so it was said, "why have you forsaken me," in the way it was said, *He who receives you receives me; and he who spurns you spurns me too*[^46]. Not, then, unto unwisdom for us, but that we may be wise: not on account of this life, in which God sometimes forsakes us into the hands of persecutors unto death, but on account of that everlasting one, we ought to be Christians; because we see this to have gone before in him, from whose name we are so called.

**11. 29.** Behold, it was so done: yet so many wish to be Christians for no other reason than to enjoy the happiness of this life; and therefore, when it fails, they fail. What, if so notable an example were not established in our very head, by which we might learn to despise earthly things for heavenly, looking not at the things that are seen, but at the things that are not seen? For the things that are seen are temporal; but the things that are not seen are eternal. Whom he deigned to transfigure into himself in those voices. For as far as pertains to himself, how would he wish to be freed from that hour, who had for this reason come into that hour? Or how would he so speak, as though what he would not happened to him, when he had power to lay down his soul, and again to take it up, nor would anyone take it from him, but he himself would lay it down and take it up again, as he says in the Gospel? But without doubt we were in those voices, and the head spoke for its body, with no disjoined signification of the voice, where the connection of unity is not disjoined.

**12. 30.** Finally in the Psalm it follows, and, praying, notice what he says: *For you have drawn me out of the belly, my hope from the breasts of my mother. Upon you I was cast from the womb; from the belly of my mother you are my God*[^47]; as though he said, From another thing, and from one thing to another you have carried me over, that you may be a good to me in place of the earthly goods of this mortality, which I obtained in my mother's belly, whose breasts I sucked. For this is the oldness from which you have drawn me out; and those are the goods of carnal nativity, from which you are my hope: for thence I was turned to you. And from the womb—that is, I who from these things began in the womb—was cast upon you; passing over, namely, to you, committing myself wholly to you. And therefore from the belly of my mother—that is, from the goods of the flesh which I took up in my mother's belly—you are my God, that of these you may be my good: it was said by that locution, as if it were said, for example, "From the earth I dwell in heaven," that is, hence I emigrated thither; and this is our transfiguration into him, by which, through the grace of the New Testament, we change our life, passing from the old to the new. For he, signifying this thing by the sacrament of his passion and resurrection, changed his flesh from mortality to immortality: but his life he did not change from oldness to newness, who was never in impiety, whence he might pass to piety.

**12. 31.** Although there have not been lacking those who held that what is said, "From the belly of my mother you are my God," therefore pertains to our very head, since the Father is his God insofar as he is a man in the form of a servant, not insofar as he is equal to him in the form of God; therefore "From the belly of my mother you are my God," as if he said, "From the fact that I was made a man, you are my God." But what is "You have drawn me out of the belly," if it is referred to Jesus himself, procreated of the Virgin? As though God did not draw out others, in whose providence is all the order of being born: or did he thereby wish to signify the childbirth of the Virgin, the integrity of her virginity being marvelously kept, when he says, "You have drawn me out of the belly of my mother"; so that what was there done marvelously, when God is said to have done it, may seem to no one incredible? What, then, is that too: "My hope from the breasts of my mother"? How can this too be referred to the very head of the Church, as though his hope, which is in God, began for him from the breasts of his mother, and not earlier in the very womb? For no other hope is to be understood than that by which it was to be that God would exalt him from the dead: for all this is said according to that by which he was made a man. Or perhaps because the breasts of women are hindered from being promoted into abundance from the very conception, he wished it to be understood thus, "from the breasts"; as though he said, "From the time I took up flesh, for which flesh immortality was to be hoped": so that he had not hope before, when he was in the form of God, in which nothing was to be changed for the better; but from the breasts of his mother—that is, from the very assumption of flesh, which was conceived in hope, of which hope the reality was to be future, when he should pass from death to immortality?

**12. 32.** But that which was said, "Upon you I was cast from the womb," how it can be fitted to the head, I do not know: as though, when he was in the womb, he were not in God, in whom we live, move, and are; or as though the rational soul of that infant, from whom the flesh proceeded from the womb, began to hope in God. Unless perhaps it is to be believed that the rational soul, which within the vitals of the mother had still been lacking to him, came to him already born: and since the same soul, which was added to the flesh now born, cleaved to God, therefore it is to be esteemed said according to that same flesh, "Upon you I was cast from the womb"; as though he said, "I obtained from the womb that soul which cleaved to you." But who would dare rashly to cast himself into this opinion, when the reasoning about the coming or arising of the soul, in so great a deep of nature, lies so hidden that it is better always to seek it, as long as we are in this life, than at some time to presume to have found it? But from that transfiguration of ours into himself, how these things could be taken, it has been said. But if anything could be said more fittingly, or shall be able to be, we prejudge no one's talent, we envy no one's teaching.

**13. 33.** Now that which follows: *Do not depart from me, for tribulation is near*[^48], see how it illuminates the way in which it was said, "Why have you forsaken me." For how did he forsake one to whom it is said, "Do not depart from me," except that he forsook the temporal happiness of the old life? But he is asked not to depart and desert the hope of eternal life. But what is "for tribulation is near"? As though the passion were still impending over him, when in the very midst of the passion he is understood to say these things which were prophesied about him in this psalm. For here he is about to say even those things which in the Gospel are most plainly written: *They divided my garments among themselves, and over my clothing they cast lots*[^49]; which was done when he already hung on the wood. What is it, then, "tribulation is near," in the midst of which he was speaking? But doubtless he wishes this to be understood, that when the flesh is in pains and in punishments, surely the soul then has a great struggle of patience, in which, lest it fail, it must labor and pray. But nothing is nearer to the soul than its own flesh: therefore any great and perfect despiser of this world, when he suffers elsewhere, suffers nothing. For he can apply a vigilant reason when he loses goods which are outside, and which are without doubt far from the mind of the wise man, who does not cleave to them by desire; and not care for what he suffers, because he does not even suffer. But when he loses the chief goods of the body—that is, the life and health of the body—now tribulation is near to the goods of the mind, of which the soul is within as it were the lord of the body: what will he do, by any reason, that he not grieve when the body is wounded or burned, to which he is bound by so great a fellowship that he can suffer, cannot but grieve?

**13. 34.** Therefore the devil too, holding this order of harming, first received into his power the external goods of that great man whom he had asked to tempt: and when these were taken away and lost, and he saw him unshaken—for he had said, *The Lord gave, the Lord has taken away; as it has pleased the Lord, so it is done: blessed be the name of the Lord*[^50]—he asked that his flesh too be tortured with a wound, fighting with him in such a contest that he might invade the nearer goods, that is, the goods of the body; which being lost, if perhaps he should succumb, and incline his heart to impiety, the goods of the mind too would perish, for whose perishing the tempter, now raging in the body, stood more near at hand. Finally that man, in so great a temptation, where tribulation was near to the goods of the mind, although he speaks many things prophetically, yet speaks far otherwise than when those external goods had perished, in which he had not lost his sons, but sent them ahead.

**13. 35.** The soul of the martyr, then, transfigured into Christ, cries out, when it has now begun to be in tribulation in the flesh, and says to God, by whom it was forsaken in earthly happiness, but who is with it in the hope of eternal life: *Do not depart from me, for tribulation is near*: it is not in my field, not in gold, not in cattle, not in roofs and walls, not in the bereavements of my own; but it is in my flesh, to which I am coupled, to which I am connected, of which I cannot not have the sense; whence it presses me from near, that I fail from the virtue of patience. Do not depart from me; *for there is none to help*[^51]: neither friend, nor kinsman, nor human praise, nor the memory of past pleasure, nor anything of those things by which the ruin of earthly happiness is propped up, nor that very human virtue which is in my mind; because if you desert, what is the virtue of man?

**14. 35.** For what is man, except that you are mindful of him[^52]?

**14. 36.** *Many calves have surrounded me*: that is, the lesser ones among the people. *Fat bulls have beset me*[^53]: that is, the proud and the rich, the leaders of the people. *They opened their mouth against me*[^54]: crying, "Crucify, crucify!" *Like a lion seizing and roaring*: for they seized him, caught, leading him to the governor, and roared by demanding his death. *I was poured out like water*[^55]: as though my persecutors, slipping upon me, should fall. *All my bones were scattered*: what are the bones, except the firmaments of the body? But the body of Christ is the Church: and the firmaments of the Church—who, except the Apostles, who elsewhere are also called columns? These surely were scattered when he himself was led to the passion, or when he had suffered and died. *My heart became like melting wax in the midst of my belly*. How indeed this is fitted to our head, the Savior of the very body, is difficult to find. For it does not happen, except by great terror, that a human heart melts like wax: whence could this be in him who had power to lay down and take up his soul? But surely either he transferred into himself the cause of his weak ones; whether of those who tremble with fear of death, like Peter himself, made from an excellent presumer so frequent a denier; or of those who pine away with wholesome sadness, like that same Peter when he wept bitterly. For sadness too, as it were, loosens the heart: whence it is even called in Greek *kardia*. Or surely he wished us to understand a deep sacrament, so that by the name of his heart he might signify his Scriptures, where surely his counsel lay hidden, which was then opened when, by suffering, he fulfilled the things which had been prophesied about him. His Scriptures, then, were loosened in those things which were accomplished by his coming, nativity, passion, resurrection, glorification. For who does not now understand them in the Prophets, when they have come even to the understanding of the carnal multitude? Which perhaps he signified by "the midst of his belly," so that in his body, which is the Church, the carnal and weaker multitude has, as it were, the place of the belly. Or if the name of belly suits rather the inner ones, the understanding of the Scriptures was shown to pertain rather to those who are more perfect, when his heart—that is, his Scriptures, which contain his counsel—is dissolved like wax in the midst of them, that is, in their thought; that is, it is opened by the fervor of the spirit, disputed, expounded.

**15. 37.** *My strength has dried up like a potsherd*[^56]. A potsherd is made firm by fire: so too the strength of the body of Christ is not, like hay, consumed by fire, but, like a potsherd, made firm by the passion as by fire. For *the furnace proves the vessels of the potter*[^57], as Scripture says in another place, and the tribulation of trouble proves just men. *And my tongue cleaved to my jaws*. This indeed can seem to signify his silence, which another prophet too commends, when he says, *Like a lamb before its shearer he was without voice*[^58]. But if by his tongue we understand those in his body, which is the Church, through whom he speaks his Gospel, then they cleave to his jaws when they do not withdraw from his precepts.

**15. 38.** But that which follows: *And you have led me down into the dust of death*[^59], how will it be fitted to the head; since his body, which rose on the third day, was surely not dissolved into dust? Nor did the Apostles otherwise expound what is set in another psalm: *Nor will you give your holy one to see corruption*[^60], except that his flesh, which rose so quickly, was not corrupted. Whence likewise in another psalm he says, *What profit is there in my blood, while I go down into corruption? Will the dust confess to you, or announce your truth?*[^61] saying this, namely, that if in the way the rest do, he were dismissed dead into dust, and his flesh were kept for the last resurrection, there would be no profit in his blood; because his death would profit nothing, nor would the truth of God be announced, which had foretold that he would at once rise. What, then, does he mean by what he says in this place: "And you have led me down into the dust of death"? Unless we take his body to be the Church, in which those who suffered, or suffer, for his name, do not rise so quickly as he himself, but are led down into the dust of death, then to rise when that time shall come of which he says in the Gospel: *The hour will come when all who are in the tombs will hear his voice, and will come forth*[^62]. Or surely tropically he wished the Jews themselves to be understood as the dust of death, into whose hands he was led down: for it is written, *Not so the impious, not so; but like the dust which the wind drives from the face of the earth*[^63].

**16. 39.** Finally it follows: *For many dogs have surrounded me; the council of the malignant has beset me*[^64]: as though he called those the dust of death into which he was led down, whom he called many dogs and the council of the malignant; calling them dogs, namely, who for the most part bark against the innocent, with whom they have no acquaintance. Now in those things which he subjoins, it is, as it were, the Gospel that is recited. For his crucifixion is expressed in that which he says: *They pierced my hands and my feet, they numbered all my bones. But they themselves considered and looked upon me*[^65]. For his hands and feet were pierced with nails, and when he was stretched on the wood, his bones were in a manner numbered. But it was considered and looked upon what was to befall him, and whether Elijah would come and free him.

**16. 40.** Now that which follows needs no exposition: *They divided my garments among themselves, and over my clothing they cast lots*[^66]. The words then which are woven in next are of one praying, both from the person of the head, that is, of the man the mediator, and from the person of the body, which is the Church, which he calls also his only one. *But you, O Lord,* he says, *do not put your help far off*[^67]: this pertains to his flesh, whose resurrection was not put off long, like that of others. *Look to my defense*: that the enemies do nothing to harm, who seem to themselves to have been able to do something by destroying mortal flesh. But they do not harm, if, by the grace of God defending, the soul, overcome, does not consent to them for evil: for so it was prophesied elsewhere too, *The earth was handed over into the hands of the impious*[^68]; that is, the earthly flesh.

**16. 41.** *Rescue my soul from the sword*[^69]. The "sword" is a blade; nor surely was Christ killed by such iron, but by the cross, nor did they strike his side with a sword, but with a lance. By a transferred word, then, he called the tongue of the persecutors a sword, as in another psalm it is said, *And their tongue a sharp sword*[^70]. Whence, because in his flesh the tongue of the malignant prevailed, he prays that it harm nothing to the soul, when he says, "Rescue my soul from the sword": so that this prayer in prophecy, if you refer it to the head of the body, may seem not, as it were, the petition of one in need, but rather a figured prediction of a thing to come. Or surely, because his body, that is, the Church, was to endure grave persecutions, he called it a sword, by which most of all the martyrs were slaughtered; whence he wishes their souls to be rescued, lest they fear those who kill the body, but cannot kill the soul, and consent to unlawful things. Or even in the sufferings of these he calls the tongue of the enemies a sword, whence he wishes his soul—that is, the soul of his body, the soul of his saints—to be rescued.

**16. 42.** But that which follows, *and my only one from the hand of the dog*[^71], nothing seems to me more aptly understood than the only Church. But he called the world a dog, barking against the unaccustomed truth not by reason but by habit. For this is the nature of dogs, that toward those with whom they have acquaintance—whether they be good or bad—they do not bark; but, at the sight of the persons of unaccustomed ones, even innocent ones, they are provoked. By "the hand of the dog" he signified the power of the world. Whose kingdom too, to be hostile to his body, that is, to his Church, he figured by the name of the lion, in that which he added: *Save me from the mouth of the lion*[^72]. Whence it is written, *There is no difference between the threats of a king and the wrath of a lion*[^73]: although the apostle Peter too compares the devil to a lion roaring, and seeking, by going about, whom he may devour. And, showing that the proud of this world would be adversaries to the humble Christians, he consequently says: *And my humility from the horns of the unicorns*. For therefore in unicorns the proud are understood, because pride hates fellowship, and, as far as is in him, every proud man desires to be eminent alone.

**17. 43.** Now attend to where the fruit is: either that he was forsaken, not to be heard for earthly happiness—not unto unwisdom, but that we might be wise what we ought to desire by the grace of the New Testament; or that he was not forsaken but heard in that which he says, "Do not depart from me," he who had said, "Why have you forsaken me"; which surely is contradictory, unless the one be referred to one thing, the other to another. Attend and hear as much as you are wise, draw as much as you grasp, and as much as I myself am able to express so great a thing—nay, as much as he grants who hears us, and in Christ, insofar as the man is mediator between us and God, and with Christ, insofar as he is God equal to God, and is able to do, as the Apostle says, above what we ask or understand: see in this psalm the grace of the New Testament; see by the fruit of that forsaking, tribulation, entreaty, what is done, what is intimated, what is commended, what is illustrated. Gaze at what we read prophesied so long before, what we now behold fulfilled: *I will tell your name,* he says, *to my brethren; in the midst of the Church I will sing you*[^74]. Those are the brethren of whom in the Gospel he says, *Go, and say to my brethren*[^75]. That is the Church which he just now called his only one; this is the only Catholic, which is copiously spread through the whole world, which by growing reaches to the farthest nations: whence in the Gospel he says, *And this Gospel will be preached in the whole world, as a testimony to all nations; and then the end will come*[^76].

**17. 44.** But that which he says, "I will sing," is that new song, of which in another psalm it is said, *Sing to the Lord a new song, sing to the Lord, all the earth*[^77]. You have here both things: both by what song he said he would sing, and in the midst of what Church: the former pertains to the new song, the latter to all the earth. For he himself sings in us, by whose grace we sing, as the Apostle says, *Or do you wish to receive proof of him who speaks in me, Christ?*[^78] But "the midst of the Church" you may refer to eminence, namely, and to the very manifestation, because all things, the more clearly they are known, the more they are said to be in the midst; or surely to the inner ones of the Church, because the inner things are the middle. For not everyone who sounds with his lips sings the new song, but he who sings in the way the Apostle admonishes, saying, *Singing and making melody in your hearts to the Lord*[^79]. For within is this joy, where the voice of praise is both sung and heard; by which voice is praised he who is to be loved freely with the whole heart, the whole soul, the whole mind, and who kindles his lover toward himself by the grace of his Holy Spirit: for what else is the new song, than the praise of God?

**18. 45.** Finally it follows, and shows this more evidently. For when he had said, "I will tell your name to my brethren"—because *no one has ever seen God, but the only-begotten Son who is in the bosom of the Father, he himself has declared him*[^80]—and had added, "in the midst of the Church I will sing you": how he sang, that is, because he sang in us, when we progress in the name which he told to his brethren; and because he sang the praise of God in us, he at once manifested, saying, *You who fear the Lord, praise him*[^81]. But who truly praises, except he who sincerely loves? It is, then, as much as if he said, "You who fear the Lord, love him." For he said to man, as it is written, *Behold, piety is wisdom*[^82]. But piety is the worship of God, nor is he worshiped except by loving. The highest and true wisdom, then, is in that first precept: *You shall love the Lord your God with your whole heart, and with your whole soul*: and through this, wisdom is the love of God; nor is it poured out in our hearts except through the Holy Spirit who was given to us. But *the beginning of wisdom is the fear of the Lord*[^83]: and *fear is not in love, but perfect love casts out fear*[^84]. Accordingly fear, sent ahead into our heart, drives thence the habit of evil works, and keeps a place for love, which, as the mistress coming so that she may settle there, it departs.

**18. 46.** Therefore, you who fear the Lord, praise him: that you may worship God not servilely but freely, learn to love him whom you fear, and you will be able to praise what you love. For the men of the Old Testament, fearing God on account of the letter that terrifies and kills, not yet having the Spirit that gives life, ran with sacrifices to the temple; and, although in figure of the future blood by which we are redeemed, yet, not knowing what was prefigured by them, they immolated bloody victims. But now, in the grace of the New Testament, you who fear the Lord, praise him. For he himself in another psalm, foretelling that those things were to be changed which were then offered in shadow of the future: *I will not take,* he says, *calves from your hand, nor he-goats from your flocks*[^85]. And a little after, to show the sacrifice of the New Testament, when those things were to cease: *Immolate to God the sacrifice of praise, and render to the Most High your vows*[^86]. And at the end of the same psalm: *The sacrifice of praise will glorify me; and there is the way by which I will show him the salvation of God*[^87]. The salvation of God is Christ, whom old Simeon, when he had recognized him in the spirit as an infant, and had taken him into his hands: *Now you dismiss,* he says, *your servant, O Lord, according to your word, in peace; for my eyes have seen your salvation*[^88].

**19. 47.** You who fear the Lord, then, praise him; *all the seed of Jacob, magnify him*[^89]. It is not idle that it does not suffice him to say "the seed of Jacob," unless he add "all"; lest it be understood only of those who were to believe from the Israelites. For the seed of Jacob is the very seed of Abraham; and to all who believe in Christ, not only to those who were faithful from Israel, the Apostle says: *Therefore you are the seed of Abraham, heirs according to the promise*[^90]. For there he recalled the prefiguration of the New Testament, in that which is written: *In Isaac will your seed be called*[^91]; not surely in Ishmael, the son of the handmaid. In which two sons of Abraham—the slave and the free, and the two women, the handmaid and the free—writing to the Galatians, he says the two Testaments were prefigured in allegory. Whence he says: *Not those who are the sons of the flesh, these are the sons of God; but the sons of the promise are reckoned in the seed*[^92]. For the word of the promise is this: *At this time I will come, and Sarah will have a son*[^93].

**19. 48.** It is much, and too long, to unfold diligently why the sons of the promise, pertaining to Isaac, are reckoned to the grace of the New Testament. Yet I will briefly touch it, whence you will think the more fruitfully the more devoutly you think. God does not promise all the things he foretells; for he foretells also those things which he himself does not do, because he is foreknowing of all future things. He foretells, then, even the sins of men; which he could foreknow, not do. But he promises the things which he himself is to do; and not evils, but goods. For who promises evils? Although, then, he brings evils upon the bad—not sins, but punishments—he yet threatens these rather than promises them. He bestows all things and foreknows; but he foretells sins, threatens punishments, promises benefits. The sons of the promise, then, are sons of a benefit. This is the grace which is given freely, not from the merits of the one working, but from the compassion of the one giving. Hence we give thanks to the Lord our God, which is a great sacrament in the sacrifice of the New Testament; which, where, and when, and how it is offered, when you have been baptized, you will find.

**20. 49.** Then it follows and says: *Let all the seed of Israel fear him*[^94]. He who was himself Jacob and Israel was one man, having two names, not in a small sacrament: but in one book all things cannot be said, in which we have already progressed much, and have not yet said anything about the other three questions; that is, about the exterior darknesses; and the breadth and length, height and depth; and the five and five virgins. What, then, he said above, "all the seed of Jacob," here he consequently says, "all the seed of Israel." But why above, "magnify him," and afterward, "let him fear him"? Magnifying suits praise; of which he had said, "You who fear the Lord, praise him": on which I have already discoursed much. For there is the love or charity of God, which, perfected, casts out fear. Why, then, again, "Let all Israel fear him"? *For you have not received,* says the Apostle, *the spirit of servitude again unto fear*[^95]. But the same apostle, the wild olive being grafted into the olive, enjoins fear—that is, the Gentiles being added to the root of Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, that they too might become Israel, that is, might pertain to the seed of Abraham.

**20. 50.** Which grafting of the wild olive—the natural branches being now cut off on account of the pride of unbelief—the Lord himself too foretold in the Gospel, on the occasion of that Centurion who believed in him from the Gentiles. For then he said: *Amen I say to you, I have not found so great faith in Israel*[^96]: and he added: *Therefore I say to you that many will come from the east and the west, and will recline with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob in the kingdom of heaven: but the sons of the kingdom will go into the exterior darknesses; there will be weeping and gnashing of teeth*[^97]: thus signifying that the wild olive is grafted on account of humility—for that Centurion had said, *I am not worthy that you enter under my roof; but only say with a word, and my servant will be healed*[^98]—but the breaking of the natural branches on account of pride, namely of those who, being ignorant of the justice of God, and wishing to establish their own, have not been subject to the justice of God. For of these, swelling with vain elation, it was said that they will go into the exterior darknesses, who, boasting themselves of the seed of Abraham, were unwilling to become the seed of Abraham, so as to be sons of the promise: because they did not receive the faith of the New Testament, where the justice of God is commended, wishing to establish their own; that is, as though presuming on their own merits and works, they scorned to be sons of the promise, that is, sons of grace, sons of mercy, so that *he who glories, let him glory in the Lord*, believing in him who justifies the impious—that is, who from the impious makes the devout—that his faith may be reckoned to justice, and there may be fulfilled in him, not what his merit demanded, but what was promised by the Lord as a benefit.

**20. 51.** Dealing, then, with those who were grafted into the olive by grace, the Apostle says: *You will say, the branches were broken, that I might be grafted in. Well; on account of unbelief they were broken. But you, stand by faith; do not be high-minded, but fear*[^99]. For it is the benefit of God, not your merit; which elsewhere too he says: *By grace you have been saved through faith; and this not from yourselves, but it is the gift of God; not from works, lest perhaps anyone be lifted up. For we are his workmanship, created in Jesus Christ in good works, which God prepared that we should walk in them*[^100]. In this understanding of grace, then, there is fear, of which it is said, "Do not be high-minded, but fear." But this fear is another; it is not that servile one which love casts out. For by that one it is feared lest one fall into the torment of punishment; but by this, lest the grace of the benefit be lost.

**21. 52.** Wherefore, although to the faithful pertaining to the New Testament the Apostle says what I recalled a little before, *For you have not received the spirit of servitude again unto fear; but you have received the spirit of adoption of sons, in which we cry, Abba, Father*[^101]—that is, that there be in us faith which works through love, not so much by fearing punishment as by loving justice: yet, because a soul is not made just except by the participation of a better one who justifies the impious (for *what does it have that it has not received?*), it ought not, by attributing to itself what is God's, so to glory as though it had not received. On account of this it was said to it, "Do not be high-minded, but fear." And this fear is enjoined even on those who, living by faith, are heirs of the New Testament, and called into liberty. For "to be high-minded" was said for "to be proud": which in another place, on the contrary, he sufficiently shows, where he says, *Not high-minded, but consenting to the humble*[^102]. From this that he says, "consenting to the humble," he surely disclosed that he had said nothing else by "high-minded" than "proud."

**21. 53.** Fear, then, is not in love, because perfect love casts out fear; but that servile fear, that by which one, when he abstains from an evil work, is terrified by punishment, not delighted by justice; this love casts out, love which iniquity does not delight, even if impunity be proposed: not that fear by which the soul fears lest it lose that very grace by which it was brought about in it that it delights not to sin; by which it fears lest God desert it, even if he punish it with no torments of pains. This fear is chaste; love does not cast it out, but adopts it. For of it it is written: *The chaste fear of the Lord, enduring forever and ever*[^103]: it would in no way call this enduring, unless it knew another which would not endure. And it well called it chaste; for this is not lacking to that love by which the soul cleaves to God, which says in another psalm, *You have destroyed everyone who fornicates away from you; but for me to cleave to God is good*[^104]. For even a wife who bears an adulterous mind, although by fear of her husband she does not perpetrate adultery, yet what is lacking to the deed is present in the will: but the chaste one fears otherwise; for she too fears her husband, but chastely. Finally, the former fears lest her husband come hostile; the latter, lest, offended, he depart: for to one not loving the presence of the husband is troublesome; but to one loving, his absence. By a chaste fear, then, enduring forever and ever, let all the seed of Israel fear God. Let them fear him whom they love, not by being high-minded, but by consenting to the humble; *with fear and trembling let them work out their own salvation. For it is God who works in them both to will and to work, for good will*[^105].

**22. 54.** This is the justice of God, this is what God gives to man when he justifies the impious. The proud Jews, being ignorant of this justice of God, and wishing to establish their own, are not subject to the justice of God; by this pride they are cast down, that the humble wild olive may be grafted in. And they will go into the exterior darknesses, about which, among other things, you inquired, when many will come from the east and west, who will recline with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob in the kingdom of heaven. For now they are in the outer darknesses, whence correction is not to be despaired of; which if they shall despise, they will go into the exterior darknesses, where there will be no place of correction: since *God is light, and in him there are no darknesses*[^106]; but a light of the heart, not of these eyes which are in the flesh; nor at all such as the phantasm of this visible light is thought; although there too is seeing, but far otherwise, far dissimilarly. For what sort of light love itself is, who could unfold in words? Who could demonstrate by any example of those things which lie adjacent to the senses of the flesh? Or is love perhaps not light? Hear the apostle John: for he himself said what I just recalled, *that God is light, and there are no darknesses in him*[^107]; who again says, *God is love*[^108]. And through this, if God is light, and God is love, surely love is that very light, which is poured out in our hearts through the Holy Spirit who was given to us. Likewise he says: *He who hates his brother is in the darkness even until now*[^109]. These are the darknesses into which the devil and his angels, by greatly being proud, advanced. For *love is not envious, is not puffed up*[^110]. Therefore it is not envious, because it is not puffed up: for where inflation goes before, envy at once follows; since pride is the mother of envy.

**22. 55.** The devil, then, and his angels, averted from the light and fervor of love, and advanced too far into pride and envy, grew torpid, as it were, with an icy hardness. And therefore by a figure they are placed, as it were, in the north: whence, when the devil lay brooding over the human race, the grace of the Savior, about to come, is said in the Song of Songs: *Rise up, north wind, and come, south wind, blow through my garden, and the spices will flow*[^111]. Rise up, you who rushed in, who lie heavy on the subjected, who oppress the possessed: rise up, that, relieved of your weight, those may be raised up whose souls, by pressing, you bowed down. *And come, south wind,* it says; invoking the spirit of grace, blowing from the south, as it were from the fervid and luminous part, that the spices may flow. Whence the Apostle says, *We are the good odor of Christ in every place*[^112]. Hence too it is said in a certain psalm: *Turn, O Lord, our captivity, like a torrent in the south*[^113]: the captivity, namely, by which they were held under the devil as under the north, where, iniquity abounding, they had grown cold, and in a manner frozen. For hence too the Gospel says: *Because iniquity will abound, the love of many will grow cold*[^114]. But when the south wind blows, the ice is loosed, and the torrents flow—that is, sins being remitted, the peoples run together to Christ by love. Hence elsewhere too it is written: *Like ice in fair weather, so will your sins be loosed*[^115].

**23. 56.** Accordingly the rational creature—whether in the angelic spirit or in the human soul—was so made that it cannot be for itself the good by which it becomes blessed; but its changeableness, if it be turned to the unchangeable good, becomes blessed: whence, if it be turned away, it is wretched. Its turning away is its vice, and its turning toward is its virtue. The nature, then, is not evil, because the creature of the spirit of rational life, even deprived of the good by participation of which it is beatified—that is, even when vicious—is better than that body which is the highest among bodies, that is, than this light which is felt by the eyes of the flesh, because it too is a body: but any incorporeal nature whatever is more excellent than every body: not by mass, because mass is of none but bodies, but by a certain force by which it surpasses every phantasm which the mind turns over, drawn from the senses of the body. But just as in bodies themselves those things which are lower—like earth and water and the air itself—become better by participation of a better thing, that is, when they are illuminated by light, and enlivened by warmth: so incorporeal rational creatures become better by participation of the Creator himself, when they cleave to him by the purest and holiest love; from which, if they shall altogether be lacking, they grow dark, and in a manner grow hard.

**23. 57.** Accordingly unbelieving men are darkness; who, turned to God by faith, become light, a certain illumination being sent ahead. In which, by progressing, if from faith they shall have come to sight, so as to deserve even to behold what they believe—as so great a good can be beheld—they will receive the perfect image of God: for to such the Apostle says, *You were once darkness; but now light in the Lord*[^116]. Moreover the devil and his angels are the exterior darkness to unbelieving men: for they have been more averted from that love, and advanced into their own arrogance and obstinacy. And since at the final judgment Christ is to say to those whom he shall separate on the left, *Go into the eternal fire, which was prepared for the devil and his angels*[^117]—to be joined to these malign spirits, and to be condemned together—he says they will go into the exterior darknesses, that is, into the penal fellowship of the devil and his angels. To this punishment is contrary that which is said to the good servant, *Enter into the joy of your lord*[^118]; so that, as great as these darknesses are exterior, so great may that light be interior. These things are not to be thought of through intervals of places by an empty phantasm: local spaces are occupied only by the masses of bodies. This is not the spirit of life, this is not the rational soul, much less God, the most kindly founder of all and the most just orderer. By wills and affections these things are said either to draw near or to become far off, either to enter or to go out.

**23. 58.** But because those who do an evil—that is, dark—work are delighted while doing it, but a punishment is to follow which will torture, therefore the Lord, where he said "exterior darknesses," added also: *There will be weeping and gnashing of teeth*[^119]: lest they madly suppose that they will, in that punishment too, use such delights as the unjust here enjoy when, by unbelief and injustice, they grow dark; for because, willing, they unjustly use goods, justly, unwilling, they will be tortured by evils. Whence the exterior darknesses can also be understood as bodily punishments; for the body is exterior to the soul: so that the evils of the soul, by which the mind, averted from the light of love, takes delight in sins, are the outer darknesses; but the evils of the body, by which it will be tortured eternally in the end, are the exterior darknesses, which alone those fear who are still held by servile fear. For if it were permitted them to be rolled and enwrapped forever with impunity in those outer things which are in sins, surely they would never wish to draw near to God, and be illuminated, and cleave to him by love, where is the chaste fear, enduring forever and ever: which fear does not torture, but makes the soul more tenacious of that good, which, if it shall let go, it will fall.

**24. 59.** *Let all the seed of Israel fear him.* And see the cause which he joins: *For he has not spurned,* he says, *nor despised the prayer of the poor man*[^120]. By "poor man" he means the humble: hence is, "Do not be high-minded, but fear." Let all the seed of Israel, then, fear him, because he did not despise the prayer of him who was not high-minded, but feared. This too can be fitted to the head; because the Savior of the body himself was made poor for us, when he was rich, that by his poverty we might be enriched. For from the form of a servant he was made poor, from which too he poured out his prayer: for in it he humbled himself, made obedient unto death. See, then, what he says: *For he has not spurned nor despised the prayer of the poor man, nor turned his face away from me.* Where is that, "Why have you forsaken me," if he did not even turn his face away from him? Except that even by forsaking he does not forsake, when in temporal goods he does not hear; and not unto unwisdom for us, but that we may be wise what he takes away, and what he offers us. *He has not,* he says, *spurned nor despised the prayer of the poor man, nor turned his face away from me: and when I cried to him, he heard me*[^121]. He did, then, what a little before he was asked, where, entreating him, he said, "Do not depart from me." For if he heard, surely he did it, surely he did not depart. He did not, then, forsake in this way, who forsook in another way, that we might be wise in what way we ought rather to wish not to be forsaken by him.

**24. 60.** *With you is my praise*[^122]. What harm, then, do those who insult as though over one conquered, because you have forsaken me in temporal things? *In the great Church I will confess to you*: not as great as this Synagogue is, which mocks the death of the forsaken one, but in the great Church spread through all nations, which believes in the resurrection of him who was not forsaken. This is that only one which he asks to be rescued from the hand of the dog, of which a little before too he said, "In the midst of the Church I will sing you." And now that he says, "I will confess to you," is surely in those who will confess, in whom he also speaks. But confession is not only of sins, but also of the praise of God; as he himself in the Gospel: *I confess to you,* he says, *Father, Lord of heaven and earth, because you have hidden these things from the wise and prudent, and revealed them to little ones*[^123]. Therefore it follows and says: *I will render my vows before those who fear him. The poor will eat and be satisfied; and those who seek him will praise the Lord*[^124]. These are the little ones of whom he said, "and revealed them to little ones"; fearing him, and poor—that is, humble—who are not high-minded, but fear with that chaste fear, not by which punishment is dreaded, but by which grace is conserved.

**24. 61.** But by "my vows" he wishes to be understood the sacrifice of his body, which is the Sacrament of the faithful. Therefore, when he had said, "I will render my vows before those who fear him," he at once subjoined: "The poor will eat and be satisfied." For they will be satisfied with the bread which descended from heaven, who, cleaving to him, and keeping his peace and love, imitate his humility: therefore poor. In this poverty and satisfaction the Apostles especially shone. *And those who seek him,* he says, *will praise the Lord*: understanding that it is not of their own merits, but of his grace, that they are satisfied. For they seek him, because they are not of those who seek their own things, not the things of Jesus Christ. Finally, even if the flesh of those who praise him suffers temporal tribulation or death, their hearts will live forever and ever. This life of the heart is not in the senses of the body; it is secret, in the light which is within, not in the darkness which is without; in the end of the precept, not in the beginning of sin. But the end of the precept is love from a pure heart, and a good conscience, and unfeigned faith: love which is not envious, is not puffed up, because it is not high-minded, but fears; and therefore cleaves by chaste fear, enduring forever and ever. But the beginning of every sin is pride, by which the devil irrevocably advanced into the exterior things, and, by envying man and persuading him to something like it, cast him down. To which man it is said in a certain Scripture: *Why is earth and ashes proud? For in his life he cast away his inmost parts*[^125]. "In his life" it was said, as in his own, and as it were private, in which all pride is delighted.

**25. 62.** Whence love, taking counsel for the common rather than for the private, is said not to seek the things that are its own. By this hearts live forever and ever, as it were satisfied with the heavenly bread: of which the satisfier himself says, *Unless you eat my flesh and drink my blood, you will not have life in you*[^126]. Deservedly, then, the hearts of those who are satisfied will live forever and ever. For the life is Christ, who dwells in their hearts, meanwhile through faith, afterward also through sight. For now they see in a riddle through a glass, but then face to face. Whence love itself is now exercised in the good works of charity, by which it reaches out to succor wherever it can; and this is the breadth: now by long-suffering it tolerates adverse things, and perseveres in that which it has truly held; and this is the length: but all this it does for the sake of attaining eternal life, which is promised to it on high; and this is the height. But this love arises out of the hidden, where we are in a manner founded and rooted; where the causes of the will of God are not investigated, by whose grace we have been saved, not from the works of justice which we ourselves did, but according to his mercy. For voluntarily he begot us by the word of truth: and this his will is in the secret. Whose profundity of secret, in a manner dreading, the Apostle cries out: *O depth of the riches of the wisdom and knowledge of God! how unsearchable are his judgments, and his ways past finding out! For who has known the mind of the Lord?*[^127] and this is the depth. For "height" is a name common to the lofty and the deep: but when it is said of the lofty, the eminence of sublimity is commended; but when of the deep, the difficulty of investigation and knowledge. Whence to God too it is said: *How magnified are your works, O Lord! exceedingly deep are your thoughts*[^128]. And again: *Your judgments are like a great deep*[^129]. Hence, then, is that of the Apostle, which you set among the rest to be inquired about: *For this cause,* he says, *I bow my knees to the Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, from whom all fatherhood in heaven and on earth is named; that he may grant you, according to the riches of his glory, to be strengthened with power through his Spirit, that Christ may dwell through faith in your hearts in the inner man, that, rooted and founded in love, you may prevail to comprehend with all the saints what is the length and breadth, the height and depth; to know also the surpassing knowledge of the love of Christ, that you may be filled unto all the fullness of God*[^130].

**26. 63.** Attend to all things diligently. *For this cause,* he says, *I bow my knees to the Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, from whom all fatherhood in heaven and on earth is named.* You ask, for what cause: this he had said above: *On account of which I ask not to be weakened in my tribulations for you*[^131]. This, then, he wishes for them, that the Apostle not be weakened in the tribulations which he was enduring for them, and on account of this he bowed his knees to the Father. Accordingly, whence not to be weakened might be for them, it follows and he says: *That he may grant you, according to the riches of his glory, to be strengthened with power through his Spirit.* These are the riches of which he says, "O depth of the riches!" For they have hidden causes, where, no merits going before, what do we have that we have not received? Then it follows, and he adds what he wishes: *That Christ dwell,* he says, *through faith in your hearts in the inner man.* This is the life of hearts, by which we live forever and ever, from the beginning of faith to the end of sight. *That,* he says, *rooted and founded in love, you may prevail to comprehend with all the saints.* This is the communion of a certain divine and heavenly republic; hence the poor are satisfied, not seeking their own things, but those of Jesus Christ; that is, not pursuing private advantages, but taking counsel in common, where is the welfare of all: for of the very bread by which such are satisfied, the Apostle says in a certain place, *One bread, one body, we many are*[^132]. What, then, is to comprehend? *What is,* he says, *the breadth*—as I have already said, in good works, by which benevolence reaches out even to the loving of enemies; *and the length*, that, with long-suffering, on account of this breadth, troubles be tolerated; *and the height*, that for these the eternal reward which is on high, not some empty temporal thing, be hoped; *and the depth*, whence the gratuitous grace of God arises according to the secret and hidden of his will. For there we are rooted, there we are founded: rooted, on account of agriculture; founded, on account of building: which, since it is not from man, the same apostle says in another place, *You are God's agriculture, you are God's building*[^133]. All this is done when, in this our pilgrimage, faith works through love. But in the age to come, perfect and full love, without any toleration of evils, neither by faith believes what it does not see, nor by hope desires what it does not hold; but eternally it will contemplate the unchangeable form of truth, whose work, quiet without end, will be to praise what it loves, and to love what it praises. Of this he consequently says: *To know also the surpassing knowledge of the love of Christ, that you may be filled unto all the fullness of God.*

**26. 64.** In this mystery the figure of the cross is shown. For he who, because he willed it, died, in the way he willed died. Not in vain, then, did he choose such a kind of death, except that in it too he might be the master of this breadth, and length, and height, and depth. For the breadth is in that wood which is fixed crosswise above: this pertains to good works, because there the hands are stretched out. The length is in that which, from the very wood down to the ground, is visible: for there one in a manner stands—that is, persists and perseveres; which is attributed to long-suffering. The height is in that part of the wood which is left, from that which is fixed crosswise, upward, that is, toward the head of the crucified; because the expectation of those who hope well is the things above. But now that part of the wood which does not appear, which, fixed, is hidden, whence all that rises up, signifies the depth of gratuitous grace: in which the talents of many are worn down, trying to investigate it, so that at last it is said to them: *O man, who are you who answer God?*[^134]

**26. 65.** The hearts of the satisfied poor, then, will live forever and ever; that is, of the humble, burning with love, not seeking their own things, but rejoicing in the fellowship of the saints. This first was done in the Apostles. But by praising God—that is, by preaching the grace of God (since it was said, "Those who seek him will praise the Lord")—what of the peoples they acquired, see in that which follows.

**27. 66.** *All the ends of the earth,* he says, *will remember and be turned to the Lord; and all the families of the nations will worship in his sight: for the kingdom is the Lord's, and he will rule over the nations*[^135]. He who was mocked, he who was crucified, he who was forsaken acquires this kingdom: and he will hand it over at the end to God and the Father; not that he himself loses it, but that what he sowed in faith, when he came lesser than the Father, this he leads to the sight in which, equal, he did not withdraw from the Father. *All the rich of the earth have eaten and adored*[^136]: by "the rich of the earth" we ought to understand the proud, if above we rightly understood the poor as the humble, of whom in the Gospel he says, *Blessed are the poor in spirit, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven*[^137]: for they are the meek, the mourning, those who hunger and thirst for justice, the merciful, the pure in heart, the peacemakers, and those who suffer persecution for justice, to which preachings he added blessedness one by one. Over against the poor, then, in this place the rich of the earth are to be understood as the proud. For not in vain are they so distinguished, that of the poor it was said above, "The poor will eat and be satisfied"; but here, "All the rich of the earth have eaten and adored." For these too were brought to the table of Christ, and receive of his body and blood; but they only adore, not also are satisfied, because they do not imitate. For, eating the poor one, they disdain to be poor: because Christ suffered for us, leaving us an example that we should follow his footsteps. Nevertheless that which he humbled himself, made obedient unto death, even the death of the cross, the rich spurn, and refuse to endure like things, by swelling, not by greatness; therefore by weakness, not by health. But because God raised him from the dead, and gave him a name which is above every name, that at the name of Jesus every knee should bow, of those in heaven, on earth, and under the earth; moved by the fame of his loftiness and the glory of his name, spread everywhere in the Church, they too come to the table, eat and adore; yet are not satisfied, because they do not hunger and thirst for justice: for such will be satisfied. Although perfect satisfaction will be in that eternal life, when from this pilgrimage we shall have come from faith to sight, from the glass to the face, from the riddle to perspicuous truth; yet not unfittingly is he said to be satisfied with the poverty of Christ, who for his justice—that is, for the participation of the eternal Word, which he has begun meanwhile by faith—not only temperately despises all temporal goods, but even patiently endures evils.

**27. 67.** Such were the fishermen and the publicans; because he chose the abject things of this world, that he might confound the strong: of these it was said, "The poor will eat and be satisfied." But because they did not hold this satisfaction within themselves (for, in a manner belching, they praised the Lord—that is, they preached, seeking him, that is, not seeking their own things, but burning toward him with love), by their preaching the world was moved, so that all the ends of the earth might remember and be turned to the Lord, and all the families of the nations might worship in his sight; for the kingdom is the Lord's, and he will rule over the nations. By this spreading of the Church even the proud—that is, the rich of the earth—were brought to eat; and although not satisfied, yet they adore. For the prophecy of the Psalm in this place held that order which we see fulfilled. But he adds: *In his sight will fall down all who go down into the earth*[^138]: that is, all who, by loving earthly goods, do not ascend into heaven. For they do not do what the Apostle says: *If you have risen with Christ, seek the things that are above, where Christ is, sitting at the right hand of God; relish the things that are above, not the things that are on earth*[^139]: but rather, the happier they seem to themselves with earthly goods, the more they go down into the earth—that is, they are pressed down into earthly things. And therefore they fall down "in his sight," that is, where he sees, not where men see who esteem them lofty and sublime.

**28. 68.** *And my soul,* he says, *will live for him*[^140]: for him surely, not for itself, like the proud who rejoice in their private good of their own, and recoil by an empty elation from the common good of all, which is God. Let us surely avoid this, and seek rather to enjoy the common true good of all, than to rejoice in our own private one; so that those who live may now not live for themselves, as the Apostle says, but for him who died for them and rose again. For he was made mediator for this, that he might reconcile us to God by humility, from whom by impious pride we had departed far. For not only is that written which I set down above, "The beginning of every sin is pride"; but this too is read there: *The beginning of man's pride is to apostatize from God*[^141]. Let no one, then, live for himself, but for Christ; doing not his own, but his will, and abiding in his love; just as he himself too did the will of the Father, and abides in his love. For, admonishing us of these things, and exhorting by his example, he spoke in his Gospel. But if he himself, when in the form of God he was equal to the Father, yet through the form of a servant, which he took for our sake, commended that he did not his own will, but the Father's; how much more we, our own private will despised, by which we were darkened, ought to draw near to that common light, that we may be illuminated and our faces not blush, which illuminates every man coming into this world, so that our soul may live for him! For this he consequently adds about us, when he says: *And my seed will serve him*[^142]: since *he who sows the good seed is the son of man; but the good seed are the sons of the kingdom*[^143].

**29. 69.** Moreover all these things which were said in this psalm—because they pertained not to the present time, but to the prophecy of things to come, as appears even from the things themselves—he wished so to conclude, that he might show that he was not demonstrating present things, or narrating past ones, but prophesying future ones: *The coming generation will be announced to the Lord, and the heavens will announce his justice to a people that will be born, whom the Lord has made*[^144]. He did not say, "The Lord will be announced to the coming generation"; but, "The coming generation will be announced to the Lord": which is not to be taken as though something is announced to one ignorant that he may know; but as the Angels announce, not only to us the benefits of God, but even to him our prayers. For it is written, where an angel says to men, *I offered the memorial of your prayer*[^145]: not that God then knew what we wished, or of what we have need; *For your Father knows,* says the Lord, *what is necessary for you before you ask him*[^146]: but because the rational creature, obeying God, has need to refer temporal causes to eternal truth, whether by asking what is done toward itself, or by consulting what it should do; which is a devout affection of the mind, that it itself be built up, not that God be instructed. For this too is a certain attestation of the rational creature, that it is not for itself the good by which it becomes blessed, but that unchangeable one by participation of which it is even made wise.

**29. 70.** Or it was so said, "The coming generation will be announced to the Lord," as if it were said, "Those who will announce will please the Lord, not themselves"; so that to announce to the Lord is in the same way as to live to the Lord: thus it is said, *He who eats, eats to the Lord; and he who does not eat, does not eat to the Lord*[^147]. For to this he added, *And gives thanks to God*, that it might be shown what "he does to the Lord" was—that is, he does it for his praise. For then rightly, then justly, then devoutly is it done, when a good work is done for the praise of him by whose grace it is granted that it be done. And through this, even if anyone should wish to understand these words in such a way that their order is, "The coming generation will be announced to the Lord"—that is, that generation will be announced which is to come to the Lord, namely the generation of the devout and holy, because the generation of the impious and criminal is to come not to the Lord, but to itself—from that same signification one does not withdraw, by which is understood the participation of the soul in the selfsame; that is, that the rational creature, since it is changeable, does not become blessed except, from its own changeable good, being turned by humble piety to the unchangeable good, the same common good which is God, from which by proud impiety it had departed, it should subsist. Progressing in which affection, whatever good it does, it does to the Lord—that is, for his praise, whose grace it received that it might do it; whence is the giving of thanks, which is celebrated in the inmost mystery of the faithful.

**30. 71.** But that which follows, *And they will announce his justice to a people that will be born, whom the Lord has made*, is a confirmation of the earlier sense. For what was said there, "The coming generation will be announced to the Lord," is said here, "they will announce his justice." For that very generation which was prophesied to be coming, of the devout and holy, is the justice of God, not their own: lest they be of those who, being ignorant of the justice of God, and wishing to establish their own, are not subject to the justice of God. For that justice of God is commended in that it is said, "being ignorant of the justice of God," by which we are just from his grace: so that we ourselves are his very justice, when we live justly, believing in him who justifies the impious; not that by which he himself is just, by his eternal and unchangeable justice. This justice, then, by which we are just by the gift of God, is signified in that psalm where it is written: *Your justice is like the mountains of God*[^148]. For the mountains of God are his holy ones; of whom elsewhere it is said, *Let the mountains receive peace for your people*[^149]. And many things are written about these mountains in a figured locution, which it is now long to recall. But because this very thing—that God justifies men—he does by a too hidden judgment (since he does it by gratuitous grace; but if by grace, then not from works, otherwise grace is now not grace; for from this point good works begin, from which we are justified; not because they went before are we justified: and this is the depth, about which above we have already said much), at once in the same psalm, when he had said, "Your justice is like the mountains of God," he subjoined, "Your judgments are like a great deep." Thence he comes to the salvation common to men and beasts, because that too is from the mercy of God, and says, *Men and beasts you will save, O Lord; as your mercy is multiplied, O God*[^150]: so that hence we may understand that even that everlasting and immortal salvation, of which the Apostle says, *For by hope we have been saved*, like this which is common to men and beasts, we receive freely, not from works, lest perhaps anyone be lifted up; because we work good works from his very justification. For we are his workmanship, created in Christ Jesus in good works, which God prepared that we should walk in them. That salvation too, then, is gratuitous, of which in another psalm it is said: *Salvation is the Lord's, and your blessing is over your people*[^151].

**30. 72.** As, then, what is read, "Salvation is the Lord's," is not understood as that salvation by which the Lord is saved, but by which those are saved whom he himself saves: so when the justice of God is read, in that which is written, "being ignorant of the justice of God, and wishing to establish their own," it is not to be understood as that by which God is just, but by which men are just whom he justifies by his grace. For thence they are saved, whence they are just; since that which he says, *The physician is not needed by the healthy, but by those who are ill*, he expounded in what follows, saying, *I came not to call the just, but sinners*[^152]. Not, therefore, according to the works of justice which we ourselves did, but according to his mercy he saved us through the washing of regeneration. In which grace by hope we have been saved. Whence in that psalm it is subjoined: *But the sons of men will hope under the cover of your wings. They will be inebriated by the abundance of your house, and you will give them to drink of the torrent of your pleasure; for with you is the fount of life, and in your light we will see light. Hold out your mercy to those who know you, and your justice to those who are upright of heart*[^153]. To this justice of God, then, pride is contrary, by which one trusts as though in his own works; and therefore there it follows: *Let not the foot of pride come to me*[^154].

**30. 73.** This justice, by which his faithful are just, meanwhile living from faith, until by perfect justice they are brought to sight, just as by perfect salvation even to the immortality of the body itself, is the grace of the New Testament. Whence in another place the Apostle says: *For Christ we act as ambassadors, as though God exhorting through us; we beseech you, for Christ, be reconciled to God*[^155]: and thence he subjoins, *Him who knew no sin he made sin for us*—that is, a sacrifice for sins; for in the Law too those things were called sins which were offered for sins—*that we might be the justice of God in him*[^156]: that is, in his body which is the Church, of which he is the head, we may be the justice of God; which those, being ignorant of, and wishing to establish their own—that is, as though glorying in their own works—are not subject to the justice of God. Hence too in this psalm, when he had said, "They will announce his justice," it follows and he adds, "to a people that will be born, whom the Lord has made." For what people is there which the Lord has not made, according to that by which they are men, who created the beasts too, by whom is all life, and all nature made and created? But it is so to be understood, "whom the Lord has made," that they be understood made by him not only in that they are men, but even in that they are just; according to that which I have now more than once recalled as said by the Apostle: *For we are his workmanship, created in Christ Jesus in good works, which God prepared that we should walk in them*[^157].

**31. 74.** The changeableness, then, of the rational soul is admonished, that it may know that, except by the participation of the unchangeable good, it cannot be just, saved, wise, blessed, nor can that good be for it, by its own will, but evil. For by its own will it is turned away from the unchangeable good, and by that turning away it is vitiated: nor can it be healed through itself, but by the gratuitous mercy of its Creator, which in this life establishes it, living from faith, in the hope of eternal salvation. Whence let it not be high-minded, but fear; and by that chaste fear let it cleave to God, who cleansed it from its own uncleanness—by which it inordinately loved lower goods—as from a certain spiritual fornication: nor let it be lifted up by human praises, lest it be among the foolish virgins, rejoicing in another's praise (for this remains last of your questions), and working good things on account of that very empty praise, not on account of their own conscience, where God is their witness; but let it be among the wise virgins, where it may say what the Apostle says: *For this is our glory, the testimony of our conscience*[^158]. For this is to carry oil with oneself, not to buy it from sellers—that is, from flatterers. For flatterers sell their praise like oil to the foolish. Of this oil it is said in the Psalm: *The just man will amend me in mercy, and reprove me; but let not the oil of the sinner anoint my head*[^159]. He chose to be mercifully reprehended by the just, and in a manner buffeted, rather than to be praised, that his head might grow in pride, by the flattery of the sinner.

**31. 75.** For it seems to me the answer of those mocking, when it is said to them by the wise virgins, *Go rather to those who sell, and buy for yourselves*[^160]: just as in a certain book of Wisdom it is written, where he says to the despisers, *I too will laugh over your perdition*[^161]. But that answer by which it was said to those asking oil, *Lest perhaps it not suffice for us and for you*[^162], was said not despairingly, but humbly. For who would so presume of his own conscience as to be certain that it could suffice for him in the judgment of God, unless the merciful judge mercifully? *For judgment is without mercy to him who has not done mercy*[^163]. But the lamps themselves, kindled, are the good works, namely, of which the Lord says: *Let your good works shine before men, and let them glorify your Father who is in heaven*[^164]. So far does the intention of the wise virgins reach, that they therefore wish their good works to be seen by men, not that they themselves be praised by them, but that God be glorified, from whom they have it that they work well: and through this they rejoice in the interior good, which is before God, where the alms is in secret, that the Father, who sees in secret, may repay. And therefore the lamps do not fail, because they are enlivened by interior oil—that is, by the intention of a good conscience, by which, before God, for his glory, is done whatever shines before men in good works. But the lamps of the foolish virgins, not carrying this oil with them, fail; that is, the good works do not perseveringly shine, when human praise has been withdrawn, for which they did them, leading their intention only so far as to be seen by men, not that the Father who is in heaven be glorified. Which intention is of everlasting glory, by which the soul knows that it owes to God that it has been justified for doing good works, and therefore loves to be praised in him, not in itself. Whence elsewhere the man of God sings: *In the Lord my soul will be praised*[^165], so that he who glories may glory in the Lord.

**32. 76.** But what is it that is written in the same Gospel reading, that all, the bridegroom delaying, slept? If we understand this sleep to be that, as it were the judgment delaying—for which to be done Christ is to come—iniquity abounding, love grows cold, how shall we place there the wise virgins, when they are rather among those of whom it is said, *But he who perseveres unto the end, he will be saved*[^166]? It was said, then, "all slept"; because not only the foolish, who for the sake of human praise work good things before men, but even the wise, who do this in order that God may be praised, experience that death: for from both kinds they die. And this death is often in the Scriptures called sleeping, on account of the future resurrection as a kind of waking. Hence the Apostle says: *But about those who sleep I do not wish you to be ignorant, brethren*[^167]; and in another place: *Of whom very many remain until now; but some have fallen asleep*[^168]: and innumerable other testimonies on this are openly found through the Scriptures of both Testaments. Whence that one too says, *Sleep, the kinsman of death*: and if you notice, you will find many things even in secular writings, where death is compared to sleep. The Lord, then, signified that there would be a time, in which, amid the tribulations and temptations of this world, his coming would now be awaited and hoped, as it were near and imminent, for which those who seem to be in his family should prepare themselves. For this is what he says, that they went out to meet the bridegroom and the bride. The bridegroom, indeed, the Son of God: but the bride, either because he is to come in that body which he took from the Virgin; or because the Church herself will then appear more illustrious, so that to the assembly of the whole body the members may run together, by whose running together its greatness will be shown.

**33. 77.** He called them virgins on account of continence. But ten—that is, five and five—on account of the number by which the senses of the body are reckoned, in which continence dwells, when one abstains from base and unlawful pleasures. The lamps, as was said, are the good works, and most of all those pertaining to mercy; and that praiseworthy conduct itself which shines even before men: but it matters greatly with what intention of mind it is done; therefore he says some are wise, others foolish. But he distinguishes them by this, that the foolish did not take oil with them, but the wise received oil in their vessels—that is, in their hearts, where the very participation of the inmost and supernal good takes place. Whence in a certain psalm, when it had been said, *Sacrifice the sacrifice of justice, and hope in the Lord*; *Many,* he says, *say, Who shows us good things?* Then, to manifest by the love of what good we ought to work justice—that is, to sacrifice the sacrifice of justice: *The light of your countenance is signed upon us, O Lord: you have given gladness in my heart*[^169]. By the intention of this good, somewhat participated, and to be more fully and perfectly participated, he who works well, and conducts himself praiseworthily even in the sight of men, has oil with him, by which his good works, shining even in the sight of men, are not extinguished; because in him love does not grow cold, iniquity abounding, but perseveres unto the end. This oil the foolish virgins do not have with them, since, attributing it to themselves if they do any good work, they must needs be puffed up with pride, and on account of this vice be so delighted by human praise that they seem to glow and shine by the joy of it, if they do any good.

**34. 78.** But the bridegroom delaying, all slept. For he will not come then when he is hoped, but in the middle of the night, when it will be very dark—that is, hidden—whether he comes. Whence he says, in the middle of the night a cry was made: *Behold, the bridegroom comes, go out to meet him*[^170]. Surely this cry is that trumpet which the Apostle, recalling, says: *For the trumpet will sound, and the dead will rise incorrupt*[^171]. By the name of trumpet he wishes some most evident and most illustrious sign to be understood: which in another place he calls the voice of the archangel and the trumpet of God. Which voice is in the Gospel said to be that of the Lord Jesus Christ himself, which those who are in the tombs will hear, and will come forth. They rise, then, all those virgins, both the wise and the foolish, and trim their lamps—that is, they prepare to render an account of their works.

**34. 79.** But then, in the evidence of that cry and the resurrection of the dead, because there will be no doubt of the now instant and present judgment, every solace of human praise will be withdrawn. For there will be no leisure to dispute about another, or to judge about another, or to gratify and favor another, when each one will bear his own load, and will think of the account to be rendered of his own deeds: and through this the mind of the foolish virgins will indeed be borne by its own habit, but, finding no human praises, will fail. For they did not genuinely say, "With you is my praise"; or, "In the Lord my soul will be praised"; nor did they glory in the Lord, since, being ignorant of the justice of God, they established their own justice. And through this they ask oil from the wise—that is, some consolation; and they neither find nor receive it, those answering that they do not know whether even their own conscience suffices for them, by which they await mercy under that judge who, *when he shall sit on the throne, who will glory that he has a chaste heart, or who will glory that he is clean from sin*[^172], unless mercy exult over judgment? which will be over those who did those works of mercy with this intention, that he might have mercy on them, from whom they knew they had received what they had; nor did they glory as though they had not received, but had through themselves what to please by, like the foolish, who both please themselves as though about a good prepared by themselves for themselves, and are praised by flatterers or by those in error as though they themselves were something. But he who thinks himself something, when he is nothing, as the Apostle says, deceives himself. But let each prove his own work; and then he will have glory in himself only, and not in another: this is to carry oil with oneself, not to depend on another's praise. But what glory will he have in himself, except him to whom it is said, *My glory, and the one lifting up my head*[^173]; so that—what must often be said—he who glories may glory in the Lord?

**34. 80.** Accordingly the wisdom which dwells in the wise virgins, according to that which it foretold above, when it says to the despisers and to those refusing to receive sound doctrine, "I too will laugh over your perdition," says to the foolish virgins, "Go rather to those who sell, and buy for yourselves": as though it said, "Where are those who deceived you with most lying praises, when you were both deceived by them, because in yourselves, not in the Lord, you gloried?" But that which it says, *And while they go to buy, the bridegroom came; and those who were ready entered with him*[^174], seems to me to be understood thus: that by a vicious affection of the heart they will gape after the empty glory which they pursued by the elation of a vain mind. And that very appetite was signified in that which was said, "While they go to buy, the bridegroom came"; and they entered with him to the wedding, those who were ready: that is, those who bore in their heart true faith and true piety, by which they could be conformed to the number and fellowship of the saints, glorying not in themselves, but in the Lord, that they might enter with them into that joy, of which it is said, *Enter into the joy of your lord*[^175]; where there will be the perfect participation of the unchangeable good, of which now, as a certain pledge, is held through faith, that according to this grace we may live, insofar as we live to God, not to ourselves.

**35. 81.** Accordingly that which follows, *Last of all the rest of the virgins come too, saying, Lord, Lord, open to us*[^176]: it was not said that they bought oil, and so came—for there was now no source whence—but that they sought mercy late, when it was now the time of judging, and of separating the upright from the reprobate. But rightly it is answered them: *Amen I say to you, I do not know you*[^177]. For he says this from whom nothing is hidden: but "I do not know you" is nothing else than "You do not know me," when you choose to trust rather in yourselves than in me. For when it is said that God knows us, he grants us the knowledge of himself, so that by this we may understand that not even this is to be attributed to us, that we know God, but that we attribute that knowledge too to his mercy. Whence, when in a certain place the Apostle said, *But now, knowing God*, he corrected himself and said, *nay rather, known by God*[^178]: wishing nothing else to be understood than that he himself made them his knowers? But no one knows God, except he who understands him to be the highest and unchangeable good, by participation of whom he becomes good: which is set in the conclusion of this psalm, "They will announce his justice to a people that will be born, whom the Lord has made." Hence is that too in another psalm: *He himself made us, and not we ourselves*[^179]. For this is to be referred, not to that nature by which we are men, of which nature the same is the creator who is of heaven and earth, of the stars, and of all living things; but rather to that which the Apostle says: *For we are his workmanship, created in Jesus Christ in good works, which God prepared that we should walk in them*[^180].

**36. 82.** I judge that your five questions have been sufficiently solved for you, while I lingered on this as it were sixth of mine, which I set for myself, about the Grace of the New Testament, on account of which the Word was made flesh—that is, he who was the Son of God was made man, taking up our nature, not losing his own: through which, to us too receiving him, power might be given that we, who were men, might become sons of God, changed for the better by participation of the unchangeable good, not unto temporal happiness, but unto the adoption of eternal life, which alone is blessed. Whence it pleased me also to run through the prophetic Psalm, whose first verse he recalled in the passion, showing how God forsakes us, and in what other way he does not withdraw from us; gathering us to eternal goods, but sometimes usefully bestowing temporal ones, and sometimes usefully withdrawing them; so that we may learn not to cleave to them, lest, the interior light despised—which pertains to the new life (whence this very psalm received its name, "for the morning receiving," as it were for the new light)—we willingly dwell in the outer darknesses, whence into the exterior ones are sent those who from these outer things do not turn themselves to the interior; lest, joined to the devil and his angels, we be punished by the final condemnation. Understanding, then, our pilgrimage in this life, let us be crucified to the world, stretching out our hands into the breadth of good works, and by long-suffering persevering unto the end, and having our heart on high, where Christ is, sitting at the right hand of God, and attributing all this not to ourselves, but to his mercy, whose deep judgments weary every searcher. For this is the not idly fabulous, but usefully true, breadth, length, height, depth, whence we may come even to the surpassing knowledge of the love of Christ, and be filled unto all the fullness of God.

**37. 83.** But I know with how not superfluous a solicitude, on the occasion of these questions set forth to me by you, I wished to commend more copiously to you the grace of the New Testament. For it has adversaries, who, disturbed by its profundity, wish to attribute not to God, but rather to arrogate to themselves, that they are good. Nor are they such as you would easily despise; but living continently, and praiseworthy in good works: nor believing in a false Christ, like the Manichees and very many other heretics, but in the same true Christ, equal to the Father and coeternal, and truly made man, and that he has come, and awaiting that he will come; yet being ignorant of the justice of God, and wishing to establish their own. For not in vain did the Lord, when he both recalled those who entered with him to the wedding, and those against whom he closed the doors, and to whom he answered, "I do not know you," call both virgins, on account of continence; and five, on account of the concupiscence of the flesh subdued, endowed with the fivefold sense; both adorned with lamps, on account of the most brilliant praise of good works and good conduct in the sight of men; both going to meet the bridegroom, on account of the expectation by which the coming of Christ is hoped: but yet some wise, others foolish, because the wise received oil in their vessels, but the foolish did not carry this with them; in so many things equal, he shows them unequal in this alone, by this alone imposes on them diverse and contrary names.

**37. 84.** For what is so conjoined as virgins and virgins, five and five adorned with lamps, going to meet the bridegroom, both these and those? And what is so contrary as wise and foolish? namely, that these carry oil in their vessels—that is, the understanding of the grace of God in their hearts, knowing that no one can be continent unless God gives it, and reckoning this very thing to wisdom, to know whose gift this is: but those, not giving thanks to the bestower of all goods, became futile in their thinking, and their senseless heart was darkened; saying they were wise, they became fools. About whom indeed we must in no way now despair, before we sleep: but if they shall so sleep that, when that cry shall be made by which the bridegroom is announced to be now present, they, waking—that is, rising—remain outside; not because they are not virgins, but because, not knowing whence they have what they are, they are foolish virgins, and deservedly will be outside, because they do not carry with them the affection of internal grace.

**37. 85.** When, then, you shall find such, let them not persuade you of the emptiness of their vessels, but do you rather persuade them of fullness. Whence the Apostle says: *Whoever thinks he knows something, does not yet know as he ought to know.* And soon, showing what he meant: *But whoever,* he says, *loves God, this one is known by him*[^181]. Nor did he wish to say, "Knows him," but by saying, "is known by him," he wished more expressly to commend that even this is from him for us, that we love him. For the love of God is poured out in our hearts, not through ourselves, but through the Holy Spirit who was given to us. But it must needs be that he loves God too little, who esteems himself made good not by him, but by himself: but whence can it be that such a one glories not in himself, but in the Lord? For he who glories that he is good ought to glory in him by whom he was made good: and through this, he who esteems himself made good by himself, it follows that he glories in himself, not in the Lord. But the whole intention of the grace of the New Testament, by which we have our heart on high (because *every best gift, and every perfect gift, is from above*[^182]), does this, that we be not ungrateful; and in the very giving of thanks nothing else is done than that he who glories may glory in the Lord. You have a book, prolix indeed, yet, as I judge, not superfluous. But love also to read ecclesiastical writings, and you will find not many things to require of me; but by reading and ruminating, if you also purely entreat the Lord, the bestower of all good things, all things that are worthy of knowledge, or at any rate very many, you will thoroughly learn, with him rather inspiring than with any man admonishing. Although, by the very fact that we approve, by a judgment not erring, one who admonishes well from without, what else do we attest than that we have the internal light as master?
