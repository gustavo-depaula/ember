# Letter 230

DARIUS, TO AUGUSTINE HIS LORD, GREETINGS.

**1.** *Would, holy lord father, that, as my name was brought to your ears—by the kindly favor toward me of your fellow bishops, as you say, Urbanus and Novatus—so the God of all, your God, had offered my very self to your hands and eyes: not that a better file of your judgment would have proved me greater, or perhaps such as the benevolent speech and the commentary of letters of such great men had made me out to you; but that I might have taken in the most true and immortal fruits of your heavenly wisdom rather from your own mouth, like the sweetness of some pure water, from the very present and perpetual stream of the fountain. O me, not only three or four times, as it is in I-know-not-whom, but a thousand times and more than any number blessed!*[^1] *if it had been given me to behold in person your truly star-like countenance, and your divine voice singing divine things, and it had been permitted to take and drink not only the fruits of the mind, but also with the delight of the ears. I would surely think that I was receiving certain laws of immortality, not only from heaven, but established in heaven itself, and hearing certain words of God not far from the temple, but near the very judgment seat of God, standing by it.*

**2.** *That this should befall me, perhaps I deserved on account of my most ardent desire for you; on account of my conscience, I confess, I did not deserve it. But absent too I have gathered no small fruits of a good longing, and I have, from the secondary goods of good men, the highest perfection. I have been commended to him to whom I would wish, by the mouth of holy men, and of two priests distant from each other in places and regions; the one, as I have already said, by his benevolent and, so to speak, present testimony about me; the other, by writings flying to you of the same mind and a like opinion, concurring. Such great and such men have woven for me a kind of crown before you, not from the germ of green flowers, but by the testimony of a glorious voice, as by certain little gems of perpetuity. On account of which I pray to the Most High God for you, and demand your intercession, holy father, so that, since I am by no means conscious to myself of so great a testimony, it may someday come about that I am such. Have not those things now overcome all the losses of my absence—that you yourself deign to address us, to write, to greet, and, with me absent, do not allow me to be absent? I grieved that I had now not been seen, after God: but you attend not to the face of the flesh—for so you say—but, what is more, to that of the heart; and I have seemed to you the more delightful the more deeply you looked. May God grant that I correspond to your judgment, my father, and that I be not guilty before my own conscience, since I do not inwardly see myself such as you yourself have fashioned me to you.*

**3.** *In that same divine and heavenly letter of yours, with eloquence ministering to you, as is your custom, in what you wished to praise, you say that I slay wars—I say, slay wars—with a word. Here indeed, my holy father, my mind emerged, as it were, from certain shadows of thoughts, as though it had recognized its own true praises. For, to confess the whole to Your Beatitude briefly and simply: if we have not extinguished wars, certainly we have deferred them; and, with the prince of all aiding, the evils which had already grown to a certain summit of calamities have been put to rest. Yet I hope from him from whom whatever is good we ought wholly to hope—nay, I even augur of your letter a blessing so generous, so sure, that this very deferral of wars, of which I spoke, may have and hold the perennial and perpetual firmness of peace. For you have said, and by the perpetual law of God you have fixed it, that I should rejoice in this, as you say, my so great and truly good thing, and enjoy in God, from whom you say I received it, that I should be such, and should undertake such things to be done. You then connect: "May God confirm," you say, "what he has wrought in us through you." O vows pronounced not only for me, but for the salvation of all! For this glory of mine cannot be separated from the salvation of all, and, that I may by your prayers be happy, it is necessary that all be happy together with me. May you, father, long take up and pronounce such vows for the Roman empire, for the Roman commonwealth, for those too whom it may occur to you are worthy; whenever, late, you raise yourself to heaven*[^2]*, may you hand them down to posterity and command them to those who follow.*

**4.** *I have gone on perhaps further than I ought, but spoken certainly less than I wished. For I confess that, writing to you, I imagine your countenance to me as if you were present; and although my rude speech and my poor tongue have long been failing me, yet, as if conversing and talking with you in person, I am not sated. Accordingly, measure hence too our longings for you. When the perhaps unwelcome wordiness of an epistolary page had long deserved an end, we set aside shame; while we take counsel for our longing, and suspect that to leave off the discourse is to withdraw from you. I do not wish, then, not to cease, but I cannot: for, if you believe, my father, you have so clung to our senses and our heart, from the time when, not content with your fame so glorious, so great, we preferred to behold you in your writings, since this one and brief letter of yours has stirred up such flames and conflagrations of ardor. Hence we beseech you, and with our whole mind demand, that—since, even by reading you, as never otherwise, we have abundantly and amply despised the gentile rites (for although we received the laws of Christ from our parents, from our grandparents, and even to the last offspring of the line, yet sometimes the proud vanity of superfluous superstition crept into our senses)—I ask that you deign also to send and grant us the books of the* Confessions *written out by you. For if others too, with a ready spirit and a kindly mind, have lavished your writings on us, how much more ought you yourself not to excuse yourself concerning your own?*

**5.** *There is reported a letter of a satrap, or rather of a certain king*[^3]*: beseeching the Lord God Christ, while he was still dwelling within the regions of Judea, and had not yet returned to his heaven, since he himself was hindered by illness from going and proceeding to him, and did not believe he could be healed otherwise, that the salvation and medicine of the world might hasten to him, if he would deign; and lest an injury should seem to be done to so great a majesty, which the king, ignorant, had conceived with a provident but not perfect mind, he is said moreover to have praised his own city, so that God, allured by the beauty of the city and the hospitality of the king, might not disdain the prayers of the suppliant. God came to the aid of the king; he was healed: and, the gift of the petition being amplified, by a letter he transmitted not only health, as to a suppliant, but also security, as to a king; he commanded moreover that his city be forever and always immune from enemies. What can be added to these benefits? I, lowly, and a servant of kings, ask of you my lord that you would not delay to intercede daily with this Christ and the prince God for my sins, and that you would pray unwearied for me, and ask whatever you yourself shall will.*

**6.** *If my long letter brings you weariness, moderate this by the patience of your magnanimity, and impute it to yourself, since you yourself commanded it. Yet we pray, and again and again beseech, that you write again; for so it will come about that we may suspect you to have gladly received my writings. May God grant us Your Beatitude, praying for us through many years, lord truly holy father. Our son Verimodus greets Your Beatitude, who was very glad that in your letter to us you deigned to make mention of him. Some medicines, I know not what, received from the chief physician who is with us, we have given to Lazarus, the holy priest, to be carried to Your Beatitude; which, as the aforesaid man affirms, will profit not a little toward the alleviation of pain and the cure of disease.*

[^1]: See Virgil, Aeneid 1.94
[^2]: See Virgil, Georgics 1.503; Aeneid 9.641
[^3]: See Eusebius, Ecclesiastical History 1.13
