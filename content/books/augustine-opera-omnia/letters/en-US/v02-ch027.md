# Letter 126

To the holy and venerable lady, the handmaid of God Albina, Augustine sends greetings in the Lord.

**1.** The grief of your mind, which you write you cannot unfold, it is fitting to console, not to increase; so that, if it can be, we may heal your suspicions, not, by being angry at them for our own cause, the more disturb your venerable heart, which is dedicated to God. To our holy brother, your son Pinian, no fear of death was inflicted by the people of Hippo, even if perhaps he himself feared something of the sort. For we too feared lest by some lost men—who often mingle with a multitude by hidden conspiracy—there should break out a violent audacity, an occasion of sedition being found which they would stir up as though by just indignation. But, as we could afterward hear, nothing of the sort was said or attempted by anyone: but truly against my brother Alypius they cried out many insulting and unworthy things, from which so great a guilt would that they might deserve, through his prayers, to be absolved. But I, after their first clamors, when I had said to them about not ordaining him against his will, by what promise I was now detained, and had added that, if, my faith being violated, they had him as priest, they would not have me as bishop, returned to our benches, leaving the crowd. Then they, somewhat hesitant and disturbed by my unexpected answer—like a flame a little pressed by the wind—then began to be roused much more ardently, thinking it could happen that either it might be extorted from me not to keep that promise, or, with me keeping the faith of the promise, he might be ordained by another bishop. I was saying to those I could—who had come up to us into the apse, the more honorable and graver men—that I could neither be moved from the faith of the promise, nor could he be ordained by another bishop in the church handed over to me, unless I were questioned and gave permission; and that if I should permit it, I would nonetheless deviate from faith. I added too that they wished nothing, if he were ordained against his will, except that, once ordained, he should depart. They did not believe this could happen. But the multitude, set before the steps, persisting in the same will with a horrible and most persevering roar of clamors, made us uncertain of mind and counsel. Then those unworthy things were cried out against my brother; then graver things were feared from us.

**2.** But although I was moved by so great a stirring of the people and so great a disturbance of the Church, and had said nothing else to that throng than that I could not ordain him against his will; yet not even so was I brought—because I had promised this too, that I would not do it—to persuade him anything about taking up the priesthood: which if I could have persuaded, he would no longer be ordained against his will. I kept the faith of both promises, not only of that which I had already laid open to the people, but even of that in which, with one witness, as far as pertains to men, I was held. I kept, I say, the faith of the promise, not of an oath, in so great a danger; which, though falsely, as we afterward learned, was feared, yet, if it existed, hung in common over all: and the fear itself was common, and, fearing most of all on account of the church in which we were, I was thinking of departing. But it had to be feared lest, more in my absence, he should do something of the sort, both with less reverence and with more ardent grief. Then, if I departed with brother Alypius through the throng of people, it had to be guarded against lest anyone dare to lay hand on him: but if without him, what countenance of esteem would there be, if perhaps something happened to him, and I should seem to have deserted him for this reason, that he might be handed over to a raging people?

**3.** Amid these heats of mine and grave mourning, and the breathing-space of no counsel, behold, suddenly and unexpectedly our holy son Pinian sends to me a servant of God, to tell me that he wished to swear to the people that, if he were ordained against his will, he would altogether depart from Africa; thinking, I believe, that they, since he could not forswear, would no longer cry out with fruitless perseverance to expel from here a man whom we ought at least to have as a neighbor. But to me, because it seemed that their more vehement grief after this oath was to be feared, I held it silently with myself; and because he had at the same time asked that I come to him, I did not delay. When he had said this very thing to me, he at once joined to that oath this also, which, while I was going to him, he had charged me through another servant of God—namely about his presence, if no one should lay the burden of the clergy upon him unwilling. Here I, in such straits, as though refreshed by a breathing breeze, answered him nothing; but to brother Alypius I went with a more hurried step, and told him what he had said. But he, as I think, avoiding lest anything be done with himself as author whence he thought you would be offended, said, "Let no one consult me about this." Which heard, I went to the tumultuous people, and, silence being made, I laid open what had been promised, with the promise of an oath also. But they, who thought of and desired only his priesthood, did not, as I supposed, accept what had been offered; but, muttering a little among themselves, asked that there be added to the same promise and oath, that if at any time it should please him to consent to take up the clergy, he should consent only in the Hippo church itself. I reported it to him; without hesitation he assented. I announced it to them; they were glad; and soon they demanded the promised oath.

**4.** I returned to our son, and found him wavering by what words that promise with an oath could be comprehended, on account of the necessities rushing upon him which could compel him to depart. At the same time too he showed what he feared, lest some hostile incursion should rush in, which was to be avoided by departure. Holy Melania wished there to be added the pretext of unhealthy air too; but she was rebuked by his answer. But I said that a grave cause of necessity, and not to be despised, had been brought forward by him, which would compel even citizens to emigrate: but that if these things were said to the people, it had to be feared lest we should seem to bode ill; and that if the excuse were made under the general name of necessity, it would be thought nothing but a fraudulent necessity. Yet it pleased us to test the mind of the people about this matter; and we found nothing else than what we had supposed. For when his words were recited, said by the deacon, and all had pleased, when the name of the interposed necessity sounded, at once there was an outcry, and the promise displeased, the tumult breaking out afresh, and the people thinking nothing else than that it was being dealt with them by fraud. When our holy son saw this, he ordered the name of necessity to be removed from there, and again the people returned to gladness.

**5.** And when I excused myself by weariness, he was unwilling to go to the people without me; we went together. He told them that he had charged what had been heard from the deacon, that he had sworn, and that he would do these things, and at once pursued everything in the same tenor in which he had dictated it. The answer was, "Thanks be to God," and it was asked that the whole writing be subscribed. We dismissed the catechumens, and at once he subscribed the writing. Then we bishops began to be asked, not by the voices of the people, but yet by the people through honorable faithful men, that we too should subscribe. But when I began to subscribe, holy Melania objected. I marveled why so late, as though we could make that promise and oath undone by not subscribing: but yet I complied; and so my subscription remained incomplete, nor did anyone think we should be further pressed to subscribe.

**6.** But what stirrings, or tongues of men, there were the next day, after they learned that he had departed, I have taken care, as far as I judged sufficient, to intimate to Your Holiness by a memorandum. Whoever, then, has perhaps narrated to you things contrary to what I have narrated, either lies or is deceived. For I feel that I have passed over certain things which seemed to me not to pertain to the matter; yet I have said nothing false. Accordingly, that our holy son Pinian swore in my presence and with my permission is true: but that he swore at my command is false. He himself knows it; the servants of God whom he sent to me know it—first holy Barnabas, then Timasius, through whom too he charged me about the promise of his presence. The people themselves too compelled, by clamoring, to the priesthood, not to the oath: but they did not reject what was offered to them, in the hope that, with him dwelling among us, the will could be brought about by which he would consent to ordination, lest, as he had sworn, he should depart if ordained against his will. And through this both they clamored on account of the work of God (for the sanctification of the priesthood is not other than the work of God); and the fact that they afterward were not glad about the promised presence unless there were added that, if at any time he should wish to consent to take up the clergy, he would consent only in the Hippo church—this is sufficiently in plain view, that they hoped even for his very dwelling among them, and therefore they did not withdraw from that desire of the work of God.

**7.** How then do you say that they did this from a most shameful appetite for money? First, because it in no way pertains to the people who clamored: for just as the people of Thagaste have nothing but joy of your good work from the things you bestowed on the church of Thagaste, so too the people of Hippo and of any other place where, from the mammon of iniquity, you have done the Lord's precepts, or are anywhere going to do them. Not, then, did the people, demanding most ardently that they might take counsel for their church about so great a man, seek their own pecuniary advantage from you; but they loved in you your contempt of money. For if they loved in me what they had heard, that, despising a few little paternal fields, I had been converted to the free service of God, and did not in this envy the church of Thagaste, which is my carnal fatherland; but, since it had not imposed the clergy on me, they, when they could, invaded the holding of me: how much more ardently could they love in our Pinian so great a desire for this world, so great wealth, so great hope, overcome and trodden under by so great a conversion! For I, according to the sense of many who compare themselves with themselves, seem not to have given up riches, but to have come to riches. For scarcely can my paternal property be esteemed a twentieth part, in comparison with the estates of the Church, which I am now esteemed to possess as lord. But in any church—especially of the African churches—let this one of ours be, I do not say priest, but bishop, compared with his former wealth: even if he acts with the mind of one lording it, he will be most poor. Much more clearly, then, and more securely, is Christian poverty loved in him in whom no desire of larger things can be supposed. This kindled the minds of the people, this raised them to that violence of most persevering clamor. Let us not over and above accuse them of shameful covetousness, but rather permit them, without crime, at least to love in others the good which they themselves do not have. For even if there were mixed into that multitude needy ones or beggars who clamored together, and hoped from your venerable abundance a supplement for their own indigence, neither is this covetousness, I think, shameful.

**8.** It remains, then, that this most shameful appetite for money is directed obliquely against the clerics, and most of all against the bishop. For we are esteemed to lord it over the goods of the Church, we to enjoy its wealth. Finally, whatever of these things we have received, we either still possess, or, as it pleased, have disbursed; nothing of it to the people set outside the clergy or outside the monastery, except to a very few needy ones, have we lavished. I do not say, then, that these things ought to have been said by you even chiefly against us; nevertheless, against us alone they could credibly be said. What then shall we do? By what reason do we clear ourselves, if not with our enemies, at least with you? This is a matter of the mind, it is within, far from the eyes, hidden from mortals, known to God alone. What then remains, but to call God to witness, to whom it is known? When, then, you think these things of us, you do not command (which is much better, and which in your letter you thought should be objected to me as blameworthy), but altogether compel us to swear; not by threatening the fear of the death of our flesh, which the people of Hippo were thought to have done, but by threatening the fear of the death of our esteem, which, on account of the weak to whom we try to offer ourselves by whatever conduct as an example of good works, is surely to be preferred even to the life of this flesh.

**9.** Yet we are not angry at you for so compelling us to swear, as you are angry at the people of Hippo. For you believe, as men of men, things that, even if they are not in us, yet are not such as cannot be in us. These things in you are to be healed, not accused; and our fame is to be cleared by you, if our conscience is cleared before the Lord. Who perhaps will grant—as, before this temptation befell, I and my brother Alypius conferred—that it may become most well known, not only to you, our most dear fellow members, but even to our very bitterest enemies, that we are stained by no desire of money in ecclesiastical affairs. Until which be done, if the Lord grant that it be done, behold, meanwhile we now do what we are compelled to do, lest we defer the medicine of your heart to however long a delay of time. God is witness that all that procuration of ecclesiastical affairs, whose dominion we are believed to love, I tolerate, not love, on account of the servitude which I owe to the love of the brethren and the fear of God; so that, if I could without harm to my office, I would desire to be without them. Nor that I feel otherwise about my brother Alypius, God himself is witness. Yet, the people thinking otherwise about him too—and, what is graver, the people of Hippo—he has been precipitated into so great injuries against him: and you, holy ones of God and full of the bowels of mercy, by believing such things of us, in the name of the same people, who in no way pertain to the cause of such covetousness, wished to touch and admonish us; surely to correct us, for not out of hatred, which be far from you: whence I ought not to be angry, but to give thanks, that you could act neither more modestly nor more freely, so as not to object to a bishop, as it were reproachfully, what you felt, but to leave it obliquely to be understood.

**10.** Nor let it be troublesome to you, that you should think yourselves, as it were, burdened, because I thought there must be swearing. For the Apostle did not burden, nor love too little, those to whom he said, *We were not among you in flattering speech, as you know; nor in an occasion of covetousness, God is witness*[^1]. For of an open matter he adduced themselves as witnesses; but of a hidden matter, whom but God? If, then, he rightly feared lest human ignorance think anything of the sort about him, whose labor was in plain view to all, that he took nothing into his own uses from the peoples to whom he dispensed the grace of Christ except in the greatest necessity, but the rest necessary for his living he accomplished by his own hands: how much more must we labor to be believed, who are far unequal both in merit of holiness and in strength of mind, and can work nothing with our hands for the supports of this life; and if we could, with so great occupations, which I do not believe they then endured, we would in no way be permitted? Let not, then, in this cause, the most shameful appetite for money be objected further to the Christian people, which is the Church of God. For it is more tolerably objected to us, upon whom the suspicion of this evil—though false, yet verisimilar—could fall, than to those who, it is agreed, are foreign to this appetite and suspicion.

**11.** But to deny an oath—for minds endowed with any faith, how much more with the Christian faith—is not right; I do not say to affirm anything contrary, but altogether to doubt. About which matter what I think I have sufficiently, I judge, laid open most fully in the letter which I wrote to my brother. Your Holiness wrote to me: "If either I or the people of Hippo hold this, that satisfaction is to be made for an oath violently extorted." But what do you yourself hold? Does it please you that, even with certain death imminent—which then was feared in vain—a Christian should take the name of the Lord his God for a deception, that a Christian should adduce his God as witness to a falsehood? Who surely, if, apart from an oath, he were compelled to false witness with death imminent, ought rather to fear to stain his life than to end it. Hostile battle-lines, armed against each other, surely fight with the most open threat of death; and yet, when they swear to each other, we praise those who keep faith, but deservedly detest those who deceive. But in order to swear, what did each fear from the other but to be killed or captured? And through this, unless an oath extorted by fear of death or captivity is obeyed, unless the faith there given is kept, they are held by the crime of sacrilege, of perjury, even such men as fear more to forswear than to kill a man: and do we set up, as though about to dispute, the question whether an extorted oath of servants of God—preeminent in the gift of holiness, monks running by the distribution even of their own goods to the perfection of the commandments of Christ—is to be fulfilled!

**12.** For why, I beseech you, is that promised presence weighed down by the name of exile, or deportation, or relegation? I think that the priesthood is not exile. Would our friend, then, choose this rather than that exile? Far be it from us that a holy one of God, and most dear to us, be so defended: far be it, I say, that he be said to have preferred exile to the priesthood, or to have preferred perjury to exile. These things I would say, if truly by us, or by the people, an oath of promising his presence had been extorted from him; but now it was not extorted while it was being denied, but accepted while it was being offered. And this in the hope, as we said above, that through that presence it was believed he could even consent to take up the clergy for those desiring it. Finally, whatever be thought of us or of the people of Hippo, far other is the cause of those who compelled him to swear than of those who—I do not say compelled, but—persuaded him to forswear. Let even he himself, of whom we treat, not refuse to consider whether it is worse, an oath under any fear, or, that fear being removed, perjury.

**13.** Thanks be to God, that the people of Hippo do not feel the promise about him to be fulfilled otherwise than that he be present with the will of dwelling, and go where it shall be necessary with the disposition of returning. For if they attended to and exacted the words of the oath, the servant of God ought in no way to depart, just as in no way ought he to have forsworn. But because it would be their crime so to hold—I do not say such a man, but any man—and they themselves proved that they had no other expectation, who, hearing that he had departed about to return, were glad, neither does a truthful oath owe them anything other than that which they expected of it. But what is it that is said, that he, by an oath expressed by his own mouth, made an exception of necessity? As though he did not again, by his own mouth, order this to be removed. Surely, when he himself spoke to the people, then too he would interpose it: which if he had done, the answer would surely not be "Thanks be to God," but it would return to that outcry which had been made, when it was so recited by the deacon. And does it pertain to the matter at all, whether an excuse of necessity for departing be interposed, or not? Nothing else was expected of him than that which we said above. But whoever deceives the expectation of those to whom it is sworn cannot but be a perjurer.

**14.** Let what was promised, then, be done, and let the hearts of the weak be healed, lest, by so great an example, those to whom this pleases be built up to imitating perjury; but those to whom it displeases may most justly say that none of us is to be believed, not only when promising something, but even when swearing. For hence rather are the tongues of enemies to be guarded against, which, like darts, that greater enemy uses to kill the weak. But far be it that we should hope of such a soul anything other than what the fear of God inspires, and what so great an excellence of holiness as is in it exhorts. But I, whom you say ought even to have forbidden it, confess that I could not be so wise as to wish, by so great either tumult or offense, the church which I serve rather to be overturned than to accept that which was offered to us by such a man.
