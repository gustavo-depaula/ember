# Letter 125

To the most blessed and reverently most dear lord, brother and fellow priest Alypius, and to the brothers who are with you, Augustine and the brothers who are with me send greetings in the Lord.

**1.** We grieve indeed grievously, nor can it be that we make light of the fact that the people of Hippo cried out so much to the injury of Your Holiness; but much more grievously is it to be grieved, good brother, that such things are thought about us than that those things are cried out. For when we are believed to wish to retain the servants of God by desire of money, not by love of justice, is it not to be wished that those who believe this should testify the secret of their heart by their voice, and so that some remedies—if it can be—so much the greater may be sought, rather than that they perish in silence, poisoned by pernicious suspicions? Wherefore we must rather be busy—whence we spoke even before this happened—about how it may be persuaded to men, to whom we are commanded to offer ourselves as an example of good works, that what they suspect is false, than about how those are to be reproved who declare their suspicions by voices and words.

**2.** Accordingly I am not angry with holy Albina, nor do I judge her to be reproved, but to be healed of such a suspicion. She, because she did not direct the same words against my person, but as though she complained about the people of Hippo, that they had laid open their covetousness, that they had wished to keep with them a rich man, and one of this kind a despiser and bestower of money—not for the sake of the clergy, but of the money—yet what she felt about us she well-nigh cried out: nor she only, but even her holy sons, who said this too on that very day in the apse. These, then, as I said, I judge to be healed of suspicions of this kind, rather than to be reproved on account of them. For where can security and rest from such thorns be prepared or afforded us, if against us they could sprout in hearts so holy and most dear to us? For of you the unskilled crowd thought this; of us, the lights of the Church: whence you see which is the more to be grieved. But neither, I judge, is to be accused, but to be healed: for they are men, and they suspect such things—false, indeed, yet not incredible—of men. For surely such men are not so foolish as to believe that the people desire their money; especially having already experienced that the people of Thagaste received nothing of it: so therefore neither the people of Hippo. But all this odium boils only against the clerics, and most of all against the bishops, whose dominion seems to be preeminent, who are esteemed to use and enjoy the goods of the Church as possessors and lords. To this so harmful and deadly covetousness, if it can be, my Alypius, let not the weak be built up through us. Remember what we spoke before this temptation, which compels more to this, happened. From this rather, by conferring, with the Lord helping, let us try to provide; and let not our own conscience suffice for us, because the cause is not such that it ought to suffice alone. For if we are not reprobate servants of God, if there is alive in us anything of that little fire by which love does not seek the things that are its own; we ought surely to provide good things, not only before God, but also before men, lest, drinking tranquil water in our conscience, we be convicted of acting with incautious feet, so that the Lord's sheep drink the troubled.

**3.** But as for what you wrote, that we should inquire among ourselves about the kind of oath violently extorted, I beg you that our disputation not make most lucid things obscure. For if certain death were threatened so that a servant of God should swear that he would do something unlawful and wicked, he ought to prefer to die rather than to swear, lest he fulfill the oath by a crime. But now, since only the most persevering clamor of the people compelled the man to no wickedness, but to that which, if it were done, would be lawfully done; and since it was indeed feared lest some lost men—who for the most part are mixed even into a multitude of the good—having found an occasion of sedition and, as it were, just indignation, should break out into some criminal violence by the lust of plundering, yet that which was feared was uncertain; who would judge that, on account of uncertain things—I do not say losses and any bodily injuries, but on account of the very death to be avoided—a certain perjury ought to be committed? That certain Regulus, I know not who, had heard nothing in the holy Scriptures about the impiety of false swearing, had learned nothing about the sickle of Zechariah, and surely had sworn to the Carthaginians not by the sacraments of Christ but by the defilements of demons; and yet most certain torments and a death of horrid example he did not dread, of necessity, so as to swear, but by free will, because he had sworn, took upon himself, lest he forswear. And that Roman censorship of the time was unwilling to have—not in the number of the saints, but in the number of senators; not in heavenly glory, but in the earthly senate-house—not only those who, by fear of death and cruel punishments, had preferred most openly to forswear rather than to return to the savage enemies, but even him who had thought himself absolved of the guilt of perjury because, after the oath, he had returned by some feigned necessity. Thus those who drove him from the senate did not attend to what he himself had thought in swearing, but to what those to whom he had sworn expected of him. Nor had they read what we everywhere sing: *He who swears to his neighbor and does not deceive him*[^1]. We are wont to praise these things, though in men foreign to the grace and name of Christ, with great admiration; and do we still think it must be inquired in the divine Books whether we may ever lawfully forswear—where, lest by the facility of swearing we slip into perjury, it has even been commanded us that we not swear!

**4.** This indeed I do not doubt is most rightly said, that the faith of an oath is fulfilled not according to the words of the one swearing, but according to the expectation of him to whom it is sworn, which the one swearing knows. For words most difficultly comprehend, especially briefly, the sense of which faith is exacted from the one swearing. Whence those are perjurers who, keeping the words, deceived the expectation of those to whom it was sworn; and those are not perjurers who, even the words not being kept, fulfilled that which was expected of them when they swore. Accordingly, because the people of Hippo wished to have holy Pinian not as one condemned, but as a most dear inhabitant of their city, even if it could not be sufficiently comprehended by his words, yet it is so far in plain view what they expected of him, that the fact that he is now absent after the oath moves none of those who could hear, but he will set out for a certain cause, with the will of returning. And through this he will neither be a perjurer, nor be thought one by them, unless he deceives their expectation: but he will not deceive it, unless he either changes the will of dwelling among them, or at some time departs without the disposition of returning; which be far from his character and the faith which he keeps owed to Christ and the Church. For—to omit what you know with me, how dreadful the divine judgment is about perjury—this at least I know, that we ought henceforth to be angry with no one who does not believe us when we swear, if we shall think that the perjury of such a man is not only to be borne with an even mind, but even to be defended. Which may his mercy turn away both from us and from him, who rescues from temptation those who hope in him. As, then, you wrote back in the memorandum, let him fulfill the promise by which he so promised that he would not depart from Hippo, in the way that I, or the people of Hippo themselves, do not depart—who yet have free faculty both of going away and of returning: except that those who are not held by an oath have the power even altogether of migrating and not returning without the guilt of perjury.

**5.** Whether it can be proved that our clerics, or the brothers set in the monastery, were partakers or instigators of the insults against you, I do not know. For when I had inquired about this, it was said that only one of the Carthaginians from the monastery cried out with the people, when they were asking for that priest, not when they were flinging unworthy things against you. I have joined to this letter a copy of his very promise, transcribed from the same little paper which he himself subscribed, and which was emended in my sight.

[^1]: Ps 15:4 (14:4)
