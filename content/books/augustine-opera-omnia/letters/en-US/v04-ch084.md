# Letter 20\*

TO THE MOST RELIGIOUS AND MOST REVEREND AND MOST EXCELLENT LADY, HIS DAUGHTER FABIOLA, AUGUSTINE SENDS GREETINGS IN THE LORD

**1.** Through my lord and brother [\*\*\*] I was gladdened by the reply of your Holiness, to which I wish I could repay the discourse of a greeting without weariness. But now, first crucified by grief myself, I have become importunate and troublesome to your holy repose; yet bear with me patiently—so may you persevere with profit to the end in the grace of Christ. For I know that my letters are never a burden to you but rather a delight. Forgive this letter, for it contains much that you will grieve over; and in Christ share my sorrows with me in mutual love, and join your prayers to mine before the Lord our God, that he may console us.

**2.** I have learned with what kindly devotion you received my beloved son and fellow bishop Antoninus, and with what Christian humanity you consoled the poverty of his journey. Receive, then, who Antoninus is to me and who I am to him, and what I owe him and what I ask of you. As a small child he came to Hippo with his mother and stepfather; they were so poor that they lacked daily food. At length, when they had taken refuge in the help of the Church, and I had learned that Antoninus's father was still living and that the mother, separated from her own husband, had joined herself to another man, I persuaded both to continence; and so the man with the boy lived in the monastery, the woman among the roll of the poor whom the Church supports, and thus all of them began to be under our care in the mercy of God. Then, as time went on (not to dwell on many details), the man died, the woman grew old, the boy grew up; he discharged the office of reader among his companions and had already begun to show such promise that brother Urbanus—who was then a priest with us and prior of the monastery, but is now bishop of the Church of Sicca—wished, in my absence, to have him made a priest on a certain large estate situated in our diocese, because on setting out I had enjoined him to provide someone whom the neighboring bishop might ordain for that place without waiting for my return. This indeed could not be done, since Antoninus shrank from it. Yet when I afterward learned of it, I began to regard him as suited, as it were, to such a charge—not that he was known to me as fully as he ought to have been, but on the testimony of his prior.

**3.** Meanwhile, since I could not, as necessity required, govern so very wide a parish—for not only had many people in the city, but also many congregations in the countryside, come over from the party of Donatus—after deliberating the matter with the brothers, it seemed good to me that in a certain fort called Fussala, which was subject to the see of Hippo, someone should be ordained bishop to whom the care of that region would belong. I sent to the bishop of the first see; he deigned to come; at the very hour the priest whom I thought I had ready deserted me. What was I then to do, if I were to act rightly, except to put off so great a work? But fearing that, if the holy old man—who had barely come to us from afar—returned from us without result, the resolve of all those for whom this had been necessary would be broken, and that they would find people whom the enemies of the Church, mocking the frustration of our effort, might lead astray, I believed it useful to offer for ordination this man who was present, since I had also heard that he knew the Punic tongue; and when I offered him, I was trusted. For they did not ask for him of their own accord, but, since he was one of my own who pleased me, they did not dare to refuse him.

**4.** I thrust, then, upon so great a burden a young man scarcely more than twenty years of age, proved by no prior grades of the clerical office and, in the very things about him that I ought to have known beforehand, unknown to me. You see, of course, my great sin; look at what followed. The young man's mind took fright, raised by no preceding merits of labor to the sudden honor of the episcopate; then, seeing clergy and people subject to him, he was puffed up—as the matter itself has taught—with the insolence of domination, and, teaching nothing by word but driving himself to everything by command, he rejoiced to be feared where he saw himself not loved.

**5.** To fulfill this role, he sought out men suited to himself. There was in our monastery a certain man, formerly one of my notaries, who—to my grief—had turned out not good, and who, because he had been found alone at an improper hour talking with certain consecrated women, had been disciplined with blows and was held in contempt. As soon as this man had deserted the monastery and betaken himself to the bishop of whom we are speaking, he was ordained priest by him, without my counsel and without my knowledge. For I heard the deed done before I could have believed it would happen, even if someone whom I had to believe had told me of it. Then indeed how great a grief invaded my heart—since I dreaded the destruction that would one day come upon that Church through him—I would have you believe, for I cannot put it into words. Yet I took pains, when an opportunity came—the very bishop bringing the gravest complaints to me about such a priest of his—to have him barred from communion and sent back to his own homeland, from which he had been given to me. And it was done; but somehow, again without consulting me, the bishop restored him to his fellowship and friendship. He also made another a deacon—one given to him from the monastery in due order, indeed, but who, once a deacon, proved nothing if not unruly.

**6.** Through these two clerics, the priest and the deacon, and through the defender of the Church, and through a certain other man—whether an ex-soldier or a deserter—whom he commanded more intimately, and through those whom he had made night watchmen from the men of the same fort, and whom he used wherever some slightly larger force of hands was needed: what evils that fort and the neighborhood all around endured, anyone may in some measure learn who does not find it tedious to read the records that were drawn up before the bishops in the Church of Hippo, where I myself also sat, from the laments of many recorded in petitions. He will find there the wretched complaints of poor men and women and—what is graver—of widows, whom not even the very name that Sacred Scripture especially commends to us to defend, nor even old age, could in any degree protect from the robberies and plunderings and unspeakable injuries that were done through these men. Money, household goods, clothing, livestock, crops, timber, finally even stones—whatever fell into their hands a person lost; the houses of some were seized, the houses of others even pulled down, so that from them might be carried off what the construction of new buildings demanded; some things were bought and the price not paid; the fields of some were invaded and, after the crops had been carried off for several years, given back; and certain of their fields were even held and possessed right up to episcopal judgment.

**7.** Besides those things that were entered in the records, we have learned in some part of many others as well, and through those lands they are tossed about and pressed for proof by the groans—not the murmurs—of those who suffered them, but rather by their shouted outcries, if only judges would sit there where the poverty of those people is not worn out, or if those who do sit could manage to hear it all, since scarcely anyone can hold out to review even the things we heard in the ecclesiastical records. Of these, indeed, very few were in any way cleared up, while many were partly excused and put off on account of the absence of those through whom they had been done. But how the presence of those clerics—the priest, that is, and the deacon—was withdrawn from episcopal judgment, and is still being withdrawn, would be long to tell. Yet his own words are on record, where he himself confessed that, though they were with him, they refused to come, even though he had warned them to come.

**8.** We, for our part, ordered the plundered goods to be restored, but we preserved the bishop's episcopate safe and whole for him—lest those evils remain altogether unpunished and be left either for him to pursue further or for others to imitate—going so far in our punishment only as to require that the bishop should indeed sit in one of his cathedral seats, lest he be said to have been transferred to another contrary to the canons, but should not preside any longer over the people of Fussala against their will. This kind of punishment I think is to be reckoned even a benefit, that he should not live among them against their will and most dangerously inflame by his very presence their most bitter hatreds. We decided, to be sure, that he should not be admitted to communion until the things taken away were first restored. He embraced this sentence of ours to such a degree that he neither appealed and, after very few days, paid back coin borrowed in place of the plunder, lest communion be denied him any longer. And many of our brothers and sons, who pitied him along with us—since, of the great and capital charges of four acts of fornication that not the Fussalans but certain others, injured by him in various matters, had either brought or seen to having brought against him, he was, with a very probable truth, cleared—rejoiced with brotherly exultation that it had been so judged about him.

**9.** He also petitioned, by a written request, the holy old man who holds the first see in Numidia, that he would deign to defer until the council the desire of the Fussalans, who were most vehemently demanding that a bishop be ordained for them; and he deferred it. When the council had been reached and it had pleased all who were present that our decisions be carried out, he did not appeal from there either; and if he had, he would even then certainly have done so too late, since he had not appealed from us several months before. Then the senior elder sent bishops to Fussala, in whose presence it should be chosen by votes who would be ordained bishop for them and sent to him to be ordained; and it was done. But when the day of ordination dawned, then it came into this man's mind to appeal. Yet, after the holy old man had given him an account, he acquiesced; and since he was doing this after so long a time had passed since judgment had been given about him, understanding that he was acting in vain, he consented that the eight congregations that had assembled at the Church of Fussala for various reasons to cast votes about ordaining a bishop should be assigned to him. But, so as to sow quarrels once again, he extorted that one even of those congregations that had come to Fussala to request a bishop should be added to him and entered in the letters of the holy old man—namely, the estate of Thogonoetum, where he might have the cathedral seat to which his other congregations would be subject.

**10.** Now this estate is so near the fort that he seemed to have sought in it nothing but occasions for lawsuits by which he might disturb the peace of the Church. Moreover, these same tenant farmers, since they had already had a taste of him from the neighborhood and had endured those evils along with others, wrote to the lady who owned the estate that, if she allowed this to be done, they would immediately migrate, and likewise to me, asking that I intervene on their behalf so that it should not happen; and on their account both she and I wrote to the old man.

**11.** So when this man saw that this had not been granted to him, he thought he should sail, carrying letters of recommendation from the same senior elder—not received then but earlier, when that grave man had simply believed that Antoninus had no faults at all and wished to sail for the sake of freeing men whom the vicar was holding, but had not yet learned clearly from the records the evils done to the Fussalans and their just grief. He gave, then, a petition to the venerable bishop Boniface, in which he lied that, from the day on which judgment was given about him, he had been in communion (for, as I have noted above, he had been excommunicated until the things taken from the Fussalans should be restored; on which account, after a few—but still some—days, he had paid back the coin so that communion might be restored to him); he also kept silent about the whole necessary sequence of the matters of the case, and obtained letters that were certainly most cautiously worded.

**12.** For the bishop Boniface, of venerable memory, appointed judges to determine whether the account he asserted was sound, whether he had faithfully set forth the sequence of events, whether matters were as he had stated in the wording of his petition; and only then would the Church of Fussala be restored to him, as to one having no faults on account of which it had been justly taken from him. Those who could come gathered at a certain place in Numidia, that is, the Church of Tegulata; there were present there also others whom he had not summoned, having other reasons for coming; and, although the number of bishops requested by him had not been met, he nevertheless said that they were sufficient for him. We too were present, namely brother Alypius and I, summoned by the letters of the primate—not to judge him again (for what could be more improper?), but so that, if the matter required, we might render an account of our judgment, which would be reported to the Apostolic See. So the things he had brought were read out; the old man Aurelius, primate of Numidia, set forth the reason why he had ordained a bishop for the Fussalans. And in his exposition there came to light the things this man had omitted in order to obtain such letters from Rome, and that he had not faithfully reported the sequence of events.

**13.** Then the bishop Antoninus petitioned that the priest whom the Fussalans had sent be admitted; and when he had entered, the letters of the priests and of the Fussalans were read out. When Antoninus saw that these were full of wretched complaints against him, on account of which they refused in every way to receive him as bishop—of whom they had been justly and rightly rid—he did not believe that they had been sent by them, and he asked the holy old man to deign to go himself to those very places with some of the number of bishops who had been granted to him, and to explore the dispositions of the priests and the people, on this condition: that, if the Fussalans should refer to receiving him, he would accept against their will the people of Thogonoetum added to those eight congregations that he already held before; and that the holy old man should also ask of me to confirm, by recording it as well, what I had promised them apart from any formal record concerning five others of those congregations, so that he would not be hostile to the Fussalans.

**14.** When I had done this without any difficulty, we parted as if in peace, except that I saw that the place of Thogonoetum was resisting him no less than the Fussalans, and that the lady owner would not grant it—which seemed so also to the venerable old man Aurelius. Finally, the old man promised in those records, as he had been asked, that he would come to Fussala; but no one promised him in those records the people of Thogonoetum; and the bishop Antoninus's further pursuit of the matter was such that the bishops might recognize from the Fussalans' response what ought to be done.

**15.** After some days, as had been agreed, they came to Fussala. There were with the old man two bishops whom he had requested from among those who had been in the city, and likewise others granted to him, the nearest to that fort whom he was able to find; three others also accompanied the old man as an escort, as is customary. I was absent, because I do not dare even to see the Fussalans, to whom—now at rest after our judgment, with their bishop accepted, and again disturbed by this man's restlessness—I myself have become hateful; they no longer mutter under suppressed murmur about the great calamity brought upon them by him, but cry out with open voices and wailings; and this very man, with a most ungrateful mind, suspects me to be nothing other than his enemy. Brother Alypius too was absent, returning to his own affairs.

**16.** And so, under the sight of six bishops, that people, gathering with frequent eagerness, was questioned and found to be such as it had shown itself when it sent a priest with letters to the Church of Tegulata—indeed even more vehement and bitter. For what he did, wishing first to terrify them, there is no need to write; he himself, perhaps, will confess it to you if he is pressed, although those who deliver our letters can also faithfully report it to your Reverence. So when the multitude had been questioned and on one day had sufficiently expressed what it thought of this man, it most insistently demanded the presence of its own bishop, since he had not been present when the first inquiry was made. And so, with one day intervening and their own bishop now present, they answered much against this man on their own behalf, cried out much, and everything was written down.

**17.** Then the old man wrote to me to meet him at some place where together we might all see what was being done. As I was going there, on the journey I received a letter from the most distinguished woman to whom the estate of Thogonoetum belongs, indicating that her man had written to her that the holy old man had told him he had heard from the bishop Antoninus that she had consented that Thogonoetum should be his. "This," she says, "I never knew. Rather, when he had come to me, he himself asked me not to consent." This letter of the religious woman I carried with me, since I saw it to be very necessary—although I had already come to know that he had done this, but I did not see by what sure proof he could be convicted if he denied it in the absence of that honorable woman.

**18.** When, therefore, we had come together to a certain place ten miles distant from the fort of Fussala, to which I was unwilling to go, we all began, each as best he could, to plead with him not to contrive any further disturbance and overthrow for catholic Christians, a catholic bishop. And when discussion had begun about that estate of Thogonoetum, I produced the letter of the lady owner; and when, on its being read, all our brothers and fellow bishops who were present began to be horrified and to rebuke him gravely, he answered that he had not said it so, but that, when she said first that she would not grant him the place, he had said, in the voice not of one asking but of one indignant: "If you do not wish to, do not grant it; I do not wish it either." From there we passed to other proposals, by which we sought, if it could be done, that he should accept two other places in exchange for that estate and be no longer troublesome to anyone of those that had already begun to belong to the bishop of Fussala; but it could not be done, since they all most vehemently refused him.

**19.** We were also in a certain one of those eight places that had been assigned to him and where he presided without any controversy. For the manager of his estate had asked us to meet there, and there too we did much with him in vain. There, to be sure, I took another letter from the lady owner of Thogonoetum, since I had written back to her what he had answered and had asked her to indicate in order, by a letter there, how the matter had been carried out. She wrote, first, that through her son-in-law this had been charged to her by him: that he should ask her to grant him this favor, not to consent that Thogonoetum be his, nor that it be in his diocese, if the bishop should sit somewhere other than Fussala; afterward she confirmed that he had asked this of her also with his own mouth. And of this she most openly wrote not only her son-in-law but also the bishop of the place where they then were as witnesses.

**20.** When this letter had been read out before the brothers, he was so disturbed that he answered me nothing but abuse. And since the old man had told me that he had complained that, on the day on which the people of Fussala were last questioned, their bishop was present, it was decided that they should be consulted a third time in his absence, the tenant farmers of each estate separately, with their stewards or agents, without their managers. But we had to pass through Thogonoetum on the way to Fussala, with me earnestly asking that he restore communion to them, since, when they had gravely rioted against the bishop Antoninus before him, he had excommunicated them; and I greatly feared that through rustic sadness they might utterly perish. For they had been so left between the two bishops that I had come to know that they had begun to apostatize in some places. Hence I feared that the dreadful wound of my heart would grow, and I hastened to heal it as quickly as possible.

**21.** When we had come there toward evening, on the next day we saw them gathered in the church. But when the venerable old man began, in Punic words, to make a discourse to them about the bishop Antoninus, they declared that will of theirs with great outcries; and when he had asked them what wrong he had done them, that they resisted him with such obstinacy, each began to tell what he had suffered. When they were ordered to do this by name in the records, they answered that they were afraid lest those become known to him whom he would persecute and ruin one by one. But when they were ordered rather to do what had been said, suddenly they all, leaving us, departed with most resentful groans, so that not even any of the consecrated women remained. Who can express how greatly we were all troubled with fear, lest in the judgment of Christ their ruin be bound to our neck more heavily than that millstone of the ass that is in the Gospel?[^1]

**22.** When, therefore, we had gone out from the church after the divine offices were finished, we found also two of the Fussalans sent with a petition of theirs, through which they said that a rumor had reached them that we wished to question them separately, although their will had already so often appeared without discrepancy as to which bishop they hold, and that they would say nothing one by one other than what all together had been able to say. But if this were being done in order that their enemy might know by their designated names whom he ought to persecute, then we must understand that by this rule they were being handed over to death; and that surely it ought to be enough that we had killed their souls by giving them Antoninus—their bodies too were not to be handed over by us, so that through Antoninus they might die a second time. In the same petition they also set down this: that, if it seemed good to us, we should order the case to be heard anew, with their petitions restored which they had submitted against Antoninus at Hippo Regius, and other things that it would be long to recount. But since it could be said that this petition did not have the will of the people, but had been sent by some one or two, or at least by very few, it seemed that the plan of coming to them should not be changed.

**23.** Accordingly, the old man, with those whom it was fitting, went on to Fussala at midday; I and their bishop remained in the same place; but the following day, on which the old man questioned the people a third time, we met him at a certain place through which he was going to pass from there, and there we spent the whole day while he was at Fussala. After this the bishops came from there to us with the holy old man, carrying the groans and the recorded voices of the wretched, in which they did not think even I should be spared. For they cried out even about me myself what I deserved to hear: that I had been the author of so great a calamity of theirs by giving them a man whom they had not asked for, by whom they were afflicted with such great evils.

**24.** Then a letter was written to the same bishop Antoninus, and he met us at a certain fort, Gilva, to which a definite necessity of the Church was compelling the old man to come; for he had so parted from us that he would meet us wherever he was summoned by letter. There, when he had heard from the very bishops whom he had requested among the other judges what they had seen and heard while present, and when we were all urging him, each as best he could, that he ought, if he thought of himself as a bishop, to do nothing else than to govern peaceably those congregations that had received him without any scandal to the Church and without any disturbance, he answered that he did not wish to have even these, and that his plan was fixed: to sit in some most secret place, separated from the crowds, removed from ill will, serving God; and this he was eager, if we wished, to prove to us through witnesses to whom he said he had said this before he saw us. Since I was unwilling to believe so great a good of his mind easily, I told him that, if he truly thought and said such things, it should not irk him to offer this also as a sacrifice of mercy to our God—to make his Church secure, with the fear it had of him removed—by expressing this settled will of his in episcopal records; and when he had affirmed that he would say nothing at all in the records, we said that he should at least express it in his own writing; and when he answered that he would not do even this, he heard what he ought: that he was not truly thinking of serving God, since it pleased him to leave the Church of him whom he was to serve in such a turmoil of fear.

**25.** But then, when he saw himself pressed by the words of the bishops to which he could not reply, at last he burst out into what he was hiding in his heart and said, with terrible face and voice, that he could in no way be persuaded not to strive to return to the Church of Fussala by whatever means he could. Hearing this, I began to press the holy old man that something be done with him, according to the records of Tegulata, in ecclesiastical records, which could be reported to the Apostolic See. "I," he said, "will say nothing in the records," and he rose up in turmoil and went off, and immediately returning, with troubled motion of body and mind, declared that he would go to the Apostolic See—as if we were going to send to another whatever had been transacted with him in the records.

**26.** It remained, therefore, that the Apostolic See should be informed by letters sent and by records. To see that this was done we took care with all the speed we could. See what a long tale we have become to Jews and Gentiles and heretics and whatever inner enemies there are—would that it were without the ruin of those who, freed from heresy and now drawing breath into some light of unity, find the catholic name made hateful by us, unless their weakness will be consoled at least to this extent: that they not have a bishop whom they cry out with just sorrows that they cannot have.

**27.** I thought, then, that these things should be written to your Excellency so that, if he comes to see you, you may not be ignorant of what you ought to admonish him. For you will much better bestow upon such a needy man counsel for eternal life than support for the present; for that alms is more dangerously lacking by whose want the heart dies even while the flesh is safe. Let him cease to desire to lord it over the members of Christ gathered by the blood of others. For ever since he began to be bishop there, he has suffered no losses or wounds from the Donatists, neither he himself nor any of his priests or clerics or any of the men placed under his rule. But what evils our people endured in order that he might find such peace there it is horrifying to tell; let the congregations be enough for him that God willed him to receive without scandal, even one of which, ruled piously and diligently, wins a great reward with God. But this man does not consider this, who desires—with blasphemy of the name of Christ and the deadly groaning of wretched men—to rejoice in a multitude of congregations, seeking not to gain many for God but to vaunt himself through many. Otherwise he would not desire with such effort to make his own those whom he sees to be already Christ's.

**28.** Hear these things from me, I beseech you, and whatever the Lord grants you to say, do not keep silent toward a man of whose soundness of mind I long to rejoice. For you are of such an age in relation to him that you may fittingly show him the affection of a parent. For if he does not live with God too greatly angered, he does not despise in you the counsels of his mother; I know that you have so risen with Christ that you *seek the things that are above, not the things that are on earth*[^2]; do not, then, be afraid to give faithful counsel to a bishop who seeks the things that are upon earth. For you in this world seek God; he in the Church seeks this world.

**29.** For—what you would perhaps not believe if another had told you—he did not hesitate to buy estates in his own name, not the Church's, a man who was made bishop from the monastery, having nothing beyond what he was wearing on the very day. You ask, perhaps, from what he bought them; far be it from me to say it was from those robberies that the Fussalans complain they suffered; the things thus seized were consumed at once. But for his support and that of those who were with him I had given him an estate of the Church of Hippo situated in the same territory of Fussala. This he leased out and, having received the rent of a whole five years, found the price with which he could buy. And if I should wish to relate what the seller complained of about him in a petition to the emperor, and to what peril of a lawsuit it came, and how the defender of the Church of Fussala—before whom the seller lamented that he had been held in private custody so that he might sell the property to the bishop at the lowest price—scarcely escaped, with our laboring, the penalty of public condemnation, because he had already confessed that he had done this at the bishop's command (although the bishop said that he had given the order not to press him into selling the property to himself, but, on account of another fault, that he be held in that custody)—if I should wish to tell it, what end would there be to the letter?

**30.** He bought another small property, this too in his own name, but from what means I do not know; and in this very one he is said to have so treated, by his petition in our court, his partner—with whom he possessed an undivided half—that he himself took the whole produce and stripped the common house of its roof tiles. We heard it, it was proved to us, we ordered it restored; he also produced for us, and it was read out in our court, a letter of his brother writing that he had been pressured by the bishop to sell that very share of the property and had not received the price he ought to have. But since it was not made clear to us whether the letter was really his brother's, we settled the suit as best we could between those present, and reserved the action for the one absent.

**31.** But this man gave that property to another, whose house he pulled down, and transferred to his own building everything of which it had been constructed; with which man I myself intervened, lest he burden the bishop with a petition and complaint of his in our court. And so the case was ended between them by private settlement, on the terms that he should receive from this man, for his share, that estate from which he might recover his own losses; and even now he—a bishop from a most poor monk—says to the Fussalans: "Restore to me my house, which I built in your fort"—a house he was seen to be building not for himself, of course, but for the Church; and would that it were from good and just offerings, not robberies! For almost nothing in the fabric of that house is reported to be that is not shown to have been taken from another's property, and the place from which it was taken pointed out with the finger.

**32.** But from here another question arises; this I wished to lament before your Sincerity: that a young man brought up in the monastery by us—who, when we received him, left nothing of his own, neither distributed to the poor nor contributed to the common society—now glories as it were over his estates and his house; and he wishes to make not only these things his own but even the very flock of Christ, while he wishes to be of the number of those of whom the Apostle says, *They seek the things that are their own, not the things that are Jesus Christ's*[^3]; and how great a wound of my heart this is, he sees who heals it.

**33.** Through Christ I beseech you, and through his mercy and judgment, that you help me in this case both for his sake and for the sake of the Church. For to this end I wished you to be instructed—perhaps more talkatively than modestly—not that you should hate him, but rather that you should counsel him truly and spiritually, as far as the Lord has willed you to be able, by not allowing him to harm himself. For to whom else will he do graver harm than to himself, if he strives to disturb and overthrow the Church, which he ought to wish to gain for Christ, not for himself? I believe he will obey your holy Kindness and will not raise his pride against you, if the fountain of mercies hears, on his behalf, my tears, so frequent and so abundant.

[^2]: See Col 3:1-2
