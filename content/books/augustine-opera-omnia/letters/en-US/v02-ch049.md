# Letter 148

A MEMORANDUM TO THE HOLY BROTHER FORTUNATIANUS.

**1. 1.** As I asked when present, so now too I admonish you that you would deign to see and ask our brother of whom we spoke together, that he would forgive me if he took anything said about him too harshly and roughly in that letter which I do not now regret having written, because I said that the eyes of this body neither see God nor will see him. For I added the reason why I said this—namely, lest God himself be believed to be bodily, and visible in the space and interval of a place; for the eye of this body can see nothing otherwise. And lest what was said, *face to face*, be so taken as though God were bounded by the members of a body. I do not therefore regret having said that, lest we be so impiously minded about God himself as to suppose him not everywhere whole, but divisible through local spaces: for such are the things we know by these eyes.

**1. 2.** But if anyone, holding no such thing about God, but believing him to be an unchangeable and incorporeal spirit, everywhere whole, thinks that so great a change of this body is to come, when from animal it shall be spiritual, that we shall be able to see, through such a body, even an incorporeal substance—one not divisible by intervals or spaces of places, nor bounded by the outlines and limits of members, but everywhere whole—I wish that he would teach me, if he is truly wise. But if in this he is mistaken, it is far more tolerable to arrogate something to the body than to derogate from God. And if that view is true, it will not be contrary to my words, which I set down in that letter. For I said that the eyes of this body would not see God, regarding this: that the eyes of this body can in no way discern anything but bodies which are separated from them by some interval of place; for if there is no interval, we do not see even the bodies themselves through them.

**1. 3.** Further, if our bodies shall be changed into such unlikeness to themselves that they shall have eyes through which may be seen that substance which is neither diffused nor bounded through spaces of places, having one part here and another there, in a smaller place smaller and in a larger larger, but is everywhere incorporeally whole, these bodies will be something far different, and will not be the same; nor will they be something other merely by having mortality and corruption and the heaviness of weight removed, but they will in a manner be turned into the very power of the mind, if they shall be able to see in the way it is then granted to the mind, but is now not granted even to the mind itself, to see. For if by changed habits we say a man is not the one who was, if, finally, by changed ages we say the body itself is not what it was, how much more will it itself not be, changed by so great a transformation that it not only lives immortally but even sees the invisible? Wherefore, if they shall see God, the eyes of this body shall not see him; because in this too the body itself will not be the same, changed unto that force and power: and that opinion is not contrary to those words of my letter. But if thus far it shall not be the same, because now mortal, then immortal; now weighing down the soul, then most easy for every movement under no weight: yet for seeing those things which are discerned by spaces and intervals of places, if it shall be nothing other than itself, it will in no way see an incorporeal substance, everywhere whole. Whether, then, this be true or that, by either it is true that the eyes of this body shall not see God. For either they shall be of this body, and shall not see; or they shall not be of this body, if they shall see; since by so great a change they shall be of a far different body.

**1. 4.** But I am prepared, if this brother knows anything better about this matter, to learn it either from him or from the one from whom he learned it. And if I were speaking mockingly, I would say that even concerning a bodily God, divisible through places by members, I was prepared to learn—which I do not say, because I do not speak mockingly, and I do not doubt at all that God is not such, and it was lest he be believed to be such that I wrote that letter. In which, while I was anxious in admonishing—which I wrote with the names left unspoken—I was excessive and improvident in rebuking, and did not think of a fraternal and episcopal person as a brother and bishop, in the way that would have been fitting: this I do not defend but reprehend; this I do not excuse but accuse. Let it be forgiven, I ask; let him remember our former love, and forget the new offense. Let him surely do what he was vexed that I did not do; let him have leniency in granting pardon, which I did not have in writing that letter. This I ask through your charity, which I had wished, when present, to ask of him when present, if I had the opportunity. When I had tried this, with a venerable man, to be preferred in honor by us all, writing to him, he refused to come—suspecting in him, perhaps, as most human things are, some guile, I think; from which that I am far removed, do you make him believe as much as you can, who, being present, can do it more easily. Tell him with how great and how true a grief I spoke with you about the offense of his mind. Let him know how I do not despise him, and how much I fear God in him, and think of our head, in whose body we are brothers. To the place where he lives I thought I ought not to come, lest we should make a spectacle laughable to outsiders, grievous to our own, shameful to ourselves. Through your holiness and charity the whole thing can be done rightly; for it is done by him who through his faith dwells in your heart, whom I believe he does not spurn in you, since he recognizes him in himself.

**1. 5.** I certainly, in this matter, have found nothing better to do than to ask pardon of the brother who complained that he was wounded by the harshness of my letter. He too will do, as I hope, what he knows is commanded him by him who, speaking through the Apostle, says: *Forgiving one another, if anyone has a complaint against another, as God also in Christ has forgiven you. Be therefore imitators of God, as most beloved children, and walk in love, as Christ also loved us*[^1]. Walking in this love, let us, if we can, inquire more diligently and in harmony about the spiritual body which we shall have in the resurrection; for even if we are otherwise minded in anything, this too God will reveal to us, if we abide in him. *But he who abides in love abides in God, and God abides in him; for God is love*[^2]—whether by ineffably existing as its fountain, or by bestowing it on us through his Spirit. If, then, it can be taught that love will at some time be seen by bodily eyes, perhaps God too will be able to be; but if love can never be so, much less can its very fountain, or whatever can be said more excellently and fittingly about so great a matter.

**2. 6.** Certain great men, most learned in the holy Scriptures, who greatly aided the Church and the good zeal of the faithful by their writings, when occasion was given them, said that the invisible God is seen invisibly—that is, through that nature which is invisible in us also, namely a pure mind or heart. The blessed Ambrose, when he was treating of Christ according to that which he is the Word, says: "For Jesus is seen not with bodily, but with spiritual eyes." And a little after: "The Jews," he says, "did not see him; for their foolish heart was darkened"—here showing whence he is seen. Likewise, when he was speaking of the Holy Spirit, he interposed the words of the Lord, saying: *I will ask the Father, and he will give you another Paraclete, that he may be with you forever; the Spirit of truth, whom this world cannot receive, because it does not see him, nor know him*[^3]. "Rightly, then," he says, "did he show himself in a body, since in the substance of the divinity he is not seen. We have seen the Spirit, but in a bodily form: let us see the Father also; but because we cannot see him, let us hear him." And a little after: "Let us hear, then," he says, "the Father; for the Father is invisible. But the Son too is invisible according to the divinity; for *no one has ever seen God*[^4]: since, then, the Son is God, in that very thing in which the Son is God, he is not seen."

**2. 7.** But holy Jerome says: "The eye of man cannot see God as he is in his nature: not only man, but neither the angels, nor the Thrones, nor the Powers, nor the Dominions, nor every name that is named; for a creature cannot behold its Creator." By these words the most learned man sufficiently shows what he thought even about the age to come, as far as pertains to this matter. For however much the eyes of our body are changed for the better, they will be made equal to the eyes of the angels. But here he said the nature of the Creator is invisible both to these and to the whole heavenly creation altogether. Or if from this too a question arises, and any doubt is brought in, whether we shall not be greater than the angels, there is on this the plain view of the Lord himself, where he says of those who shall rise into the kingdom: *They shall be equal to the angels of God*[^5]. Whence the same holy Jerome elsewhere says thus: "Man, therefore, cannot see the face of God; but the angels even of the least in the Church always see the face of God. And now we see in a mirror, in a riddle; but then face to face, when we shall have advanced from men into angels, and shall be able to say with the Apostle: *But we all, with unveiled face beholding the glory of the Lord, are transformed into the same image from glory to glory, as by the Spirit of the Lord*[^6]—although no creature sees the face of God according to the property of his nature, and it is then discerned by the mind, when it is believed to be invisible."

**2. 8.** In these words of the man of God many things are to be considered. First, that, according to the most plain view of the Lord, he too holds that we shall then see the face of God, when we shall have advanced into angels, that is, been made equal to the angels; which will surely be in the resurrection of the dead. Then, by apostolic testimony he sufficiently made clear that, when we shall see face to face, the face of the inner man, not of the outer, is to be understood; for the Apostle was speaking of the face of the heart, when he said what he here recalled: *But we all, with unveiled face beholding the glory of the Lord, are transformed into the same image*. And if anyone doubts this, let him review the same passage, and attend to what the Apostle was speaking of: namely, the veil that remains in the reading of the Old Testament, until each one passes over to Christ, that the veil may be taken away. For there he says: *But we all, with unveiled face beholding the glory of the Lord*; which face was not unveiled in the Jews, of whom he says: *A veil is placed upon their heart*[^7], to show that the face of the heart is unveiled to us, the veil being taken away. Finally, lest anyone, regarding these things too little and distinguishing too little, should believe God to be visible either to angels or to men, when we shall have been made equal to the angels, whether now or in the future, he expressed most evidently what he thought, saying that "no creature sees the face of God according to the property of his nature, and it is then discerned by the mind, when it is believed to be invisible." Whence he signified sufficiently that, when he was seen by men through the eyes of the body, as though he himself were bodily, he was not seen according to the property of his nature, in which he is then discerned by the mind, when he is believed to be invisible. To whom invisible, except to bodily sights, even heavenly ones, as he said above of the angels and Powers and Dominions? How much more to earthly ones!

**2. 9.** Whence in another place he says more evidently: "Not only the divinity of the Father, but not even that of the Son and of the Holy Spirit, which is one nature in the Trinity, can the eyes of the flesh behold, but the eyes of the mind: of which the Savior himself says: *Blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God*[^8]." What could be clearer than this manifestation? For if he had only said: Neither the divinity of the Father, nor of the Son, nor of the Holy Spirit can the eyes of the flesh behold, and had not afterward added, "but the eyes of the mind," perhaps it would be said that it ought no longer to be called flesh, since the body will be spiritual: by adding, then, and saying, "but the eyes of the mind," he set apart a vision of this kind from every kind of body. And lest anyone should think he spoke only of the present time, he added also the testimony of the Lord, wishing to show what eyes of the mind he meant; by which testimony the promise not of present but of future vision is declared: *Blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God*.

**2. 10.** The most blessed Athanasius too, bishop of Alexandria, when he was contending against the Arians, who say only God the Father is invisible, but think the Son and the Holy Spirit visible, asserted the equal invisibility of the Trinity by the testimonies of the holy Scriptures and the diligence of his disputation, most insistently urging that God was not seen except by the assumption of a creature; but that, according to the property of his deity, God is altogether invisible—that is, the Father, and the Son, and the Holy Spirit—except in so far as he can be known by the mind and spirit. Gregory too, a holy bishop of the East, says most plainly that God, invisible by nature, when he was seen by the fathers, as by Moses, with whom he spoke face to face, could be seen, the arrangement of some visible matter being assumed, while his invisibility was preserved. This is what our Ambrose too says: that the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit are seen in that form which the will has chosen, not which nature has fashioned: so that both that is true, that *no one has ever seen God*, which is the voice of the Lord Christ himself; and, *whom no man has seen, nor can see*, which is the voice of the Apostle, or rather of Christ through the Apostle; and those testimonies of the Scriptures by which God is told to have been seen are not rejected, since he is both invisible through the proper nature of his deity, and, when he wills, can be seen through an assumed creature, as it shall please him.

**3. 11.** Further, if invisibility belongs to his nature, as does incorruptibility, that nature surely will not be changed in the age to come so as to become visible from invisible; since neither will it be able to become corruptible from incorruptible: for it is at once also unchangeable. And the Apostle surely commended his nature when he set those two together, saying: *To the King of the ages, invisible, incorruptible, to God alone be honor and glory unto the ages of ages*[^9]. Whence I do not dare so to distinguish as to say: Incorruptible indeed unto the ages of ages, but invisible not unto the ages of ages, but only in this age. But because these testimonies too cannot be false—*Blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God*[^10]; and: *We know that when he appears we shall be like him, for we shall see him as he is*[^11]—we cannot deny that the sons of God shall see God, but as invisible things are seen, as he who was appearing to men, visible in the flesh, promised that he would show himself, when he said: *And I will love him, and will show myself to him*[^12], when he was speaking, conspicuous before the eyes of men. But whence are invisible things seen, except by the eyes of the heart? Of which I said a little before what Jerome thought as to seeing God.

**3. 12.** Hence it is also that the aforementioned bishop of Milan said that in the resurrection itself it is not easy to see God, except for those who are pure in heart; and that therefore it is written: *Blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God*[^13]. "How many blessed," he says, "he had already numbered, and yet to them he had not promised the power of seeing God!" Then he adds and says: "If, then, those who are pure in heart shall see God, surely others shall not see him." And lest we should take those others to be those of whom it was said, *Blessed are the poor, Blessed are the meek*[^14], he immediately added: "For neither shall the unworthy see God." Wishing those certainly to be understood as unworthy who, although they rise, will not be able to see God, since they will rise unto damnation, because they were unwilling to cleanse the heart through true faith which works through love. And therefore it follows and he says: "Nor can he who has not willed to see God see God." Then, because it occurred to him that even all the ungodly wish to see God, at once, to show why he had said, "who has not willed to see God"—because surely in that way the ungodly does not will to see God, in that he does not will to cleanse the heart by which he can be seen—he went on and added and said: "Nor is God seen in a place, but with a pure heart; nor is God sought with bodily eyes, nor circumscribed by sight, nor held by touch, nor heard by address, nor perceived by his coming." By which words blessed Ambrose wished to admonish what men ought to prepare who wish to see God; that is, to cleanse the heart through faith which works through love, by the gift of the Holy Spirit, of which we received a pledge, by which we may know how to desire that vision.

**4. 13.** For concerning the members of God, which Scripture continually mentions, lest anyone should believe that we are like God according to the form and figure of this flesh, for that reason the same Scripture said that God has wings too, which we certainly do not have. As, then, when we hear "wings," we understand protection, so when we hear "hands," we ought to understand activity; and when we hear "feet," presence; and when we hear "eyes," the vision by which he knows; and when we hear "face," the knowledge by which he becomes known; and whatever else of the kind the same Scripture mentions, I think is to be understood spiritually. And not I alone, or I first, but all who, by whatever spiritual understanding, resist those who for this reason are named Anthropomorphites. Lest by mentioning many things from their writings I cause greater delays, I insert this one thing of holy Jerome, that this brother may know that he ought to deal in this matter not so much with me as with those before me, if anything moves him against it.

**4. 14.** When, then, that man, most learned in the Scriptures, was expounding the psalm where it is said, *Understand, then, you who are foolish among the people; and be wise at last, you fools. He who planted the ear, shall he not hear? Or he who formed the eye, does he not consider?*[^15], among other things he says: "This passage tells especially against those who are Anthropomorphites, who say that God has the members which we too have. For example, God is said to have eyes, because *the eyes of the Lord behold all things*[^16]; *the hand of the Lord makes all things*; and: *Adam heard the sound of the feet of the Lord walking in paradise*[^17]: these things they hear simply, and refer human weaknesses to the magnificence of God. But I say that God is wholly eye, wholly hand, wholly foot. He is wholly eye, because he sees all things. He is wholly hand, because he works all things. He is wholly foot, because he is everywhere. See, then, what he says: *He who planted the ear, shall he not hear? Or he who formed the eyes, does he not consider?* And he did not say: He who planted the ear, therefore he himself has no ear; he did not say: Therefore he himself has no eyes; but what did he say? *He who planted the ear, shall he not hear? He who formed the eyes, does he not consider?* He took away the members, he gave the efficiencies."

**4. 15.** All these things from the writings of those, both Latins and Greeks, who, living before us in the catholic Church, treated the divine utterances, I have thought should be mentioned, that this brother may know that, if he is otherwise minded than these, with the bitterness of dissension laid aside, and the sweetness of fraternal charity preserved and restored to the full, the matter must be sought or learned or taught by diligent and tranquil consideration. For we ought not to hold the disputations of any people, however catholic and praised, as though they were the canonical Scriptures, so that it should not be lawful for us—saving the honor owed to those men—to disapprove and reject something in their writings, if perchance we find that they were otherwise minded than the truth holds, whether understood by others through divine help or by ourselves. Such am I in the writings of others; such I wish the readers of mine to be. Finally, in all those things which I have mentioned from the little works of the holy and learned, of Ambrose, Jerome, Athanasius, Gregory, and whatever such things of others I have been able to read which I thought it long to mention—that God is not a body, nor has the members of a human form, nor is divisible through spaces of places, and is by nature unchangeably invisible, and appeared not through that same nature and substance, but, a visible form being assumed, as he willed, to those to whom he appeared, when he is told in the holy Scriptures to have been seen through the eyes of the body—these, with the Lord's help, I unshakenly believe, and understand as much as he himself grants.

**5. 16.** But concerning the spiritual body, which we shall have in the resurrection—how great a change for the better it admits: whether it passes into the simplicity of spirit, so that the whole man is now spirit; or, what I rather think, but do not yet confirm with full confidence, the spiritual body will be such that, on account of a certain ineffable ease, it is called spiritual, yet keeps a bodily substance, which cannot live and feel through itself, but through that spirit which uses it—for not even now, because the body is called animal, is the nature of the soul the same as that of the body; and whether, if the nature of the body, though now immortal and incorruptible, shall be kept, it then aids the spirit at all in seeing visible things themselves, that is, bodily things, just as now we cannot see anything of the kind except through the body; or whether even then our spirit can know bodily things without the organ of the body (for neither does God know such things through bodily senses); and many other things which can be raised in this question—I confess that I have not yet read anywhere what I would judge sufficient for me, whether for learning or for teaching.

**5. 17.** And through this, if my caution, such as it is, does not displease this brother, let us meanwhile, on account of what is written, *that we shall see him as he is*[^18], prepare, as much as we can, a pure heart for that vision, with his help. But concerning the spiritual body let us inquire more peaceably and diligently, in case perhaps God may deign, according to his Scriptures, to show something certain and clear, if he knows it to be useful to us. For if more diligent inquiry should find that so great a change of the body is to come that it can see invisible things, such a power of the body will not, I think, take away vision from the mind, so that the outer man could then see God and the inner could not: as though God were only outside man, and were not within in man, when it is written most plainly that God may be *all in all*[^19]; or as though he who without any spaces of places is everywhere whole were so within that he could be seen only outside by the outer man, but within could not be seen by the inner. Which, if it is most absurdly held—for the saints will rather be full of God; they will not, empty within, be surrounded by him without; nor, struck blind within, fail to see him with whom they will be full, and, only with eyes outside, see him by whom they will be surrounded—it remains that we be meanwhile most certain about the vision of God according to the inner man. But if the body too, by a marvelous change, shall be capable of this, something will be added, that will not be taken away.

**5. 18.** We affirm, then, the better thing, of which we least doubt: that the inner man will see God, who now alone can see love, of which, when it was being praised, it was said: *God is love*[^20]; who alone can see peace and sanctification, *without which no one can see God*[^21]. For neither love, peace, sanctification, nor anything of the kind does any eye of the flesh now see; yet all these the eye of the mind already sees, as much as it can, the more purely the purer it is: so that without doubt we may believe that we shall see God, whether or not we find what we seek about the quality of the future body; while yet we do not doubt that the body will rise and will be immortal and incorruptible, since we hold from this most plain and most firm statements of the holy Scriptures. But if this brother already affirms for himself as most certain what I still inquire about the spiritual body, unless I shall hear him teaching peaceably, in such a way that he too hears me peaceably inquiring, he will have reason justly to be vexed. Now, however, I beseech you through Christ that you obtain pardon for me from him concerning that harshness of my letter, by which I have learned he was not undeservedly offended, and that you gladden me with a reply, the Lord helping.
