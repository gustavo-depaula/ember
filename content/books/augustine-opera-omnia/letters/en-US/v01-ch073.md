# Letter 73

To the venerable and most longed-for lord and brother, fellow presbyter Jerome, Augustine sends greeting in the Lord

**1. 1.** Although I think that, before you received these, my letter had come into your hands, which I sent through the servant of God, our son Cyprian the deacon; by which you would most certainly recognize that it is my letter, copies of which you recalled to have come there; whence I now judge that, by your reply, as by the great and sharp gauntlets of Entellus, I, like a bold Dares, have begun to be struck and tossed about[^1]: now nevertheless I answer those very letters of yours, which you deigned to send me through our holy son Asterius; in which I found many tokens of your most benevolent charity toward me, and again certain indications of some offense at me on your part. And so, where I was caressed in reading, there I was at once struck; wondering at this especially, that, whereas you say you did not think rashly to be believed the copies of my letters, lest perhaps, being wounded by your answering, I should justly complain, that you ought first to have proved the discourse to be mine, and so to write back, you afterward bid me, if the letter is mine, write openly, or send truer copies, that without any rancor of stomach we may engage in the disputation of the Scriptures. For in what way can we engage in this disputation without rancor, if you prepare to wound me? or, if you do not prepare it, how would I, you not wounding me, justly complain that I was wounded by you, that you ought first to have proved the discourse to be mine, and so to write back—that is, so to wound? For unless by writing back you had wounded me, I could not justly complain. Accordingly, since you so write back that you wound, what place is left to us of engaging in the disputation of the Scriptures without any rancor? Far be it indeed from me to be wounded, if with sure reasoning you should wish and be able to demonstrate to me that you have understood that passage from the Apostle's Epistle, or anything else of the holy Scriptures, more truly than I: nay rather, far be it that I should not reckon it with thanksgiving among my gains, if I be either instructed by your teaching, or corrected by your amending.

**1. 2.** Nevertheless, my most dear brother, unless you thought yourself wounded by my writings, you would not think that I could be wounded by your replies. For in no way would I have judged this of you, that, not thinking yourself wounded, you yet so write back as to wound. Or if, you not so writing back, I was thought able to be wounded on account of my too great foolishness, by this very thing you have plainly wounded me, that you so judged of me. But in no way would you, who have never experienced me to be such, rashly believe me to be such, you who, even when you knew my style, were unwilling rashly to believe the copies of my letters. For if you saw, not undeservedly, that I would justly complain, if you rashly believed those to be my letters which were not mine; how much more justly would I complain that I myself was rashly judged to be such as he who so judged had not experienced me to be? By no means, then, would you so slip as to think me, you not writing back so as to wound me, yet too unwise, able to be wounded even by such a reply of yours.

**2. 3.** It remains, then, that you disposed to wound me by writing back, if by sure document you should know those letters to be mine. And so, because I do not believe that you would think me to be wounded unjustly, it remains that I recognize my sin, that I first wounded you by those letters, which I cannot deny to be mine. Why, then, do I strive against the current of the stream, and not rather ask pardon? I beseech you, then, by the meekness of Christ[^2], that, if I have wounded you, you forgive me, and do not in turn, by wounding me, render evil for evil. But you will wound me, if you keep silent to me about my error, which perhaps you have found in my deeds or words. For if you should reprove in me things which are not to be reproved, you wound yourself more than me; which far be it from your morals and holy purpose, that you do this with the will of wounding, blaming in me with a slandering tooth something which with a truth-telling mind you know is not to be blamed. And thereby either with a benevolent heart accuse, even if it lacks fault which you think is to be accused; or with a fatherly affection soothe one whom you cannot improve. For it can come about that something seems to you otherwise than the truth holds, provided yet that nothing be done by you otherwise than charity holds: for I too will most gratefully accept a most friendly reproof, even if what can rightly be defended did not deserve to be reproved; or I will recognize at once both your benevolence and my fault, and, as much as the Lord grants, be found in one thing grateful, in another amended.

**2. 4.** Why, then, should I dread your words, perhaps harsh, but surely wholesome, as the gauntlets of Entellus? He was beaten, not cured; and therefore he was conquered, not healed. But I, if I shall have accepted your medicinal correction tranquilly, shall not grieve: but if weakness, as human, or mine, even when I am truly accused, cannot but be saddened a little, it is better that the swelling of the head ache while it is cured, than that, while it is spared, it not be healed. For this is what he acutely saw who said that enemies quarreling are for the most part more useful than friends fearing to chide[^3]. For the former, while they quarrel, sometimes say true things which we may correct; but the latter exhibit less freedom of justice than they ought, while they fear to exasperate the sweetness of friendship. Wherefore, even if the ox, as you seem to yourself, is tired perhaps by the old age of the body, yet not by the vigor of the mind, sweating with fruitful labor on the Lord's threshing floor; behold, I am here: if I have said anything amiss, plant your foot more firmly. The weight of your age ought not to be burdensome to me, provided the chaff of my fault be threshed out.

**2. 5.** Accordingly, that which you set at the end of your letter, I either read or recall with a sigh of great longing. *Would that*, you say, *we deserved your embraces, and by mutual conference might either teach something or learn!* But I say: Would that at least we dwelt in places near on earth; that, if our conversations could not be mingled, our letters might be more frequent! But now we are so far apart by so great an interval of places from our senses, that I remember to have written to your Holiness about those words of the Apostle to the Galatians as a young man, and behold, now an old man, I have not yet deserved a reply, and the copies of my letter have more easily reached you—some occasion, I know not what, anticipating—than the letter itself by my care. For the man who then received it neither conveyed it to you, nor brought it back to me. But so great writings of yours, which were able to come into our hands, appear to me, that I would prefer nothing of my studies, if I could, to clinging to your side. Which, because I cannot, I think of sending to you for instruction some one of our sons in the Lord, if for this matter too I deserve your reply. For neither is there in me so much knowledge of the divine Scriptures, nor can there now be, as I see to be in you. And if I have any faculty in this matter, I spend it, such as it is, on the people of God. But to have leisure for studies more diligently than for instructing the things which the people hear, on account of ecclesiastical occupations, I am altogether unable.

**3. 6.** What slandering writings under your name have reached Africa, we do not know. We have received, however, what you deigned to send, answering those slanders. On reading which, I confess, I greatly grieved that, among persons so dear and familiar, joined by a bond of friendship most well known to nearly all the Churches, so great an evil of discord has arisen. And you indeed, how much you moderate yourself, and how much you hold the stings of your indignation, lest you render slander for slander, stands out sufficiently in your letter. Nevertheless, when I had read those very things, I wasted away with grief, and grew stiff with fear; what would those things which he wrote against you do to me, if perhaps they came into my hands? *Woe to the world because of scandals*[^4]. Behold it comes to pass, behold it is wholly fulfilled which the Truth says: *Because iniquity will abound, the love of many will grow cold*[^5]. For what hearts faithful to one another may now be safely poured back into one another? into whose senses may a love secure cast itself wholly? what friend, finally, is not to be feared as a future enemy, if that which we bewail could arise between Jerome and Rufinus? O wretched and pitiable condition! O faithless knowledge of the present wills of friends, where there is no foreknowledge of future things! But why should I think this is to be groaned over by one about another, when not even a man himself is known to himself for the future? For he knows, somehow, scarcely perhaps, now what sort he is; but what sort he will be afterward, he does not know.

**3. 7.** Now this, not only knowledge of what sort each one is, but also foreknowledge of what sort he will be, if it is in the holy and blessed Angels; and how the devil could have been once blessed, while he was still a good angel, knowing his future iniquity, and his everlasting punishment, I do not see at all. About which matter, if indeed it is needful to know it, I would wish to hear from you what you feel. See what the lands and seas do which bodily separate us. If this my letter which you read were myself, you would already be telling me what I asked: but now, when will you write back? when will you send? when will it arrive? when will I receive it? And yet would that at some time it come to pass, which, because it cannot come to pass as quickly as we wish, we sustain with as much toleration as we can! Whence I return to those most sweet words of your letter, and most full of your holy desire, and make them in turn mine: "Would that we deserved your embraces; and by mutual conference might either teach something or learn"! if indeed it could in any way be that I should teach you anything.

**3. 8.** But in these words, now not only yours, but also mine, where I am delighted and refreshed, and even by the very desire of both of us, hanging and not attaining, I am consoled in no small part: there again I am dug with the sharpest stings of griefs, while I think that among you, to whom God had granted, largely and abundantly, this very thing which both of us wished, that, most closely joined and most familiar, you should lick together the honeys of the holy Scriptures, so great a ruin of bitterness has crept in—when, where, in what man is it not to be feared; since at that time, when, your worldly burdens now cast off, now unencumbered, you followed the Lord, and lived together in that land in which the Lord, walking with human feet, said: *My peace I give to you, my peace I leave to you*[^6]—it could happen to you, men mature in age, and dwelling in the eloquence of the Lord? Truly *the life of man on earth is a temptation*[^7]. Alas for me, who cannot find you anywhere together! perhaps, as I am moved, as I grieve, as I fear, I would fall at your feet, would weep as much as I could, would ask as much as I love, now each of you for himself, now both for one another, and for others, and especially for the weak, for whom Christ died[^8], who behold you, as it were in the theater of this life, with great peril to themselves, lest you scatter abroad, by writing about each other, things which, since, in concord, you will not be able to delete, in concord you are unwilling; or which, in concord, you would fear to read, lest you again quarrel.

**3. 9.** I tell your charity the truth, that nothing has made me tremble more than this example, when I read in your letter certain indications of your indignation; not so much those about Entellus and the tired ox, where you seemed to me rather cheerfully to jest than angrily to threaten, as that which it sufficiently appears you wrote in earnest, whence I have spoken above—perhaps more than I ought, but not more than I feared—where you said: "Lest perhaps, being wounded, you should justly complain." I ask you, if it can be done, that there come to pass between us that we seek and discuss something whereby our hearts may be fed without the bitterness of discord. But if I cannot say what seems to me to be amended in your writings, nor you in mine, except with the suspicion of envy, or the wounding of friendship, let us rest from these things, and spare our life and salvation. Let one less attain that which puffs up, provided one not offend that which builds up[^9]. I feel myself far from that perfection of which it is written: *If anyone does not offend in word, this is a perfect man*[^10]. But plainly, in the mercy of God, I think I can easily ask pardon of you, if I have offended in anything; which you ought to disclose to me, that, when I have heard you, you may gain your brother[^11]. For neither, because on account of the distance of lands you cannot do this between me and you, ought you on that account to allow me to err. Plainly, as far as concerns the very matters which we wish to know, if I know, or believe, or think I hold something true, in which you feel otherwise, as far as the Lord grants, without injury to you I will try to assert it. But as far as pertains to your offense, when I shall feel you indignant, I will beg nothing else but pardon.

**3. 10.** And I by no means think that you could be angry, unless either I said what I ought not, or did not say it as I ought; because I do not wonder that we know one another less than we are known by our most close and most familiar friends. Into whose love, I confess, I easily cast myself wholly, especially when wearied by the scandals of the age; and in it without any solicitude I rest: for I feel that God is there, into whom, secure, I cast myself, and in whom, secure, I rest. Nor in this my security do I at all dread that uncertain morrow of human frailty, over which I groaned above. For when I feel that a man, glowing with Christian love, has by it been made a faithful friend to me; whatever of my counsels and thoughts I commit to him, I commit not to a man, but to him in whom he abides, that he may be such. For *God is love; and he who abides in love abides in God, and God in him*[^12]: which if he should desert, he must needs cause as much grief as, abiding, he had caused joy. Nevertheless, from an intimate friend made an enemy, let him rather seek something to invent, crafty; let him not find something to betray, angry. But this each one easily attains, not by hiding what he has done, but by not doing what he would wish to be hidden. Which the mercy of God grants to the good and pious, that among any whatever who will be friends, they may engage freely and securely, and not betray another's sins committed to them; that they themselves commit none which they would fear to be betrayed. For when something false is invented by a slanderer, it is either not believed at all, or surely, the health being intact, only the reputation is vexed. But the evil which is perpetrated is an intimate enemy, even if it be made public by the loquacity or quarrel of no intimate. Wherefore which of the prudent does not see, how tolerably you bear the now incredible enmities of one once most friendly and most familiar, conscience consoling you; and how you reckon either what he boasts, or what is perhaps believed by some, among the left-hand arms, by which no less than by the right one fights against the devil[^13]? Nevertheless I would prefer him to be in some way milder, than you to be in this way more armed. This is a great and sad wonder, to have come from such friendships to these enmities; it will be a glad and much greater one to have returned from such enmities to the former concord.
