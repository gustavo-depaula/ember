# Letter 136

To the exceedingly venerable lord, and to be uniquely cultivated by me with all offices, father Augustine, Marcellinus.

**1.** The illustrious man Volusianus read me the letter of your Beatitude—nay, indeed, at my compelling, read several—at which all the things said by you, since they are truly to be marveled at, I marveled at on every side. For the grace of divine discourse, humbly swelling, easily earned to please. For from this it pleased very much, that you strive to set and firm up the somewhat tottering steps of a man by the exhortation of a good purpose. For we have with him a daily disputation, according to our powers and the poverty of our talent. For, compelled by the prayer of his holy mother, I have a care to meet him more frequently for the sake of greeting him, although he himself too deigns to render the like in this part. But the letter of your Venerableness having been received, the man—who is called back from the stability of the true God by the persuasion of many, of whom there is an abundance in this city—was so moved that, if (as he himself affirms) he had not at all dreaded the length of a letter, he would have intimated to your Beatitude every ambiguity he can have. But yet, as you yourself too will deign to approve, he asked, with cultivated and careful discourse, and with the lucid splendor of Roman eloquence, that some things be resolved for him at length. Which question indeed has been worn down on every side, and the cleverness of those who defame the dispensation of the Lord's incarnation is well enough known on this part. But yet I too in this part—because I trust that whatever you write back will profit very many—would gladly approach as an entreater, that you deign to answer more vigilantly those things in which they lie, saying that the Lord did nothing more than other men could do. For they bring forward to us their own Apollonius and Apuleius, and other men of the magic art, whose miracles they contend were greater.

**2.** But that same illustrious man mentioned above said, with several present, that there were many things which could be joined not without merit to this question, if, as I said before, epistolary brevity had not been considered by his party. Which things, however, though he was unwilling to write, he did not suffer to be kept silent. For he said that, even if today an account of the Lord's incarnation were rendered him, it could scarcely be rendered to clarity why this God, who is also affirmed to be the God of the Old Testament, despising the old sacrifices, was delighted with new ones. For he asserted that nothing could be corrected except what, having been done before, were proved not right; or that what was once rightly done ought in no way to be changed. For he said that things rightly done could not be changed except unjustly; especially because this variety could accuse God of inconstancy. Then, next, that his preaching and teaching agree in no part with the customs of the republic; inasmuch as—as is said by many—it is his precept that we ought to repay evil to no one for evil, and to offer the other cheek to one striking, and to give a cloak to one persisting to take away a tunic, and to go a doubled space of the journey with one who shall wish to compel us: all which he asserts are contrary to the customs of the republic. For who would suffer something to be taken from him by an enemy, or would not wish to repay evils, by the law of war, to a plunderer of a Roman province? And the rest which can be said to the remaining points, your Venerableness understands. All these things, then, he esteems can be joined to that question, so much so that, through Christian princes keeping the Christian religion in the greatest part, it is manifest (even if he himself keeps silent about this part) that such great evils have befallen the republic.

**3.** Whence, as your Beatitude deigns to recognize with me, to all these things (since the desired response of your Holiness will without doubt be handed to the hands of many) a full and elaborated splendor of solution ought to be shown; especially because, when these things were going on, the excellent possessor and lord of the region of Hippo was present, who both praised your Holiness under the flattery of irony, and contended that, when he asked about these things, he was least of all satisfied. But I, not unmindful of the promise but an exactor of it, entreat that books be composed to all these things, books that will incredibly profit the Church, especially at this time.
