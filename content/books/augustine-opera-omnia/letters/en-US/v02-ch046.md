# Letter 145

TO MY HOLY AND BELOVED BROTHER ANASTASIUS, FROM AUGUSTINE, GREETINGS IN THE LORD.

**1.** A most trustworthy occasion for greeting your Sincerity has presented itself in the persons of the honorable servants of God, our brothers Lupicinus and Concordialis, through whom, even if I were not writing, your Charity could learn all that is going on with us. For I know how much you love us in Christ, since you too know how much you are in turn loved by us in him; and therefore I did not doubt that you could be saddened if you saw them without a letter from me, since you could not but know that they set out from us and are joined to us by so great a familiarity—and also because I was a debtor of a reply. For from the time I received your letter, I do not know that I have written back until now for the first time; for we are so bound and stretched by such great cares that I do not even know this.

**2.** But we greatly desire to know how things are with you, and whether the Lord has granted you any rest, as far as is possible on this earth; for *if one member is glorified, all the members rejoice together*[^1]. And so it often happens that, when in the midst of our anxieties we think of some of our brothers settled in even a little rest, we are refreshed in no small part, as though we too were living more quietly and tranquilly in them. Yet even when troubles multiply in the frailty of this life, they compel us to long for eternal rest. For this world is more dangerous when it caresses than when it troubles, and more to be guarded against when it entices us to love it than when it warns and compels us to despise it. For since *all that is in it is the desire of the flesh, and the desire of the eyes, and the ambition of the world*[^2], often even into those who set spiritual, invisible, and eternal things above such things there creeps a craving for earthly sweetness, and our duties are accompanied by their own delights. For the better the things to come are to love, the more violent are the things present to weakness. And would that those who have learned to see and to groan over them might merit to conquer and escape them! And this the human will in no way fulfills without the grace of God; nor is the will to be called free as long as it is subject to conquering and binding desires. *For by whatever a man is overcome, to this he is bound as a slave*[^3]; and: *If the Son sets you free, then you will be truly free*[^4], says the Son of God himself.

**3.** The law, therefore, by teaching and commanding what cannot be fulfilled without grace, shows a man his own weakness, so that the weakness thus shown may seek the Savior, by whom the will, once healed, may be able to do what in its weakness it could not. The law, then, leads to faith; faith obtains the Spirit more abundantly; the Spirit pours out love; love fulfills the law. For this reason the law is called a pedagogue, under whose most threatening severity *whoever calls upon the name of the Lord shall be saved*[^5]. But *how shall they call upon him in whom they have not believed?*[^6] Accordingly, lest the letter without the spirit should kill, the life-giving Spirit is given to those who believe and call upon him; and *the love of God is poured out in our hearts through the Holy Spirit who has been given to us*[^7], so that what the same Apostle says may come about: *Love is the fullness of the law*[^8]. Thus the law is good for one who uses it lawfully; and he uses it lawfully who, understanding why it was given, takes refuge through its threat in liberating grace. Whoever is ungrateful to this grace, by which the ungodly is justified, and trusts as it were in his own powers to fulfill the law, being ignorant of the righteousness of God and wishing to establish his own, is not subject to the righteousness of God[^9]; and through this the law becomes for him not a help to absolution but a bond of guilt. Not that the law is evil, but that sin, as it is written, *works death*[^10] for such people through what is good. For through the commandment he sins more grievously who through the commandment knows how evil is what he commits.

**4.** But in vain does he think himself the conqueror of sin who does not sin out of fear of punishment; for even if the business of evil desire is not carried out outwardly, that evil desire itself is within as an enemy. And who is found innocent before God who wills to do what is forbidden, if you take away what is feared? And through this he is guilty in the will itself who wills to do what is not lawful to do but does not do it because it cannot be done with impunity. For as far as it lies in him, he would prefer that there be no righteousness forbidding and punishing sins. And surely, if he would prefer that there be no righteousness, who would doubt that he would do away with it if he could? And through this, how is he righteous who is so much an enemy of righteousness that, were the power given him, he would do away with it as it commands, rather than endure it as it threatens or judges? An enemy of righteousness, therefore, is he who does not sin out of fear of punishment; but he will be its friend if out of love of it he does not sin, for then he will truly be afraid to sin. For he who dreads hell does not dread to sin but to burn. But he dreads to sin who hates sin itself as he hates hell. This is the chaste *fear of the Lord, which endures forever and ever*[^11]. For that fear of punishment *has torment, and is not in love, and perfect love casts it out*[^12].

**5.** Now everyone hates sin just so much as he loves righteousness; and this he will be able to do not by the law terrifying through the letter, but by the Spirit healing through grace. Then comes about what the Apostle urges: *I speak in a human way, because of the weakness of your flesh; for as you presented your members to serve uncleanness and iniquity unto iniquity, so now present your members to serve righteousness unto sanctification*[^13]. For what does *as this, so that* mean, except: just as no fear compelled you to sin, but the very lust and pleasure of sin, so let no dread of punishment drive you to live righteously, but let the delight and love of righteousness lead you? And this, as it seems to me, is not yet perfect righteousness, but in a manner full-grown righteousness. For not in vain would he premise *I speak in a human way, because of the weakness of your flesh*, unless because something more should have been said, had they been able now to bear it. For more service is owed to righteousness than men are wont to render to sin. For bodily punishment, even if not from the will, yet calls a man back from the work of sin. And there is hardly anyone who openly commits an act by which he would gain unlawful and unclean pleasure, if it is certain that the torments of vengeance will follow at once. But righteousness is to be loved in such a way that not even bodily punishments ought to restrain us from its works, and that even in the hands of cruel enemies our works may shine before men, so that those to whom they can be pleasing may *glorify our Father who is in heaven*[^14].

**6.** Hence it is that that most valiant lover of righteousness cries out: *Who shall separate us from the love of Christ? Shall tribulation, or distress, or persecution, or famine, or nakedness, or peril, or the sword? As it is written: For your sake we are put to death all the day long; we are accounted as sheep for the slaughter. But in all these things we more than conquer through him who loved us. For I am certain that neither death, nor life, nor angel, nor principality, nor things present, nor things to come, nor power, nor height, nor depth, nor any other creature will be able to separate us from the love of God which is in Christ Jesus our Lord*[^15]. See how he does not say merely, Who shall separate us from Christ; but, showing whereby we cleave to Christ, he says: Who shall separate us from the *love* of Christ? By love, therefore, do we cleave to Christ, not by fear of punishment. Then, having recounted those things that seem to have violence yet do not have the power of separating, he so concluded that he called the same the love of God which he had called the love of Christ. And what is *the love of Christ*, if not the love of righteousness? For of him it is said: *who was made for us wisdom from God, and righteousness, and sanctification, and redemption*[^16], so that, as it is written, *he who glories may glory in the Lord*[^17]. As, then, that man is most wicked whom not even bodily punishments deter from the unclean works of foul pleasure, so that man is most righteous whom not even the terror of bodily punishments calls back from the holy works of most luminous love.

**7.** This love of God, as must be held with perpetual reflection, *is poured out in our hearts through the Holy Spirit who has been given to us*[^18], so that *he who glories may glory in the Lord*[^19]. When, therefore, we feel ourselves poor and needy in this love by which the law is most truly fulfilled, we ought not to demand its riches out of our own want, but by praying to ask, to seek, to knock, so that he with whom is *the fountain of life*[^20] may grant us *to be inebriated by the abundance of his house, and to be given to drink of the torrent of his pleasure*[^21]; so that, flooded and quickened by it, we may not only not be swallowed up by sorrow, but may even *glory in tribulations, knowing that tribulation works patience, and patience trial, and trial hope; and hope does not disappoint*[^22]—not because we can do this through ourselves, but because *the love of God is poured out in our hearts through the Holy Spirit who has been given to us*[^23].

**8.** It has been a delight to me to speak with you in this way at least through a letter, which I could not do when you were present—and this not for your sake, who, *not being wise in lofty things, give your assent to the lowly*[^24], but for the sake of certain people who arrogate too much to the human will, which they think can suffice of itself to fulfill the law once it has been given, with no grace of holy inspiration aiding it beyond the teaching of the law. By their argument it is urged upon the wretched and needy weakness of men that we ought not even to pray that we may not enter into temptation. Not that they dare to say this openly; but their position, whether they will it or not, certainly follows from this. For why is it said to us, *Watch and pray that you do not enter into temptation*[^25]? Why too, in keeping with this exhortation, when he taught us to pray, did he command us to say, *Do not bring us into temptation*[^26], if this is not fulfilled by the help of divine grace, but the whole of it lies in the choice of the human will? Why say more? Greet the brothers who are with you, and pray for us, that we may be saved according to that salvation of which it is said: *The healthy have no need of a physician, but those who are ill; I came not to call the righteous, but sinners*[^27]. Pray, therefore, for us, that we may be righteous—which indeed a man cannot be unless he knows and wills it, and there will at once be one who has fully willed it; but this very thing will not be in him unless he is healed by the grace of the Spirit and aided so that he may be able.
