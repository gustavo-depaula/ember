# Letter 14

Augustine to Nebridius

**1.** I have preferred to answer your most recent letter; not because I have despised your earlier questions, or because they delighted me less, but because in answering them I am undertaking greater things than you suppose. For although you have prescribed that I send you a letter longer than the longest, we nevertheless do not have as much leisure as you think, and as you know we have always wished and still wish to have. And do not ask why this is so: for I could more easily set forth those things by which I am hindered than why I am hindered.

**2.** You write asking why, though you and I are each single individuals, we do many of the same things, while the sun does not do the same as the other heavenly bodies. I will try to explain the cause of this. For if we do the same things, the sun too does many things along with the other stars; if it does not, neither do we. I walk and you walk, the sun moves and they move; I am awake and you are awake; the sun shines and they shine; I argue and you argue; the sun circles and they circle around—although the act of the mind is in no way to be compared with the things we see. But if you compare mind with mind, as is fair, then the stars, if any mind is in them, are rather to be considered as thinking or contemplating the same thing—or whatever else is more aptly said—than men are. As for the rest, in the motions of bodies, if you attend carefully, as you usually do, nothing at all can be done identically by two things. Or do you suppose that, when we walk together, we are at once doing the same thing? Far be that from your prudence. For of the two of us walking, the one nearer the north pole must either go ahead of the other with an equal motion or step more slowly; yet neither can be perceived. But you, unless I am mistaken, are waiting for what we understand, not what we perceive. But if we should go from the pole toward the south, joined and clinging to one another as closely as we can, and lean on smooth and level marble or even on ivory, the motion of the two can no more be the same than the pulse of the veins, than the shape, than the face. Remove us and set in our place the offspring of Daucus, and you will have accomplished nothing. For even in twins as alike as these the necessity that they move each in their own way is as great as was the necessity that they be born one by one.

**3.** But this, you will say, is so only to reason: whereas the sun's difference from the stars is clear and manifest even to the senses. If you compel me to consider its magnitude, you know how many things are said about distances, and to what great uncertainty that apparent clarity is reduced. But to grant that it is as it appears—for so I too believe—whose sense, after all, has not been deceived by that height of Naevius, who was taller by a foot than the tallest man of six? whom I believe you sought too hard for as a man's equal, and, having failed utterly to find one, you wanted to stretch our letter to the very measure of his form. Therefore, since something of this sort exists on earth too, I think there is nothing to wonder at concerning the heavens. But if it moves you that no star except the sun fills the day with its light—who, I ask you, has appeared to men so great as that man whom God assumed, far otherwise than the other holy and wise men? If you compare him with other men, they are separated by a greater distance than the other stars are by comparison with the sun. Observe this likeness carefully. For it may be brought about by the mind in which you excel that, in passing, we have resolved a certain question about the man Christ proposed by you.

**4.** You ask likewise whether that supreme truth and supreme wisdom and form of things, through which all things were made, whom our sacred writings profess to be the only Son of God, contains the rational principle of man in general, or also of each one of us. A great question. But it seems to me that, as far as concerns the making of man, the rational principle of man only, and not mine or yours, is there; but as far as concerns the cycle of time, the various rational principles of men live in that purity. But since this is most obscure, by what likeness it can be illustrated I do not know—unless perhaps we must take refuge in those arts that are in our mind. For in the discipline of measuring there is one rational principle of an angle, one of a square. And so, whenever I want to demonstrate an angle, only one rational principle of an angle occurs to me. But I would by no means draw a square unless I beheld the principle of four angles at once: thus any man whatever was made by one rational principle, by which a man is understood. But that a people may come to be—although this too is one rational principle—it is nevertheless not the principle of a man, but of men. If, therefore, Nebridius is a part of this universe, as he is, and the whole universe is composed of parts, God, the founder of the universe, could not lack the rational principle of the parts. Wherefore, that the rational principle of very many men is there does not pertain to man as such—although again, in wondrous ways, all things are brought back to one. But you will think this out more aptly: be content with this for the time being, I ask, although I have already gone beyond Naevius.
