# Letter 214

TO THE MOST BELOVED LORD AND BROTHER VALENTINUS, TO BE HONORED IN THE MEMBERS OF CHRIST, AND TO THE BROTHERS WHO ARE WITH YOU, AUGUSTINE SENDS GREETINGS IN THE LORD.

**1.** There came to us two young men, Cresconius and Felix, saying that they were of your community, who reported to us that your monastery was disturbed by some dissension, because certain ones among you preach grace in such a way as to deny that man has free choice and—what is more serious—say that on the day of judgment God will not render to each according to his works.[^1] Yet they also indicated this, that many of you do not think so, but acknowledge that free choice is helped by the grace of God so that we may think and do rightly; so that, when the Lord comes to render to each according to his works, he may find our good works, *which God has prepared that we should walk in them*[^2]. Those who think this think rightly.

**2.** *I beseech* you, therefore, *brothers*, as the Apostle besought the Corinthians, *by the name of our Lord Jesus Christ, that you all say the same thing, and that there be no schisms among you*[^3]. For first the Lord Jesus, as it is written in the Gospel of the Apostle John, *did not come to judge the world, but that the world might be saved through him*[^4]. But afterward, as the apostle Paul writes, *God will judge the world*[^5], when *he is to come*, as the whole Church confesses in the Creed, *to judge the living and the dead*. If, therefore, there is no grace of God, how does he save the world? And if there is no free choice, how does he judge the world? Accordingly, understand the book or letter of mine, which the aforesaid men brought with them to us, according to this faith: that you neither deny the grace of God, nor so defend free choice that you separate it from the grace of God, as though without it we could by any reasoning either think anything or do anything according to God—which we are altogether unable to do. For on this account the Lord, when he was speaking of the fruit of righteousness, said to his disciples: *Without me you can do nothing*[^6].

**3.** Hence you should know that the aforesaid letter to Sixtus, a priest of the Roman Church, was written against the new heretics, the Pelagians, who say that the grace of God is given according to our merits, so that he who glories may glory not in the Lord but in himself, that is, in man, not in the Lord. This the Apostle forbids, saying: *Let no one glory in man*[^7]; and in another place: *He who glories,* he says, *let him glory in the Lord*[^8]. But those heretics, thinking that they are made righteous by themselves, as though God had not given this to them but they to themselves, glory not at all in the Lord but in themselves. For to such the Apostle says: *For who distinguishes you?*[^9] He says this because no one but God distinguishes a man out of that mass of perdition which came from Adam, so as to make him a vessel unto honor, not unto dishonor.[^10] But because a carnal man, vainly puffed up, when he had heard, *For who distinguishes you?* might answer, whether in voice or in thought, and say, "My faith distinguishes me, my prayer distinguishes me, my righteousness distinguishes me," the Apostle at once meets his thoughts and says: *For what have you that you have not received? But if you have received it, why do you glory as if you had not received it?*[^11] And those who think themselves justified by themselves glory as though they had not received, and thereby glory in themselves, not in the Lord.

**4.** Therefore in this letter that has reached you I have proved, by the testimonies of the holy Scriptures, which you can examine there, that our good works, our devout prayers, and our right faith could in no way have been in us unless we had received these from him of whom the apostle James says: *Every good endowment and every perfect gift is from above, coming down from the Father of lights*[^12]; lest anyone say that the grace of God was handed over to him by the merits of his works, or by the merits of his prayers, or by the merits of his faith, and lest it be thought true, as those heretics say, that the grace of God is given according to our merits—which is altogether most false: not because there is no merit, whether the good merit of the devout or the evil merit of the impious—otherwise how will God judge the world?—but because the mercy and grace of God converts a man, of which the Psalm says: *My God, his mercy will go before me*[^13]; so that the impious one may be justified, that is, may be made righteous out of impious, and may begin to have good merit, which the Lord will crown when the world is judged.

**5.** There were many things I desired to send you, by reading which you might be able to know more carefully and fully the whole case that was conducted in the councils of bishops against these same Pelagian heretics; but the brothers who came to us from your number were in haste, by whom we have not written these things back to you, but written to you. For they brought no letter of your Charity to us; yet we received them, since their simplicity sufficiently indicated that they could have fabricated nothing to us. They were in haste, moreover, so as to keep Easter among you, that, with the Lord's help, so holy a day might find your peace rather than your dissension.

**6.** But you do better (which I greatly ask) if you do not find it burdensome to send to me the very one by whom they say they were disturbed. For either he does not understand my book, or perhaps he himself is not understood, when he tries to solve and unravel a most difficult question, intelligible to few. For this very question is the one about the grace of God, which has caused men who do not understand to suppose that the apostle Paul says: *Let us do evil, that good may come*[^14]. Hence the apostle Peter, in his second Letter: *Wherefore,* he says, *beloved, while you wait for these things, be diligent to be found by him without spot and blameless, in peace; and count the patience of our Lord as salvation, as also our most beloved brother Paul, according to the wisdom given him, wrote to you, as he does also in all his letters when he speaks in them of these matters; in which there are some things hard to understand, which the ignorant and unstable twist, as they do the other Scriptures, to their own destruction*[^15].

**7.** Beware, therefore, of what so great an apostle says so terribly; and where you feel that you do not understand, for the time believe the divine words, that both man's choice is free, and there is the grace of God, without whose help free choice can neither be converted to God nor make progress in God. And what you devoutly believe, pray that you may also wisely understand. For to this very end—that is, that we may wisely understand—free choice certainly exists. For unless we understood and were wise by free choice, it would not be commanded us, since Scripture says: *Understand, therefore, you who are foolish among the people, and you fools, be wise at last*[^16]. Indeed, by the very fact that it is commanded and enjoined that we understand and be wise, our obedience is required, which can be none without free choice. But if this very thing could be done through free choice without the help of God's grace—that we should understand and be wise—it would not be said to God: *Give me understanding, and I will learn your commandments*[^17]; nor would it be written in the Gospel: *Then he opened their minds, that they might understand the Scriptures*[^18]; nor would the apostle James say: *But if any of you lacks wisdom, let him ask of God, who gives to all generously and does not reproach; and it will be given him*[^19]. But the Lord is able to grant both to you and to us that we may rejoice with the swiftest messengers over your peace and devout agreement. I greet you, not only in my own name, but also in the name of the brothers who are with me, and I ask that you pray for us harmoniously and earnestly. May the Lord be with you. Amen.
