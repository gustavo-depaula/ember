# Letter 159

To the most blessed lord, and venerable and longed-for brother and my fellow priest Evodius, and to the brothers with you, from Augustine, and the brothers with me, greetings in the Lord.

**1.** This brother named Barbarus is a servant of God, established now for a long time at Hippo, and a fervent and zealous hearer of the word of God. He desired a letter from us to your holiness; in which we commend him to you in the Lord, and through him send you the greeting due. But to answer the letters of your holiness, in which you have woven enormous questions, is a most laborious task even for men at leisure and endowed with a far greater capacity for discussion and acuteness of understanding than we are. Of your two letters, indeed, in which you inquire into many great things, one somehow went astray, and though long sought could not be found; but the other, which was found, contains a most sweet commendation of a servant of God, a good and chaste young man—how he migrated from this life, and by what attestations of fraternal visions his merit could be made known to you. Then, from this occasion, you set forth and turn over a most obscure question about the soul: whether it goes out of the body with some body, by which it can be carried to corporeal places, or be contained in corporeal places. The treatment of this matter, then, if indeed it can be examined to clarity by such men as we are, demands a most busy care and effort, and through this a mind most free from these occupations. But if you wish briefly to hear what seems to me, I in no way judge that the soul goes out of the body with a body.

**2.** But how those visions and predictions of future things come about, let him now try to explain who knows by what power such great things are wrought in each mind when it thinks. For we see, and plainly perceive, that in it there come to be innumerable images of many visible things, and of things pertaining to the other senses of the body: and it makes no difference how orderly or confused they are, but only that they are, which is manifest. Whoever can explain by what power and how they come to be (all of which are certainly daily and continuous), let him dare to presume something and to define even concerning those rarest of visions. But I dare this so much the less, the less I am able to explain how that too comes about which, waking and sleeping, we experience as a continuous fact of life. While I was dictating this letter to you, was I not beholding you yourself in my mind, you being of course absent and unaware, and imagining, according to the knowledge I have of you, how you might be moved by these words? And in what way this came to be in my mind, I could not grasp or investigate; yet certain that it did not come to be by corporeal masses, nor by corporeal qualities, although it came to be most like a body. Hold this for now, as dictated by one occupied and hastening. But in the twelfth book of those that I wrote on Genesis, this question is treated vigorously, and from many examples of things experienced and credibly heard, that discussion grows into a forest. What we have been able to do or accomplish in it, you will judge when you read it; if, that is, the Lord deigns to grant that I may now publish those books, emended as fittingly as I can, and no longer keep the expectation of many brothers in suspense by a long discussion.

**3.** But I will briefly narrate one thing, from which you may reflect. Our brother Gennadius, known to almost everyone, and a physician most dear to us, who now lives at Carthage and at Rome was preeminent in the practice of his art—as you know him, a religious man, and most kindly toward the care of the poor with tireless mercy and a most ready heart—nevertheless once doubted, as he recently told us, when he was still a young man and most fervent in these almsgivings, whether there was any life after death. Since, then, God would in no way desert his mind and his works of mercy, there appeared to him in sleep a young man conspicuous and worthy of attention, who said to him: Follow me. While he followed him, he came to a certain city, where he began to hear, on the right side, the sounds of a most sweet song, beyond the usual and familiar sweetness; then, when he was intent on what it was, the youth said that these were the hymns of the blessed and the saints. But what he reported having seen on the left side, I do not remember well enough. He woke, and the dream fled, and he thought of it only as much as one thinks of a dream.

**4.** But on another night, behold, that same youth appeared to him again, and asked whether he was recognized: he answered that he recognized him well and plainly. Then the youth asked where he knew him. Nor did it escape his memory what he repeatedly answered; and he narrated that whole vision, and the hymns of the saints which he had come, with the youth as guide, to hear, with the ease of one recalling the freshest things. Here the youth asked whether what he had narrated he had seen in sleep or waking: he answered, in sleep. But the youth said: You recall well; it is true, you saw those things in sleep; but know that even now you are seeing me in sleep. When he had heard this, he believed it to be so, and confirmed it by his answer. Then the one who was teaching the man added and said: Where is your body now? He answered: In my bedroom. Do you know, said the youth, that your eyes in that same little body are now bound, and closed, and at rest, and that you see nothing with those eyes? He answered: I know. Then the youth said: What, then, are these eyes by which you see me? At this, finding nothing to answer, he fell silent. To him, hesitating, the youth disclosed what he was striving to teach by these questions; and at once said: Just as those eyes of your flesh, while you sleep and lie on your bed, are now idle and do nothing, and yet there are these by which you behold me and use this vision, so when you are dead, while the eyes of your flesh do nothing, there will be in you a life by which you live, and a sense by which you perceive. Beware henceforth lest you doubt that life remains after death. So the faithful man says that the doubt about this matter was taken from him: by whose teaching, except by the providence and mercy of God?

**5.** By this narration someone might say that we have not loosed the question of so great a matter, but bound it. Nevertheless, since it is free to each to believe or not to believe these words, each has himself, to which he may call himself away to the most profound question. A man wakes, and a man sleeps daily, and a man thinks: let him say whence come those things like the forms, like the qualities, like the motions of bodies, and yet not of corporeal matter; let him say if he can. But if he cannot, why does he hurl himself, concerning the rarest or untried things, to bring forward as it were a settled opinion, when he does not loose the continuous and daily ones? But I, although I cannot at all explain in words how those, as it were, corporeal things come to be without a body, nevertheless, just as I know that they do not come to be by a body—would that I likewise knew how to distinguish the things which are sometimes seen through the spirit, and are thought to be seen through the body; or in what way the visions of those whom error or impiety very often deludes are to be distinguished, when very many things are narrated like the visions of the pious and the holy! If I had wished to recall examples of these, time rather than abundance would have failed me. May you be made vigorous in the mercy of the Lord, most blessed lord, and venerable and longed-for brother.
