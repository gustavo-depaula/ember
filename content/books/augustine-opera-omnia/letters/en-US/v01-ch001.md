# Letter 1

Augustine to Hermogenian

**1.** I would never dare, not even in jest, to challenge the Academics. For how could the authority of such great men fail to move me, unless I supposed that their view was far other than is commonly believed? And so I have imitated them as best I could, rather than refuted them, which I am wholly unable to do. For it seems to me to have suited their times well enough that, if anything pure flowed from the Platonic spring, it should be channeled through shady and thorny thickets to feed a very few men, rather than that, flowing through open ground, it should be impossible to keep clear and pure with cattle bursting in everywhere. For what is more fitting for cattle than to think that the soul is a body? Against men of this sort I judge that art and method of concealing the truth to have been usefully devised. But in this age, when we see no philosophers at all—except perhaps in the cloak that covers the body, and these I would hardly think worthy of so venerable a name—it seems to me that men must be led back (if the Academics' view, through cleverness with words, has frightened any away from grasping reality) to the hope of finding the truth, lest what was suited to the times for rooting out the deepest errors should now begin to be an obstacle to implanting knowledge.

**2.** For in those days the pursuits of the various schools burned so fiercely that nothing was to be feared but the approval of what is false. And anyone driven by their arguments from what he had believed he held firm and unshaken sought something else all the more steadily and cautiously, the greater his diligence in conduct and the more deeply and intricately he sensed that the truth lay hidden in the nature of things and of minds. But now there is such a flight from labor and such neglect of the liberal arts that, as soon as the word has sounded that it seemed to the sharpest philosophers that nothing can be grasped, men let their minds go and shroud them in everlasting darkness. For they do not dare to trust those men so far as to find evident to themselves what Carneades, with all his study, talent, and leisure, with all his manifold learning, and finally with the longest of lives, could not discover. And if, struggling a little against laziness, they do read the very books in which, as it were, the denial of perception to human nature is shown, they fall asleep in such torpor that they would not wake even at the trumpet of heaven.

**3.** Therefore, since I hold your faithful judgment of my little books most dear, and set such store by you that neither error can befall your prudence nor pretense your friendship, I ask all the more that you consider carefully and write back to me whether you approve of what, toward the end of the third book, I thought ought to be believed—more cautiously perhaps than confidently, yet, as I judge, more usefully than incredibly. For my part, however those writings stand, I am pleased not so much that, as you write, I have defeated the Academics (for you write this more affectionately perhaps than truly) as that I have broken for myself a most hateful tether by which I was held back from the breast of philosophy through despair of the truth, which is the food of the mind.
