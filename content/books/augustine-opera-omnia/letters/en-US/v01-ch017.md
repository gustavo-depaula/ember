# Letter 17

To Maximus of Madaura

**1.** Are we conducting something serious between us, or do you wish to jest? For as your letter speaks, I am uncertain whether it was from the weakness of the cause itself, or from the affability of your character, that you preferred to be more witty than prepared. For first a comparison was made between Mount Olympus and your forum: which I do not know to what end it tended, unless to remind me that on that mountain Jupiter pitched his camp when he was waging war against his father, as that history teaches which your people too call sacred; and to make me recall that in that forum there are, in two statues, one Mars naked and another armed, whose divinity, most hostile to the citizens, a human statue set opposite, with three fingers extended, holds in check. Could I ever, then, have believed that, in mentioning that forum, you wanted to renew for me the memory of such divinities, unless you preferred to jest rather than to deal seriously! But that point, plainly, where you said that such gods are certain limbs of the one great God, I warn you—since you think it worthy—to abstain greatly from sacrilegious witticisms of this kind. For if you call him the one God about whom, as the ancients said, the learned and the unlearned agree, do you say that these are limbs of him whose monstrousness—or, if you prefer, whose power—the image of a dead man holds in check? I could say more on this; for you see, with your prudence, how widely this point lies open to censure. But I restrain myself, lest I be thought by you to be acting as a rhetorician rather than a teller of truth.

**2.** Now as for the fact that you have collected certain Punic names of dead men, with which you thought to hurl festive insults, as it seemed to you, against our religion, I do not know whether I ought to repel them or pass them over in silence. For if these matters seem as trivial to your gravity as they are, I have not much leisure to jest. But if they seem grave to you, I marvel that, moved by the absurdity of the names, it did not come into your mind that you too have Eucaddires among your priests and Abaddires among your divinities. I do not think these were absent from your mind when you wrote, but, after the manner of your humanity and charm, you wanted to remind us, for the relaxing of the mind, how many things in your superstition are laughable. For you could not so far have forgotten yourself that, an African writing to Africans, since we are both settled in Africa, you would think Punic names worthy of mockery. For if we interpret these words, what else does Namphamo mean than a man of good foot—that is, one whose coming brings some happiness; as we are accustomed to say that one has entered with a favorable foot whose entrance some prosperity has followed? If this language is disapproved by you, deny that many things were wisely committed to memory in Punic books, as is reported by most learned men. Surely you ought to regret having been born where the cradle of this language is still warm. But if the sound too displeases us not without reason, and you recognize that I have interpreted that word well, you have grounds for being angry with your Virgil, who invites your Hercules to the rites celebrated for him by Evander in this way: *come favorably to both us and your rites with a foot of good omen*[^1]. He wishes him to come with a favorable foot. Therefore he wishes Hercules to come as a Namphamo, about whom you deign to insult us greatly. But nevertheless, if it delights you to laugh, you have among yourselves great material for witticisms: the god Stercutius, the goddess Cloacina, the bald Venus, the god Fear, the god Pallor, the goddess Fever, and countless others of this sort, to whom the ancient Romans, worshippers of images, built temples and decreed that they be worshipped: which if you neglect, you neglect the Roman gods; from which it will be understood that you are not initiated into Roman rites, and yet you scorn and despise Punic names as though you were devoted to the altars of the Roman divinities.

**3.** But you seem to me to set little or no value on those rites—perhaps even less than we do—and rather to seek from them some pleasure for the passage of this life: since you did not even hesitate to take refuge in Maro, as you write, and to defend yourself by his verse, where he says: *each is drawn by his own pleasure*[^2]. For if the authority of Maro pleases you, as you indicate it does, then surely this too pleases you: *first from heavenly Olympus came Saturn, fleeing the arms of Jove, an exile from his lost kingdom*[^3], and the rest, by which he wishes it to be understood that he and your gods of this sort were men. For he had read much history, strengthened by ancient authority, which Tully too had read, who recounts this same thing in his dialogues more than we would dare to demand, and strives to bring it to the knowledge of men, as far as those times allowed.

**4.** But as for your saying that your rites are to be preferred to ours because you worship the gods publicly, while we use more secret little assemblies: first I ask you this—how you have forgotten that Liber whom you think must be entrusted to the eyes of a few initiates. Next, you yourself judge that you wanted to do nothing else, when you recounted the public celebration of your rites, than to set before our eyes, as it were spectacles, your decurions and leading men of the city raving and frenzied through the streets of your city: in which celebration, if you are inhabited by a divinity, surely you see what sort of thing that is which takes away the mind. But if you are pretending, what then are these secrets of yours even in public, or to what does so base a lie pertain? Next, why do you not chant future things, if you are seers? Or why do you despoil the bystanders, if you are sane?

**5.** Since, then, you have made us recall these things and others which I now think should be passed over, through your letter, why should we not laugh at your gods, whom everyone understands to be subtly laughed at by you yourself—everyone who both knows your talent and has read your letter? And so, if you want us to deal with some matter of these things that befits your age and prudence, and that can finally rightly be demanded of us by our dearest friends concerning our purpose, seek something worthy of our discussion: and take care to say, on behalf of your divinities, things in which we may not think you a betrayer of your own cause, whereby you remind us more of what can be said against them than say anything on their behalf. To sum up, however, lest this escape you and draw you unawares into sacrilegious abuse, know that by catholic Christians, whose church is established even in your town, no dead man is worshipped, and nothing at all is adored as a divinity that has been made and founded by God, but only that one God himself who made and founded all things. These matters will be discussed at greater length, with the help of the true and one God himself, when I learn that you wish to act seriously.

[^1]: Virgil, Aeneid 8.302
[^2]: Virgil, Eclogues 2.65
[^3]: Virgil, Aeneid 8.319–20
