# Letter 180

To the deservedly most dear lord, and to be honored in the members of Christ, brother Oceanus, from Augustine, greetings.

**1.** I have received together two letters of your charity, in one of which you make mention of a third, and recall that you sent it first; which I do not recall having received—or rather I seem to recall well that I did not receive it. But for those which I received, I give abundant thanks to your kindness toward us. That I did not answer them immediately, I was snatched away by one and another storm of various occupations. Whence now, having gotten a drop of free time, I preferred to answer something, rather than to hold a long silence toward your most sincere charity, and to become, by taciturnity, more importunate than by loquacity.

**2.** What holy Jerome thinks about the origin of souls, I already knew, and I had already read these very words which you set down from his book in your letter. But it is not this that makes the question troublesome, which moves some—how God justly bestows souls even on adulterous conceptions; since to those living well, and converted to God by faith and piety, not even their own sins, much less their parents', can be a hindrance. But it is deservedly asked, if it is true that new individual souls are made from nothing for individuals being born, how so innumerable souls of little ones—which it is certain to God will go out of bodies, before the years of reason, before they can have a taste of or grasp anything just or unjust, without baptism—are justly given over to damnation, by him surely with whom there is no iniquity. There is no need to say more about this matter, since you know what I wish, or rather what I do not wish, to say; I think it enough for a wise man what I have said. Nevertheless, if you have read anything from this by which that question can be resolved, or heard it from his mouth, or the Lord has granted you, thinking, to know it; impart it, I beseech, to me, that I may give more abundant thanks.

**3.** But that about an officious and useful lie, which you thought should be resolved by the example of the Lord saying that not even the Son knows the day and hour of the ending of this age; I was delighted by the effort of your genius indeed, when I read it, but it seems to me in no way that a figurative manner of speaking can rightly be called a lie. For it is not a lie when we call a day glad, because it makes us glad; or a lupine sad, because it saddens the face of the taster with a bitter flavor; as God is said to know, when he makes a man know; for this saying to Abraham you yourself recalled. By no means are these lies; which you yourself most easily perceive. Accordingly blessed Hilary, when he opened an obscure question by this obscure kind of figurative speech, so that we might understand that in this he said himself not knowing, in which he makes others, by concealing, not knowing, did not excuse a lie, but showed that it was not a lie; not only in these more usual figures, but even in that which is called metaphor, which is known to all by the custom of speaking. For that the vines bud, that the crops wave, that young men flourish, someone will contend is a lie, because in these things he sees neither waves, nor stones, nor grasses, nor trees, where these words are properly said?

**4.** But further, by your genius and erudition you most easily perceive how much that differs from what the Apostle says: *When I saw that they were not walking rightly toward the truth of the Gospel, I said to Peter before all, If you, being a Jew, live in the manner of the Gentiles and not as a Jew, how do you compel the Gentiles to Judaize?*[^1] There is here no figurative obscurity, the words are the proper ones of an open manner of speaking. This surely the Teacher of the Gentiles, to those whom he was in travail with until Christ should be formed in them, and to whom under divine attestation he had foretold, *But the things I write to you, behold, before God, that I do not lie*[^2], either said truly, or falsely: if falsely—which God forbid—you perceive what follows... and, dreading both, he admonishes both an indication of truth, and in the apostle Peter an example of marvelous humility.

**5.** But why longer about this? since about this question between us, I and the aforesaid venerable brother Jerome have dealt sufficiently by letters; and in this most recent work, which under the name of Critobulus against Pelagius he has just published, he held the same opinion about this deed and the apostolic sayings, which we too have followed, that of most blessed Cyprian. Rather that about the origin of souls—not on account of adulterous births, but on account of the damnation of the innocent, which God forbid—which, I think, is not foolishly asked; if you have learned anything from so great and so excellent a man which can rightly be answered to those who are in doubt, I beg you not to refuse to communicate it with us. For you have so appeared to me, in your letters, learned and pleasant, that it is worthwhile to converse with you by letters. As for I know not what book of that same man of God, which the presbyter Orosius brought, and gave to your charity to be copied, where he is praised for having disputed splendidly about the resurrection of the flesh, I ask you now not to delay sending it to us. For this reason we did not quickly demand it, because we surely thought both to copy and to emend it; to which double work we now think the most ample time has been granted. Mindful of us, may you live to God.

[^1]: Gal 2:14
[^2]: Gal 1:20
