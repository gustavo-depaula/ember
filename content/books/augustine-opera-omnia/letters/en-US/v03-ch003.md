# Letter 158

To the venerable and most beloved brother and fellow priest Augustine, and to the brothers who are with you, from Evodius and the brothers who are with me, greetings in the Lord.

**1.** I demand the debt of the letter I sent; and I first wished to learn that which I asked, and afterward to inquire into this. Hear the matter, since you deign to, which made me impatient, so that I hastened to know this too, if it can be done, in this life. I had a certain boy as a notary, the son of Melonius, a presbyter of Armenia: this boy, as he was already plunging himself into the world—for a clerk of the proconsul was taking him up—God plucked out through my lowliness. He was indeed, as boyhood has it, quick, somewhat restless, and as his age advanced (for he died in his twenty-second year), the gravity of his character and a good guardian, a good life, so adorned him that it is quite a delight to keep his memory. He was diligent in shorthand and laborious in writing well, and he had also begun to be devoted to reading, so that he himself would exhort my slowness, for the sake of reading, in the night hours; for at some hours of the night he himself would read to me, when all had fallen silent: nor would he pass over a reading unless he had understood it, and he would repeat it a third and a fourth time, and would not let it go until what he sought had appeared to him. I had begun to hold him not as a boy and notary, but as a friend rather necessary and pleasant to me. For his conversations delighted me.

**2.** He also longed, which was granted him, *to be dissolved and to be with Christ*[^1]. For he was sick for sixteen days at his parents' home, and by the force of his memory he spoke of the Scriptures during almost his whole illness. But as he began to be near the end of life, he sang in the hearing of all: *My soul longs and hastens toward the courts of God*[^2]; and after this he sang again: *You have anointed my head with oil; and how excellent is your inebriating cup!*[^3] There he was occupied; with this consolation he was delighting himself. Then, when he began to be dissolved, he began to sign himself on the forehead, in such a way that his hand thus came down to the mouth which he desired to sign, when now the inner man, well renewed from day to day, had left the house of clay. So great a joy grew in me that I think that, having left his own body, he entered into my mind, and there bestows on me a certain brightness of his presence, because I rejoice exceedingly at his liberation and security; it cannot be told. For I bore for him no light anxiety, fearing for the youth's age. For I took care to ask of him whether perhaps he had been polluted by contact with a woman: he testified that he was free, all the more that our joy might be heaped up. He was dissolved, then. We gave him quite honorable funeral rites, worthy of so great a soul; for through three days we praised the Lord with hymns over his tomb, and on the third day we offered the sacraments of redemption.

**3.** But behold, after two days a certain respectable widow, Urbica of Figentes, who said that she had been a widow for twelve years, a handmaid of God, saw such a dream. She sees a certain deacon, stripped of his body four years before, with the servants and handmaids of God, virgins and widows, preparing a palace. It was being adorned in such a way that the brightness of the place shone, and the whole place seemed to be of silver. When she had inquired rather earnestly for whom this was being prepared, that deacon answered that it was being prepared for the boy who was carried off yesterday, the son of the presbyter. And in that same palace a certain old man dressed in white appeared, who ordered two men dressed in white to go and lift the body, taken from the tomb, up to heaven. And when, she says, the body had been taken from the tomb and lifted up to heaven, branches of roses, of the virgins (for so the cloistered ones are usually called), rose up from that same tomb.

**4.** I have narrated what happened. Now deign to hear the question, and teach what I ask. For the passing of this soul compels me to inquire into such things. While we are in the body, we have an inner sense, skillful in proportion to the agility of our zeal; and the more zealous we are, the more vigilant and fervent it is, and it seems probable that we are still held back by the impediment of the body. But who could tell all that the mind suffers from the body? Amid these disturbances and troubles, coming from suggestions, from temptations, from necessities, and from various calamities, the mind does not desert its fortitude: it resists, it conquers; sometimes it is conquered. Yet, since it remembers itself, it is made more agile and more anxious, roused by such great labors, and it breaks whatever knots of malice there are, and passes over to better things. What I mean, your holiness deigns to understand. Therefore, while we are in this life, we are hindered by such wants, and yet, as it is written, *we more than conquer through him who loved us*[^4]. Going out of the body, and escaping every burden and the easily besetting sin, who are we?

**5.** And first I ask whether there is some body which does not desert the incorporeal thing, that is, the substance of the soul itself, when it has left this earthly body—lest perhaps it be one of the four, either airy or ethereal. For since the soul is incorporeal, if it lacks every body, it is now one for all. And where will that rich man clothed in purple be, and the wounded Lazarus? How, too, will they be distinguished by their merits, so that the one has punishment and the other joy, if the soul appears as one out of all the incorporeal ones—if, that is, those things were not signified figuratively? But it is certain that things held in places are held by bodies, like that rich man in the flame and that poor man in the bosom of Abraham. If there are places, there are bodies, and in the bodies there are souls; or, if they are incorporeal, the punishments or rewards are in the consciences. Whether this one soul, made out of many souls, is afflicted and rejoices in one, so that a substance gathered out of many souls may appear; or whether this is said in the way that the incorporeal mind is one thing, and in it there is memory, and will, and understanding, and all these incorporeal causes have a fitting portion of their offices, and yet one does not impede another. In any case I think it can be answered that some are accompanied by punishment, and some by rewards, in one substance gathered out of many souls.

**6.** Or if it is not so, what hinders it, if each mind, when it lacks this solid body, has another body, so that the mind itself always animates some body; or to what does it make its passage, if there is any region to which necessity compels it to go? Since even the angels themselves cannot be called many if they are not numbered by bodies, as the Truth itself says in the Gospel: *I could ask my Father, that he would send me twelve legions of angels*[^5]. Then, since it is established that Samuel was seen in a body when he was roused at Saul's request, and that Moses, whose body was buried according to the Gospel, plainly came in a body to the Lord on the mountain when they stood together. Although in the apocrypha too, and in the secret writings of Moses himself, which lack scriptural authority, then when he went up the mountain to die, by the force of the body it is brought about that one thing was committed to the earth, another joined to the accompanying angel. But it does not press me enough to prefer the opinion of the apocrypha to those higher matters already settled. We must see, then, and trace out, whether by authority or by reason, what is sought. But it is said that the future resurrection shows that the soul has lacked every body. Nor does this hinder enough, since those angels, who are even invisible, when they wished to appear with bodies and to be seen—whatever that disposition of bodies may be, worthy of their spirits—nevertheless appeared to Abraham and to Tobit. So it can come about that, while the resurrection of this flesh, which is rightly believed to be future, takes place, the soul is nevertheless so restored that it is found never to have lacked some body. For since the body itself consists of the four elements, it seems to lose one, namely the hot, when it seems to migrate from this body. For what is earthly remains, and the moisture is not lacking; nor is the element of cold matter absent: only the hot is taken from it; which perhaps the mind itself draws with it, if it migrates from place to place. This I have said for now about the body.

**7.** It also seems to me that, if, placed in a vigorous body, as I have already said, it uses a strenuous intention of the mind, how much more, when set free, will it be agile, vigorous, fervent, strenuous, intent, and become more capacious and better—seeing that, while placed in the body, it had a taste of its power! Once that is laid aside, with the cloud also wiped away, it is made wholly serene, placed in tranquility without temptation; let it see what it has desired, let it embrace what it has loved. Let it remember its friends too, and recognize both those it had already sent ahead and those it left behind it: perhaps it is so; I do not know, I seek to learn. But it disturbs me enough, if the mind itself receives a certain drowsiness, lest it be such as when it sleeps while placed in the body, as if buried, and living only in hope, but otherwise having nothing, knowing nothing—especially if it is stirred by no dream. This thing terrifies me greatly, and seems to indicate that the mind is, as it were, extinguished.

**8.** This too I ask: if it should be found to have a body, whether it is also not without some sense? Certainly, if no necessity of smelling can be brought to bear on it, as I think, nor of tasting, nor even of touching, I am in doubt whether seeing and hearing remain. For what is it that demons are said to hear—not in all whom they vex, for there too is a question, but even when they appear in their own bodies? And as for sight, how do they pass from place to place, if they have a body, if they lack a visible sense as guide? Do you think that human souls are not so, when they go out of bodies, that they both have some body and are not without some sense? And what is that, too, that very many of the dead have been seen entering houses, as they were accustomed, either by day or by night, by those keeping watch, by those walking about? I have not heard this only once; and what is very often said to happen in some part of the night, in places where bodies are buried, and especially in the basilicas—that tumults and prayers occur. For I do not remember having heard this from one person only; for a witness of that vision is a certain holy presbyter, who noticed a multitude of such persons going out of the baptistery in luminous bodies, and afterward prayers in the middle of the church itself. All these things, then, either favor our inquiry, or, if they are tales, it is astonishing: yet I would like to know something from this matter, because they come, and visit, and are seen, apart from dreams.

**9.** From these another question arises. For I am not now concerned with the phantasm which the uneducated heart fashions for itself: I speak of visitations. As Joseph appeared through a dream, as very many have been visited. So, then, even our own whom we have sent ahead sometimes come, themselves appear through a dream, and speak. For I myself remember that Profuturus, and Privatus, and Servilius, holy men whom I remember to have gone before us from the monastery, spoke to me, and it came to pass as they said. Or if there is some better spirit which assumes their figure and visits the mind, let him see to it to whom all things are bare from the highest height. If, then, the Lord deigns to speak to your holiness through reason anything about all these things, I ask that you would deign to make me a sharer in this knowledge. But I did not wish to pass over this too; for perhaps it pertains to my inquiry.

**10.** The boy himself who is in question, at the time when he was being released, in a way set forth, proceeds. For there seemed to come, through a dream, his fellow student and fellow reader, with whom I used to take down notes, who had already been removed from the body eight months before. When he was asked by the one who then beheld him why he had come, he said: I have come to lead this friend of mine. And so it came to pass. For in that very house, to a certain old man almost awake, there appeared a man carrying a laurel in his hand, written upon. But when he was seen, there is added still further that, after the boy's departure, his father the presbyter had begun to be in the monastery with old Theasius for the sake of consoling themselves; but on the third day after the boy's death, that same boy seemed to enter the monastery, and was asked by a certain brother through a dream whether he knew that he had died. He said that he knew. Whether he had been received by God? And this too he reported about himself with great rejoicing. And when the reason was asked why he had come, he then said: I have been sent to fetch my father. The one to whom these things were shown wakes, and tells it. It reached even to the ear of bishop Theasius. He, much moved, rebuked the man who told it, lest it easily reach the ear of the presbyter and he be disturbed by such a message. Why prolong it? After perhaps four days, while he is speaking of the visitation—for he had felt a slight fever, there was no danger, the physician was away, who testified that there was utterly no cause for concern—but when he reclined on his bed, that same presbyter was released. Nor do I keep silent that on this very day on which the boy was being released, he asked his father for the kiss of peace, and asked a third time, and at each kiss said to his father, Father, let us give thanks to God; and he urged his father to say it together with him, as if exhorting his companion to migrate from this life. For between the two there are counted seven intervening days. What will come of such great matters? Who will be the most faithful teacher of these hidden causes? The fever of my heart pours itself back upon you in the time of distress. The dispensation of the boy's departure, and of his father's, is manifest, since *two sparrows will not fall to the ground without the will of the Father*[^6].

**11.** That the soul therefore cannot be utterly without every body is shown, as I think, by this: that God alone is always without every body. But that the soul, which I judge will be the more vigilant after its passing, is shown by the laying aside of so great a corporeal mass; for then, expedited in action and in cognition from so great a bond, it appears, as I think, more excellent, and all that spiritual rest shows it free from all disturbances and errors, not withered, and as it were sluggish, and torpid, and entangled: indeed it suffices it to enjoy fully even that very freedom which it has attained, when it lacks the world and the body; for you prudently said that the understanding is fed by these things, and sets a spiritual mouth to the fountain of life, where it is happy and blessed in the property of its own mind. For once, while still placed in the monastery, I saw brother Servilius through a dream after his departure, who said that we, through reason, labor to tend toward understanding, but that he and such as he remain in the very delight of contemplation.

**12.** I ask too that you would show me in how many ways wisdom is spoken of: as God is wisdom, as a wise mind is wisdom—how it is said as light, as the wisdom of Bezalel who built the Tabernacle, or as a perfumer's skill, as the wisdom of Solomon, or if there is any other, and how they differ from one another: and whether that one eternal wisdom with the Father is to be understood in these grades, in the way that the various gifts are spoken of of the Holy Spirit, who *divides to each his own as he wills*[^7]. Or is it that, apart from that one wisdom alone which was not made, these were made and have their own substance? Or were they brought into effect and received from the definition of the work? I inquire into many things; may the Lord give you both the grace of finding, and the wisdom of dictating, and of writing to us speedily. I have written unskillfully and rustically; but because you deign to know what I ask, I beseech you through Christ the Lord that you correct me in these things, and teach what you understand that I desire to know.
