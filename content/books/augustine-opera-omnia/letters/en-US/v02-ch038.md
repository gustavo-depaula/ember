# Letter 137

To the illustrious lord, and deservedly distinguished, and most excellent son Volusianus, Augustine sends greetings in the Lord.

**1. 1.** I have read your letter, in which I saw the specimen of a certain great dialogue, comprehended with praiseworthy brevity. I ought, then, to answer, and not to oppose any excuse for delay. For it happened opportunely that I had a little leisure from other businesses. But the things to whose dictation I had decided to spend this leisure I put off for a little, judging it in no way just to defer one inquiring whom I myself had exhorted to inquire. But which of us, who minister, as we can, the grace of Christ, when he reads your words, would wish you to be so instructed in Christian doctrine that it should suffice you for the salvation only of this life—which the divine word took care to admonish is most like a vapor, appearing for a little and at once vanishing and perishing—but not that salvation for whose obtaining and possessing forever we are Christians? It is too little for us, then, so to instruct you that it suffice you to be set free. For your talent and eloquence, so excellent and so brilliant, ought to profit others too, against whose slowness or perversity the dispensation of so great grace is most fittingly to be defended—the grace which proud little souls esteem at nothing, who too much affect to be able to do very much, and to heal or even rein in their own vices can do nothing.

**1. 2.** You ask, then: "Whether the Lord and ruler of the world filled the body of an unviolated woman; whether the mother bore those long disgusts of ten months, and yet a virgin gave birth with the solemnity of bearing, and after this her virginity remained inviolate. Whether he lay hidden within the little body of a wailing infancy, to whom the universe is scarcely thought equal; suffered the years of boyhood, grew up, was made solid by youth; so long that ruler was absent from his seats, and to one little body the care of the whole world was transferred; then was dissolved into sleeps; fed with food, felt all the affections of mortals; nor did any fitting signs make clear the indications of so great a majesty; since that spectral purification, the cures of the weak, life restored to the dead—these, if we consider others too, are small things for God." You write that this question was brought forward, at a certain gathering of friends, by one of the many who were present; but that you intervened upon him inquiring further, and, the gathering being broken up, deferred it to the merits of better expertise, lest, while secrets are incautiously violated, an innocent error should bend into fault.

**1. 3.** Then you direct to me the intention of your letter, and after this confession of unskillfulness, you admonish me to recognize what is desired from your party. You add too that it is in the interest of my fame that you should know what is sought, because somehow, without detriment to divine worship, ignorance is tolerated in other priests, but when one comes to me, the bishop, whatever happens to be unknown is a defect in the law. First, then, I ask that you lay down this opinion of me, so easily presumed, and that you loose and put off that mind, however most benevolent toward me; and that you believe me about myself rather than anyone else, if you repay me the like of love. For so great is the profundity of the Christian writings that I would daily profit in them, if I tried to learn them alone, from the beginning of boyhood to decrepit old age, with the greatest leisure, the highest zeal, a better talent: not that one arrives at the things necessary to salvation in them with so great difficulty; but when one has held there the faith without which one does not live devoutly and rightly, so many things, and overshadowed by so many manifold little shades of mysteries, remain to be understood by those progressing, and so great a height of wisdom lies hidden, not only in the words by which these things are said, but even in the things which are to be understood, that to the most aged, the most acute, the most ardent with desire of learning, this befalls which the same Scripture has in a certain place: *When a man has finished, then he begins*[^1].

**2. 4.** But why more of this? We must rather come to the matter which you ask. Where first I wish you to know that Christian doctrine does not hold this, that God was so infused into the flesh in which he was born of the virgin that he either deserted or lost the care of governing the universe, or transferred it to that little body as to a contracted and gathered matter. This is the sense of men able to think nothing but bodies; whether these grosser, like moisture and earth, or subtler, like those of air and light; but yet bodies, of which none can be everywhere whole, since through innumerable parts it must have one part in one place, another in another; and however great a body, or however little a little body, occupies a space of place, and so fills that place that in no part of it is it whole. And through this, to be condensed and to grow rare, to be contracted and dilated, to be worn into minute particles and to grow into a mass, belongs to none but bodies. Far other is the nature of the soul than of the body: how much more of God, who is the creator both of the soul and of the body? God is not said to fill the world as water, as air, as the light itself, so that with a lesser part of himself he fills a lesser part of the world, and with a greater a greater. He knows how to be everywhere whole, and to be contained by no place; he knows how to come without withdrawing from where he was; he knows how to depart without deserting whither he had come.

**2. 5.** The human mind marvels at this, and because it does not grasp it, perhaps does not even believe it: let it first marvel at itself, let it raise itself a little, if it can, from the body, and from those things which it is wont to feel through the body; and let it see what it itself is, which uses the body. But perhaps it cannot: for it is of great talent, as someone says, to call the mind away from the senses, and to lead thought away from habit. Let it, then, search the very senses of the body somewhat more unwontedly and vigilantly. There are certainly five distinct senses of the body, which can be neither without the body nor without the soul: because neither is feeling except of one living, which is to the body from the soul; nor without corporeal instruments, and, as it were, vessels and organs, do we see, hear, and use the other three senses. Let this rational soul attend, and consider the senses of the body, not by the senses of the body, but by the mind and reason itself. Surely a man cannot feel unless he lives; but he lives in the flesh, before death sunders both. How, then, does the soul feel the things which are outside its flesh, which lives only in its flesh? Are not the stars in the sky most far from its flesh? Does it not see the sun there in the sky? Or is not to feel to see, since sight is more excellent than the other five senses? Or does it live in the sky too, because it feels in the sky too, and there cannot be sense where there is not life? Or does it feel even where it does not live, because, although it lives only in its own flesh, it feels also in those places which, beyond its flesh, contain the things it touches with sight? Do you see how hidden this is in a sense so conspicuous, which is called sight? Attend also to hearing. For this too in a manner diffuses itself outward from the flesh. For whence do we say, "It sounds outside," unless we feel there where it sounds? Therefore there too, outside our flesh, we live. Or can we feel even where we do not live, since sense cannot be without life?

**2. 6.** The other three senses feel within themselves, although about smell one may somewhat doubt. But about taste and touch there is no controversy, that the things we taste and touch we feel nowhere but in our flesh. Accordingly let these three senses be set aside from this consideration. Sight and hearing bring a marvelous question: either how the soul feels where it does not live, or how it lives where it is not. For it is only in its own flesh; but it feels also beyond its own flesh. For there it feels where it sees; because to see is to feel: there it feels where it hears; because to hear too is to feel. Either, then, it lives there too, and through this is there too; or it feels even where it does not live; or it lives even where it is not. All these things are marvelous; none of these can be affirmed without a certain, as it were, absurdity: and we speak of a deathbound sense. What, then, is the soul itself, beyond the sense of the body—that is, in the mind by which it considers these things? For it does not, by the sense of the body, judge about the senses of the body themselves. And do we think something incredible is said to us about the omnipotence of God, when it is said that the Word of God, through whom all things were made, so took a body from the virgin, and appeared to mortal senses, that it did not corrupt its immortality, did not change its eternity, did not diminish its power, did not desert the administration of the world, did not withdraw from the bosom of the Father—that is, from the secret in which it is with him and in him!

**2. 7.** Understand the Word of God, through whom all things were made, so as not to think that anything of it passes, and from future becomes past. It abides as it is, and is everywhere whole. But it comes when it is manifested, and when it is hidden it withdraws. Yet it is present, whether hidden or manifest, as the light is present to the eyes both of one seeing and of one blind: but to the seeing it is present, to the blind absent. The voice too is present to hearing ears, present even to deaf ones: but to the former it lies open, from the latter it lies hidden. But what is more marvelous than that which happens in our voices and sounding words, in a thing, namely, rapidly transitory? For when we speak, there is no place even for the second syllable unless the first has ceased to sound; and yet, if one hearer be present, he hears the whole of what we say; and if two be present, both hear the same, and what is whole to the individuals; and if a silent multitude hear, they do not break the sounds among themselves piecemeal, like foods, but all that sounds is both whole to all, and whole to the individuals. Is it not, then, rather that incredible, if the passing word of a man, which it exhibits to the ears, the abiding Word of God should not exhibit to things, so that, just as this is heard at once whole even by individuals, so that should be at once everywhere whole?

**2. 8.** And so the little body of infancy is not to be feared, lest in it God seem to have suffered so great straits. For God is great not by mass, but by power; who by his providence gave a better sense to little ants and little bees than to asses and camels; who from the most minute grain of seed creates so great a magnitude of a fig tree, when from much larger seeds many far smaller things are born; who enriched the pupil, so small, with a keenness which, flashing out through the eyes, in an instant of time surveys nearly half the sky; who from a point and, as it were, the center of the brain diffuses all the senses by a fivefold distribution; who by the heart, a member so small, dispenses vital motion through all parts of the body: by these and such things insinuating great things from the least, who is not little in little things. For the very magnitude of his power, which feels no straits in a narrow space, made fruitful the virginal womb not by an adventitious but by an indigenous childbearing: it fitted to itself the rational soul, and through it the human body too, and altogether the whole man, to be changed for the better, in no way being changed for the worse; deigningly taking from him the name of humanity, lavishly granting him divinity. That very power, through the virginal vitals of the inviolate mother, brought forth the members of the infant, which afterward, through closed doors, it brought in as the members of the young man. Here, if a reason is sought, it will not be marvelous: if an example is demanded, it will not be singular. Let us grant that God can do something which we confess we cannot investigate. In such matters the whole reason of the deed is the power of the doer.

**3. 9.** Now that—which is dissolved into sleeps, and is fed with food, and feels all human affections—persuades men of the man whom he did not consume, but assumed. Behold, it was so done; and yet certain heretics, by perversely marveling at and praising his power, were utterly unwilling to recognize in him the human nature, where is all the commendation of grace by which he makes safe those believing in him, containing the deep treasures of wisdom and knowledge, and imbuing minds with faith, which he may advance to the eternal contemplation of unchangeable truth. What if the Omnipotent should create a man, formed anywhere, not from a maternal womb, but should bring him suddenly before the sight? What if he should change no ages from a little child into youth, take no foods, no sleeps? Would he not confirm the opinion of error, nor be believed in any way to have taken a true man; and, while he does all things marvelously, take away what he did mercifully? But now he so appeared as the mediator between God and men that, coupling both natures in the unity of person, he both sublimed the wonted with the unwonted, and tempered the unwonted with the wonted.

**3. 10.** But what does God do that is not marvelous in all the movements of creation, unless they had grown cheap by daily habit! Finally, how many usual things are trodden underfoot, which, considered, are astonishing! Like the very force of seeds—what numbers they have, and how vivacious, how efficacious, how hiddenly powerful, how, in a small thing, accomplishing great things—who can approach it in mind, who bring it forth in speech? He, then, made for himself a man without seed, who in the nature of things works even seeds without seeds. He, in his own body, kept the numbers of times and the measures of ages, who, without any changeableness of himself, by changing, wove the order of the ages. For this grew in time, which began from time. But the Word in the beginning, through which times were made, chose a time in which to take up flesh, did not yield to time so as to be turned into flesh. For man approached God, God did not withdraw from himself.

**3. 11.** But certain men demand that a reason be rendered them how God was mingled with man so that one person of Christ came to be, when this had to be done once; as though they themselves rendered a reason about the thing which happens daily, how the soul is mixed with the body so that one person of a man comes to be. For just as in the unity of person the soul is united to the body, that there may be a man, so in the unity of person God is united to man, that there may be Christ. In that person, then, is a mixture of soul and body; in this person is a mixture of God and man: if only the hearer withdraw from the habit of bodies, by which two liquids are wont to be so mixed that neither keeps its integrity; although even in bodies themselves uncorrupted light is mixed with air. The person of a man, then, is a mixture of soul and body: but the person of Christ is a mixture of God and man. For when the Word of God was mixed with a soul having a body, it took up at once both soul and body. The former happens daily for the procreation of men: the latter happened once for the liberation of men. Nevertheless, the commixture of two incorporeal things ought to have been believed more easily than that of one incorporeal and the other corporeal. For if the soul is not deceived in its own nature, it comprehends that it is incorporeal: much more is the Word of God incorporeal, and through this the commixture of the Word of God and the soul ought to have been more credible than that of soul and body. But this we experience in ourselves: that we are commanded to believe in Christ. But if both were prescribed to us to be believed, alike untried by us, which of these would we more readily believe? How would we not confess that two incorporeal things could more easily be mixed than one corporeal and the other incorporeal? If only the name of mixture or commixture is not unworthily admitted to these things, on account of the habit of corporeal things, which are far otherwise and otherwise known.

**3. 12.** The Word of God, therefore, and the same Son of God, coeternal with the Father, and the same Power and Wisdom of God, reaching strongly from the highest bound of rational creation to the lowest bound of corporeal creation, and disposing all things sweetly, present and hidden, nowhere enclosed, nowhere torn, nowhere swollen, but without mass everywhere whole, in a way far other than that by which he is present to the other creatures, took up a man, and made himself and him one Jesus Christ, the mediator of God and men, equal to the Father according to divinity, but less than the Father according to the flesh, that is, according to man; unchangeably immortal according to the divinity equal to the Father, and the same changeable and mortal according to the weakness akin to us. In which Christ, at the time which he himself knew most opportune, and had disposed before the ages, there came to men teaching and help, for grasping everlasting salvation. Teaching indeed, so that those things which were said here before to be usefully true—not only by the holy Prophets, who said all things true, but even by the philosophers and the poets themselves, and authors of letters of whatever kind (who, who doubts mixed many true things with false?)—his authority too, presented in the flesh, might confirm, on account of those who could not discern and distinguish those things in inmost truth itself: which Truth, even before he took up man, was himself present to all who could be partakers of it. But most of all by the example of his incarnation he wholesomely persuaded this: that since very many men, greedy of divinity, thought they must seek their way to God through the heavenly powers which they supposed to be gods, and through various rites of unlawful things—not sacred, but sacrilegious—more proudly than devoutly (in which matter, in place of the holy Angels, the demons by kinship of pride subordinate themselves to them), men might know that God is so near to the piety of men—to whom, as though set far off, they sought their way through interposed powers—that he deigned to take up man, and to be united with him in a certain way, so that the whole man might be so fitted to him as the body to the soul; except the changeable concretion into which God is not turned, and which we see that both the body and the mind have. But help, because without the grace of faith which is from him no one can conquer vicious concupiscences; and if anyone has not conquered any remaining of them, that they be purified by venial remission. As far, then, as pertains to his teaching, who now, the lowest illiterate, or what abject little woman, does not believe the immortality of the soul and the future life after death? Which, among the Greeks of old, when Pherecydes the Assyrian first disputed, he turned Pythagoras of Samos, moved by the novelty of that disputation, from an athlete into a philosopher. Now, then, what Maro says, we all see: *the Assyrian amomum is born commonly*. But as far as pertains to the help of grace, which is in Christ, he himself is altogether the one of whom it is said: *Under whose leadership, if any traces of our crime remain, made void, they will free the lands from perpetual dread.*

**4. 13.** "But," they say, "no indications of so great majesty shone forth by fitting signs: because that spectral purification, the cures of the weak, life restored to the dead, if others too are considered, are small things for God." We too indeed confess that the Prophets did certain such things. For among these signs, what is more excellent than that the dead rose again? Elijah did this, Elisha did this. For about the miracles of the magicians—whether they too raised the dead—let those see who try to convict Apuleius, defending himself most copiously against the charges of magic arts, not by accusing but by praising him. We read that the magicians of the Egyptians, most expert in those arts, were overcome by Moses, the servant of God, when they did certain marvels by wicked arts, and he, God being simply invoked, overturned all their machinations. But Moses himself too, and the other most truthful Prophets, prophesied the Lord Christ, and gave him great glory; they foretold that he would come, not as equal to themselves, nor as superior in the same power of miracles, but plainly the Lord God of all, and made man for the sake of men. Who therefore willed himself too to do such things, lest it be absurd that he should not do what he had done through them. But yet he ought also to do something proper: to be born of a virgin, to rise from the dead, to ascend into heaven. He who thinks this too little for God, what more he expects I do not know.

**4. 14.** For I judge that such things are demanded as, bearing a man, he ought to do. For *in the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God, and all things were made through him*[^2]. Should he, man being assumed, make another world, that we might believe him to be the one through whom the world was made? But neither a greater world, nor one equal to this, could be made in this world. But if he made a lesser one below this, this too likewise would be thought too little. Because, then, it did not behoove that he make a new world, he made new things in the world. For a man procreated of a virgin, and raised from the dead into eternal life, and exalted above the heavens, is perhaps a more powerful work than the world. Here perhaps they answer that they do not believe this was done. What, then, shall be done with men who despise the least things, do not believe the greater? Life restored to the dead is believed because others did it, and it is small for God: flesh of his own created from a virgin, and lifted from death into eternal life above the heavens, is therefore not believed, because no one did it, and it befits God. And through this, what each one thinks easy for himself—not in deed, but in grasp—he receives with an even mind; the things above these, as though feigned, he reckons for false: do not be like them, I beseech you.

**4. 15.** These things are disputed more at length, and all the recesses of the necessary questions, searched out and discussed, are laid open: but faith opens the approach to understanding, unbelief closes it. Whom would not the very order of events from the beginning move to believe, and the very connection of times, making faith about present things from past ones, confirming earlier things by later and more recent ones, and ancient things by recent? One man is chosen from the nation of the Chaldeans, endowed with a most faithful piety, to whom the divine promises—to be fulfilled, after so great a series of ages, in the last times—are revealed, and in whose seed all nations are foretold to be about to have blessing. He, worshiping the one true God, the creator of the universe, an old man begets a son from a wife whom barrenness and age had already made utterly destitute of the hope of bearing. From him is propagated a most numerous people, multiplied in Egypt, whither the divine arrangement, growing by promises and fulfillments, had transferred that stock from the eastern parts. A strong nation is led out from the servitude of Egypt by dreadful signs and miracles; and, the impious nations being driven out, it is brought into and established in the land of promise, and is even exalted by a kingdom. Then, sin prevailing, very often offending by sacrilegious attempts the true God who had conferred so great benefits on it, and scourged by various disasters and consoled by prosperities, it is brought up to the incarnation and manifestation of Christ. Which Christ—the Word of God, the Son of God, God about to come in the flesh, to die, to rise, to ascend into heaven, by his most preeminent name about to have among all nations peoples dedicated to him; and in him remission of sins, and eternal salvation about to be for believers—all the promises of that nation, all the prophecies, the priesthoods, the sacrifices, the temple, and altogether all the sacraments, sounded forth.

**4. 16.** Christ too has come; in his birth, life, words, deeds, sufferings, death, resurrection, ascension, all the proclamations of the Prophets are fulfilled. He sends the Holy Spirit, fills the faithful gathered together in one house, and awaiting this very promise by praying and desiring. Filled with the Holy Spirit, they suddenly speak the tongues of all nations, confidently refute errors, preach the most wholesome truth, exhort to repentance of a past culpable life, promise indulgence from divine grace. Congruous signs and miracles follow the preaching of piety and true religion. Savage unbelief is roused against them; they endure the things foretold, hope the things promised, teach the things commanded. Few in number, they are scattered through the world, convert peoples with marvelous ease, are increased among enemies, grow by persecutions, are spread through the straits of afflictions even to the ends of the lands. From the most unskilled, the most abject, the very fewest, are enlightened, ennobled, multiplied the most illustrious talents, the most cultivated eloquences; and they subjugate to Christ the marvelous skills of the acute, the eloquent, and the learned, and turn them to preaching the way of piety and salvation. By alternating adversities and prosperities of affairs, they vigilantly exercise patience and temperance: the world declining to its extremes, and protesting by its wearied state its last age, they all the more confidently—because this too was foretold—await the eternal happiness of the heavenly city. And amid all these things, against the Church of Christ the unbelief of the impious nations rages: she overcomes by suffering, and by professing an unshaken faith amid the savagery of those resisting. The sacrifice of the revealed truth—which was long veiled by mystical promises—succeeding, those sacrifices by which this was figured are taken away, the very temple being overthrown. The Jewish nation itself, reprobate through unbelief, is rooted out from its seats, scattered everywhere through the world, so that everywhere it may carry the holy Codices, and so the testimony of the prophecy by which Christ and the Church were foretold may be brought forth by the very adversaries—lest it be esteemed feigned by us for the time—where it was even foretold that they would not believe. The temples and images of the demons, and the sacrilegious rites, are little by little and alternately overthrown according to the prophetic predictions. Heresies against the name of Christ—yet under the veil of the name of Christ—for exercising the teaching of holy religion, as they were foretold, sprout up. All these things, as they are read foretold, so they are seen fulfilled, and from these now so many and so great, the things that remain are awaited to be fulfilled. What mind, greedy of eternity and moved by the brevity of the present life, would in the end contend against the light and summit of this divine authority?

**5. 17.** What disputations, what writings of any philosophers, what laws of any cities, are in any way to be compared with the two precepts on which Christ says the whole Law and the Prophets depend: *You shall love the Lord your God with your whole heart, and with your whole soul, and with your whole mind; and, You shall love your neighbor as yourself*[^3]? Here is physics, since all the causes of all natures are in God the creator. Here is ethics, since a good and honorable life is formed from no other source than when the things that are to be loved are loved in the way they are to be loved—that is, God and neighbor. Here is logic, since the truth and light of the rational soul is none but God. Here too is the praiseworthy welfare of the republic: for a city is not best founded and guarded except by the foundation and bond of faith, and firm concord, when the common good is loved, which is highest and most truly God, and in him men love one another most sincerely, when they love themselves for his sake, from whom, with what mind they love, they cannot hide.

**5. 18.** But the very manner of speaking by which holy Scripture is woven—how accessible to all, though penetrable by the very fewest! What it contains openly, like a familiar friend, it speaks without disguise to the heart both of the unlearned and the learned: but what it hides in mysteries, it does not lift up even by a proud eloquence, so that a mind somewhat slow and untaught should not dare to approach it, as a poor man a rich; but it invites all with humble discourse, whom it may not only feed with the manifest, but also exercise with the secret truth, having in the ready what it has in the hidden. But lest the open things grow cheap, those same things, again hidden, are desired, and, desired, are in a manner renewed, and, renewed, are sweetly intimated. By these things both depraved talents are wholesomely corrected, and small ones nourished, and great ones delighted. That mind is an enemy to this teaching which either, by erring, does not know it to be most wholesome, or, by being sick, hates the medicine.

**5. 19.** You see how long a letter I have made. If, then, anything moves you, and you hold it worth so much that it be handled between us, let not the measure of usual letters, as though to be kept, make straits for you; because you know very well how great the ancients had them, when they were doing something which they could not unfold briefly. And if the custom of authors pertaining to other writings were otherwise, the authority of our own would be set before us in this matter more worthily to be imitated. Look, then, at the measure of the apostolic Letters, or even of those who treated those divine words; and let it not irk you either to set forth many things by inquiring, if many things move you, or sometimes to turn over longer what you inquire, so that, as far as can be done through such as we are, there remain no cloud of doubt to stand in the way of the light of truth.

**5. 20.** For I know that your Excellency endures the most obstinate contradictions of certain men, who for this reason think, or wish it to be thought, that Christian doctrine does not agree with the benefit of the republic, because they do not wish the republic to stand by the firmness of virtues, but by the impunity of vices. But it is not to God, as to a king who is a man, or to any prince of a city, that whatever is sinned by many goes unpunished. But his mercy and grace, preached to men through the man Christ, but imparted through God and the Son of God, the same Christ himself, does not desert those who live from faith in him, and devoutly worship him, whether they experience the evils of this life patiently and bravely, or use his goods mercifully and temperately; being about to receive for each an eternal reward in the supernal and divine city, where now no calamity is to be troublesomely tolerated, nor desire laboriously to be reined in, but the love of God and neighbor alone is to be retained, without any difficulty, and with perfect liberty. May the most merciful omnipotence of God guard you safe and more happy, illustrious lord, and deservedly distinguished, and most excellent son. Your holy mother, most worthy to be honored in Christ, whose prayers for you may God hear, I greet most dutifully according to your merits. My holy brother and fellow bishop Possidius greatly greets your Eminence.

[^1]: Sir 18:6 (7)
[^2]: John 1:1, 3
[^3]: Matt 22:37-40
