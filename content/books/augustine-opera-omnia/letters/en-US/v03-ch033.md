# Letter 185

*A Book on the Correction of the Donatists*

Augustine to Boniface

**1. 1.** I praise, and rejoice, and marvel, most beloved son Boniface, that amid the cares of wars and arms you vehemently desire to know the things which are God's. Truly from this it appears that you serve, even by your very military valor, the faith which you have in Christ. So, to intimate briefly to your charity what difference there is between the error of the Arians and that of the Donatists: the Arians say that the substances of the Father, and the Son, and the Holy Spirit are diverse; but the Donatists do not say this, but confess one substance of the Trinity. And if some of them have said that the Son is less than the Father is, they have nevertheless not denied that he is of the same substance: but very many among them say that they believe altogether about the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit what the catholic Church believes. Nor is that question at issue with them; but they unhappily litigate about communion alone, and exercise rebel enmities against the unity of Christ by the perversity of their error. But sometimes, as we have heard, some of them, wishing to conciliate the Goths to themselves, when they see that they can do something, say that they believe what those too believe. But they are convicted by the authority of their elders; because not even Donatus himself, of whose party they glory to be, is asserted to have believed thus.

**1. 2.** But let not these things disturb you, most beloved son. For heresies and scandals were foretold to come, that we may be instructed among enemies; and so both our faith and our love may be more approved: faith, surely, that we be not deceived by them; but love, that we may take counsel, as much as we can, even for their correction—not only urging that they not harm the weak, and that they be freed from the wicked error, but also praying for them, that the Lord may open their sense, and they may understand the Scriptures. Because in the holy Books, where the Lord Christ is manifested, there his Church too is declared: but these people, by a marvelous blindness, since they do not know Christ himself apart from the Scriptures, nevertheless do not recognize his Church by the authority of the divine Letters, but invent it by the vanity of human calumnies.

**1. 3.** They acknowledge with us Christ in that which is read: *They have pierced my hands and my feet; they have numbered all my bones. They themselves considered and looked upon me; they divided my garments among themselves, and over my garment they cast lots*[^1]: but they are unwilling to acknowledge the Church in that which a little after follows: *All the ends of the earth shall be reminded and shall turn to the Lord; and all the families of the nations shall adore in his sight: for the kingdom is the Lord's, and he shall rule the nations*[^2]. They acknowledge with us Christ in that which is read: *The Lord said to me, You are my Son, today I have begotten you*: but they are unwilling to acknowledge the Church in that which follows: *Ask of me, and I will give you the nations for your inheritance, and the ends of the earth for your possession*[^3]. They acknowledge with us Christ in that which the Lord himself speaks in the Gospel: *It behooved Christ to suffer, and to rise from the dead on the third day*: but they are unwilling to acknowledge the Church in that which follows: *and that repentance and remission of sins be preached in his name among all nations, beginning from Jerusalem*[^4]. And there are innumerable testimonies of the holy Books, which I ought not to crowd into this book. In which, just as the Lord Christ appears—whether according to the divinity by which he is equal to the Father, since *in the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God*[^5]; or according to the lowliness of the flesh assumed, since *the Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us*[^6]—so his Church too appears, not in Africa alone, as these people rave with most shameless vanity, but diffused throughout the whole world.

**1. 4.** For they prefer their own lawsuits to the divine testimonies, because in the case of Caecilian, once bishop of the Church of Carthage, against whom they charged crimes which they neither could nor can prove, they divided themselves from the catholic Church, that is, from the unity of all nations. Although, even if the things charged against Caecilian by them were true, and could at some time be shown to us, we would anathematize him, now dead: but yet we ought not to abandon the Church of Christ, which is not fashioned by litigious opinions, but proved by divine attestations, on account of any man whatever; because *it is good to trust in the Lord rather than to trust in man*[^7]. For—which I would say without injury to his innocence—if Caecilian sinned, Christ did not on that account lose his inheritance. It is easy for a man to believe things, whether true or false, about another man; but it is of wicked shamelessness, on account of a man's crimes, which you cannot show to the world, to wish to condemn the communion of the world.

**1. 5.** Whether Caecilian was ordained by handers-over of the divine Codices, I do not know; I did not see it, I heard it from his enemies: it is not recited to me from the Law of God, not from the proclamation of the Prophets, not from the holiness of the Psalms, not from the Apostle of Christ, not from the utterance of Christ. But the Church diffused throughout the whole world, with which the party of Donatus does not communicate, the testimonies of all the Scriptures proclaim with a consonant voice. *In your seed shall all nations be blessed*[^8], the Law of God said. *From the rising of the sun to its setting, a clean sacrifice is offered to my name; for my name is glorified among the nations*[^9], the Lord said through the Prophet. *He shall rule from sea to sea, and from the river to the ends of the earth*[^10], the Lord said through the Psalm. *Bearing fruit and growing in the whole world*[^11], the Lord said through the Apostle. *You shall be my witnesses in Jerusalem, and in all Judea, and in Samaria, and to the ends of the earth*[^12], the Son of God said by his own mouth. Caecilian, bishop of the Church of Carthage, is accused by human lawsuits: the Church of Christ, established among all nations, is commended by divine voices. Piety itself, truth, love, does not permit us to accept against Caecilian the testimony of those men whom we do not see in the Church to which God bears testimony: for those who do not follow the divine testimonies have lost the weight of human testimony.

**2. 6.** I add that they themselves sent the case of Caecilian to the judgment of the emperor Constantine by accusing him; nay rather, that they brought Caecilian himself, after episcopal judgments, where they could not crush him, to the examination of the aforesaid emperor, as most pertinacious persecutors. And what they now blame in us, to deceive the unskilled, saying that Christians ought not to ask anything from Christian emperors against the enemies of Christ, they themselves did first. Which even in the conference which we held together at Carthage, they did not dare to deny: nay, they even dared to boast that before the emperor their elders had criminally pursued Caecilian; adding moreover the lie that they conquered him there and made him condemned. How, then, are they themselves not persecutors, who, since by accusing they persecuted Caecilian, and were overcome by him, wished to arrogate to themselves a false glory by a most shameless lie; not only not thinking it a fault, but even boasting it for their praise, if they could prove that Caecilian had been condemned by their elders accusing? But how in that conference they were in every way conquered—since the proceedings are very lengthy, and for you, occupied with other matters necessary for the Roman peace, it is much that they be read—a Summary of them can perhaps be read to you, which I believe my brother and fellow bishop Optatus has; or if he does not have it, he can most easily receive it from the Church of Sitifis: since indeed even this book is now perhaps, by its own length, burdensome to your cares.

**2. 7.** For this has befallen the Donatists which befell the accusers of holy Daniel. For as against those the lions, so against these have the laws been turned, by which they wished to crush the innocent; except that, on account of the mercy of Christ, these laws which seem adverse to them are rather for them: since many have been corrected through them, and are daily corrected; and they give thanks that they are corrected, and freed from that furious ruin. And those who hated now love, and as much as in their insanity they detested the most healthful laws as troublesome to them, so much, their health recovered, do they rejoice; and they are roused, with a like love now together with us, toward the remaining ones with whom they were going to perish, so that we may equally press on lest those perish. For a physician too is troublesome to a frenzied man, and a father to an undisciplined son; the one by binding, the other by beating; but both by loving. But if they should neglect them, and permit them to perish, that mildness, rather, is a false cruelty. For if the horse and the mule, which have no understanding, resist with bites and kicks the men by whom their wounds, needing care, are handled; and although among their teeth and kicks the men are often in danger, and sometimes harassed, yet they do not abandon them, until through pains and medicinal troubles they recall them to health: how much more is a man not to be abandoned by a man, and a brother by a brother, lest he perish forever—one who, corrected, can understand how great a benefit was being bestowed on him, when he was complaining that he was suffering persecution!

**2. 8.** As, then, the Apostle says: *Untiring, while we have time, let us work good toward all*[^13]: let those who can, by the discourses of catholic preachers; those who can, by the laws of catholic princes; partly through those who obey divine admonitions, partly through those who obey imperial commands, let all be called to salvation, let all be recalled from ruin. For emperors too, when for falsehood against truth they establish evil laws, the well-believing are proved, and the persevering are crowned: but when for truth against falsehood they establish good laws, the raging are terrified, and the understanding are corrected. Whoever, then, is unwilling to obey the laws of emperors which are passed against the truth of God, gains a great reward: but whoever is unwilling to obey the laws of emperors which are passed for the truth of God, gains a great punishment. For even in the times of the Prophets, all the kings who in the people of God did not prohibit and overturn the things that had been instituted against the precepts of God are blamed; and those who prohibited and overturned them are praised above the merits of the others. And king Nebuchadnezzar, when he was a servant of idols, established a sacrilegious law that an image be adored; but those who were unwilling to obey his impious constitution did piously and faithfully: yet the same king, corrected by a divine miracle, established a pious and laudable law for the truth, that whoever should speak blasphemy against the true God of Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego should utterly perish with his house. If any despised this law, and deservedly suffered what had been established, they ought to say what these people say, that they are just, because they suffered persecution by the king's law: which they would surely say, if they were as insane as these people are insane who divide the members of Christ, and blow upon the sacraments of Christ, and glory in persecution; because they are prohibited from doing these things by the laws of emperors, which they established for the unity of Christ; and they fallaciously boast their innocence, and the glory of martyrs, which they cannot receive from the Lord, they seek from men.

**2. 9.** But the true martyrs are those of whom the Lord says: *Blessed are those who suffer persecution for righteousness' sake*[^14]. Not, then, those who suffer persecution for iniquity, and for the impious division of Christian unity, but those who suffer it for righteousness, these are the true martyrs. For Hagar too suffered persecution from Sarah; and she who did it was holy, she who suffered it was iniquitous. Is the holy David, whom the iniquitous Saul persecuted, to be compared to this persecution which Hagar suffered? It differs greatly, surely, not because he suffered, but because he suffered for righteousness. And the Lord himself was crucified with thieves: but those whom the passion joined, the cause separated. Therefore in the Psalm that voice is to be understood of the true martyrs, wishing themselves to be distinguished from the false martyrs: *Judge me, O God, and distinguish my cause from an unholy nation*[^15]: he did not say, "Distinguish my punishment"; but, *Distinguish my cause*. For the punishment of the impious can be alike, but the cause of the martyrs is unalike; whose is also that voice: *They have persecuted me unjustly, help me*[^16]: therefore he judged himself worthy to be helped justly, because they persecuted unjustly; for if they persecuted justly, he was not to be helped, but corrected.

**2. 10.** But if they think, as they said in the conference, that no one can justly persecute anyone—that the true Church is that which suffers persecution, not that which does it—I omit to say what I recalled above, that, if it is as they say, Caecilian belonged to the true Church, when their elders were persecuting him by accusing even up to the judgment of the emperor. For we say that he belonged to the true Church, not because he suffered persecution, but because he suffered for righteousness; but that they were alienated from the Church, not because they persecuted, but because they persecuted unjustly: this, then, is what we say. But if they do not inquire into the causes why anyone does persecution, or why he suffers it, but think this to be the sign of a true Christian, if he does not do persecution but suffers it; without doubt they place Caecilian within that definition, who did not do it, but suffered it: but their own elders they cast outside that definition, who did it, did not suffer it.

**2. 11.** But this, as I said, I omit; this I say: if the true Church is that which suffers persecution, not that which does it; let them ask of the Apostle what Church Sarah signified, when she did persecution to her handmaid. For he says that our free mother, the heavenly Jerusalem, that is, the true Church of God, was figured in that woman who afflicted the handmaid. But if we discuss it better, she rather persecuted Sarah by being proud, than Sarah her by restraining: for the one did injury to her mistress, the other imposed discipline upon the proud one. Then I ask, if the good and holy do persecution to no one, but only suffer it, whose do they think is the voice in the Psalm where it is read: *I will pursue my enemies, and overtake them, and I will not turn back until they fail*[^17]? If, then, we wish to speak or acknowledge the truth, there is an unjust persecution which the impious do to the Church of Christ; and there is a just persecution which the Church of Christ does to the impious. This, then, is blessed, which suffers persecution for righteousness; but those wretched, who suffer persecution for unrighteousness. Accordingly, this persecutes by loving, those by raging; this that it may correct, those that they may overthrow; this that it may recall from error, those that they may hurl into error: finally, this persecutes its enemies and overtakes them, until they fail in vanity, that they may advance in truth; but those, repaying evil for good, because we take counsel for them toward eternal salvation, try to take away even the temporal from us, so loving murders that they perpetrate them on themselves, when they cannot perpetrate them on others. For as the love of the Church labors so to free them from that perdition, that none of them die; so their fury labors either to kill us, that it may feed the lust of its cruelty, or even themselves, lest they seem to have lost the power of killing men.

**3. 12.** But those who do not know their custom think that they now kill themselves only when, by the occasion of these laws which were established for unity, such great peoples are freed from their most insane domination. But those who know what they were wont to do even before these laws do not wonder at their deaths, but recall their ways; especially when, while the worship of idols still existed, they came in enormous throngs to the most frequented solemnities of the Pagans, not to break the idols, but to be killed by the worshipers of idols. For if they had wished to do that with legitimate power received, if anything had befallen them, they could have had some kind of shadow of the name of martyrs; but they came for this alone, that, the idols left intact, they themselves might be slain: for individual most vigorous young worshipers of idols were wont to vow to those very idols how many each would kill. Some too thrust themselves to be slaughtered upon armed travelers, terribly threatening that they would strike them, unless they were slain by them. Sometimes they violently extorted from judges passing by, that they be struck by the executioners or by the staff. Whence a certain one is reported to have so mocked them, that he ordered them to be bound as if to be struck, and let go, and so escaped their onset bloodless and unharmed. And then to kill themselves over steep precipices, through waters and flames, was a daily sport to them. For the devil taught them these three kinds of death, so that, wishing to die, when they did not find anyone whom they might terrify, that they be struck by his sword, they would cast themselves over rocks, or give themselves to fires and whirlpools. But who is to be believed to have taught them these things, possessing their heart, except he who suggested to our Savior too, as it were from the Law, that he cast himself down from the pinnacle of the temple? Whose suggestion they would surely ward off from themselves, if they carried Christ the teacher in their heart. But because they have rather given place in themselves to the devil, either they perish as that herd of swine which the throng of demons cast from the mountain into the sea; or, snatched from those deaths, and gathered into the pious bosom of mother Catholic, they are so freed, as was freed by the Lord that one whom his father offered to be healed of a demon, saying that he was wont sometimes to fall into water, sometimes into fire.

**3. 13.** Whence great mercy is shown to them, when even through these laws of the emperors they are first snatched, unwilling, from that sect, where through the doctrines of lying demons they learned these evils, so that afterward in the Catholic Church they may be healed, accustomed to good precepts and morals. For many of them, whose pious fervor of faith and love we now marvel at in the unity of Christ, with great gladness give thanks to God that they lacked that error, where they thought these evil things good: which thanks they would now, willing, not give, unless they had first, even unwilling, departed from that wicked society. What shall we say of those who daily confess to us that they had long ago wished to be catholics; but they dwelt among those among whom they could not be what they wished, through the weakness of fear, where, if they should say one word for the Catholic Church, both they and their houses would be utterly overthrown? Who is so demented as to deny that aid ought to have been brought to these by imperial commands, that they might be rescued from so great an evil, while those whom they feared are compelled to fear, and by that same fear are either themselves corrected, or at least, when they feign themselves corrected, spare the corrected, by whom they were before feared?

**3. 14.** But if they wish to kill themselves, lest those who are to be freed be freed, and in that way to terrify the piety of those freeing, so that, while it is feared lest certain lost ones perish, those be not rescued from perdition who either now were unwilling to perish, or, coerced, could not perish; what does Christian love do here, especially since those who threaten their voluntary and furious deaths are very few in comparison with the peoples to be freed? What, then, does brotherly affection do? whether, while for a few it fears the transitory fires of furnaces, it sends all away to the eternal fires of Gehenna; and leaves so many, either now willing, or afterward unable, to come through catholic peace to perpetual life, to everlasting destruction, by guarding lest certain ones die by a voluntary destruction, who live to the hindrance of the salvation of others, whom they do not permit to live according to the doctrine of Christ, that they may teach them at any time, according to the custom of diabolical doctrine, to hasten to those voluntary deaths which are now feared in them? or does it rather preserve those whom it can, even if those whom it cannot preserve perish of their own accord? For it ardently longs that all may live; but it labors more that all may not perish. But thanks to the Lord, that among us too—not indeed in all, but very much in most places, and through other parts of Africa—without any deaths of these insane ones, the catholic peace has run and runs. But those deadly things happen there where there is so furious and useless a kind of men, who at other times too were wont to do these same things.

**4. 15.** And indeed, before these laws were sent by the catholic emperors, the doctrine of the peace and unity of Christ was gradually growing, and each was passing over to it from that party as he learned, and wished, and was able; while, nevertheless, among them the most demented herds of lost men were disturbing the quiet of the innocent in various causes. What master was not compelled to fear his own slave, if he fled to their patronage? Who dared even to threaten an overturner? or a creditor? Who could exact anything from a consumer of storehouses, from any debtor whatever, who sought their help and defense? By fear of cudgels and burnings and present death, the documents of the worst slaves were broken, that they might depart free. The bonds extorted from debtors were given back. Whoever had despised their hard words were compelled, by harder blows, to do what they commanded. The houses of innocent men who had offended them were either leveled to the ground, or burned with fires. Certain heads of households, born in an honorable position, and brought up in a noble manner, were carried off scarcely alive after their beatings, or bound to the mill, and driven by the lash to turn it in a circle, like contemptible beasts. For what help of the laws from the civil powers availed anything against them? what official panted in their presence? what tax-collector exacted what they did not wish? who tried to avenge those who were extinguished by their slaughters, except that their own insanity demanded the penalties for them, when some, by provoking against themselves the swords of men whom they terrified with death that they might be struck by them, others through various precipices, others through waters, others through fires, cast themselves everywhere into voluntary deaths, and threw away their savage souls by the torments inflicted on themselves by themselves?

**4. 16.** Very many, set in that very heretical superstition, shuddered at these things; and although they thought it sufficed for their innocence that such things displeased them, it was said to them by the catholics: "If these evils do not pollute your innocence, how do you say that the Christian world is polluted by the false, or at least unknown, sins of Caecilian? how do you, by a wicked crime, separate yourselves from catholic unity, as if from the Lord's threshing floor, which until the time of winnowing must necessarily have both wheat to be stored in the granary, and chaff to be consumed by fires?" And so the account was rendered to certain ones, so that some, prepared to endure even the enmities of the lost, passed over to catholic unity; but more, although they wished it, yet did not dare to make enemies of those men, to whom there had been so great a license of raging: for some, when they had passed over to us, suffered most cruel things.

**4. 17.** It also happened that at Carthage, against a certain deacon of theirs named Maximian, who was being proud against his bishop, certain bishops of that same party, a schism having been made and the party of Donatus divided among the people of Carthage, ordained a bishop against a bishop. Which, when it displeased many of them, they condemned that same Maximian with twelve others who had been present at his ordination, but to the rest belonging to that same society of schism they gave, on an appointed day, the faculty of returning. But afterward, certain ones of those twelve, and of those to whom the delay had been given, returning after the day set, they received in their own honors, for the sake of their peace; and whomever the condemned had baptized outside their communion, they did not dare to rebaptize. This deed of theirs began to avail much against them for the Catholic Church, so that their mouths were utterly closed. Which matter, when (as was fitting) it was scattered more insistently for healing the minds of men from schism, and was shown wherever it could be by the discourses and disputations of catholics—that for the peace of Donatus they had received their own condemned ones whole into their honor, and had not dared to rescind the baptism which the condemned, or even the deferred, had given outside their Church; and that they cast upon the world, against the peace of Christ, the contamination of I know not whose sins, and made void the baptism given even in those Churches from which the Gospel itself came to Africa: very many were confounded, and, blushing at the manifest truth, were corrected, more frequently than usual, and much more wherever some little liberty breathed again from their savagery.

**4. 18.** But then they so blazed up, and were incited by such goads of hatreds, that against their snares and violences and most open robberies scarcely any Churches of our communion could be secure, scarcely any road safe by which any might journey who against their rage preached the catholic peace, and convicted their dementia by perspicuous truth. To such a degree, indeed, was a hard condition set, in a way, not only before laymen, or any clerics, but even before the catholic bishops themselves. For either the truth had to be kept silent, or their savagery endured. But if the truth were kept silent, not only would no one be freed by its silence, but many would even be lost by their seduction: but if by the preaching of the truth their fury were provoked to rage, some being freed and ours strengthened, again fear would hinder the weak from following the truth. Since, then, the Church was afflicted by these straits, whoever thinks that all things should rather be endured than that the help of God should be sought, that it be brought through Christian emperors, attends too little to the fact that a good account could not be rendered of this negligence.

**5. 19.** For as to what those say who do not wish just laws to be established against their impieties—that the Apostles did not ask such things from the kings of the earth—they do not consider that it was then another time, and that all things are done in their own times. For what emperor then had believed in Christ, who would serve him by passing laws for piety against impiety, when that prophetic word was still being fulfilled: *Why have the nations raged, and the peoples meditated vain things? The kings of the earth stood up, and the princes gathered together as one against the Lord, and against his Christ*[^18]: but that was not yet being done which a little after is said in the same psalm: *And now, O kings, understand; be instructed, you who judge the earth. Serve the Lord in fear, and exult before him with trembling*[^19]. How, then, do kings serve the Lord in fear, except by prohibiting and punishing with religious severity the things that are done against the commands of the Lord? For one serves in one way, because he is a man; in another, because he is also a king: for because he is a man, he serves him by living faithfully; but because he is also a king, he serves by sanctioning with fitting vigor laws that command just things and prohibit the contrary. As Hezekiah served, by destroying the groves and temples of idols, and those high places which had been built against the precepts of God: as Josiah served, by doing such things himself too: as the king of the Ninevites served, by compelling the whole city to placate the Lord: as Darius served, by giving the idol into the power of Daniel to be broken, and casting his enemies to the lions: as Nebuchadnezzar served, of whom we have already spoken, by prohibiting all set in his kingdom from blaspheming God by a terrible law. In this, then, do kings serve the Lord, insofar as they are kings, when they do, for serving him, those things which they cannot do except as kings.

**5. 20.** Since, then, kings did not yet serve the Lord in the times of the Apostles, but still meditated vain things against the Lord and against his Christ, that all the things foretold by the Prophets might be fulfilled, impieties could surely not then be prohibited by laws, but rather exercised. For so the order of the times rolled on, that both the Jews killed the preachers of Christ, thinking they did a service to God, as Christ had foretold, and the Gentiles raged against the Christians, and the patience of the martyrs conquered all. But after it began to be fulfilled which is written, *And all the kings of the earth shall adore him; all nations shall serve him*[^20], who, sound in mind, would say to kings: "Do not care, in your kingdom, by whom the Church of your Lord is held or assailed; let it not pertain to you, in your kingdom, who wishes to be either religious or sacrilegious"—to whom it cannot be said: "Let it not pertain to you, in your kingdom, who wishes to be chaste, who unchaste"? For why, since free choice has been given divinely to man, are adulteries punished by laws, and sacrileges permitted? Or is it lighter not to keep faith with God by the soul, than a woman with a man? Or if those things which are committed not by contempt, but by ignorance of religion, are to be punished more mildly, are they therefore to be neglected?

**6. 21.** It would be better, indeed—who would doubt it?—for men to be led to worship God by teaching, than to be compelled by fear of punishment or by pain: but not because the former are better are the latter, who are not such, to be neglected. For it has profited many (which we have proved and prove by experiences) first to be compelled by fear or pain, that afterward they could be taught, or follow in deed what they had already learned in words. They set before us the opinion of a certain secular author, who said:  
By shame and by liberality, I believe,  
it is better to keep children than by fear.  
This indeed is true; but as those whom love directs are better, so those whom fear corrects are more. For, that answer may be made to these people from that same author, in him too they read:  
Unless compelled by evil, you know not how to do right.  
Further, the divine Scripture, both for the sake of those better ones, said: *There is no fear in love; but perfect love casts out fear*[^21]; and for the sake of these inferior ones, who are more, it says: *A hard slave will not be amended by words; for even if he understands, he will not obey*[^22]. When it said that he is not amended by words, it did not command that he be abandoned, but tacitly admonished by what he ought to be amended: otherwise it would not say, He will not be amended by words; but would only say, He will not be amended. For in another place it says that not only a slave, but even an undisciplined son, must be coerced by blows; and with great fruit: for, *You indeed*, it says, *strike him with the rod; but you will free his soul from death*[^23]; and elsewhere it says: *He who spares the staff hates his son*[^24]. For give me one who with right faith and true understanding says, with all the strength of his soul: *My soul has thirsted for the living God; when shall I come and appear before the face of God?*[^25]; and for such a one neither the fear of temporal punishments, nor of imperial laws, nor even of Gehenna is necessary, to whom it is so desirable a good to cling to God, that he not only dreads to be estranged from that felicity as a great punishment, but even bears it grievously to be deferred. But yet, before the good sons say, *We have a desire to be dissolved and to be with Christ*[^26], many are first, as it were bad slaves and in a way wicked fugitives, recalled to their Lord by the lash of temporal scourges.

**6. 22.** For who can love us more than Christ, who laid down his life for his sheep? And yet, when he had called Peter and the other Apostles by a word alone, Paul—formerly Saul, afterward the great builder of his Church, but before that a horrible devastator—he not only restrained by his voice, but even prostrated by his power, and, that he might urge him, raging in the darkness of unbelief, to desire the light of the heart, he first struck him with bodily blindness. If that punishment were not, he would not afterward be healed from it: and when, with open eyes, he saw nothing, if he had kept them safe, the Scripture would not narrate that at the imposition of the hand of Ananias, that their gaze might be opened, there fell from there as it were the scales by which they had been closed. Where is what these people are wont to cry out: "It is free to believe or not to believe? To whom did Christ apply force? whom did he compel?" Behold, they have the apostle Paul; let them acknowledge in him Christ first compelling, and afterward teaching; first striking, and afterward consoling. But it is wonderful how he who entered the Gospel compelled by bodily punishment, labored in the Gospel more than all those who were called by a word alone; and him whom a greater fear compelled to love, his perfect love cast out fear.

**6. 23.** Why, then, should the Church not compel her lost sons to return, if the lost sons compelled others to perish? Although even those whom they did not compel, but only seduced, if by terrible but healthful laws they be recalled into her bosom, the pious mother embraces more tenderly, and rejoices over them much more than over those whom she had never lost. Does it not pertain to pastoral diligence, even those sheep which have not been violently snatched, but blandly and gently seduced, and have strayed from the flock, and begun to be possessed by strangers, when found, to recall to the Lord's fold, if they should wish to resist, by the terrors of scourges, or even by pains: especially since, if they multiply by fecundity among fugitive slaves and robbers, there is more of right in that the Lord's mark is acknowledged in them; which in those whom we receive, and yet do not rebaptize, is in no way violated? For the error of the sheep is so to be corrected, that the seal of the Redeemer be not corrupted in it. For neither, if anyone be marked with the royal mark by a marked deserter, and they receive pardon, and that one return to the army, but this one begin to be in the army in which he was not yet; in either of them is that mark rescinded. Or is it not rather acknowledged in both, and approved with due honor, since it is royal? Because, then, they cannot show that the thing to which they are compelled is evil, they contend that they ought not to be compelled to the good. But we have shown Paul compelled by Christ: the Church, therefore, in compelling these people, imitates her Lord, who first waited to compel no one, that the prophetic preaching about the faith of kings and nations might be fulfilled.

**6. 24.** For hence too is not absurdly understood that apostolic sentence, where blessed Paul says: *Ready to avenge all disobedience, when your obedience shall first have been fulfilled*[^27]. Whence the Lord himself too commands the guests to his great supper first to be brought in, afterward to be compelled: for when his servants had answered him, *Lord, it is done as you commanded, and still there is room*; *Go out*, he says, *into the roads and hedges, and whomever you find, compel them to enter*[^28]. In those, then, who were first gently brought in, the prior obedience was fulfilled; but in these who are compelled, disobedience is coerced: for what is, *compel them to enter*, when it had first been said, *bring in*, and it had been answered, *It is done as you commanded, and still there is room*? If he had wished it understood that they should be compelled by the terrors of miracles, rather were many divine miracles done toward those who were first called, especially toward the Jews, of whom it was said, *The Jews seek signs*[^29]: among the Gentiles too such miracles in the times of the Apostles commended the Gospel, so that, if they were commanded to be compelled by such things, the former, as I said, would rather deservedly be believed to have been the guests compelled. Wherefore, if by the power which through the religion and faith of kings, at the time when it was due, the Church received by a divine gift, those who are found in the roads and hedges, that is, in heresies and schisms, are compelled to enter; let them not blame the fact that they are compelled, but attend to that to which they are compelled. The banquet of the Lord is the unity of the body of Christ, not only in the sacrament of the altar, but also in the bond of peace. Of these people, surely, we can most truly say that they compel no one to the good: for whomever they compel, they compel only to evil.

**7. 25.** Nevertheless, before those laws by which they are compelled to enter the holy banquet were sent into Africa, it seemed to some brothers, among whom I too was, that, although the rage of the Donatists raged everywhere, it ought not to be asked of the emperors that they command that the heresy itself not exist at all, by establishing a penalty for those who should wish to be in it; but that they should rather establish this, that those who either by speaking preached the catholic truth, or by establishing read it, should not suffer their furious violences. Which we thought could in some measure be done in this way: if the law of Theodosius of most pious memory, which he promulgated generally against all heretics—that whatever bishop or cleric of them should be found anywhere should be fined ten pounds of gold—they should confirm more expressly against the Donatists, who denied that they were heretics, in such a way that not all should be struck by that fine, but those in whose regions the catholic Church suffered any violences from their clerics, or from the Circumcellions, or from their peoples: so that, namely, after the protestation of the catholics who had suffered these things, the bishops or other ministers should be held, by the care of the magistrates, to pay the fine. For so we supposed that, they being terrified, and not daring to do any such thing, the catholic truth could freely be taught and held, so that no one should be compelled to it, but whoever wished should follow it without fear, lest we have false and simulating catholics. And although it seemed otherwise to other brothers, now graver in age, or attending to the examples of many cities and places where we saw a firm and true catholic Church, which had nevertheless there been established and strengthened by such benefits of God, while through the laws of former emperors men were compelled to catholic communion; we obtained, nevertheless, that rather what I have said should be asked of the emperors: it was decreed in our council, legates were sent to the court.

**7. 26.** But the greater mercy of God, who knew how necessary the terror of these laws and a certain medicinal trouble was for the depraved or cold minds of many, and for that hardness which cannot be amended by words, but yet can by some little severity of discipline, brought it about that our legates could not obtain what they had undertaken. For certain most grave complaints of bishops from other places had already anticipated us, who had suffered many evils from those very people, and had been driven from their own sees; especially the horrendous and incredible slaughter of Maximian, the catholic bishop of the Church of Bagai, brought it about that our legation now had nothing to do. For a law had now been promulgated, that the heresy of the Donatists of so great savagery, which seemed to be spared more cruelly than it itself raged, should not only not be permitted to be violent, but not to be at all with impunity: not, however, by capital punishment, on account of preserving Christian mildness even toward the unworthy, but by monetary fines set down, and exile established for their bishops or ministers.

**7. 27.** For the aforesaid bishop of Bagai, since, before the ordinary judge, a sentence having been spoken between the parties, he had obtained a basilica which they had invaded when it was catholic, as he stood at the altar they rushed in with a horrendous onset and cruel fury, and beat him monstrously with cudgels and weapons of every kind, and finally with the wood of that same altar, broken off; they even struck him in the groin with a dagger; by which wound the blood flowing out would have rendered him lifeless, had not their greater savagery profited him to life. For while they were dragging him, gravely wounded, over the ground, dust thrust into the gushing vein stopped the blood, by whose flowing he was going to his death. Then, when at length left by them, our people tried to carry him off with psalms; they, kindled with a more burning anger, snatched him from the hands of those carrying him, the catholics being badly beaten and put to flight, whom they surpassed in an enormous multitude, and easily terrified by raging. Thence, lifting him onto a certain tower, thinking he had now expired, when he still lived, they cast him down. But, caught by a certain soft mound, and beheld by certain ones passing by night by a lantern, recognized and gathered up, and carried to a religious house, with great care applied, after many days he was revived from that despair: yet rumor had announced him, by the crime of the Donatists, slain even across the sea; whither after he came, and his most unhoped-for life appeared, he showed by his scars, so many, so enormous, so recent, that rumor had not in vain announced him dead.

**7. 28.** He therefore sought help from the Christian emperor, not so much for the sake of avenging himself, as of protecting the Church entrusted to him. Which if he had omitted, his patience would not have been praiseworthy, but his negligence deservedly to be blamed. For the apostle Paul too was not taking counsel for his transitory life, but for the Church of God, when against those who had conspired to kill him he brought it about that their plan was betrayed to the tribune: whence it came about that an armed soldier led him down to the place to which he was to be conducted, lest he suffer their ambushes. He did not at all hesitate to implore the Roman laws too, proclaiming that he was a Roman citizen, whom it was then not permitted to afflict with stripes: likewise, lest he be handed over to the Jews desiring to kill him, he demanded the help of Caesar, a Roman prince indeed, but not a Christian. Where he sufficiently showed what the dispensers of Christ ought afterward to do, when they should find Christian emperors and the Church in danger. Hence, then, it came about that the religious and pious emperor, such causes being brought to his notice, preferred to correct utterly, by most pious laws, the error of that impiety, and to reduce to catholic unity, by terrifying and coercing, those who against Christ carried the signs of Christ, rather than only to take away the license of raging, and leave them to err and perish.

**7. 29.** But now, when these laws had come into Africa, especially those who were seeking an occasion, or feared the savagery of the raging, or were ashamed to offend their own people, immediately passed over to the Church. Many too who were held only by the custom handed down there by their parents, but had never before considered what cause the heresy itself had, and had never wished to inquire and consider, soon, when they began to attend, and to find nothing in it worthy on account of which they should suffer such great losses, were made catholics without any difficulty: for solicitude taught those whom security had made negligent. And many followed the authority and persuasion of all these preceding ones, who were less suited to understand by themselves what difference there was between the error of the Donatists and the catholic truth.

**7. 30.** So, while the true mother joyfully received great throngs of peoples into her bosom, there remained hard crowds, standing in that plague with an unhappy animosity. Of these too very many communicated by feigning, others lay hidden by their fewness. But those who feigned, by little and little growing accustomed, and hearing the preaching of the truth—especially after the conference and disputation which was held between us and their bishops at Carthage—were in great part corrected. But in certain places, where a more pertinacious and unpacified multitude prevailed, which the fewer ones could not resist whose opinion about communion was better, or the crowds, subject to the authority of a few more powerful ones, obeyed toward the bad part, it was labored somewhat longer. Of these there are some in whom it is still labored, in which labor the catholics, and especially the bishops and clerics, have suffered many horrendous and hard things, which it is long to recall; when even the eyes of certain ones were put out, and a certain bishop's hand and tongue were cut off; some too were slaughtered. I pass over the most cruel slaughters, and the plunderings of houses, through nocturnal assaults and burnings, not only of private dwellings, but even of churches; into which flames there were not lacking those who would cast even the Lord's Codices.

**7. 31.** But us, afflicted by evils of this kind, the consequent fruit consoled. For wherever these things were committed by the lost, there Christian unity advanced more fervently and more perfectly, and the Lord is more abundantly praised, who deigned to grant that his servants by their sufferings should win their brothers, and gather his sheep, dispersed by a deadly error, into the peace of eternal salvation by his blood. The Lord is powerful and merciful, whom we daily ask that he give to the rest too repentance, and that they come to their senses from the snares of the devil, by whom they are held captive according to his will: who seek nothing but whence they may calumniate us, and repay evil for good; because they do not know how to understand what mind and love we keep toward them, and how we wish to recall the straying and to find the lost, according to the precept of the Lord, which he gave to the shepherds through the prophet Ezekiel.

**8. 32.** But they—which we have said elsewhere too at some time—what we do to them, they do not impute to themselves; and what they do to themselves, they impute to us. For who of us would wish not only that any of them perish, but even that they lose anything? But if the house of David could not otherwise deserve to have peace, except that Absalom his son were extinguished in the war which he was waging against his father—although with great care he commanded his men to keep him alive and safe as much as they could, that there might be one whom paternal affection might pardon when penitent—what remained for him but to weep for the lost one, and to console his sorrow by the peace acquired for his kingdom? So, then, the catholic mother—when those wage war against her who are none other than her sons, since surely from that same great tree which by the extension of its branches is diffused throughout the whole world this little branch in Africa is broken off, while she is in travail with them in love that they may return to the root without which they cannot have true life—if by the perdition of some she gathers so many others, especially because these perish not, like Absalom, by the chance of war, but rather by a voluntary destruction, soothes and heals the grief of her maternal heart by the liberation of such great peoples. Whose joys in the peace of Christ, frequent gatherings, alacrities, and the crowded and cheerful assemblies for hearing and singing hymns and for receiving the word of God, and the recollection in many of them, with great grief, of past error, and the consideration, with joy, of the truth recognized, and with indignation and detestation of their lying masters, that they now know about our sacraments how false the things were that they boasted; the confessions too of many of them that they had long wished to be catholics, but did not dare among men of so great fury: if, then, you should see under one view the congregations of these peoples through very many regions of Africa, freed from that perdition; then you would say it had been of excessive cruelty, if, while it was feared lest desperate men, comparable by no estimation to the innumerable multitude of these, should be burned by their own and voluntary fires, these were left to be lost forever and tortured by everlasting fires.

**8. 33.** For if two persons dwelt in one house, which we knew most certainly was going to fall, and they were unwilling to believe us foretelling it, and persisted in remaining in it; if we could rescue them from there even unwilling, to whom we would afterward show the ruin impending over it, that they should not dare further to return under its danger, I think that, unless we did so, we would not undeservedly be judged cruel. Further, if one of them should say to us, When you enter to rescue us, I will at once slay myself; but the other should be unwilling either to go out from there, or to be rescued, but yet not dare to kill himself; what would we choose—whether to leave both to be crushed by the ruin, or, at least one being freed by our merciful effort, to let the other perish, not by our fault, but rather by his own? No one is so unhappy as not most easily to judge what ought to be done in such cases. And about two men, namely one lost and one freed, I have set forth this likeness: what, then, is to be thought about a few lost ones, and an innumerable multitude of freed peoples? For neither are those men who perish by their own will as many as the estates, hamlets, villages, fortresses, towns, cities, freed by these laws from that pestiferous and eternal destruction.

**8. 34.** But if we think more diligently about the matter of which we speak, I think that, if there were very many in a falling house, and at least one could be freed from there, and, when we tried to do this, the others should kill themselves by precipice, we would grieve about the rest, but console ourselves by the salvation of at least one; yet we would not, lest the others destroy themselves, permit all to perish with none freed. What, then, is to be judged about the work of mercy which, for the sake of obtaining eternal life and avoiding eternal punishment, we owe to bestow on men; if, for the sake of this salvation, not only temporal, but even brief, to be freed for that very brief time, true and kindly reason compels us so to come to the aid of men?

**9. 35.** But as for what they charge against us, that we covet and take away their property; would that they might become catholics, and possess in peace with us and in love not only the things they call theirs, but even ours! But they are so blinded by the desire of calumniating that they do not attend to how contrary among themselves are the things they say. They certainly say, and seem most enviously to complain to themselves, that we coerce them into our communion by the violent command of laws: which surely we would in no way do, if we wished to possess their property. What greedy man seeks a co-possessor? what man inflamed with the desire of domineering, or lifted up by the pride of domination, desires to have a partner? Let them attend to those very ones, formerly their own, now our associates and joined to us by brotherly love, how they hold their own—not only the things they had, but even ours which they did not have: which, however, if we are poor among the poor, are both ours and theirs; but if we possess privately the things which suffice us, those are not ours, but the poor's, of whom we bear in a way the stewardship, not claiming the ownership to ourselves by a damnable usurpation.

**9. 36.** Whatever, then, was possessed in the name of the Churches of the party of Donatus, the Christian emperors, by religious laws, commanded to pass over to the Catholic Church along with those same Churches. Since, then, the poor of those same Churches, who were fed by those same little possessions, are with us, let them rather, placed outside, cease to covet others' property; but let them enter into the society of unity, that we may govern equally, not only those things which they call theirs, but even those which are called ours too. For it is written: *All things are yours; but you are Christ's; and Christ is God's*[^30]. Under that head, in his one body, let us be one, and of these such things let us do what is written in the Acts of the Apostles: *They had one soul and one heart; and no one called anything his own, but all things were common to them*[^31]. Let us love what we sing: *Behold, how good and how pleasant it is for brothers to dwell together in one!*[^32] so that they may thus experience and know how truly the catholic mother cries to them what blessed Paul writes to the Corinthians: *I do not seek the things that are yours, but you*[^33].

**9. 37.** But if we consider what was said in the book of Wisdom: *Therefore the just took the spoils of the impious*[^34]; likewise what is read in Proverbs: *But the riches of the impious are laid up as treasure for the just*[^35]; then we shall see that it is not to be asked who has the property of heretics, but who is in the society of the just. We know indeed that they arrogate to themselves so great a righteousness that they boast not only to have it, but even to give it to other men. For they say that one whom they have baptized is justified by them; where nothing remains for them but to say to him who is baptized by them, that he believe in his own baptizer: for why should he not do so, when the Apostle says: *To one who believes in him who justifies the impious, his faith is reckoned unto righteousness*[^36]? Let him, then, believe in him, if he himself justifies him, that his faith may be reckoned unto righteousness. But I think that they themselves too shudder at themselves, if indeed they deign even to consider these things. For there is none just and justifying, except God. But of these too can be said what the Apostle said of the Jews, that, *being ignorant of God's righteousness, and wishing to establish their own, they have not submitted to the righteousness of God*[^37].

**9. 38.** But far be it that any of us should so call himself just, that he should either wish to establish his own righteousness, that is, as if given to himself by himself—when it is said to him: *For what have you that you did not receive?*[^38]—or dare to boast himself without sin in this life, as they themselves said in our conference, that they are in that Church which now does not have spot or wrinkle or any such thing: not knowing that this is fulfilled in those of them who either immediately after baptism, or with the debts forgiven which are asked to be forgiven in the prayer, go out from this body; but that in the whole Church it will then come to pass, that it should be altogether without spot or wrinkle or any such thing, when it shall have to be said: *Where, O death, is your victory? Where, O death, is your sting? For the sting of death is sin*[^39].

**9. 39.** But in this life, where the body which is corrupted weighs down the soul, if their Church is already such, then let them not say to God what the Lord taught us to pray: *Forgive us our debts*. For since in baptism all things have been forgiven, why does the Church ask this, if even in this life it does not have spot, or wrinkle, or any such thing? Let them despise also the apostle John crying out in his Epistle: *If we say that we have no sin, we deceive ourselves, and the truth is not in us. But if we confess our sins, he is faithful and just, to forgive us our sins, and to cleanse us from all iniquity*[^40]. On account of this hope the whole Church says: *Forgive us our debts*, that, not being proud, but confessing, he may cleanse us from all iniquity, and so the Lord Christ may present to himself in that day a glorious Church, not having spot, or wrinkle, or any such thing; which he now cleanses by the washing of water in the word: because neither does anything remain in baptism which is not forgiven of all past sins (provided, however, that baptism itself be not held in vain outside, but either be given within, or, if it has already been given outside, one do not remain outside with it); and whatever, by those who live here after baptism received, of any faults whatever is contracted by human weakness, is forgiven on account of that very washing. For it profits nothing to say to one not baptized: *Forgive us our debts*.

**9. 40.** So now he cleanses his Church by the washing of water in the word, that he may then present it to himself not having spot or wrinkle, or any such thing, namely wholly beautiful and perfect, when death shall be swallowed up in victory. Now, then, insofar as that flourishes in us by which we are born of God, living by faith, we are just: but insofar as we draw from Adam the remnants of mortality, we are not without sin. For both this is true: *He who is born of God does not sin*[^41]; and this is true: *If we say that we have no sin, we deceive ourselves, and the truth is not in us*[^42]. The Lord Christ, therefore, is both just, and justifying; but we are justified freely by his grace. But he justifies none but his body, which is the Church: and therefore, if the body of Christ takes the spoils of the impious, and the riches of the impious are laid up as treasure for the body of Christ, the impious ought not to remain outside, that they may calumniate, but rather to enter, that they may be justified.

**9. 41.** Whence also that which is written about the day of judgment: *Then shall the just stand in great constancy against those who afflicted them, and took away their labors*[^43], is surely not so to be understood that the Canaanite shall stand against Israel, since the Canaanite took away the labors of Israel; but Naboth shall stand against Ahab, since Ahab took away the labors of Naboth: for the Canaanite is impious, Naboth just. In the same way, the pagan shall not stand against the Christian who took away his labors, when the temples of idols were despoiled or demolished; but the Christian shall stand against the pagan who took away his labors, when the bodies of the martyrs were laid low. So, then, the heretic shall not stand against the catholic who took away his labors, when the laws of catholic emperors prevailed; but the catholic shall stand against the heretic who took away his labors, when the furies of the impious Circumcellions prevailed. For the Scripture itself resolves the question, which does not say: "Then shall men stand," but: *Then shall the just stand*: and therefore in great constancy, because in a good conscience.

**9. 42.** But no one here is just by his own righteousness, that is, as if made for himself by himself; but, as the Apostle says: *As to each one God has divided a measure of faith*. And he follows on and adds: *For as in one body we have many members, but all the members do not have the same function; so we, being many, are one body in Christ*[^44]. And through this, no one can be just, as long as he is separated from the unity of this body. For just as a member, if it be cut off from the body of a living man, cannot keep the spirit of life; so a man who is cut off from the body of Christ the just can in no way keep the spirit of righteousness, even if he keep the figure of a member, which he took up in the body. Into the framework, then, of this body let them come, and let them possess their labors not by the desire of domineering, but by the piety of using them well. But we purge our will, as has already been said, from the filth of this desire, by whatever enemy judging, when we inquire after those very ones whose labors are spoken of, as much as we can, that they may use, with us, both their own and ours, in catholic society.

**10. 43.** But this, they say, is what moves us: if we are unjust, why do you seek us? To whom we answer: We seek you unjust, that you may not remain unjust; we seek the lost, that we may be able to rejoice over the found, saying: *The brother was dead, and is alive again; he was lost, and is found*[^45]. Why, then, he says, do you not baptize me, that you may wash me from sins? I answer: Because I do not do injury to the mark of the emperor, when I correct the error of the deserter. Why, he says, do I not even do penance before you? Nay, unless you do, you cannot be saved: for how will you rejoice that you are corrected, unless you grieve that you were perverse? What, then, he says, do we receive among you, when we pass over to you? I answer: You do not, indeed, receive baptism, which could be in you outside the framework of the body of Christ, but could not profit; but you receive the unity of the Spirit in the bond of peace, without which no one will be able to see God, and love, which, as it is written, *covers a multitude of sins*[^46]. Which so great a good—without which the Apostle testifies that neither the tongues of men and angels, nor the knowledge of all sacraments, nor prophecy, nor so great faith that mountains be moved, nor all that a man possesses distributed to the poor, nor the suffering of the body in fires, profit anything: this so great a good, then, if you think it small or nothing, you deservedly err unhappily; you deservedly, if you do not pass over into catholic unity, perish.

**10. 44.** If, then, they say, it behooves us to repent of having been outside the Church and against the Church, that we may be able to be saved; how do we, after that penance, remain among you clerics, or even bishops? This would not be done—since in truth, which must be confessed, it ought not to be done—were it not healed by the compensation of peace itself. But let those say this to themselves, and much rather humbly grieve, who lie in so great a death of cutting-off, that by this as it were wound of the mother Catholic they may revive. For when a cut-off branch is grafted, another wound is made in the tree, by which it can be received, that it may live which without the life of the root was perishing: but when, received, it has coalesced with the receiver, both vigor and fruit follow; but if it has not coalesced, that branch indeed withers, but the life of the tree will remain. For there is also such a kind of grafting, that, no branch which is within being cut off, that which is outside is grafted in, yet not with no wound, but with even a very slight wound of the tree. So, then, these too, when they come to the catholic root, and the honor of clerical office or episcopate is not taken from them, even after the penance of their error, something is indeed done as it were in the bark of the mother tree against the integrity of severity: yet because *neither he who plants is anything, nor he who waters*[^47], prayers being poured out to the mercy of God, the peace of the grafted branches coalescing, love covers a multitude of sins.

**10. 45.** For that it was established in the Church, that no one after the penance of any crime should receive clerical office, or return to clerical office, or remain in clerical office, was done not by despair of pardon, but by the rigor of discipline: otherwise it will be disputed against the keys given to the Church, of which it was said: *Whatever you loose on earth shall be loosed also in heaven*[^48]. But lest perhaps, even crimes being detected, the mind, swelling with the hope of ecclesiastical honor, should proudly do penance, it was most severely resolved that, after penance done for a damnable crime, no one be a cleric, so that, by the despair of temporal elevation, the medicine of humility might be greater and truer. For holy David too did penance for deadly crimes, and yet stood firm in his honor. And blessed Peter, when he poured out the most bitter tears, surely repented of having denied the Lord, and yet remained an Apostle. But not on that account is the diligence of later men to be thought superfluous, who, where nothing was taken from salvation, added something to humility, by which salvation might be more safely fortified, having experienced, I believe, the feigned penances of certain ones through affected powers of honors. For the experiences of many diseases compel many medicines to be found. But in causes of this kind, where through grave rents of dissensions it is not the danger of this or that man, but the slaughters of peoples lie, something must be taken from severity, that sincere love may come to the aid of healing greater evils.

**10. 46.** Let these, then, have, about their past detestable error, as Peter had about his lying fear, a bitter grief, and let them come to the true Church of Christ, that is, the mother Catholic: let them be in her clerics, let them be bishops usefully, who against her were hostilely. We do not envy, nay rather we embrace, we wish it, we exhort, and those whom we find in the roads and hedges, we compel to enter. And so we persuade some who do not yet believe that we seek not their property, but themselves. The apostle Peter, when he denied the Savior, and wept, and remained an Apostle, had not yet received the promised Holy Spirit: but much more these did not receive him, where, divided from the framework of the body—which body alone the Holy Spirit gives life to—they held the sacraments of the Church outside the Church and against the Church, and fought, as it were in a civil war, with our standards and arms raised against us. Let them come; let there be peace in the strength of Jerusalem, which strength is love: to which holy city it was said, *Let there be peace in your strength, and abundance in your towers*[^49]. Let them not lift themselves up against the maternal solicitude, which she both had and has for them, and for the gathering of so great peoples whom they deceive or were deceiving: let them not be proud because she so receives them; let them not refer to the evil of their own elation that which she does for the good of peace.

**10. 47.** Thus she has been accustomed to come to the aid of multitudes perishing through schisms and heresies. This displeased Lucifer, because it was done in receiving and healing those who had perished by the Arian poison; and he to whom it displeased fell into the darkness of schism, the light of love being lost. This toward these people the African Catholic Church kept from the beginning, by the sentence of the bishops who in the Roman Church judged between Caecilian and the party of Donatus, and, one certain Donatus being condemned, who was shown to have been the author of the schism, judged that the rest, corrected, even if they had been ordained outside the Church, should be received in their honors: not because even outside the unity of the body of Christ they could have the Holy Spirit; but chiefly on account of those whom, placed outside, they could deceive, and hinder from the reception of that gift; then that even their own weakness, more gently received within, might become curable, no pertinacity now closing the eyes of the heart against the evidence of truth. But what else did they themselves too think, when the Maximianists, whom they had condemned in a sacrilegious schism, as their council indicates, and into whose place they had already ordained others, after they saw that the peoples did not depart from them, lest they lose all, they received in their honors; and to the baptism which the condemned, outside, had given them, raised no contradiction or question? Why, then, do they wonder, or complain, or calumniate, that we so receive them for the true peace of Christ, and do not recall what they themselves did for the vain peace of Donatus, which is against Christ? Which deed of theirs, if it be held against them and intelligently asserted, they will have nothing at all to answer.

**11. 48.** But as for what they say: "If we have sinned against the Holy Spirit, because we blew upon your baptism, why do you seek us, since this sin can in no way be forgiven us, the Lord saying: *Whoever sins against the Holy Spirit, it will not be forgiven him, neither in this world, nor in the world to come*[^50]?"—they do not attend that, according to this sense, no one is to be freed. For who does not speak against the Holy Spirit and sin against him—whether one who is not yet a Christian, or one who is an Arian heretic, or a Eunomian, or a Macedonian, who say that he is a creature; or a Photinian, who denies that there is any substance of his at all, but that there is only one God the Father; or other heretics, whom it is long to recall? Are none of these, then, to be freed? Or were the Jews themselves, to whom the Lord cast this, if they had believed in him, not to be baptized? For the Savior did not say: "In baptism it will be forgiven"; but, *it will not be forgiven*, he says, *neither in this world, nor in the world to come*.

**11. 49.** Let them understand, then, that not every sin against the Holy Spirit is signified, which will in no way be forgiven, but some particular one. For just as, when he said, *If I had not come, they would not have sin*[^51], he surely did not wish every sin to be understood, since they were full of many and great sins; but some particular sin, which, if they did not have it, all that they had could be forgiven them—that is, that they did not believe in him when he came; for this sin they would not have, if he had not come: so too, when he said, *Whoever sins against the Holy Spirit*, or, *Whoever speaks a word against the Holy Spirit*[^52], he did not wish every sin against the Holy Spirit by deed or word to be understood, but some certain and particular one. But this is hardness of heart even to the end of this life, by which a man refuses, in the unity of the body of Christ which the Holy Spirit gives life to, to receive the remission of sins. For when he had said to the disciples, *Receive the Holy Spirit*, he immediately added: *If you forgive anyone his sins, they will be forgiven him; if you retain anyone's, they will be retained*[^53]. Whoever, then, resists and fights against this gift of the grace of God, or in any way is estranged from it up to the end of this temporal life, it will not be forgiven him, neither in this world nor in the world to come; this, namely, so great a sin, that by it all sins are held, which is not proved to have been committed by anyone except when he has gone out of the body. But as long as he lives here, the patience of God, as the Apostle says, leads him to repentance: but if he himself, by a most persevering iniquity, as the Apostle consequently adds, *according to the hardness of his heart and his impenitent heart, treasures up for himself wrath in the day of wrath and of the revelation of the just judgment of God*[^54], it will not be forgiven him, neither in this world nor in the world to come.

**11. 50.** But these people with whom we deal, or about whom we deal, are not to be despaired of; for they are still in the body: but let them not seek the Holy Spirit except in the body of Christ, whose sacrament they have outside, but they do not hold within the very thing whose that sacrament is; and therefore they eat and drink judgment to themselves. For the one bread is the sacrament of unity, since, as the Apostle says, *One bread, one body, we many are*[^55]. Accordingly the catholic Church alone is the body of Christ, of which he the Savior is the head of his own body. Outside this body the Holy Spirit gives life to no one; because, as the Apostle himself says, *The love of God is poured out in our hearts by the Holy Spirit who has been given to us*[^56]: but he is not a partaker of the divine love who is an enemy of unity. They do not, therefore, have the Holy Spirit who are outside the Church: for of them it is written, *Those who segregate themselves, animal, not having the Spirit*[^57]. But neither does he receive him who is feigned in the Church, since of that too it is written, *For the Holy Spirit of discipline will flee the feigned*[^58]. Whoever, then, wishes to have the Holy Spirit, let him beware of remaining outside the Church, let him beware of entering it as a pretender: or if he has already entered such, let him beware of persisting in that same pretense, that he may truly coalesce with the tree of life.

**11. 51.** I have directed to you a lengthy book, and perhaps burdensome to your occupations. If, then, it can be read to you, even in parts, the Lord will give you understanding, that you may have what to answer to those to be corrected and healed, whom mother Church commends, even to you yourself as to a faithful son, to be corrected and healed, where you can and how you can, whether by yourself speaking and answering, or by leading them to the teachers of the Church, with the help of the Lord.
