# Letter 153

AUGUSTINE, BISHOP, SERVANT OF CHRIST AND OF HIS FAMILY, TO HIS BELOVED SON MACEDONIUS, GREETINGS IN THE LORD.

**1. 1.** A man most busy in the commonwealth, and most attentive not to his own advantages but to those of others—such as we congratulate both you and human affairs that you are—we ought neither to abandon without an address, nor to occupy with a preface. Receive, then, what through me you either wished to know, or to test whether I myself knew. Which, if you judged it small or superfluous, you would in no way think it should be cared for among your so great and so necessary cares. You ask me why we say that it is the office of our priesthood to intervene for the accused, and, unless we obtain it, to be offended, as though we brought back nothing of what was our office. Whereupon you say you vehemently doubt whether this descends from religion. Then you add by what reasoning you are so moved: For if sins, you say, are so forbidden by the Lord that not even the opportunity of repenting is granted after the first; how can we contend from religion that, whatever that crime may have been, it should be remitted? You urge still more grievously, and say that we approve what we wish to be unpunished; and if it is agreed that the author no less than the approver is held guilty in all things that are sinned, that it is certain we are bound by fellowship in the fault, as often as we wish him to be unpunished who is held liable to the fault.

**1. 2.** Whom would you not terrify by these words, were he ignorant of your gentleness and humanity? Whence we who know you, and do not doubt at all that you wrote these things for the sake of asking, not of deciding, answer them the more quickly by other words of yours. For as though you were unwilling that we should hesitate in this question, either you foresaw what we were going to say, or you admonished what we ought to say; and you said: Then, besides, there is added this, which is more grievous. For all sins seem more pardonable when he who is accused promises correction. Before, then, I discuss what that more grievous thing is which follows in your letter, let me meanwhile take this which you have granted, and use it to remove this mass by which our intercessions seemed able to be suppressed. For we therefore intercede, as far as opportunity is given, for all sins, because "all sins seem more pardonable when he who is accused promises correction." This is your view, and this is ours too.

**1. 3.** In no way, then, do we approve the faults which we wish to be corrected, nor do we wish what is wrongly committed to be unpunished because it pleases us; but pitying the man, while detesting the deed or the disgrace, the more the vice displeases us, the less we wish the vicious man to perish unamended. For it is easy and downhill to hate the evil because they are evil; but rare and devout to love those same ones because they are men; so that in one and the same you both disapprove the fault and approve the nature, and therefore hate the fault the more justly, because by it the nature you love is defiled. He, then, is bound not by fellowship of iniquity, but rather of humanity, who is for this reason a persecutor of the crime, that he may be the liberator of the man. Further, there is no other place for correcting morals than in this life; for after this, each will have that which he has gained for himself in this. Therefore we are compelled by love of the human race to intervene for the accused, lest they so end this life through punishment that, this life being ended, they cannot end their punishment.

**2. 4.** Do not doubt, then, that this office of ours descends from religion, since God, with whom there is no iniquity, whose power is supreme, who not only sees what sort each is, but even foresees what sort he is going to be, who alone can not stumble in judging, because he cannot be deceived in knowing, yet, as the Gospel says, *makes his sun rise upon the good and the evil, and rains upon the just and the unjust*[^1]. That we may be imitators of this marvelous goodness, the Lord Christ, exhorting, says: *Love your enemies; do good to those who hate you; and pray for those who persecute you: that you may be sons of your Father who is in heaven, who makes his sun rise upon the good and the evil, and rains upon the just and the unjust*[^2]. Who does not know that many abuse this divine indulgence and gentleness unto their own ruin? Reproaching and gravely rebuking whom, the Apostle says: *But do you suppose, O man, every one who judges those who do such things and does them, that you will escape the judgment of God? Or do you despise the riches of his kindness, and patience, and long-suffering? Do you not know that the kindness of God leads you to repentance? But according to your hardness and impenitent heart you store up for yourself wrath in the day of wrath and of the revelation of the just judgment of God, who will render to each according to his works*[^3]. Is it that, because these persist in their wickedness, God therefore does not persist in his patience—punishing a few things in this age, lest divine providence be believed not to exist, and reserving many for the final examination, that the judgment to come may be commended?

**2. 5.** That heavenly master, I think, does not command us to love impiety, when he commands that we love our enemies, do good to those who hate us, pray for those who persecute us; since without doubt, if we worship God devoutly, we cannot have enemies, and those incited against us with the keenest hatreds, and persecutors, except the impious. Are the impious, then, to be loved? Is good to be done to the impious? Is one even to pray for the impious? Yes, plainly. For he who commands this is God; nor yet does he thereby join us to the fellowship of the impious, with whom he himself, by sparing them and bestowing life and salvation, is surely not joined. For his counsel, as far as it is granted a devout man to know, the Apostle sets forth, saying: *Do you not know that the patience of God leads you to repentance?*[^4] To this we wish those for whom we intervene to be led; we neither spare nor favor their sins.

**3. 6.** For certain ones whose crimes are manifest, though freed from your severity, we nonetheless remove from the fellowship of the altar, that by repenting they may be able to appease him whom by sinning they had despised, by punishing their very selves. For he who truly repents does nothing else than not allow what he did evilly to be unpunished: for in this way he who does not spare himself is spared by him whose deep and just judgment no despiser escapes. But if he, by sparing the unjust and wicked, and bestowing on them life and salvation, shows patience even to very many of them whom he knows will not perform repentance, how much more ought we to be merciful toward those who promise correction, and of whom we are uncertain whether they will do what they promise, so that by interceding we may bend your rigor for those for whom we also pray to the Lord—from whom nothing of their morals, even future ones, lies hidden—yet not shamelessly, because he himself commanded this!

**3. 7.** But sometimes the iniquity of men goes so far that even after repentance has been done, after reconciliation at the altar, they commit either like or graver things: and yet God makes his sun rise even upon such, nor bestows less than before he bestowed the most ample gifts of life and salvation. And although that place of repentance is not granted them in the Church, yet God does not forget his patience toward them. If anyone of their number should say to us, Either grant me again that same place of repenting, or permit me, despaired of, to do whatever pleases me, as far as I am aided by my means and not forbidden by human laws, in harlotries and every self-indulgence—damnable indeed before the Lord, but even praiseworthy before very many men; or if you call me back from this wickedness, tell me whether it profits me anything for the life to come, if in this life I despise the enticements of most alluring pleasure, if I bridle the incitements of lusts, if for chastising my body I withdraw from myself many even lawful and permitted things, if by repenting I torment myself more vehemently than before, if I groan more miserably, if I weep more abundantly, if I live better, if I support the poor more generously, if I burn more ardently with love, which *covers a multitude of sins*[^5]—who of us is so foolish as to say to this man, These things will profit you nothing hereafter; go, at least enjoy the sweetness of this life? May God avert so monstrous and sacrilegious a madness! Although, then, it has been cautiously and healthfully provided that the place of that most humble repentance be granted once in the Church, lest a cheap medicine should be less useful to the sick, which is the more healthful the less contemptible it has been—yet who would dare to say to God: Why do you still again spare this man, who after the first repentance again binds himself in the snares of iniquity? Who would dare to say that toward these there is not done what the Apostle says: *Do you not know that the patience of God leads you to repentance?* Or that these are excepted from what is written: *Blessed are all who trust in him*[^6]? Or that to these does not pertain what is said: *Act manfully, and let your heart be strengthened, all you who hope in the Lord*[^7]?

**3. 8.** Since, then, there is so great patience, so great mercy of God toward sinners, that in this temporal life, their morals being amended, they are not damned eternally—he who awaits the mercy of no one shown to him, since no one is more blessed than he, no one more powerful, no one more just—what sort ought men not to be toward men, we who, however much we heap up this life of ours with praise, do not say it is without sin? And if we say it, we deceive ourselves, as it is written, and the truth is not in us[^8]. Accordingly, although the person of the accuser is one, of the defender another, of the intercessor another, of the judge another—of whose proper offices it is too long, and not necessary, to discourse in this discourse—yet the divine censure has so terrified even those very avengers of crimes, who in this office are stirred not by their own anger but are ministers of the laws, and avengers not of their own but of others' examined injuries—what sort of judges they ought to be—that they should reflect that for their own sins the mercy of God is necessary to them, and should not think it pertains to the fault of their office, if they should act mercifully toward those over whose life and death they have lawful power.

**4. 9.** For when the Jews had brought to the Lord Christ a woman caught in adultery, and, tempting him, said that she had been ordered by the Law to be stoned, and then asked what he himself commanded about her, he answered them: *Let him of you who is without sin first cast a stone at her*[^9]. Thus he both did not disapprove the Law, which ordered such accused women to be slain, and by terrifying recalled to mercy those by whose judgment she could be slain. I think that, when this sentence of the Lord was heard, if the husband himself too was present, who demanded that the violated faith of his bed be avenged, terrified, he turned his mind from the desire of avenging to the will of sparing. For how was the accuser not admonished not to pursue his own injuries, when the very judges were so forbidden to avenge, who in punishing the adulteress were compelled to serve not private grief but the Law? Whence Joseph, to whom the virgin Mary, the mother of the Lord, had been betrothed, when he had found her to be pregnant—with whom he knew he had not been joined, and for this had believed her nothing other than an adulteress—yet did not wish her to be punished; nor was he an approver of the disgrace. For this will of his is even reckoned as righteousness: for thus it is written of him: *And since he was a just man, and was unwilling to expose her, he resolved to dismiss her secretly*[^10]. While he was thinking these things, an angel appeared to him to teach that it was of God's power what he had thought to be a crime.

**4. 10.** If, then, by reflecting on the common weakness both the accuser's grief and the judge's rigor are broken, what, in the end, do you judge ought to be the office, for the accused, of the defender or of the intercessor? Since you too, good men who are now judges, and once, employed in the forum, took up the causes of men, know how much more gladly you were wont to defend than to accuse. And yet the defender differs greatly from the intercessor: for the one expends his effort chiefly on dissolving or covering crimes; but the intercessor, even when the fault is established, takes care for removing or tempering the punishment. This the righteous are before God for sinners; this they are admonished to do for one another, themselves sinning: for it is written: *Confess your sins to one another, and pray for one another*[^11]. These parts of humanity, where he can, every man claims for himself with a man. For what each would punish, committed in his own house, he wishes unpunished in another's house. For whether he is brought in before a friend, or that one, in whose power vengeance lies, is vexed at someone in his presence, or he chances upon one who is angry, he is judged not most just, but most inhuman, if he does not intervene. I myself know that you, with other friends of yours, in the church of Carthage interceded for a cleric at whom the bishop was deservedly vexed; and surely no peril of blood under that unbloody discipline was feared there: nor, when you wished that what displeased even you should not be unavenged, did we judge you as approvers of the offense, but heard you as most humane intercessors. If, then, it is lawful for you to mitigate ecclesiastical correction by interceding, how should a bishop not intercede with your sword, since that one is drawn that he against whom it is drawn may live well, but this one that he may not live!

**4. 11.** Finally, the Lord himself interceded with men, that the adulteress might not be stoned, and in that way commended to us the office of intercession; except that he did by terrifying what we do by asking. For he is the Lord, we are servants: nevertheless he so terrified that we all ought to fear. For who of us is without sin? When he had said this to those by whom the sinful woman was offered to him to be punished, that whoever knew himself to be without sin should first cast a stone at her, their savagery fell, their conscience trembling: for then, having slipped away from that assembly, they left the wretched woman alone with the merciful one. Let the piety of Christians yield to this sentence, to which the impiety of the Jews yielded: let the humility of the obedient yield to it, to which the pride of the persecutors yielded: let the confession of the faithful yield to it, to which the dissimulation of the tempter yielded. Spare the evil, good man: the better you are, the gentler be; the higher you rise in power, the humbler become in piety.

**5. 12.** I indeed, regarding your morals, have called you a good man; but you, regarding the words of Christ, say to yourself: *No one is good but God alone*[^12]. Since this is true (for Truth said it), yet neither ought I to be thought to have said that by deceitful flattery, and to have stood as it were contrary to the Lord's words, so that, while he says, "No one is good but God alone," I have called you a good man: for the Lord himself too did not speak contrary to himself, where he says: *A good man out of the good treasure of his heart brings forth good things*[^13]. God, then, is singularly good, and this he cannot lose; for he is good by the participation of no good, since the good by which he is good is himself to himself: but a man, when he is good, is good from him; which he cannot be of himself. For by his Spirit they are made good, whoever are made good; of whom our nature was created capable through its own will. It pertains, then, to us, that we be good, to receive and have what he gives who is good of his own; which being neglected, each is evil of his own. Accordingly, in so far as a man acts rightly, that is, knowingly and lovingly and devoutly works good, in so far is he good; but in so far as he sins, that is, deviates from truth and love and piety, in so far is he evil. But who in this life is without any sin? But we call him good whose good things prevail; and him best who sins least.

**5. 13.** Therefore the Lord himself calls those whom he calls good on account of the participation of divine grace, the same also evil on account of the vices of human weakness; until the whole of which we consist, healed of all viciousness, passes into that life where nothing at all will be sinned. For surely he was teaching the good to pray, not the evil, when he commanded them to say: *Our Father, who are in heaven*[^14]. For from this they are good, by which they are sons of God, not begotten by nature but made by grace, as those *to whom, receiving him, he gave the power to become sons of God*[^15]. Which spiritual generation, in the manner of the Scriptures, is also called adoption, that it may be distinguished from that generation of God from God, of the coeternal from the eternal, whence it is written: *His generation who shall declare?*[^16] Since, then, he has shown those to be good whom he willed truly to say to God, "Our Father, who are in heaven," he yet commanded that in that same prayer, among other things, they say: *Forgive us our debts, as we also forgive our debtors*[^17]. That these debts are sins, although it is manifest, he yet afterward declared more manifestly, saying: *For if you forgive men their sins, your Father also will forgive you your sins*[^18]. This prayer the baptized pray, nor is there at all any of past sins which is not forgiven to those baptized in the holy Church. But afterward, living in this mortal frailty, unless they contracted what was to be forgiven, they would not truly say: Forgive us our debts. They are good, then, in so far as they are sons of God; but in so far as they sin (which they testify by their unfeigned confession), they are surely evil.

**5. 14.** Unless perhaps someone should say that the sins of the good are one thing, those of the evil another; which is not said altogether improbably. Nevertheless the Lord Jesus, without any ambiguity, called those same ones evil whose Father he said was God. For in the same sermon where he teaches that prayer, in another place, exhorting to pray to God, he says: *Ask, and you will receive; seek, and you will find; knock, and it will be opened to you. For everyone who asks receives; and he who seeks finds; and to him who knocks it will be opened*[^19]: and a little after: *If, then, you, since you are evil, know how to give good gifts to your children, how much more will your Father, who is in heaven, give good things to those who ask him?*[^20] Is God, then, the father of the evil? By no means. How, then, is he called "your heavenly Father" to those to whom it is said, "since you are evil," except because Truth shows both—what we are by the good of God, what by human vice; commending the one, amending the other? Rightly does Seneca say (who lived in the times of the Apostles, certain of whose letters to the apostle Paul are even read): "I hate all who hate the evil." And yet the evil are therefore to be loved, that they may not be evil; just as the sick are loved, not that they may remain, but that they may be healed.

**5. 15.** But whatever we sin, by remaining in this life, after that abolition of sins which is made in Baptism, even if it is not such as to compel removal from the divine altars, is expiated not by barren grief but by sacrifices of mercy. What, then, we do for you, when at our intercession you act, know that we offer to God for you. For you need the mercy which you afford; and see who has said: *Forgive, and it will be forgiven you; give, and it will be given you*[^21]. Although even if we so lived that there were no reason to say, Forgive us our debts, the purer the soul were from wickedness, the fuller of clemency it ought to be, so that, if we were not pricked by the sentence of the Lord saying, *Let him of you who is without sin first cast a stone at her*, we should follow the example of him who said this, who, since he was certainly without sin, said to the woman whom those terrified men had left: *Neither will I condemn you; go, and from now on do not sin*[^22]. For the disgraceful woman could fear lest, those departing who had reflected on their own sins so as to spare another's sin, he himself, who was without sin, might now most justly condemn her. But he, not fearful in conscience, but full of clemency, when she had answered that she had been condemned by no one, said: *Neither will I condemn you*: as though he said, If wickedness could spare you, why do you fear innocence? And lest he should seem not a granter, but an approver, of offenses, *Go*, he says, *and from now on do not sin*; that he might show that he had spared the man, not that the man's fault had pleased him. Now you see, then, that it descends from religion, and that we are not bound by fellowship of crimes, in that we intercede very often even for the wicked—though not wicked ourselves, yet sinners for sinners; and, as you may take it said truly rather than injuriously, I think, among sinners.

**6. 16.** Nor indeed have the power of the king, the right of the sword of the magistrate, the hooks of the torturer, the arms of the soldier, the discipline of the ruler, even the severity of a good father, been instituted in vain. All these things have their measures, causes, reasons, uses. When these are feared, both the evil are coerced, and the good live more quietly among the evil: not because those are to be pronounced good who do not sin by fearing such things, for no one is good by fear of punishment, but by love of righteousness; nevertheless human boldness is not uselessly coerced even by fear of laws, so that both innocence may be safe among the wicked, and in the wicked themselves, while the faculty is bridled by dreaded punishment, the will, God being called upon, may be healed. But to this ordering of human affairs the intercessions of bishops are not contrary; rather, indeed, there would be neither cause nor place for interceding, if these things were not. For the benefits of those interceding and sparing are the more welcome, the more just the punishments of those sinning. Nor for any other reason, as far as I understand, did the sterner vengeance of the Law burn hot in the Old Testament, in the times of the ancient prophets, except that it might be shown that penalties were rightly appointed for the unjust: so that the fact that we are admonished by the indulgence of the New Testament to spare them may be either a remedy of salvation, by which our sins too are spared; or a commendation of gentleness, so that through those who spare, the truth proclaimed may not only be feared but also loved.

**6. 17.** But it makes the greatest difference with what intention each one spares. For as there is sometimes a mercy that punishes, so there is also a cruelty that spares. For, to set down something manifest by way of example, who would not more truly call him cruel who spared a boy wishing most obstinately to play with serpents? But who would not call him merciful who, forbidding such things, amended the despiser of his words even with blows? And therefore discipline is not to be extended even to death, that there may be someone whom it can profit. Although even when a man is killed by a man, it makes much difference whether it is done from the desire of harming, or of unjustly taking something away, as is done by an enemy, as by a robber; or in the order of avenging or obeying, as by a judge, as by an executioner; or by the necessity of escaping or aiding, as a robber is slain by a traveler, an enemy by a soldier. And sometimes he who was the cause of death is rather in fault than he who killed; as if someone should deceive his surety, and that one should pay the lawful penalty for him. Nor yet is everyone who is the cause of another's death guilty. For what if someone should seek a defilement, and, if he did not obtain it, should kill himself? What if a son, fearing the devout blows of his father, should perish by a fall? What if another, a man being freed, or lest another be freed, should bring death upon himself? Are we, on account of these causes of others' deaths, either to consent to crime, or to abolish even paternal vengeance of sin—which is done not from the zeal of harming but of correcting—or to restrain works of mercy? When these things happen, we owe them human grief; we do not on account of them, lest they happen, repress the will of right deeds.

**6. 18.** So too, when we intercede for a sinner who is to be condemned, sometimes things follow that we do not wish; either in the very one who is freed by our intercession, so that, unpunished, boldness ranges more monstrously, subject to desire, ungrateful to gentleness, and one snatched from death slays very many; or, that one being changed for the better by our benefit and corrected in morals, another perishes by living evilly, and, the impunity of this one being set before him, commits like or graver things. These evils, I think, are not to be imputed to us, when we intercede with you: but rather those good things which, when we do this, we regard and will; that is, the commendation of gentleness for winning love by the word of truth, and that those who are freed from temporal death may so live as not to incur eternal death, from which they would never be freed.

**6. 19.** Your severity profits, then, by whose ministry our peace too is aided; our intercession profits too, by whose ministry your severity too is tempered. Let it not displease you that you are asked by the good; since neither does it displease the good that you are feared by the evil. For the apostle Paul too terrified the iniquity of men, not only with the judgment to come, but even with your present axes, asserting that these too pertain to the dispensation of divine providence. *Let every soul*, he says, *be subject to the higher powers: for there is no power but from God; and those that are, are ordained by God. Wherefore he who resists the power resists the ordinance of God; and those who resist acquire judgment for themselves: for princes are not a terror to the good work, but to the evil. Do you wish not to fear the power? Do good, and you will have praise from it: for it is God's minister to you for good. But if you do evil, fear; for it bears not the sword in vain: for it is God's minister, an avenger unto wrath to him who does evil. Therefore be subject of necessity, not only because of wrath, but also because of conscience. For this reason you pay tribute too: for they are God's ministers, persevering in this very thing. Render to all their dues: tribute to whom tribute, custom to whom custom, fear to whom fear, honor to whom honor. Owe no one anything, except to love one another*[^23]. These words of the Apostle show the usefulness of your severity. Accordingly, as those who fear are commanded to owe love to those who terrify, so those who terrify are commanded to owe love to those who fear. Let nothing be done from the desire of harming, but all things from the love of taking counsel; and nothing would be done monstrously, nothing inhumanly. Thus the vengeance of the magistrate will be dreaded, in such a way that the religion of the intercessor too is not despised; because both by punishing and by pardoning this alone is done well, that the life of men may be corrected. But if there is such perversity and impiety that neither discipline nor pardon can profit to correct it, yet by the good, in the intention and conscience which God discerns, whether by severity or by gentleness, nothing but the office of love is fulfilled.

**6. 20.** But as for what follows in your letter, where you say: But now, as our morals are, men both desire the punishment of their crime to be relaxed for them, and to possess that on account of which the crime was committed—you mention the worst kind of men, to whom the medicine of repenting is of no profit at all. For if another's property, on account of which the sin was committed, when it can be returned, is not returned, repentance is not done, but feigned: but if it is truly done, the sin will not be remitted unless what was taken is restored; but, as I said, when it can be restored. For very often he who takes loses it, either by suffering other evil men, or by living evilly himself, and has nothing else from which to restore. To this man, certainly, we cannot say, Return what you took, except when we believe that he has it and denies it. Where indeed, if he endures some torments from the one demanding, while he is supposed to have what he may return, there is no injustice; because even if there is nothing from which to pay the money taken, yet deservedly, while he is compelled to render it through bodily troubles, he pays the penalties of the sin by which it was evilly taken. But it is not inhuman to intercede even for such, as for the accused of crimes; not to this end that another's property not be restored, but that a man may not rage in vain against a man—especially that one who has already remitted the fault, but seeks the money, and, if he fears to be cheated, does not seek to be avenged. Finally, in such cases, if we can persuade them that those for whom we intervene do not have what is demanded, their troubles are at once relaxed for us. But sometimes the merciful, even in that very doubt, are unwilling to inflict certain punishments on a man for uncertain money. To this mercy it is fitting that we too provoke and exhort you: for it is better, even if he has it, to lose it, than, if he does not have it, either to torment or to kill him. But for these it is fitting to intercede rather with those who demand than with those who judge; lest he himself seem to take away who, though he has the power, does not compel restitution: although in compelling he ought so to employ integrity that he not lose humanity.

**6. 21.** But this I would say most confidently, that he who intervenes for a man to this end, that he may not restore what was evilly taken, and who does not compel one fleeing to him, as far as he honorably can, to restore, is a partner of the fraud and the crime. For we more mercifully withdraw our help from such than we expend it: for he does not bring help who aids to sinning, and does not rather subvert and oppress. But are we therefore able or bound either to exact, or to hand over for exacting? We act as far as the episcopal faculty is given, threatening sometimes indeed human, but most of all and always divine, judgment. But those unwilling to restore, whom we know both to have taken evilly and to have whence to restore, we reprove, rebuke, and detest; some secretly, some openly, as the diversity of persons seems able to receive a diverse medicine, and so as not to be stirred to greater madness unto the ruin of others. Sometimes too, if a matter more to be cared for does not hinder, we deprive them of the communion of the holy altar.

**6. 22.** But it often happens that they deceive us, either by denying that they took, or by affirming that they have nothing from which to restore: often too you too are deceived, supposing either that we do not act so that they restore, or that they have whence to restore; and all, or almost all, men love to call or esteem our suspicions knowledge, when we are moved by credible signs of things, since some credible things are false, just as some incredible things are true. Therefore, mentioning certain ones who both desire the punishment of their crime to be relaxed for them, and to possess that on account of which the crime was committed, you added and said: For these too your priesthood thinks it must intervene. For it can happen that you know what I do not know, and therefore I think it must be intervened for someone who could deceive me, though he could not deceive you, so that I do not believe him to possess what you know he possesses. Thus it comes about that, although our view about the accused is not one, yet it pleases neither of us that another's property is not returned. About the man, as men, we are of diverse views; but in righteousness itself we are together. In the same way too it can happen that what I know someone does not have, you hold him to have, not most certainly, but credibly suspect; and on this account I seem to you to intervene for one who both desires the punishment of his crime to be relaxed for him, and to possess that on account of which the crime was committed. In sum, then, neither with you, nor with such men, if any are found such as we rejoice you to be, nor with those very ones who with great zeal seek others' goods that will profit nothing, and are moreover much perilous and ruinous, nor with my own heart, to which God is witness, would I dare to say, to feel, to decide, that it must be intervened for anyone so that he may possess by the impunity of crime what he took by crime: but that, the injury being remitted, he may restore what he injuriously took; if, however, he has what he took, or something else from which to restore it.

**6. 23.** Not indeed is whatever is taken from an unwilling person taken injuriously. For very many are unwilling to render the physician his fee, or the laborer his wage; nor yet do those who receive these from an unwilling person receive them through injury—things which would rather not be given through injury. But the judge ought not therefore to sell a just judgment, or the witness a true testimony, because the advocate sells just patronage and the jurist true counsel: for those are employed between both parties for the examination; these stand on one side. But when judgments and testimonies, which, even when just and true, are not to be sold, are sold unjust and false, money is surely taken much more wickedly, because it is wickedly given even by the willing. Yet he is wont to demand back the money as evilly taken from him who buys a just judgment, since it ought not to have been venal; but he who gave it for an unjust judgment would indeed wish to demand it back, were he not afraid or ashamed to have bought it.

**6. 24.** There are other persons of lower rank, who not unusually receive from both parties, like the officer, both from him who applies and to whom the office is applied: things extorted from these by immoderate wickedness are wont to be demanded back; things given by tolerable custom are not wont to be; and we reproach more those who have demanded back such things in an unusual way than those who took them by custom; since many persons necessary to human affairs are either invited by gains of this kind, or held by them. These, if they have changed the way of life, or ascended to a more excellent degree of sanctity, more easily bestow on the poor, as their own, the things they acquired in this way, than restore them, as another's, to those from whom they received them. But he who, against the right of human society, by thefts, robberies, calumnies, oppressions, invasions, has taken away, we judge them to be returned rather than given away: by the evangelical example of Zacchaeus the publican, who, when he had received the Lord into his lodging, suddenly changed unto a holy life, said: *Half of my goods I give to the poor, and if I have taken anything from anyone, I restore it fourfold*[^24].

**6. 25.** Nevertheless, if righteousness is more sincerely consulted, it is more justly said to an advocate, Return what you received, when you stood against the truth, were present to iniquity, deceived the judge, oppressed a just cause, prevailed by falsehood (which you see many most honorable and most eloquent men think they commit not only with impunity but even gloriously), than to anyone serving in any office, Return what you received, when, the judge ordering it, you held a man necessary to any cause, bound him lest he resist, shut him up lest he flee, finally either produced him while the suit lasted, or dismissed him when it was ended. But why it is not said to the advocate is at hand: namely, because a man is so unwilling to demand back what he gave to his patron in order to win wrongly, as he is unwilling to return what he received from his adversary when he had won wrongly. What advocate, in the end, or what man, from being an advocate, becomes so excellent that he is easily found, who would say to his client: Take back what you gave me, when I was supporting you wrongly; and return to your adversary what, by my action, you injuriously took away? And yet he who most rightly repents of his former not-right life ought to do even this: that if he who litigated unjustly will not, when admonished, correct the injustice, yet he himself should be unwilling to have the wage of that injustice—unless perhaps another's property which is secretly taken away by theft is to be restored, and that is not to be restored which, in the very forum where sins are punished, is obtained by deceiving the judge and circumventing the laws! What shall I say of usuries, which even the laws and judges themselves order to be returned? Or is he crueler who withdraws or snatches something from a rich man, than he who butchers a poor man by interest? These and such things are surely possessed evilly, and I would wish them to be restored; but there is no judge before whom they may be demanded back.

**6. 26.** Now, then, if we prudently regard what is written: *The whole world of riches belongs to the faithful man, but to the unfaithful not even a penny*[^25], do we not convict all who seem to themselves to rejoice in things lawfully acquired, and know not how to use them, of possessing another's? For surely that is not another's which is possessed by right; and that by right which justly; and that justly which well. Everything, then, that is possessed evilly is another's; but he possesses evilly who uses evilly. You see, then, how many ought to restore another's property, if even a few could be found to whom it might be restored: who, however, where they are, despise these things the more, the more justly they could have possessed them. For righteousness, no one has evilly, and he who does not love it does not have it. But money is both had evilly by the evil, and had so much the better by the good, the less it is loved. But among these things the iniquity of those who have evilly is tolerated, and certain rights are established among them, which are called civil; not that hence it comes about that they are good users, but that the evil users may be less troublesome: until the faithful and devout, to whom all things belong by right, who either are made from among them, or, living for a while among them, are not bound by their evils but exercised by them, arrive at that city where the inheritance is eternity; where none but the just has place, none but the wise has rule, where whoever shall be there shall possess things truly their own. But yet even here we do not intercede that another's property not be restored according to earthly morals and laws: although we wish you placable toward the evil, not that the evil may please or remain, but because from among them are made whoever are made good, and by the sacrifice of mercy God is appeased, whom unless the evil had propitious, none would be good. It is now long that I seem, by speaking, burdensome to your occupations, since to you, an acute and learned man, what you sought could quickly have been resolved. But I ought long ago to have finished, if I knew that you only were going to read what you demanded that I write back. Live happy in Christ, dearest son.
