# Letter 127

To the most excellent lords, deservedly honorable and longed-for sons, Armentarius and Paulina, Augustine sends greetings in the Lord.

**1.** The distinguished man, my son Ruferius, your kinsman, reported to me what you have vowed to the Lord: by which narrative of his made glad, and at the same time fearing lest that tempter, who from of old envies such good things, persuade something else, I thought your Love should be exhorted in a few words, most excellent lord, deservedly honorable and longed-for son, that you consider what is read in the divine words: *Do not delay to be converted to the Lord, and do not put it off from day to day*[^1]; and that you seize and take care to render what you know you have vowed to him who both exacts debts and pays his promises. For this too is written: *Vow, and render to the Lord your God*[^2]: although, even if you had not vowed, what else was to be persuaded to you, or what better can be done by a man, than that he restore himself to him by whom he was established; especially since so great a sign of God's love toward us has appeared and shone forth, that he sent his only-begotten Son to die for us? It remains, then, that what the Apostle says be done, that Christ died for this reason, that *those who live should no longer live to themselves, but to him who died for them and rose again*[^3]. Unless perhaps the world is still to be loved, worn down by so great a ruin of things that it has lost even the appearance of seduction. For as much as those are to be praised and proclaimed who did not deign to flourish even while the world flourished, so much are those to be rebuked and accused whom it delights to perish with what perishes.

**2.** The labors and dangers and ruins of this transitory life, if they are undergone for the sake of that same life, to be at some time ended, so that its death may not be altogether taken away, but a little deferred; how much more are they to be undergone for the sake of the eternal life, where death neither nature solicitously guards against, nor cowardice basely fears, nor wisdom bravely endures! For it will be to no one, who will not be. Let eternal life, then, have you among its lovers. Do you not see how vehement lovers this wretched and needy life has, and how greatly it obliges its lovers to itself? Who yet, often disturbed by its danger, end it the sooner by the very thing by which they dread to end it, and hasten death while they avoid it, as if anyone destined to be snatched by a river should rush in, fleeing a robber or a beast. They cast even their food into the sea sometimes, when the storm rages; and that they may live, they throw away that whereby they live, lest soon end what is lived even in labor. With how great labors is it carried on, that one may labor a longer time! And death, when it has begun to impend, is guarded against for this reason, that it may be the longer feared. For amid so many mischances of human frailty, how many deaths are feared—of which surely, when one has come, it remains that the rest are not feared! And yet one is fled, that all may be feared. With what griefs are those tortured who are cared for and cut by physicians! Surely not that they may not die? But that they may die somewhat later. Many certain torments are undergone, that a few uncertain days may be added: and sometimes, conquered by the very griefs which they undergo through fear of death, they die at once; and when they choose in no way to end life lest they suffer pain, but to suffer pain lest they end it, it happens to them that they both suffer pain and end it: not only because even those healed surely end life after their griefs—a life which, bought with such penalties, can neither be everlasting, because it is mortal, nor long-lasting, because it is wholly brief, nor secure of even its own brief space, because it is always uncertain; but also because sometimes they have ended in grief that which, lest they end it, they wished to suffer grief.

**3.** The excessive love of this life has also this great evil, and vehemently to be execrated and dreaded, that many, while they wish to live a little longer, grievously offend God, with whom is the fount of life; and so, while the end of life, which must necessarily come, is feared by them in vain, they are thence prohibited from where one lives without end. To this is added that a wretched life, even if it could be perpetual, is in no way to be compared with a blessed life, even the briefest: and yet these, by loving the most wretched and briefest, lose the most blessed and everlasting, since in this very life, which they ill love, they wish that which in the other they lose; because surely in this they love neither the wretchedness, for they wish to be blessed, nor the brevity, for they do not wish it ended; only because it is life, it is so loved that often, on account of it, though wretched and brief, the blessed and everlasting is lost.

**4.** These things considered, what great thing does eternal life command its lovers, when it commands itself to be so loved as this is loved by its own? Or is it worthy, or to be borne, that, when all things that are loved in the world are despised, the life soon to be ended should at least retain that little span itself in the world; and the world should not be despised, that the life which is without end with him through whom the world was made should be obtained? Just now, when Rome herself, the home of the most illustrious empire, was laid waste by the barbarian incursion, how many lovers of this temporal life, that they might ransom it—even to be prolonged unhappily and bare—gave all that they had been keeping not only to delight and adorn it, but even to sustain and protect it! Lovers surely are wont to confer many things on those they love, that they may have them: these would not have had their beloved unless, by loving her, they had rendered her destitute; nor did they confer many things on her, but rather took all away, lest the enemy take her from them. Nor do I reprehend their counsel: for who would not know that she herself would have perished if those things which had been stored up for her sake had not perished? Although some lost those things first, then soon herself; but some, though prepared to lose all for her sake, lost her first. But from this we are to be admonished what lovers of eternal life we ought to be, so that for its sake we should despise all superfluous things, when, for this transitory life, things that had been necessary to it were despised.

**5.** For we do not despoil our beloved, as those did theirs, in order to keep her; but we make this temporal life serve, as a readier handmaid, for acquiring that eternal one, if we neither bind it with the chains of vain ornaments nor burden it with the loads of harmful cares, and hear the Lord, who most faithfully promises us that life to be desired with the highest ardor, as it were crying out in the assembly of the whole world: *Come to me, all you who labor and are burdened, and I will refresh you. Take my yoke upon you, and learn from me, for I am meek and humble of heart; and you will find rest for your souls. For my yoke is gentle, and my burden is light*[^4]. This discipline of devout humility drives out of the mind, and in a manner breathes out, the windy and turbid desire greedy for things set outside our power. For there is labor where many things are sought and loved, for the acquiring and retaining of which the will is not enough, because it does not have the attending faculty. But the just life is present when we will it, because to will it fully is itself justice; nor does justice, to be perfected, require anything more than a perfect will. See whether there is labor where to will is enough. Whence it was divinely said, *Peace on earth to men of good will*[^5]. Where peace, there rest; where rest, there the end of longing, and no cause of laboring. But for this will to be full, it must be sound: but it will be sound if it does not flee the physician, by whose grace alone it can be healed of the disease of harmful desires. He, then, is the physician who cries, *Come to me, all you who labor*, calling his yoke gentle and his burden light; because, love being poured out through the Holy Spirit in our hearts, surely what is commanded will be loved, and it will be neither harsh nor burdensome, if under this one yoke one serves with a neck the less swollen the more free. And this is the one load by which its bearer is not pressed down, but lifted up. If riches are loved, let them be kept where they cannot perish. If honor is loved, let it be had where no one unworthy is honored. If health is loved, let it be desired to be obtained where, once obtained, nothing is feared. If life is loved, let it be acquired where it is ended by no death.

**6.** Render, then, what you have vowed, because you yourselves are it, and to him you render yourselves from whom you are; render, I beseech. For neither will what you render be diminished by rendering, but rather kept and increased: for he is a kindly exactor, not a needy one; and one who does not grow from what is rendered, but makes those who render grow in him. To him, then, what is not rendered is lost: but what is rendered is added to the renderer; nay rather, in him to whom it is rendered, the very renderer is kept. For the rendered and the renderer will be the same thing, because the debt and the debtor were the same. For man owes himself to God, and is to be rendered to him, that he may be blessed, from whom he received that he might be. This signifies what the Lord says in the Gospel: *Render to Caesar the things that are Caesar's, and to God the things that are God's*[^6]. For he said this when, a coin being shown him, and it being asked whose image it had, the answer was, "Caesar's"; that hence they might understand that God exacts from man his own image in man himself, just as Caesar exacted his own in the coin. How much more, then, is it to be rendered when it is promised, to him to whom even unpromised it is owed!

**7.** Wherefore, dearest one, I could indeed, according to my own little faculty, praise more richly the fruit of the holy purpose which I have learned you have vowed to God, and show what difference there is between Christian lovers of this world and despisers, although both are called faithful. Both have been washed by the same laver of the sacred font, imbued and consecrated by the same mysteries, both not only hearers but even preachers of the same Gospel; nor yet are both partakers of the kingdom and the light of God, and coheirs of eternal life, which alone is blessed. For the Lord Jesus distinguished, not from those who do not hear, but among themselves, the hearers of his words, by a most broad boundary, not a thin distinction: *He who hears,* he says, *these my words and does them, I will liken him to a prudent man who built his house upon the rock: the rain came down, the floods came, the winds blew, and they beat upon that house, and it did not fall; for it was founded upon the rock. But he who hears these my words and does not do them, I will liken him to a foolish man who built his house upon the sand: the rain came down, the floods came, the winds blew, and they beat upon that house, and it fell, and great was its ruin*[^7]. To hear those words, then, is to build; in this both are equal: but in doing and in not doing what they hear, they are as unequal as a building founded on the solidity of rock is unequal to one which, without any foundation, is overturned by the easy mobility of the sand. Nor does whoever does not hear at all acquire for himself anything safer: for, building nothing, without any roof, much more easily to be overwhelmed, snatched away, and scattered, he is given over to the rains, floods, and winds.

**8.** I could also, according to my small measure, distinguish those same ones pertaining to the right hand and the kingdom of heaven in their grades and merits, and show how the conjugal life of fathers and mothers of families who beget children—yet religious and devout ones—differs from that life which you have vowed to God, if you were now to be exhorted to vow it; but because you have already vowed, you have already bound yourself, it is not lawful for you to do otherwise. Before you were guilty of a vow, it was free in what you should be lower; although that liberty is not to be congratulated by which it comes about that what is rendered, when I shall have been, is not owed. But now, because your promise is held with God, I do not invite you to a great justice, but deter you from a great iniquity. For you will not be such, if you do not do what you vowed, as you would have remained if you had vowed nothing of the sort. For then you would be lesser, not worse: but now, by so much, which God forbid, the more wretched if you break faith with God, by as much the more blessed if you pay it. Nor on this account let it repent you to have vowed; nay rather rejoice that it is now not lawful for you to do what, to your own detriment, would have been lawful. Set about it, therefore, intrepid, and fulfill your words by deeds; he himself will help, who demands your vows. Happy is the necessity which compels to better things.

**9.** There could be one sole cause by which we should not only not exhort you, but even forbid you, to fulfill what you vowed, if perhaps your wife should refuse, by weakness of mind or flesh, to undertake this with you. For such things are not to be vowed by married persons except by common consent and will: and if it has been done too hastily, the rashness is rather to be corrected than the promise paid. For God does not exact if anyone has vowed something out of another's, but rather forbids to usurp what is another's. For the divine sentence about this matter has been brought forth through the Apostle: *The wife does not have power over her own body, but the husband; likewise also the husband does not have power over his own body, but the wife*[^8]: he named the sex by the name of the body. But since I hear that she is so ready to dedicate continence to God that she is hindered only by this, if you are compelled to render her the debt by conjugal right; both of you render to God what both have vowed, that there may be paid to him what you do not exact of each other. If continence is a virtue—as it is—why should the weaker sex be readier for it, when virtue (virtus) seems to be named rather from man (vir), as the likeness of the word resounds? Do not, then, as a man (vir), shrink from the virtue (virtus) which a woman is ready to undertake. Let your consent be an oblation to the supreme altar of the Creator, and, concupiscence being conquered, let the bond of love be the stronger the holier it is. Let us rejoice over you in the abundant grace of Christ, most excellent lords, deservedly honorable and longed-for sons.
