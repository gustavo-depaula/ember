# Letter 15

Augustine to Romanianus

**1.** This letter does not so much indicate a lack of paper as testify that parchment, at least, is plentiful. The ivory tablets that I have I sent to your uncle along with a letter. For you will more easily pardon this little scrap of skin, because what I wrote to him could not be put off, and not to write to you I judged the height of folly. But the tablets, if any of ours are there, I ask you to send, for needs of this kind. I have written something about the catholic religion, as much as the Lord has deigned to grant, which I want to send you before my arrival, if paper does not run out meanwhile. For you will tolerate whatever sort of writing comes from the workshop of Maiorinus. As for the books, except the books on the orator, the whole matter has slipped my mind. But I could write back nothing more than that you yourself take whichever ones you please, and I still hold to the same opinion. For being absent, I find nothing more that I can do.

**2.** It is most welcome to me that in your last letter you wished to make me a sharer in your domestic joy. But, *do you bid me be ignorant of the calm sea's face and its quiet waves*[^1]? Although you neither bid me nor are yourself ignorant. Therefore, if some rest has been granted you for thinking better thoughts, make use of the divine benefit. For we ought not to congratulate ourselves when these things come to us, but rather him through whom they come: since a just and dutiful administration of temporal things, more peaceful and tranquil after its own kind, begets the merit of receiving eternal things—if it does not hold on when it is held, nor become entangled when it is multiplied, nor wrap itself up when it is set at peace. For it has been said by the mouth of Truth itself: *if you have not been faithful in what is another's, who will give you what is your own*[^2]? Therefore, with our cares about changeable things loosened, let us seek the stable and certain goods, let us fly above our earthly riches. For even amid an abundance of honey the little bee does not have wings in vain; for the honey kills it when it sticks fast.

[^1]: Virgil, Aeneid 5.848–49
[^2]: Luke 16:12
