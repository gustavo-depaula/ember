# Letter 28\*

TO THE MOST BLESSED AND VENERABLE LORD, THE BROTHER AND PRIEST NOVATUS, AND THE OTHER BROTHERS, AUGUSTINE AND THE BROTHERS WITH ME SEND GREETINGS IN THE LORD

**1.** Although your Love sent me a very lengthy letter in the case of the people of Aba, it was not burdensome to me, since I vehemently wished to know everything, and so I received it wholly as it was set forth by you. I therefore give thanks to the Lord our God, because, after the greatest despair of their communion and salvation that the earlier part of the letter—in which you narrated everything—had caused me, you flooded me with sudden and unhoped-for joy, announcing both that all the basilicas had been handed over to the catholic Church and that their whole multitude had turned to the peace of Christ and to unity with the greatest eagerness, except for a few men of the curial order whom the interlocutory ruling of the judge—whom by what cunning they could they deceived against their own salvation—had made more sluggish and as it were estranged from the bonds of the laws; although these too, I believe, once his later letter was received (a copy of which, when it arrived, you deigned to send me), with the excuse cut away, were able to be converted.

**2.** But if this has been done or shall be done, I ask that you not neglect to indicate it to us by whatever occasion; I admonish indeed—although I am not unaware that you of your own accord, in the Spirit of the Lord, take more zealous care of this than I—that, with the records of the conference becoming known to them, and any other writings useful in this case, they may learn from how great an evil they are freed by such great insistence, so that they be held in catholic peace not only by fear of temporal troubles but also by fear of eternal fire and by love of the truth. For unless I am mistaken, I recall that I have already at some time written that the same records be read solemnly in the Church every year throughout the days of Lent, when those who fast have leisure to hear, as our Church of Carthage does and some Churches diligently—and would that it could be done throughout all Africa. We have also written one book treating of those very records, which, when the same records are finished, is read among us and heard by the people with the greatest delight; and if you do not yet have it, you ought to have it.

**3.** But these things are not read among us from the grade of reader as the canonical Scriptures are, since that is not fitting; rather the readers read them in such a way that they do not even divest themselves of their chasubles and may sit when they wish, where they can conveniently be heard by those sitting who have wished to sit, as if they were being read privately in a house. But it is done in the Church for this reason, that it holds a multitude and both sexes are not forbidden to hear, until the hour comes at which the divine readings are heard and the sacraments celebrated according to custom.

**4.** I believe, moreover, that the vicar gave to your Holiness too the letter that, by his interlocutory ruling, he promised he would give to us as well; and if this was done, you have without doubt written back. And I wonder that your Charity took care to inform us neither of his letter given to you, except these later ones, nor of your reply, which you were now able to send—since you sent so many things that you seem to have omitted nothing; or if they had not come to you, or he himself had not sent them, when he sent them to us, I wonder all the more. But what we are to do I do not know—whether we should write back to him after the case is known through your letter, or rather now stir up nothing about this matter in his mind, since by these later letters of his he has recalled even those to whom he had as it were given security to the fear of fulfilling the laws, with fitting dread, so that he may not henceforth easily believe heretics, if their deceit has now been proved to him. The matter itself will make him sufficiently more certain, although I too have not kept silent about this matter in the letter I wrote back to him, when I received that of his of which I sent you a copy. But since I had not sent your Charity a copy of my reply given to him, I have now appended it to this letter; nevertheless, if a suitable occasion arises, writings will be sent to him in our common name, that is, mine and brother Alypius's, with the Lord's help.

**5.** That Victorinus is in the Church, about whom I had written earlier to your Holiness, that you should handle his business about the grain levies, which you also deigned to do; he has a case with his mother and his stepfather, on account of which he fled to the Church. This too I did not keep silent in the letter given on this matter to the religious count; but the tribune Peregrinus said that he had received a memorandum of the count by which the Church was gravely disturbed, as if he had been ordered to drag him out of the Church. This indeed was done in my absence; but when I had come, the said tribune read me the memorandum itself, which I did not believe to be genuine. And therefore I wrote, so that, by the replies of the same count, the same tribune might know that he ought not to disturb the Church with falsehoods of this kind. And even if he himself did not fabricate them, yet if he readily believes them, you see what troubles we may have to endure.

**6.** But more than I have done I could not do; for I could not send the memorandum itself, which he only read to me; he did not, moreover, give it or enter it among the municipal records. Hence, if I were to keep silent, the Christian man and religious count ought rather to blame me; but I do not wish anything to be said to him about this now, lest perhaps this tribune deny the whole thing when he sees that he is indignant with him, and we be compelled to convict the man, which is not expedient. But if perchance he himself should ask anything about this of his own accord, you will deign to read to him, at the proper point, this which I have written to you. I wish nothing more.

**7.** I commend indeed to your Holiness the man of our said son Victorinus, that you deign to help him, so that he may be able to do that for which he was sent. The notary about whose sending your Venerableness wrote, I gladly accept. About Olympius we heard as soon as he came; I have now received his letter too. But whether I shall come to the count I do not know. For there came a certain one of the brothers of Carthage who said that he was arranging to go still farther on account of the case of the bishop Rogatus, whose tongue and hand the heretics cut off, since even the imperial agent who had gone with the said bishop and by imperial command, before he came, Olympius was there gravely beaten by those same reprobates. Nevertheless we await letters from Carthage to instruct us more certainly about everything.
