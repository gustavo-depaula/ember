# Letter 102

To his most sincere brother and fellow priest Deogratias, Augustine sends greetings in the Lord

**1.** You preferred to pass on to me the questions put to you, not, I think, out of laziness, but because you hear even those things you already know more gladly through us, since you love us too much. But I, for my part, preferred that they be opened up by you, because that very friend who put them forward—so far as can be conjectured from the fact that he has not written back to me about certain letters—seems, as it were, too shy to follow us; he will see for what reason. This, however, I suspect, and my suspicion is neither malevolent nor absurd, since you know very well how much I love him and how great a grief it is to me that he is not yet a Christian; and surely I think it not inappropriate to judge that he, whom I see was unwilling to write back to me, did not want anything written to him by me. So I beg you that, just as I obeyed you, and amid my most pressing occupations feared to offend your holy and most dear wish if I did not do what you asked, so you too may do what I ask. This is it: that you not find it burdensome to answer all his questions briefly, in the way that, as you told me, he requested of you—something you could have done even before. For you will see when you read it that almost nothing has been said by me that you did not already know, or that you could not have known though I were silent. But this work of mine I ask you to keep with the rest, with which you know it is fitting to keep it. As for that work I demand of you, let the very man for whom it is most suited have it, and the rest who are not a little delighted by such things, among whom I too am one. May you ever live in Christ, mindful of us.

**2.** Some are troubled and inquire about the two resurrections, which of them accords with the promised resurrection, that of Christ or that of Lazarus? "If Christ's," they say, "how can it accord with the resurrection of those born from seed, since he was born under no condition of seed? But if the resurrection of Lazarus is asserted to accord with it, not even this seems to fit: for Lazarus's resurrection took place from a body not yet decayed, from that very body which was called Lazarus, whereas ours will be raised many ages later out of confusion. Furthermore, if after the resurrection the state is to be blessed, with no injury to the body, no necessity of hunger, what does it mean that Christ ate and showed his wounds? But if he did so for the sake of an unbeliever, he pretended; and if he showed something true, then wounds will exist in the resurrection that has been received."

**3.** To these the answer is that not the resurrection of Lazarus but rather that of Christ accords with the promised resurrection, because Lazarus rose in such a way that he would die again, whereas Christ, as is written of him, *being raised from the dead, dies no more, and death will no longer have dominion over him*[^1]. This too is promised to those who will rise at the end of the age and will reign with him forever. But the difference between Christ's nativity and ours has no bearing on the resurrection—that he was born without male seed, while we were created from man and woman—just as it has no bearing on the difference of death itself. For his death was not untrue on account of his having been born without male seed; just as the flesh of the first man, which arose otherwise than ours (since he was created without parents from the earth, while we are from parents), introduced no difference in death, such that he should die in one way and we in another. As the difference of nativity does not affect death, so neither does it affect the resurrection.

**4.** But lest the unbelievers be likewise unwilling to believe this very thing that is written about the first man, let them ask or observe, if they can manage even this, how many kinds of animals are brought forth from the earth without parents, which nevertheless by mating also bring forth offspring like themselves; nor does anything in their nature differ on account of the diversity of nativity between those that were brought forth from the earth and those that were born from the mating of the former. For they live alike and die alike, though they were born unlike. Thus it is not absurd that bodies should rise alike which arose unlike. But men of this sort, unable to discern in what respect some difference matters and in what it does not, wherever they have noticed some difference in beginnings, contend that all the consequences too must differ. Such men might suppose that oil from animal fat ought not to float upon water, as does the oil that comes from the olive, since the origin of each is very unlike, the one having flowed from wood, the other from flesh.

**5.** As for that difference—that Christ's body, not dissolved by decay and rottenness, rose on the third day, whereas ours will be restored after a long time out of a certain confusion into which they had departed once dissolved—both are impossible to human capacity, but both are most easy to the divine power. For just as the ray of our eye does not reach nearer things more quickly and farther things more slowly, but touches both intervals with equal speed, so when, *in the twinkling of an eye*[^2], as the Apostle says, the resurrection of the dead takes place, it is as easy for the omnipotence of God and his ineffable command to raise up fresh corpses as those that fell to pieces long ago. These things are incredible to some, because untried; whereas the whole nature of things is so full of marvels that they are not marvels to us—not because of any easy investigation by reason, but because of the habit of seeing them—and for this reason they seem worthy neither of consideration nor of inquiry. For I, and with me whoever strives to understand the invisible things of God through the things that have been made, marvel no less, indeed more, that in one tiny grain of seed all the things we praise in a tree lie hidden, as though woven within, than that the so vast bosom of this world should restore whole and entire, for the future resurrection, what it takes up from human bodies as they fall to pieces.

**6.** But how is it contradictory both that Christ ate after the resurrection and that in the resurrection which is promised there will be no need of food, when we read that the angels too took food of the same kind in the same way, not by a feigned and empty phantasm but by the most manifest truth, and yet not by necessity but by power? For the earth thirstily absorbs water in one way, the burning ray of the sun in another: the one by need, the other by power. The body of the future resurrection, therefore, will be of imperfect happiness if it cannot take food; of imperfect happiness if it needs food. I could here dispute more fully about the changes of bodily qualities and about the preeminent power of higher bodies over lower ones; but my purpose is to answer briefly, and this is written for minds of such a kind that it will be enough to give them a hint.

**7.** Let the one who put forward these questions know well that Christ after the resurrection showed scars, not wounds, to those who doubted; for whose sake he also willed to take food and drink, not once but often, lest they suppose that he was not a body but a spirit, and was appearing to them not solidly but in imagination. Now those scars would have been false if no wounds had preceded them; and yet they would not even have existed had he not willed them to exist. But he willed it, for the sake of a certain dispensation, so that to those whom he was building up in unfeigned faith he might show that what they had seen crucified, and not something else in its place, had risen. What, then, is the meaning of the saying, "If he did it for the sake of an unbeliever, he pretended"? As if, indeed, when some brave man fighting for his country took many wounds in front, and a most skilled physician, who could so heal them that no scars would appear, were told by the man himself that he preferred to be healed in such a way that the traces of his wounds, as titles of glory, might rather remain on his body—as if that physician would then be said to have feigned the scars because, when by his art he could have brought it about that they did not exist, for a definite reason he by his art brought it about rather that they did exist; scars which, as I said above, could be convicted of being false in one way only, namely if no wounds had been healed.

**8.** Likewise they put forward other things, which they said were culled from Porphyry against the Christians as being, so to speak, more forceful. "If Christ," they say, "declares himself the way of salvation, grace, and truth, and places in himself alone the return for souls that believe in him, what did the men of so many ages before Christ do? To pass over," he says, "the times before Latium was ruled, let us take, as it were, the beginning of the human name from Latium itself. In Latium itself, before Alba, gods were worshiped. In Alba likewise the religions and rites of the temples held sway. For not fewer ages Rome herself, through a long succession of ages, was without the Christian law. What, then," he says, "became of so countless souls who are in no fault at all, since he in whom one could believe had not yet imparted his coming to men? The world too, along with Rome herself, was fervent in the rites of the temples. Why, then," he says, "did the one who was called the Savior withdraw himself for so many ages? But lest they say that the human race was tended by the old Jewish law, the law of the Jews appeared and flourished long afterward in the narrow region of Syria, and only later crept even to the borders of Italy—but after Caesar Gaius, or at any rate during his reign. What, then, became of the Roman or Latin souls who were bereft of the grace of Christ, who had not yet come, right up to the time of the Caesars?"

**9.** To this proposition the answer is that first let them say whether the rites of their own gods, which it is agreed were instituted at definite times, were of profit to men. If they deny that these were of any profit to the salvation of souls, they destroy them along with us and confess that they are empty. We for our part show them to be even pernicious; but it is no small thing that they should meanwhile first confess them to be empty. But if they defend them and assert that they were wisely and usefully instituted, I ask what became of those who died before these things were instituted: for they were surely cheated of this salvation and benefit. But if they could be purified in some other way, why did not that same way continue for their descendants? What need was there to institute novel consecrations which did not exist in antiquity?

**10.** Here, if they say that the gods themselves indeed always existed and were equally able everywhere to free their worshipers, but that, owing to the variety of temporal and earthly affairs, which they knew to be suited to definite times and places, they willed to be served in these now in one way and now in another, here and there, in this manner and in that—why do they press this question upon the Christian religion, in which, on behalf of their own gods, they themselves either cannot answer us, or, if they can, in that very answer let them answer also on behalf of our religion: that it makes no difference, given the diverse suitability of times and places, with what diverse sacred rites that is worshiped, provided that what is worshiped is holy, just as it makes no difference, given the diverse suitability of tongues and hearers, with what diverse sounds a thing is said, provided that what is said is true—with this difference, to be sure, that the sounds of language, by which men communicate their thoughts to one another, men themselves can establish by a certain compact of society, whereas by what sacred rites they might be in accord with the divinity, those who rightly understood have followed the will of God. And that will was never wanting to the salvation of mortals' righteousness and piety; and if some such rites are variously celebrated among different peoples joined together in one and the same religion, it matters very much how it is done, so that human weakness may be either encouraged or tolerated, and divine authority not opposed.

**11.** Therefore, since we call Christ the Word of God through whom all things were made, and Son for that very reason that he is the Word—not a word spoken and gone, but the unchangeable Word abiding unchangeably with the unchangeable Father, under whose governance every creature, spiritual and corporeal, is administered according to the suitability of times and places, and in whom resides the wisdom and knowledge of what, when, and where ought to be done concerning the creature he moderates and governs—surely, both before he propagated the Hebrew nation, through which he would prefigure by suitable sacred rites the manifestation of his coming, and in the very times of the Israelite kingdom, and then when he showed himself mortally to mortals in the flesh he took from the Virgin, and thereafter even until now, when he fulfills all that he foretold through the Prophets, and from now until the end of the age, when he will separate the holy from the impious and render to each his own—he himself is the same Son of God, coeternal with the Father, and the unchangeable Wisdom through whom all nature was created, and by participation in whom every rational soul is made blessed.

**12.** And so from the beginning of the human race, whoever believed in him and understood him in whatever measure, and lived devoutly and justly according to his precepts, whenever and wherever they lived, were beyond doubt saved through him. For just as we believe in him both as abiding with the Father and as having already come in the flesh, so the ancients believed in him both as abiding with the Father and as about to come in the flesh. Nor, because owing to the variety of times what was then foretold as future is now announced as accomplished, is the faith itself therefore varied, or the salvation itself different. Nor, because one and the same thing is preached or prophesied by different sacred rites and sacraments, ought we therefore to understand different things, or different kinds of salvation. But as to when that should be done which pertains to the one and the same liberation of the faithful and the devout, let us ascribe the counsel to God and hold to our own obedience. Accordingly, by some names and signs then, by others now, first more hiddenly, afterward more manifestly, first by fewer, afterward by more, yet one and the same true religion is signified and observed.

**13.** Nor do we object against them that Numa Pompilius established the worship of the gods for the Romans in one way, while they were worshiped in another way earlier by them or by the Italians; nor that in the times of Pythagoras that philosophy was celebrated which earlier either did not exist at all, or perhaps lay hidden among a very few who held the same views, though they did not live by the same rite. Rather, whether those gods are true, or to be worshiped, and whether that philosophy is of any profit to the salvation of souls—this is what we deal with against them, this we call into question, this we tear apart by disputing. Let them therefore cease to object against us what can be objected against every sect and every name of religion. For since they confess that the times are ordered not by chance but by divine providence, what is fitting and opportune for each time surpasses human counsel and is dispensed from that source whence providence itself takes thought for things.

**14.** For if they say that the Pythagorean teaching was not always and everywhere present because Pythagoras was a man and could not have this in his power, can they also say that at the very time when he existed, and in the regions where that philosophy flourished, all who could hear him were also willing to believe and follow him? And by this token, all the more, if Pythagoras had been of such great power that he could preach his doctrines where he willed and when he willed, even if along with that power he had also the highest foreknowledge of things, he would nowhere and never appear except where and when he foreknew that men would believe him. Accordingly, since they do not object against Christ that not all follow his teaching—for they themselves perceive that this could in no way rightly be objected, either against the wisdom of the philosophers or even against the divinity of their own gods—what will they answer if, setting aside that height of the wisdom and knowledge of God, where perhaps some far more secret divine counsel lies hidden, and without prejudice even to other possible causes which can be investigated by the prudent, we say to them this one thing only, for brevity's sake, in the discussion of this question: that Christ willed to appear to men and to have his doctrine preached among them then when he knew, and where he knew, that there were those who would believe in him? For at those times and in those places where his Gospel was not preached, he foreknew that all would be, at his preaching, such as not indeed all, but yet many, were in his bodily presence, who were unwilling to believe in him even when he raised the dead; such as we see many even now, who, though the heralds of the Prophets are fulfilled concerning him with so great a manifestation, are still unwilling to believe, and prefer to resist by human cunning rather than to yield to divine authority so clear and conspicuous and so sublime and so sublimely proclaimed. As long as man's understanding is small and weak, it ought to yield to divine truth. What wonder, then, if Christ knew that the world in earlier ages was so full of unbelievers that he rightly willed neither to appear to them nor to have his message preached to them, whom he foreknew would believe neither his words nor his miracles? For it is not incredible that all were then such as we marvel that so many have been and are from his coming until this present time.

**15.** And yet from the beginning of the human race, sometimes more hiddenly, sometimes more evidently, as it seemed by divine appointment to suit the times, he never ceased to be prophesied, nor were there lacking those who believed in him, from Adam until Moses, and among the people of Israel itself, which by a certain special mystery was a prophetic nation, and among other nations before he came in the flesh. For since some are mentioned in the holy Hebrew books, already from the time of Abraham, neither of the stock of his flesh, nor of the people of Israel, nor from any adventitious association with the people of Israel, who nevertheless shared in this sacrament, why should we not believe that among the other nations here and there there were also others, although we do not read of them mentioned in those same authoritative writings? Thus the salvation of this religion, through which alone true salvation is truly and truthfully promised, was never wanting to anyone who was worthy, and to whom it was wanting, he was not worthy. And from the beginning of human propagation until the end, it is preached to some for reward, to some for judgment. And by this, both those to whom it was not announced at all were foreknown as those who would not believe; and those who, not about to believe, nevertheless had it announced to them, are shown as an example to those others; while those who, about to believe, have it announced to them, are prepared for the kingdom of heaven and the fellowship of the holy angels.

**16.** Now let us look at the question that follows. "They accuse," he says, "the rites of the sacred things, the victims, the incense, and the rest that the worship of the temples carried on, although this same worship," he says, "began among them, or with the God whom they worship, in ancient times, when God is brought in as having had need of firstfruits."

**17.** To this the answer is that, since this question is recognized to be put forward from that place of our Scriptures where it is written that *Cain offered a gift to God from the fruits of the earth, but Abel from the firstlings of his sheep*[^3], it should rather be understood from this how ancient a thing is sacrifice, which the truthful and sacred Scriptures admonish ought to be offered to none but the one true God: not because God has need of it, since in those same writings it is most plainly written, *I said to the Lord, You are my God, for you have no need of my goods*[^4]; but because even in these things, whether in accepting or rejecting or receiving them, he takes thought for none but men. For it profits us to worship God, not God himself. When, therefore, he inspires and teaches how he is to be worshiped, he does so not from any need of his own, but for our greatest benefit. Now all such sacrifices are significative, and are likenesses of certain things by which we ought to be admonished toward those very things of which they are likenesses, whether to be examined, or known, or recalled. On this matter, to be discussed as fully as is sufficient, no brief discourse must be demanded, such as we have now purposed to make in reply; especially since in other little works of ours we have already said much about this matter. And those who before us treated the words of God have spoken copiously about the likenesses of the sacrifices of the Old Testament as shadows and figures of things to come.

**18.** This much, indeed, must not be passed over even in such brevity: that unless the false gods—that is, the demons, who are prevaricating angels—knew that temple, priesthood, sacrifice, and whatever else pertains to these are owed to the one true God, they would never have demanded these things for themselves from their worshipers, whom they deceive. But when these are rendered to God, according to his inspiration and teaching, it is true religion; whereas when they are rendered to demons, according to their impious pride, it is harmful superstition. Therefore those who know the Christian writings of both Testaments do not blame this in the sacrilegious rites of the pagans, that they build temples and institute priesthoods and make sacrifices, but that they render these to idols and demons. And that idols are devoid of all sense, who would doubt? Yet when they are placed in these seats, with honorable loftiness, so as to be regarded by those who pray and sacrifice, by their very likeness to animate members and senses—though they are senseless and lifeless—they so affect weak minds that they seem to live and breathe; especially with the addition of the veneration of the multitude, by which so great a worship is lavished upon them.

**19.** These diseased and pestilential affections divine Scripture heals, which inculcates a thing indeed well known, but yet with the salutary remedy of admonition, saying, *They have eyes and do not see, they have ears and do not hear*[^5], and other such things. For these words, the more open and popularly true they are, the more they strike a salutary shame into those who, while they render divine worship with fear to such images, and gazing on them venerate and adore them as though they were living, address prayers to them as though present, immolate victims, pay their vows, are so affected altogether that they do not dare to think them devoid of sense. But lest these men suppose that our books mean only this, that an affection of this kind is born in the human heart from idols, it is most plainly written: *For all the gods of the nations are demons*[^6]. Whence the apostolic teaching not only says what is read in John, *Brethren, beware of idols*[^7], but also what is in Paul: *What then? Do I say that anything sacrificed to idols is anything, or that an idol is anything? But what the nations sacrifice, they sacrifice to demons and not to God; and I do not want you to become partners with demons*[^8]. Whence it can be sufficiently understood that it is not the immolation itself (for the ancient saints immolated to the true God), but that it is offered to false gods and impious demons, which is reproved by true religion in the superstitions of the nations. For just as truth exhorts men to become partners of the holy angels, so impiety seduces them to the fellowship of demons, for whom eternal fire is prepared, just as an eternal kingdom for the fellowship of the saints.

**20.** Nor do the impious excuse their sacrilegious rites and images on the ground that they elegantly interpret what each thing signifies. For all that interpretation is referred to the creature, not to the Creator, to whom alone is owed that service of religion which by one name is called in Greek *latreia*. Nor do we say that the earth, the seas, the sky, the sun, the moon, the stars, and certain heavenly powers not set in plain view are demons; but since every creature is partly corporeal, partly incorporeal—which latter we also call spiritual—it is manifest that what is done by us devoutly and religiously proceeds from the will of the mind, which is a spiritual creature and to be set above every corporeal one. From which it is gathered that one must not sacrifice to a corporeal creature. There remains the spiritual creature, which is either devout or impious: devout, namely, in just men and angels who duly serve God; impious in unjust men and angels, whom we also call demons. And by this, neither must one sacrifice to a spiritual creature, however just. For the more devout it is and subject to God, the less does it deem itself worthy of such honor, which it knows is owed to none but God. How much more pernicious, then, is it to sacrifice to demons—that is, to the unjust spiritual creature, which, dwelling in this nearby and gloomy sky as in its airy prison, is predestined to everlasting punishment! Wherefore, even when men say they sacrifice to the higher heavenly powers, which are not demons, and suppose that only a matter of name is at issue—that they call them gods, we call them angels—they do not set themselves to deceive them by manifold deceit, except the demons, who delight in error and are in a manner fed by human error. For the holy angels approve no sacrifice except what is offered, from the teaching of true wisdom and true religion, to the one true God, whom in holy fellowship they serve. Accordingly, just as impious pride, whether of men or of demons, either commands or desires that these divine honors be rendered to itself, so devout humility, whether of men or of the holy angels, has refused these when offered to itself, and has shown to whom they are owed. Of which thing the most manifest examples are shown in our sacred writings.

**21.** Now that the sacrifices were apportioned by the divine words according to the suitability of the times, so that some were done before the manifestation of the New Testament, which is administered from the very truth as the victim of the one priest—that is, from the shed blood of Christ—and another now, which we offer, suited to this manifestation, we who are now called Christians by a name openly declared: this is shown not only by the evangelical but also by the prophetic writings. For the change—not of God, not of religion itself, but of sacrifices and sacraments—would now seem to be proclaimed shamelessly, had it not been foretold beforehand. For just as one and the same man, if he offers one thing to God in the morning and another in the evening, according to the suitability of the time of day, does not change God or religion—just as neither does he change his salutation who greets in one way in the morning, in another in the evening—so in the whole course of the ages, when one thing was offered by the ancient saints and another is offered by those who are now, not by human presumption but by divine authority, the sacred mysteries suited to the times are celebrated, and neither God nor religion is changed.

**22.** Now next let us see what sort of thing it is that he put forward about the measure of sin and punishment, thus calumniating the Gospel: "Christ," he says, "threatens eternal punishments to those who do not believe in him; and elsewhere he says, *With what measure you measure, it will be measured back to you*[^9]. Ridiculously enough," he says, "and contradictorily: for if he is going to repay punishment according to measure, and every measure is circumscribed by a limit of time, what is meant by the threats of infinite punishment?"

**23.** That this question was raised and put forward by any philosopher is hard to believe, since he says, "Every measure is circumscribed by time," as though there were not measures of other things than time, such as of hours and days and years; or as we say that a short syllable has one unit of time in relation to a long syllable. For I suppose that pecks and rabones, jars and amphorae, are not measures of time. How then is every measure circumscribed by time? Do not they themselves say that this sun is everlasting? Yet how great it is in relation to the earth they dare to investigate and report by geometric measures. Whether they can or cannot, it is agreed that it nevertheless has its own proper measure of its orb. For if they comprehend how great it is, they comprehend its measure; and if they do not attain to this, they certainly do not comprehend its measure; nor is it therefore none, because men cannot know it. Something can therefore be both everlasting and have a fixed measure of its own kind. For I have spoken of the eternity of the sun according to them, that they may be convicted by their own opinion, and may concede that something can be everlasting along with measure. And so let them not suppose that the everlasting punishment which Christ threatened is not to be believed, because the same one said, *With what measure you measure, it will be measured back to you*[^10].

**24.** For if he had said, "What you measure, this will be measured to you," even so it would not be altogether necessary to refer the saying to the very same thing in every respect. For we can rightly say, "What you plant, this you will pluck," although no one plants an apple but a tree, and plucks an apple rather than a tree; but we say that according to the kind of tree, because one does not plant a fig from which to pluck a nut. So it could be said, "What you do, you will suffer": not that if one has committed defilement, he should suffer defilement; but that what by this sin he did to the law, the law should do to him—that is, because he removed from his own life a law forbidding such things, the law itself should also remove him from the life of men which it governs. Likewise, if he had said, "As much as you measure, so much will be measured back to you," not even so would it follow that we ought to understand punishments equal in every way to the sins. For, for example, wheat and barley are not equal; and surely it could be said, "As much as you measure, so much will be measured back to you," that is, as much barley as wheat. But if it were a question of pains and it were said, "As much pain as you have inflicted, so much will be inflicted on you," it could happen that the pain were as great, though longer in time—that is, greater in duration—equal in force. For if we say of two lamps, "This fire was as hot as that one," it will not therefore be false because one of them was perhaps put out sooner. And so, if something is as great according to one respect but is not as great according to another, because it is not as great in every way, the respect in which it is as great is not therefore false.

**25.** But when he said, *With what measure you measure, it will be measured back to you*[^11], and since it is manifest that the measure in which something is measured is one thing, and the thing itself which is measured is another, it can now happen that, in the measure in which men have measured—for example, a peck of wheat—thousands of pecks be measured to them, so that there is so much difference in the quantity of grain, and none in the measure; to say nothing of the diversity of the very things, since it can happen not only that, in the measure in which someone has measured barley, wheat be measured to him, but that, in the measure in which he has measured grain, gold be measured to him, and there be one peck of grain, but of gold very many. Thus, when both the kind and the quantity differ without comparison of the things themselves, it can yet most rightly be said: "In the measure in which he measured, in that it was measured to him." Now from what occasion Christ said this is sufficiently clear a little above: *Do not judge,* he says, *that you may not be judged: for with what judgment you have judged, you will be judged*[^12]. Surely, if they judge with an unjust judgment, will they be judged with an unjust one? Far be it. For there is no injustice with God. But it was said thus, "With what judgment you have judged, with that you will be judged," as though it were said, "With what will you have done well, in that very will you will be set free"; or, "With what will you have done evil, in that very will you will be punished." Just as if someone, using his eyes for base lust, were commanded to be blinded, he would rightly hear, "With what eyes you sinned, in them you have deserved punishment." For everyone uses the judgment of his own mind, whether good or evil, either for doing well or for sinning. Whence it is not unjust that in what one judges, in that he should be judged—that is, that in the very judgment of his own mind he should pay the penalty, when he suffers those evils which follow upon a mind that judges evilly.

**26.** For there are other, manifest torments which are prepared as future, themselves too drawn from the same hinge of the evil will; but in the mind itself, where the appetite of the will is the measure of all human deeds, the penalty follows the fault at once, and for the most part a greater penalty, because of a graver blindness in one who does not feel it. Therefore, when he had said, *With what judgment you have judged, you will be judged*, he went on to add, *and with what measure you measure, it will be measured to you*[^13]. For in his own will the good man measures good deeds, and in it blessedness will be measured to him. And likewise in his own will the evil man measures evil works, and in it misery will be measured to him; since where each is good, when he wills well, there too he is evil when he wills evilly. And by this, there too he becomes either blessed or wretched—that is, in the very affection of his own will, which is the measure of all deeds and merits. For from the qualities of wills, not from spans of time, we measure both right deeds and sins. Otherwise it would be held a greater sin to fell a tree than to kill a man. For the former is done with long delay, by many strokes; the latter by one stroke, in the briefest time: yet for so great a sin of so brief a time, if a man were punished by perpetual exile, it would be said that he was even more leniently dealt with than he deserved; although, in a span of time, the length of the punishment is in no way to be compared with the brevity of the crime. What, then, is contradictory if the punishments will be alike long, or even alike eternal, but some milder, some sharper than others; so that, for those whose time is equal, the severity is not equal, on account of the measure also of the sins, not in the prolongation of time but in the will of those who sin?

**27.** For the will itself is punished, whether by the punishment of the mind or of the body, so that what delights in sins may itself be smitten in punishments, and so that he who judges without mercy may be judged without mercy; and in this sentence too, the same measure holds for this alone: that what one did not show, is not shown to him; and thus what he is judged with will be eternal, although what he judged could not be eternal. In the same measure, therefore, though of misdeeds that are not eternal, eternal punishments are measured back; so that, because he willed to have an eternal enjoyment of sin, he may find an eternal severity of vengeance. But the brevity I have set for my reply does not allow me to gather all, or at any rate very many, of the things that the holy books contain about sins and about the punishments of sins, and to draw out from them a single opinion without any ambiguity—if indeed I were able to do so by the powers of my mind, even were I to find suitable leisure. Yet now I think it has been sufficiently shown that it is not contradictory to the eternity of punishments that they are repaid in the same measure in which the sins were committed.

**28.** After this question, the one who put them forward from Porphyry added this: "Indeed," he says, "you will also deign to instruct me about this, whether Solomon truly said that God has no Son."

**29.** The answer is quick: not only did he not say this, but he even said that God has a Son. For in him Wisdom speaking says, *Before all the hills he begot me*[^14]. And what is Christ but the Wisdom of God? Likewise in a certain place of Proverbs he says, *God taught me wisdom, and I knew the knowledge of the saints. Who has ascended into heaven and descended? Who has gathered the winds in his bosom? Who has turned water in a garment? Who has held the ends of the earth? What is his name, or what is the name of his Son?*[^15] Of these two which I recalled at the end, the one he referred to the Father, that is, "What is his name?"—because of what he had said, "God taught me wisdom"; and the other evidently to the Son, when he says, "or what is the name of his Son?"—because of the rest, which are better understood of the Son, that is: "Who has ascended into heaven and descended?"—which Paul thus recalls, *He who descended is the same who also ascended above all the heavens*[^16]; "Who has gathered the winds in his bosom?"—that is, the souls of believers into a hidden and secret place; to whom it is said, *For you have died, and your life is hidden with Christ in God*[^17]; "Who has turned water in a garment?"—so that it could be said, *As many of you as were baptized into Christ have put on Christ*[^18]; "Who has held the ends of the earth?"—he who said to his disciples, *You will be my witnesses in Jerusalem, and in all Judea, and Samaria, and to the ends of the earth*[^19].

**30.** The last question put forward is about Jonah, and this one not, as it were, from Porphyry, but as though from the mockery of the pagans; for it is put thus: "Then what," he says, "ought we to think about Jonah, who is said to have been in the belly of the whale for three days? This is incredible and beyond belief, that a man swallowed down with his clothing should have been in the heart of a fish. Or if it is a figure, you will deign to disclose it. Then what is also meant by that gourd which grew up over Jonah after he had been vomited out: what was the reason that this should grow? For I have noticed that this kind of question has been gravely derided by the pagans with much loud laughter."

**31.** To this the answer is that either none of the divine miracles is to be believed, or there is no reason why this one should not be believed. As for Christ himself, that he rose on the third day, we would not believe it if the faith of Christians feared the laughter of the pagans. But since our friend did not raise a question on this point—whether it is to be believed either that Lazarus was raised on the fourth day, or that Christ himself rose on the third day—I greatly wonder that he set down as incredible this thing that was done concerning Jonah; unless perhaps he thinks it easier for a dead man to be raised from the tomb than for a living one to have been preserved in so vast a belly of a beast. For, to omit recalling how great a size of sea-beasts is reported by those who have had experience of them, the belly that those ribs fenced in—ribs which are fixed in a public place at Carthage and are known to the people—how many men it could hold in its space, who would not conjecture? With how great a gaping stood open that mouth, which was, as it were, the door of that cave? Unless perhaps, as he put it, his clothing was a hindrance to Jonah's being swallowed unharmed, as though he forced himself through a narrow place—he who, hurled down a steep height of air, was so received that he was taken into the belly of the beast before he was torn by its tooth. Although Scripture did not say that he was cast into that cavern either naked or clothed, so that it can be understood that he rushed in there even naked, if perchance there was need, like a hide from an egg, so to have his clothing drawn off him that he might be swallowed more easily. For men are so anxious about this prophet's clothing as though he were said either to have crept through a small window, or to have entered a bath; into which, even if he had to enter clothed, it would scarcely be troublesome, yet not a marvel.

**32.** But they have, in truth, something they would not believe in the divine miracle—that the vapor of the belly, by which foods are softened, could have been so tempered as to preserve a man's life. How much more incredible, then, would they propose the case of those three men, sent by an impious king into the furnace, who walked unharmed in the midst of the fire? Wherefore, if these men are unwilling to believe any divine miracles, they must be refuted by another kind of disputation. For they ought not to set forth some one thing as incredible and call it into question, but all things that are narrated as such, or even as more marvelous. And yet, if this thing that is written about Jonah were said to have been done by Apuleius of Madaura or Apollonius of Tyana, of whom they boast of many marvels on no trustworthy authority—although demons too do some things similar to those of the holy angels, not in truth but in appearance, not in wisdom but plainly in deceit—nevertheless, if of these men, as I said, whom they laudably name magicians or philosophers, some such thing were narrated, laughter would no longer burst from their cheeks, but rather pride. So let them laugh at our Scriptures; let them laugh as much as they can, while day by day they see themselves rarer and fewer, whether by dying or by believing; while all things are fulfilled which were foretold by those who, so long before, laughed at these men who would vainly fight against the truth, who would bark idly, who would little by little fail, and who left these things to us, their successors, not only to be read but promised them to be experienced.

**33.** Not absurdly, indeed, nor out of place, is it asked what these things signify, so that, when this has been expounded, they may be believed to have been written not only as deeds done, but also for the sake of some signification. First, therefore, let him who wishes to investigate why this was done not doubt that the prophet Jonah was for three days in the huge belly of a sea-beast: for it was not done in vain, but yet it was done. For if things that were only said figuratively, and not done, move us to faith, how much more ought things to move us that were not only said figuratively but done? For just as human custom speaks by words, so divine power speaks even by deeds. And just as new or less usual words, sprinkled in moderately and becomingly, add splendor to human speech, so in marvelous deeds that fittingly signify something, the divine eloquence is in a manner more brilliant.

**34.** Accordingly, why is it asked by us what was prefigured by that beast's rendering the prophet, whom it had devoured, alive on the third day, since Christ expounds this? *An evil and adulterous generation,* he says, *seeks a sign, and no sign will be given it except the sign of the prophet Jonah: for as Jonah was in the belly of the whale three days and three nights, so will the Son of man be in the heart of the earth three days and three nights*[^20]. But concerning the three-day period of the Lord Christ's death, how an account is to be given of it—when the whole is understood from a part, on the first day and the last, so that the three full days, that is, with their nights, may be reckoned—it is long to discuss, and it has already been said very often in other discourses. As, then, Jonah passed from the ship into the belly of the whale, so Christ from the wood into the tomb, or into the deep of death. And as Jonah did so for those who were endangered by the storm, so Christ for those who are tossed in this world. And as it was first commanded that Jonah preach to the Ninevites, but Jonah's preaching did not reach them until after the whale vomited him out, so prophecy was sent ahead to the Gentiles, but it did not reach the Gentiles except after the resurrection of Christ.

**35.** Now as for the fact that he set up a tabernacle for himself and sat down over against the city of Nineveh, awaiting what would befall it, the Prophet bore the character of another signification. For he prefigured the carnal people Israel. For this people too was sorrowful at the salvation of the Ninevites, that is, at the redemption and liberation of the Gentiles. Whence Christ came to call, not the righteous, but sinners to repentance. The shade of the gourd, then, over his head was the promises of the Old Testament, or those very gifts in which there was, as the Apostle says, *a shadow of things to come*[^21], offering, as it were, a defense from the heat of temporal evils in the land of promise. But the morning worm, by whose gnawing the gourd withered, is again Christ himself, from whose mouth, when the Gospel was spread abroad, all those things which flourished temporally among the Israelites, or with a prior shadowy signification, wither away emptied out. And now that people, having lost the kingdom of Jerusalem, and the priesthood, and the sacrifice, which was wholly a shadow of what was to come, is scorched in its captive dispersion by the great heat of tribulation, as Jonah, it is written, was by the burning of the sun, and grieves heavily; and yet the salvation of the Gentiles and of the penitent is set before his grief and the shade which he loved.

**36.** Let the pagans still cackle, and now deride with prouder garrulity Christ the worm and this interpretation of the prophetic sacrament, while yet that same worm gradually and little by little consumes them too. For of all such things Isaiah prophesies, through whom God says to us: *Hear me, you who know judgment, my people, in whose heart is my law: do not fear the reproaches of men, and do not be overcome by their detraction, nor count it a great thing that they despise you. For like a garment, so will they be consumed by time, and like wool they will be eaten by the moth; but my justice abides forever*[^22]. Let us, then, recognize the morning worm, because even in that psalm whose title is inscribed *For the morning receiving*[^23], he himself deigned to call himself by this name: *I,* he says, *am a worm and not a man, the reproach of men and the outcast of the people*[^24]. This reproach is of those reproaches which we are bidden not to fear, through Isaiah saying, *Do not fear the reproaches of men*[^25]. By this worm, as by a moth, are eaten those who under his evangelical tooth marvel day by day at their own dwindling as they fail. Let us recognize him, and for the sake of divine salvation let us endure human reproaches. He is a worm because of the humility of the flesh; perhaps also because of the Virgin's childbearing. For this little creature is for the most part born from flesh, or from any earthly thing, without any intercourse. He is of the morning, because he rose at daybreak. That gourd, surely, could have withered even without any little worm. Finally, if God had a worm necessary for this, what need was there to add "of the morning," unless that that worm might be recognized which sings *for the morning receiving*: *But I am a worm and not a man*[^26]?

**37.** What is clearer than this prophecy, now by the very effect and fulfillment of events? If this worm was derided as he hung on the cross, as is written in the same psalm, *They spoke with their lips and shook their head: He hoped in God, let him deliver him; let him save him, since he wants him*[^27], when those things were fulfilled which he foretold there: *They pierced my hands and feet, they numbered all my bones. But they themselves considered and looked upon me, they divided my garments among themselves, and over my clothing they cast lots*[^28]—which is prophesied as future in the ancient book with as great a manifestation as it is recited as accomplished in the new Gospel—but if in this humility, as I had begun to say, this worm was derided, is he still to be derided when we behold those things fulfilled which the psalm itself has as its sequel: *All the ends of the earth will remember and be turned to the Lord, and all the families of the nations will worship in his sight; for the kingdom is the Lord's, and he will rule over the nations*[^29]? Thus the Ninevites were brought to mind and were turned to the Lord. This salvation of the repentance of the Gentiles, so long before prefigured in Jonah, Israel grieved over, as it now grieves stripped of its shade and wounded by the heat. Let it be permitted to anyone, indeed, to disclose in whatever other way—provided it be according to the rule of faith—all the other things concerning the prophet Jonah which are veiled in mysteries. But that plain fact, that he was for three days in the belly of the whale, it is not lawful to understand otherwise than as we have recalled it revealed by the heavenly Master himself in the Gospel.

**38.** We have expounded what was proposed, as we could; but let the one who proposed them now be a Christian, lest perhaps, while he waits to finish his questions on the sacred books, he first finish this life before he passes from death to life. For it can be borne that, before he is steeped in the Christian sacraments, he inquires about the resurrection of the dead. It may perhaps even be conceded that he inquired about Christ, why he came so late, and if there are any other few and great questions to which the rest are subservient. But if such a question as this, *With what measure you measure, it will be measured to you*[^30], or such a one as this about Jonah—if he thinks to finish all such questions before he is a Christian, he gives very little thought either to the human condition or to his own age now. For there are countless such questions, which are not to be finished before faith, lest life be finished without faith. But once faith has been firmly held, then, for exercising the devout delight of faithful minds, they are to be inquired into most studiously; and what shines forth in them is to be shared without the pride of arrogance; while what remains hidden is to be tolerated without loss of salvation.
