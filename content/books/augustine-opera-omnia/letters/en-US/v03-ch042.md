# Letter 193

To the most beloved lord, and in the members of Christ to be proclaimed with most sincere love, son Mercator, from Augustine, greetings in the Lord.

**1. 1.** The letters of your charity, which, first sent by you, I received at Carthage, affected me with so great a joy, that even in the later ones I most gratefully received your being angry that I had not written back to you. For that very indignation of yours was not the beginning of a quarrel, but an indication of love. But that I did not write back from Carthage, it was not the occasion of bearers that was lacking, but other more urgent matters held us most occupied and most intent, until we set out thence. But when we departed thence, we proceeded as far as Mauretania Caesariensis, whither an ecclesiastical necessity drew us; through which whole lands, while diverse things that were thrust upon the senses snatched our intention this way and that, there was the insistence of no admonisher that I write back to you, no opportunity of a bearer occurred. Then returning, I found among our people other letters of your sincerity already exasperated by complaint, and another book against the new heretics, filled with testimonies of the holy Scriptures; which being read and run through, even those which you had first sent, because the most opportune occasion of our most dear brother Albinus, acolyte of the Roman Church, offered itself, it had to be answered.

**1. 2.** I, then, most beloved son, far be it that I should negligently receive you writing to me, or sending your writings to me to be considered, or contemn them with proud vanity, especially since my joy about you is so much the greater, the more unexpected and unforeseen it comes: for I confess, I did not know that you had advanced so much. And what ought to be more desirable to us, than that there abound those who refute everywhere the errors infesting the catholic faith and lying in wait for the weak and unlearned brothers, and keenly and faithfully defend the Church of Christ against the profane novelties of words; since, as it is written: *The multitude of the wise is the health of the world*[^1]? I have inspected your heart, then, in your writings, as much as I could, and found it to be embraced, and to be exhorted that, with most persevering diligence, you stretch yourself forward, the Lord helping your strength, who gave you the strength that he might nourish it.

**2. 3.** But they have come not a little near to the truth in the question about the baptism of little ones—whom we try to recall, wandering, into the way—since they confess that an infant, however recently poured forth by the bringing-forth of the mother, nevertheless believes through those by whom it is offered to be baptized. For when they say, as you write, that infants do not so believe unto the remission of sins, as though those too be remitted to them, whom they think to have no sin; but (because they too receive the same washing by which there comes about, in whomever it comes about, the remission of sins) that they believe this to come about in others, which does not come about in them: when, then, they say, "They do not so believe, but they so believe"; surely they do not doubt that they believe. Let them, then, hear the Lord: *He who believes in the Son has eternal life; but he who is unbelieving toward the Son shall not see life, but the wrath of God remains over him*[^2]. Wherefore infants, because they are made believers through others by whom they are offered to be baptized, are surely through those unbelieving, if they are with such as do not believe that they ought to be offered, while they believe it profits nothing: and through this, if through believers they believe, and have eternal life; surely through unbelievers they are unbelieving, and shall not see life, but the wrath of God remains over them. For it was not said: "It comes over them"; but: *It remains over them*, because from origin it was already in them, nor is it taken away from them at all except by the grace of God through Jesus Christ our Lord. About this wrath it is read also in the book of Job: *Man born of woman, of short life and full of wrath*[^3]. Whence, then, the wrath of God over the innocence of a little one, except by the lot and filth of original sin? about which it is likewise written in that same book, that no one is clean from it, not even an infant whose life is of one day upon the earth.

**2. 4.** So it is not the case that the argument so insistently pressed against them has accomplished nothing, and that the catholic voices resounding on every side into their ears have had no effect, since, wishing to argue against the sacraments of the Church, they nonetheless confessed that infants believe. Let them not, then, promise life to such infants, even if they have not been baptized: for of what other life is it said, *He who does not believe in the Son shall not see life*[^4]? Nor let them so declare them estranged from the kingdom of heaven that they still defend them from damnation: for what is signified by the wrath which the Lord testifies *remains upon* the one who does not believe[^5], if not damnation? A great advance has surely been made, and the case has been settled without any troublesome dispute. For if they grant that infants believe, then beyond doubt, just as that sentence holds them, *Unless one is born again of water and the Spirit, he shall not enter the kingdom of heaven*[^6], so too does this one, which is the same Lord's: *He who believes and is baptized shall be saved; but he who does not believe shall be condemned*[^7]. Since, then, these men confess that when infants are baptized they are believers, let them not doubt that those who do not believe are condemned; and let them now dare to say, if they can, that those who draw no evil from their origin and bear no contagion of sin are condemned by a just God.

**3. 5.** But as for what you mentioned in your letter, that they object to us Enoch and Elijah, on the ground that they did not die but were taken with their own bodies out of this human way of life, I do not understand how this helps them in the matter under discussion. For, to pass over the fact that they too are said to be going to die afterward—as many expound John's Apocalypse concerning those two prophets of whom, with their names left unspoken, it speaks, namely that those two holy men will then appear with the very bodies in which they now live, so that they too, like the other martyrs, may die for the truth of Christ—to pass over this, then, and leave that question aside, however it may stand, how, I ask you, does it help these men? For they do not thereby show that human beings do not die in body on account of sin. For if God, who grants their very sins to so many of his faithful, willed also to grant to some this penalty of sin, who are we to answer God why one man is dealt with thus and another thus?

**3. 6.** We therefore say what the Apostle most plainly says: *The body indeed is dead because of sin, but the spirit is life because of righteousness. But if the Spirit of him who raised Christ from the dead dwells in you, he who raised Christ from the dead will give life also to your mortal bodies through his Spirit who dwells in you*[^8]. Yet we do not say this in such a way as to deny that God can do now, in those whom he wills, without death what we believe without doubt he will do for so many after death. Nor for that reason will it be false that *through one man sin entered into this world, and through sin death, and so it passed to all men*[^9]. For this was said because, unless death had entered through sin, there would be no death. For even when we say, "All are sent into hell because of sins," do we therefore say something false, because not all human beings are sent into hell? That is true, not because every human being is sent there, but because no one is sent there except by the deserts of sins. Of the same kind, by contrast, is also that sentence: *Through the justification of one, unto all men unto justification of life*[^10]—for not all human beings belong to the justification of Christ; but this was said because no one is justified except through Christ.

**3. 7.** That question, rather, is not without reason the troubling one: why the penalty of sin remains when the sin does not remain; that is, if the death even of the body is the penalty of sin, this is a question rather—why an infant dies once it has been baptized—than why Elijah did not die once he was justified. For in the one case, the sin being ended, it is troubling that the penalty of sin has followed; in the other, the sin likewise being ended, it ought not to be troubling if the penalty of sin does not follow. If, then, we have, as far as we could with the Lord's help, resolved that question about the death of the baptized—why, the abolition of sin having been accomplished, a certain penalty of sin nevertheless follows—in the books On the Baptism of Infants, which I know are very well known to you, how much less ought this to trouble us where it is said, "Why did the righteous Elijah not die, if death is the penalty of sin?"—as if one were to say, "Why did the sinner Elijah not die, if death is the penalty of sin?"

**3. 8.** Unless perhaps they raise one difficulty from another and say, "If Enoch and Elijah were so far from having sin that they did not even suffer death, which is the penalty of sin, how is it that no one here lives without sin?" As though it were not more probable to answer them, "Those whom the Lord willed to live with their sins ended were for that reason not permitted to live here, because here no one can live without sin." But these and similar things could be said against them only if they proved for certain, from some other source, that those men will never die. But since they cannot teach this, and since it is better believed that those men will come to death, there is no reason why they should wish to set them before us as an objection that will in no way help their cause.

**4. 9.** But those of whom the Apostle spoke, when he was speaking of the resurrection of the dead, *And we who are alive, who remain, shall together with them be caught up in the clouds to meet Christ in the air; and so we shall always be with the Lord*[^11], do indeed raise some question; but in themselves, not on account of these men. For even if they too are not going to die, I do not at all see how they help these men, since such things can be said of these as were said of those two. But in truth, as far as the words of the blessed Apostle are concerned, he seems to assert that certain people, at the end of the age, when the Lord comes and the resurrection of the dead is to take place, will not die, but, found alive, will suddenly be changed into that immortality which is given also to the other saints, and will be caught up together with them, as he says, in the clouds; nor has anything else seemed right to me, whenever I have wished to ponder these words.

**4. 10.** But on this point I would rather hear those more learned, lest the Apostle be found to say even to those who think that some will pass over to perpetual life made alive without death first preceding: *You fool, what you sow is not made alive unless it first dies*[^12]. For that too which is read in many manuscripts, *We shall all rise again*[^13]—how could this come about unless we all die? For there is no resurrection unless death has preceded. And what some manuscripts have, *we shall all sleep*, far more easily and plainly compels this to be understood; and if anything else of the kind is found in the sacred Scriptures, it seems to drive us to suppose that no human being will attain immortality unless death has preceded. Accordingly, where the Apostle said, *And we who are alive, who remain at the coming of the Lord, shall not go before those who have fallen asleep. For the Lord himself, at the command, at the voice of the archangel, and at the trumpet of God, will descend from heaven; and the dead in Christ will rise first; then we who are alive, who remain, shall together with them be caught up in the clouds to meet Christ in the air; and so we shall always be with the Lord*[^14]—I would wish, as I said, to hear those more learned on these matters, and, if these words can somehow be so expounded that in them it can be understood that all human beings who are living, or are to live after us, are going to die, to correct what I once thought otherwise on this point. For we ought not to be teachers unwilling to be taught: and surely it is better that a small man be corrected than that a hard one be broken; so that by the things we have written our own weakness or that of others may be exercised and instructed in such a way that nevertheless no canonical authority, as it were, is established in them.

**4. 11.** For if in these words of the Apostle no other sense can be found, and it becomes clear that he wished to be understood as the words themselves seem to cry out—that is, that at the end of the age and at the Lord's coming there will be people who are not stripped of the body but clothed over with immortality, so that the mortal is swallowed up by life—then to this view there will doubtless agree what we confess in the rule of faith, that the Lord is to come to judge the living and the dead; so that here we do not understand the living to be the righteous and the dead the unrighteous, although the righteous and the unrighteous are to be judged, but the living to be those whom his coming finds not yet to have departed, and the dead those whom it finds already to have departed from their bodies. And if this is established to be so, those words will have to be examined as to how we are to take them: *What you sow is not made alive unless it first dies*, and *We shall all rise again*, or *We shall all sleep*, so that they are not opposed to this view, by which some are believed to be going to live for eternity even with their bodies, without having tasted death.

**4. 12.** But whichever of these two be found the truer and more penetrating understanding, what does it have to do with the case of these men, whether all are struck with the death that is owed, or some are spared this condition—since it is nevertheless established that death not only of the soul but also of the body would not have followed had sin not preceded, and that it is by a more wondrous power of grace that the righteous come back to life from death to eternal blessedness than that they do not come into the experience of death? Let this be said enough concerning those of whom you wrote to me; though I now think that they no longer say that, even if Adam had not sinned, he would have died at least in body.

**4. 13.** As for the rest, regarding the question of the resurrection, on account of those who are believed not to be going to die but to pass from this mortality to immortality without death intervening, a more diligent inquiry must be made; and if you have heard or read, or have even been able yourself to think out, anything settled and definite on this point by reasonable and complete discussion, or if you can yet hear, read, or think it out, I ask that you not be reluctant to send it to me. For I (this must be confessed to your Charity) love to learn more than to teach. For we are admonished of this also by the apostle James when he says, *Let every man be quick to hear, slow to speak*[^15]. So that we may learn, the sweetness of truth ought to invite us; but that we may teach, the necessity of love ought to compel us—and here it is rather to be wished that this necessity by which one person teaches another something might pass away, so that we may all be *taught by God*[^16]; which indeed we are, when we learn the things that pertain to true piety, even when a human being seems to teach them. For *neither he who plants is anything, nor he who waters, but God who gives the increase*[^17]. Since, then, if God did not give the increase, the apostolic planters and waterers would be nothing, how much more I, or you, or any people of this time, when we seem to ourselves to be teachers!
