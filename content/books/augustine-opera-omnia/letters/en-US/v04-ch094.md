# Letter 29\*

TO THE LORD, DESERVEDLY MOST BELOVED AND MOST SINCERE SON AND FELLOW DEACON PAULINUS, AUGUSTINE SENDS GREETINGS IN THE LORD

**1.** How I may comply with your wish about setting out the deeds of the martyrs in a discourse of mine—though I most greatly desire it—I do not yet gather. For I have read what you deigned to send and have found some things narrated in the discourse of others, but some things expressed only from the public court records, which delighted me most. Accordingly, if I should wish afterward to narrate myself those things in which others have preceded me, I shall seem as it were a meddlesome teacher or a superfluous worker; but if I should wish in speaking to recall those things that are contained only in the public court records, I fear that I may not only fail to assist the more genuine feeling that they themselves stirred in me—when the records alone were read by me [...].

**2.** For, to be moved to do something of the sort—when I spoke to your Charity about this matter—I had been delighted by certain things composed about the martyrs by the old man of venerable memory Ambrose, which, compared with the others whose writings on these matters I had read, I did not set ahead, but the old man Ambrose narrated especially those things that could not be known from the public records. And therefore his work appears not only by no means superfluous, but even most necessary, just as is read, composed by I-know-not-whom, also about the most blessed martyr Cyprian—that, in his gardens, when he was summoned to his passion, when he was being held at Vicus Saturni and a multitude of the brothers kept watch before the doors, he ordered the girls to be guarded—and if there is anything of this kind that cannot be found in the public court records.

**3.** But what am I to do, who have nothing from which to learn what is to be known about the martyrs beyond the public records, except what I had read of those who preceded me in this work? But what I read in the public records, if I should wish to narrate this alone in a discourse of mine, I shall be attempting rather to discolor it than to illustrate it. I ask that you consider this suggestion of mine and, with brotherly confidence, write back what seems good to you.
