# Letter 26

Augustine to Licentius

**1.** I have scarcely found an occasion of writing to you; who would believe it? But Licentius, at least, must believe me. I do not want you to search out the causes and reasons, which, even if they could be given, I do not owe to the faith by which you believe me. For I did not even receive your letter through those by whom I could send back what I write. But what you asked me to ask, I have seen to, through a letter, as far as seemed to need to be brought forward; but what I have accomplished, you will see. And if it is not yet accomplished, I will either act more insistently when I learn of it, or when you yourself remind me again. Thus far let me have spoken with you of the things that make the chains of this life clamor: now receive in few words the surgings of my heart concerning your hope, which is not transitory—in what way the road to God may lie open for you.

**2.** My Licentius, while you again and again refuse and dread the fetters of wisdom, I fear that you are being fettered most strongly and most ruinously by mortal things. For those whom wisdom has first bound and tamed by certain exercising labors, she afterward looses, and gives herself to those set free to be enjoyed; and those whom she has first trained by temporal bonds, she will afterward bind by eternal embraces—than which bond nothing more pleasant or more solid can be conceived. These first bonds I confess to be somewhat hard; but those last I would call neither hard, because they are most sweet, nor soft, because they are most firm. What, then, are they but that which cannot be told, yet can be believed and hoped for and loved? But the bonds of this world have a true harshness, a false pleasantness; a certain pain, an uncertain delight; a hard labor, a fearful rest; a thing full of misery, an empty hope of blessedness. Into these do you insert your neck and hands and feet, when you both desire to be subjugated to honors of this kind, and think your deeds not otherwise fruitful, and seek to cling to that to which you ought to have gone not only uninvited but not even compelled? Here perhaps you will give me the answer of Terence's slave: Oh, you pour out words of wisdom here! Take it, then, that I may pour them out rather than pour them away. Or if I sing, while you dance to another tune, not even so do I repent. For the singing itself has its own gladness, even when it does not move to itself the limbs of the one to whom it is sung with a strain full of love. Certain words in your letters moved me, but to deal with these, while concern for your deeds and your whole life burns me, I have thought unfitting. A Poem of Licentius to Augustine his teacher

**3.** Scrutinizing the hidden path of profound Varro, my mind grows dull, and, terrified, flees the hostile light.  
No wonder, for all my care for reading lies prostrate when you do not lend a hand, and dreads to rise alone.  
For though love urged me to unroll the intricate compendia of so great a man, and to touch his sacred meanings—  
he who gave number its tones, and discoursed that the world sings to the Thunderer and dances in matching choirs—  
he has wrapped my breast in a manifold mist, and the violence of these matters has drawn a cloud over my mind.  
So I, distracted, demand the forms of figures set down without sand, and stumble upon other grievous shadows.  
In short, the causes of the stars and their bright courses, whose obscure positions he shows through the clouds,  
I lay nodding over them, so that no one who keeps us from knowing the hidden places of heaven could give such a total collapse,  
nor the persona of the dead, trusting in the caverns.  
For the old fictions of the Pelasgians tell of Proteus, who, when he is unwilling to disclose the future to the anxious,  
foams as a boar, flows as a wave, roars as a lion, hisses as a snake, yet, caught at last, gives small gifts in return for the birds.  
But as for me, who am tormented too much by graver cares, I seek the sweet things of the soul, the half-sweet fodder; the answers of Varro lie hidden. What protection, or nymph, with suppliant song should I beg, and what rivers should I ask for?  
Or should I call on you by name, whom the ruler of bright Olympus set over the springs of infants, and bade pour forth far-off streams from the hidden richness of the mind?  
Bring help at once, master, do not forsake my weak strength, and begin with me to overturn the sacred clods: for the time, unless mortal things deceive me, slips away and draws on toward old age. For you our Apollo fills the heart, and reconciles his Father and the Father of the gods, and shows the good law and the bloody peace, and, the veil drawn aside, lays open each thing.  
For you had perhaps measured twenty long circuits of the sun, when the most beautiful order of the world, richer than empires and sweeter than all nectar, seized you, and fixed you, wandering, and set you in the middle, whence you could direct your gaze to all things.  
O good man, take up the journey of the years, by how much wisdom grows by love of itself, ever finding new summits.  
Go on the way by which the glorious offspring of the Thunderer leads you, leveling the steep places into flat plains.  
And when the evening has put off in your heart the light-bringing risings, and has blessed over the holy fire, be yourself mindful of me: you who set a thirsty ear to the unconquered laws, beat your breasts with your palms, stretch your limbs on the ground, and rightly stir up your griefs, and forbid the wickedness—God commands one thing to all, the bishop admonishes, and the coming thunderbolts terrify.  
Oh, if only the former dawn would call back for me, on its gladdening wheels, the suns now spent, which, trying free leisure with you and the bright laws of the good, we passed in the midst of Italy, among the high mountains!  
The hard frosts of the hoary cold would not keep me back, nor the fierce storm of the Zephyrs and the roaring of Boreas, from pressing your footsteps with anxious step.  
Only bid this work, and let blood water my limbs: at the solstice we will follow the Neuri, in winter the Hister.  
The unknown Garamas will loose for me the bonds of his nation, and the Hypanian stream, fleeing the Xampaean lakes, sounds foaming by the Scythian waves of the Callipidae.  
We will go too to the Leuci, where Leucia stretches toward the risings of the sun; and the deserted peaks of vast Casius, with which Casia makes the Epidamnian crags equal to itself, whence I might see the resting dawn, and the chariots unyoked, and the day laid to sleep at midnight—at your urging I will seek it: for no labor or fear terrifies, where he hears the innocent by their open prayers to God.  
And now I would forsake the seats of the sons of Romulus, the empty roof-peaks of the dwelling, the riotous houses, and the empty tumults, and come once and wholly into your heart, did not my mind, set upon marriage, hold me back as I go.  
Believe, O learned one, my ills and my true grief, that without you the sails promise no harbors, and we wander far over the troubled seas of life.  
Headlong, as sailors in a dense fog, whom the southern fury and the whistling blast from Eurus have struck, and the whirlwind has bereft of their snatched-away helmsmen; straightway the wretches are tossed amid the broken waves: neither the deck nor the prow, nor then the sails, can bear the storms, and the art of steering lies stupefied:  
so the wind drives me, and the surges of desire roll me into the death-bearing sea, nor are the lands at once at hand.  
But, reckoning with myself your bright words, master, I rather think these are to be believed by you: it is a cunning thing, it deceives, and weaves nets for our minds.  
For, forgetful of things past, the present things I now offer to you, dear one, we now, slipped from your breast—alas for me, whither shall I be carried, whence I would wish to lay open my mind to you!  
Sooner shall the doves fit pious dwellings under the Aegean, and the changed halcyon build her nests on a tree; sooner shall the hungry lioness feed pursuing calves, and the wolf, long unfed, nourish tender lambs, and, changing the world divided into their own parts, either the Barcaean shall feed the bull or Hyrcania the lizards: sooner, again ill-frightened by the feasts of Thyestes, shall the interrupted day vanish into recurring risings; sooner shall the rains give the Nile, the deer wander above the upper air, the mountains grow hoary, and the rivers clap their hands, than your gifts come to me behind my back, master.  
Love wards it off, and the common bond of the honorable holds. Here, here reigns the glory of friendship, with the enemy put to flight.  
For not for the sake of glassy wealth and rebel gold do we join the assents of our minds: nor did the crumbling fortune of the crowd, which separates the lofty, join us.  
But labor, gathering up inner things, the common things in books, and the things discovered by your mind, and the noble teaching uttered, and the good answers brought back in reply.  
And my Calliope, although she shudders at you close at hand, exalted, and hides her face, handling useless things: yet this, this bond of mind and these faithful ties, not even he who broke the Alps strengthened on their mountainous crags, and crushed the Italian cities with their walls, could break, or terrify anything of our strength.  
Go far off, you waters with the swelling windings of the Oxus, or from the Aremphaei the Rhipaean heights or the towns of the Caspian, and the Cimmerian homes that a broad river separates; and let the regions of the Maeotians, which the Pontus of Helle overwhelms, stretch far the boundaries of Europe and Asia.  
Did not Dodona, wearying her herds along both flanks, cut off the Molossians from the borders of Thalarus, and the kindred Arabs? Nor did a friendly treaty of peace remain between the Sidonians and the kingdom of Pelops and the sacrilegious Phrygians, although for a time there was a common hospitality for all. What, finally, shall I sing of the discord of brothers and their fights? what of the honorable beatings by parents, what of the fury of mothers and the proud sons?  
There is even among the gods above a harmonious discord of things, and as many rites flow as opinion gives laws.  
Nor does one love hold all: not if Boreas should give me a hundred murmurs and as many lives, and a hundred tongues, stiff with adamant, should roar through my mouths, could I have the power to recount what the nature of the ancient places, joined together before, has set apart, and what the rounded gravel has filed away from the smooth world.  
But I pass over us, that we rise from one city, that one house bore us, that we are touched by one blood of the ages, that the Christian faith has joined us, and that, though an immense journey separates us, and the interfused expanse of the sea confines us, love despises both.  
He who, scorning the joys of the eyes, ever enjoys an absent friend; since he hangs from the deep heart, and searches out the fodder of the inner fiber.  
Meanwhile there will come whatever writings of the good things to come, of the saving discourse, and those to be likened to the honeycombs of the ancients, reckoning which, conceived in the deep breast, you have spewed forth into the light, the nectar-honey—these very things will render you present to me, if you grant my wish and hand over the books in which slow Music reclines upon you, for I burn wholly for them.  
Grant it, that the truth may thus lie open to us by reason, that it may flow more than the Eridanus, and that the contagions of the world may flit in vain around the fields of us your husbandmen. God, not the desires, is to be served

**4.** If your verse were turned the wrong way by ill-ordered measures, if it did not stand by its own laws, if it offended the hearer's ear by unequal measures, you would surely be ashamed, and you would neither put it off nor cease until you had ordered, corrected, settled, and made even your verse, by learning and practicing the art of meter with the sharpest zeal and any labor whatever: why, then, when you yourself are turned the wrong way, ill-ordered—when you yourself do not stand by the laws of your God, nor harmonize, in the living of your life, with the honorable prayers of your friends and with this very learning of yours—do you think it must be cast behind your back and neglected? As if you were of less worth to yourself than the sound of your tongue, and as if your offending the ears of God by ill-ordered morals were a lighter matter than if grammatical authority were angry at your ill-ordered syllables. You write: Oh, if only the former dawn would call back for me, on its gladdening wheels, the suns now spent, which, trying free leisure with you and the bright laws of the good, we passed in the midst of Italy, among the high mountains! The hard frosts of the hoary cold would not keep me back, nor the fierce storm of the Zephyrs and the roaring of Boreas, from pressing your footsteps with anxious step. Only bid this work. Wretched me, if I do not bid it, if I do not compel and command it, if I do not ask and beseech it. But if your ears are closed against my words, let them lie open to your own mouth, lie open to your own poem; hear yourself, hardest, most monstrous, most deaf of men. What good to me is a golden tongue and an iron heart? With what songs—nay, with what laments—shall I suffice to bewail your poems, in which I see what a soul, what a talent it is not permitted me to lay hold of and offer up to our God? Do you wait for me to bid you be good, be quiet, be blessed; as if any day could dawn more welcome to me than that I might enjoy your talent in the Lord—or do you truly not know how I hunger and thirst for you, or do you not confess this very thing in that poem? Call back the mind with which you wrote these things; now say to me: *Only bid this work.* Behold my bidding: give me yourself, if this is the only work; give yourself to my Lord, who is the Lord of us all, who gave you that talent. For what am I, except your servant through him, and your fellow servant under him?

**5.** Or does he himself not bid it? Hear the Gospel: *Jesus stood*, it says, *and cried out*[^1]: *Come to me, all you who labor and are heavy laden, and I will refresh you. Take my yoke upon you, and learn of me, for I am meek and humble of heart, and you will find rest for your souls. For my yoke is easy, and my burden is light*[^2]. If these things are not heard, or are heard only as far as the ears, do you wait, Licentius, for Augustine to bid his fellow servant, and not rather to bewail that his Lord bids in vain—nay, does not bid, but in a way invites and asks, that those who labor may be refreshed by him? But of course, to a most strong and overconfident neck, the yoke of the world is more pleasant than the yoke of Christ: who, if he forced us to labor, see who would be forcing, and with what reward he would force. Go into Campania, learn how Paulinus, the excellent and holy servant of God, unhesitatingly shook off so great a pride of this age, with a neck the more noble the more humble, that he might submit it to the yoke of Christ, as he did submit it; and now, with that ruler of his journey, quiet and modest, he exults. Go, learn with what riches of talent he offers him sacrifices of praise, pouring back to him whatever good he has received from him, lest he lose all, if he does not store it up in him from whom he has these things.

**6.** Why do you seethe? Why do you toss about? Why do you lend your ear to the imaginings of death-bearing pleasures, and turn it away from us? They lie, they die, they drag into death. They lie, Licentius: "that the truth may thus lie open to us by reason, as you wish; that it may flow more than the Eridanus." Nothing speaks the truth but Truth: Christ is the Truth[^3]; let us come to him, that we may not labor. Even though he himself does not refresh us, let us take his yoke upon us, and learn of him, for he is meek and humble of heart, and we shall find rest for our souls. For his yoke is easy, and his burden is light[^4]. The devil seeks to be adorned by you. If you had found a golden cup in the earth, you would give it to the Church of God. You have received from God a talent spiritually golden, and you minister with it to your lusts, and in it you offer up yourself as a drink to Satan! Do not, I beseech you; so may you at some time feel with what a wretched and pitiable heart I have written these things; and may you have mercy on me now, if you have grown cheap to yourself.

[^1]: See John 7:37
[^2]: Matt 11:28–30
[^3]: See John 14:6
[^4]: See Matt 11:29–30
