# Letter 204

TO DULCITIUS, EXIMIOUS LORD AND HONORABLE SON, AUGUSTINE, GREETING IN THE LORD.

**1.** I ought not to have disregarded your petition, by which you desired to be instructed by me how it behooves you to answer the heretics, whose salvation is sought in the mercy of the Lord, and also by the instancy of your strenuousness. For although their immense multitudes (whereat we much rejoice) understand what benefit is conferred on them, some of them, however, ungrateful to God and men by a pitiable instinct of fury, where they cannot lay us waste by their own slaughters, believe they terrify us by their own destruction—either seeking their own gladness from our deaths, or our sadness from their own deaths. But the furious error of a few men ought not to impede the salvation of so many and so great peoples. What we wish toward them not only God and prudent men, but even they themselves, although they are most hostile to us, perceive. For when they think we are to be terrified by their own ruin, they do not doubt that we fear lest they perish.

**2.** But what shall we do, seeing that many, with the Lord's help, through your occasion find the way of peace? Can we, or ought we, prohibit you from this instancy for unity, while we fear lest some, most hardened and most cruel to themselves, destroy themselves not by our will but by their own? We would indeed wish that all who bear against Christ the sign of Christ, and glory against the Gospel out of the Gospel itself, which they do not understand, should depart from their perversity, and rejoice with us in his unity. But since God, by a disposition hidden enough, yet just, has predestined some of them to the extreme penalties, doubtless it is better that, with very many of incomparable number reintegrated and gathered from that pestilent division and dispersion, some perish by their own fires, than that all alike should burn in the eternal fires of hell by the desert of their sacrilegious dissension. For the Church grieves over these who perish just as holy David grieved over his rebellious son, for whose salvation he had, with solicitous love, commanded that it be preserved. For when that son was, by the desert of his abominable impiety, destroyed, David groaned even with the testimony of a tearful voice: yet, with the proud and malignant one departing to his own place, the people of God, who had been divided by his tyranny, recognized their king; and the perfection of unity consoled the father's grief over the lost son.

**3.** We do not, then, reprehend, eximious lord and honorable son, that you judged such men at Thamugadi should first be admonished by an edict: but because there you said, "Know that you are to be given over to deserved slaughter," they supposed, as their replies indicate, that you had threatened this—that you would kill them when apprehended—not understanding that you spoke of that slaughter which they themselves wish to inflict upon themselves. For you did not receive over them the right of the sword by any laws, nor is it commanded by the imperial constitutions, whose execution was enjoined upon you, that they be killed. But in your second edict, of your Charity, you made more plainly clear what you wished. And as for the fact that you thought even their bishop himself should be addressed by letter most humanely, you showed with how great gentleness those are tempered in the catholic Church who, even by the power of the Christian Emperor, are set over errors to be corrected, whether by terrifying or by chastising; except that you treated him with more honorific words than befitted a heretic.

**4.** But as for the fact that you wished me to answer his reply, I believe you judged that this too ought to be furnished to the Thamugadians, namely, that the deceitful teaching of the very one by whom they were being seduced should be somewhat more diligently refuted: but we are both most occupied, and we have already, in very many others of our little works, refuted vain talk of this kind. For now I know not how often, by disputing and writing, we have shown that they cannot have the death of martyrs, because they do not have the life of Christians; since it is not the penalty that makes a martyr, but the cause. We have also taught that free choice was so given to man, that nevertheless, both by divine and by human laws, punishments of grave sins are most rightly appointed; and that it pertains to the religious kings of the earth to restrain with fitting severity not only adulteries or homicides or other shameful deeds and crimes of this kind, but also sacrileges: and that those are much deceived who think that such men as these are received by us as they are, because we do not rebaptize them. For how are they received as they are, when they are heretics, and by passing over to us become catholics? For depraved hearts are not therefore forbidden to be corrected, because sacraments once given are not allowed to be repeated.

**5.** But about the most furious deaths which some of them inflict upon themselves—on account of which they are accustomed to be detestable and abominable even to many of their own, whose minds so great madness has not possessed—we have often answered them according to the Scriptures and the reasonings of Christians, since it is written, *He who is wicked to himself, to whom will he be good?*[^1] Or certainly let him who thinks it expedient and lawful for himself to kill himself also kill his neighbor set in the same temptations, when he wishes to die, since the Scripture says, *You shall love your neighbor as yourself*[^2]. But that, with no laws or lawful powers commanding, it is not lawful to kill another, even one willing and asking and no longer able to live, the Scripture of Kingdoms sufficiently indicates, where King David ordered the slayer of King Saul to be killed, when that man had said that he had been asked by Saul, already wounded and half-alive, to do this, and to free, by a single stroke of a wound, his soul struggling against the bonds of the body and desiring to be loosed, from those torments. Accordingly, since everyone who, without any authority of lawful power, kills a man is a homicide, whoever kills himself would not be a homicide, only if he is not a man. All these things in many ways we have said in many other of our discourses and letters.

**6.** Nevertheless—what must be confessed—about that elder Razis, whom, constrained by the utmost lack of examples, they boast that they have at last scarcely found, in the books of the Maccabees, as if for an authority for the crime by which they destroy themselves, having searched through all the ecclesiastical authorities—I recall that I have never yet answered them. But, what may suffice for your Charity and for any prudent persons to refute them: if, for the life of Christians, they are prepared to transfer the examples of all deeds from the Jewish nation and those Writings, then let them transfer this too. But if there are there very many things even of those men who are praised by the truth of those Writings, which are either not suited to this present time or were even at that time not rightly done; of such a kind is also this which that Razis committed against himself: who, when he was noble among his own, and had much advanced in Judaism (which the Apostle says were to him, in comparison with Christian righteousness, losses and dung), and on account of this same was called the father of the Jews—what wonder is it if, as upon a man, proud exaltation crept in, so that he preferred to be slain by his own hand rather than, after that loftiness in the sight of his own, to endure an unworthy servitude in the hands of his enemies?

**7.** Such things are wont to be praised in the writings of the Gentiles. But in these books of the Maccabees, although the man himself was praised, his deed is nevertheless narrated, not praised, and set as it were before the eyes to be judged rather than imitated; not indeed to be judged by our judgment, which we too as men might have, but by the judgment of sober teaching, which is clear in those very old Books too. For that Razis was far from those words where it is read: *Whatever shall be brought upon you, accept it; and in pain endure, and in your humility have patience*[^3]. That man, then, was not wise in choosing death, but impatient in bearing humiliation.

**8.** It is written that he wished to die nobly and manfully; but is it therefore wisely? Nobly, namely, lest as a captive he lose the liberty of his birth; and manfully, in that he had such great strength of soul as to be fit to kill himself; which, since he could not accomplish with the sword, he cast himself headlong from the wall, and thus, still alive, ran to a sheer rock, and there, now bloodless, drew forth his own entrails, which, torn apart with both hands, he scattered upon the people, and then afterward, exhausted, fell. These things are great, yet not good: for not everything that is great is good; since there are great evils too. God said, *You shall not kill the innocent and just*[^4]. If, then, that man was not innocent and just, why is he set forth to be imitated? But if he was innocent and just, why is the slayer of an innocent and just man—that is, of Razis himself—besides thought to be praised?

**9.** For now, lest it become too lengthy, let these things suffice for the filling of this letter. But I owe the Thamugadians this ministry of charity, since they have been well commended to me both by your wish and by my honorable and most dear son Eleusinus, who exercised the tribuneship among them, so that I should so answer both letters of Gaudentius, the bishop of the Donatists—and especially the later one, which he thinks he made according to the holy Scriptures—that nothing be thought to have been passed over.

[^1]: Sir 14:5
[^2]: Matt 22:39
[^3]: Sir 2:4
[^4]: Exod 23:7
