# Letter 64

To his most beloved brother and fellow presbyter Quintian, Augustine sends greeting in the Lord

**1.** We do not disdain to look upon bodies less beautiful, especially since our own souls are not yet beautiful, as we hope they will be, when he, ineffably beautiful, shall appear to us, in whom now, not seeing him, we believe: for then we shall be like him, since we shall see him as he is[^1]. Which we admonish that you too feel about your soul, if you receive me gladly and as a brother, and not presume that it is beautiful, but, as the Apostle prescribes, rejoice in hope, and do what follows; for thus he says: *Rejoicing in hope, patient in tribulation; for by hope we were saved*[^2]; as again the same man himself says: *But hope that is seen is not hope: for what anyone sees, why does he hope for it? But if we hope for what we do not see, we await it through patience*[^3]. Let this patience not fail in you, and in a good conscience *wait for the Lord, and act manfully, and let your heart be strengthened, and wait for the Lord*[^4].

**2.** It is indeed manifest that if you came to us, not in communion with the venerable bishop Aurelius, neither could you communicate with us; but we would do this with that love with which we do not doubt that he too does it. Nor yet on that account would your arrival be burdensome to us; because you too ought to do this with an even mind for the discipline of the Church, especially with your conscience safe, which you and God know. For neither did he, if he deferred your cause to be discussed, do it from hatred of you, and not from his own necessities; which, if you knew them as you know your own, you would neither wonder nor be saddened. Which we ask you also to believe about ours, because you likewise cannot know them. But there are bishops greater than we, and worthier in authority, and nearer in place, through whom you can more easily pursue the causes of the Church belonging to your care. Nor yet did I keep silent, before the venerable—and, by the due honor for his merits, to-be-received—brother and colleague of mine, the old man Aurelius, your tribulation and the complaint of your letter; but through a copy of your letter I took care to convey your innocence to him. But your letter I received either the day before or two days before the Lord's Birthday, when you intimated that he was about to come to the Church of Badesilis, by which you fear that the people of God will be troubled and corrupted. Wherefore by letter indeed I do not dare to address your people; but to write back to those who write to me, I could: but to write of my own accord to a people which is not committed to my dispensation, whence could I?

**3.** Nevertheless, what I say to you alone, who wrote to me, let it reach, through you yourself, to those to whom it is needful to be said. You yourselves, first, do not put the Church into scandal, by reading among the peoples scriptures which the ecclesiastical canon has not received; for with these the heretics, and especially the Manichees, are wont to overturn unskilled minds, whom I hear lie hidden gladly in your field. I wonder, then, at your prudence, that you admonished me that I should bid that those not be received who come to us from you to the monastery, that what was established by us in council might remain; and you do not remember that it was instituted in council which are the canonical Scriptures which ought to be read among the people of God. Review, then, the council, and commend to memory all things which you read there; and there too you will find that it was established only about clergy, not also about laymen, that those coming from anywhere not be received into a monastery. Not because mention was made of a monastery; but because it was so instituted, that no one receive another's clergyman. But in a recent council it was established that those who withdraw from any monastery, or are expelled, become neither elsewhere clergy nor superiors of monasteries. If, then, something has moved you about Privatio, know that he has not yet been received by us into a monastery; but I have sent his cause to the old man Aurelius, that what he shall establish about him, this I may do. For I wonder whether one can already be reckoned a reader who has only once read scriptures even not canonical. For if on that account he is now an ecclesiastical reader, surely that scripture too is ecclesiastical. But if that scripture is not ecclesiastical, whoever reads it, even though in church, is not an ecclesiastical reader. Yet about this young man, what shall seem good to the said prelate, this I ought to observe.

**4.** But the people of Vegesela, most dear with you to us in the bowels of Christ[^5], if they have been unwilling to receive a bishop degraded in a plenary council of Africa, will do it with a sound head, and can neither be compelled, nor ought to be. And whoever shall violently compel them, will show what sort of man he is; and will make it understood what sort he was before, when he wished nothing evil to be believed about himself. For no one is so betrayed as to what sort of cause he had, as he who, through secular powers, or any violences whatever, with disturbance and complaint, tries to recover the honor which he lost. For he does not wish to serve Christ who wills it, but to lord it over Christians who do not will it. Brothers, be cautious: the devil is very crafty; but *Christ is the Wisdom of God*[^6].

[^1]: See 1 John 3:2
[^2]: Rom 12:12; 8:24
[^3]: Rom 8:24–25
[^4]: Ps 27:14 (26:14)
[^5]: See Phil 1:8
[^6]: 1 Cor 1:24
