# Letter 190

To the most blessed lord, and by sincere love longed-for brother and fellow bishop Optatus, from Augustine, greetings in the Lord.

**1. 1.** Although I have received no letters of your holiness given to me myself; yet because those which you sent to Mauretania Caesariensis came while I was present at Caesarea, whither an ecclesiastical necessity enjoined on us by the venerable pope Zosimus, bishop of the apostolic See, had drawn us: it came about that I myself too read through what you wrote, the servant of God, holy Renatus, a brother most dear to us in Christ, handing me that same letter of yours; at whose request, and with him vehemently insisting on me though occupied with other things, I was compelled to answer them. There came to this also the arrival of another holy brother of ours, to be named with due honor, and, as far as I learned from him, your kinsman Muressis, while we were staying in the aforesaid town; who reported to me that letters of your veneration had been sent to him too about this matter, and consulted me about this very question, that what I thought of it he might make to reach you, either by my reply or by his own: namely, whether souls are born, like bodies, by propagation, and are from that one which was created for the first man; or whether the omnipotent Creator, who surely works until now, makes new ones, proper to individuals, without any propagation.

**1. 2.** About which matter, before I admonish your sincerity of anything, I wish you to know that in so many of my little works I never dared to bring forth a defined opinion about this question, and shamelessly to put into letters, for instructing others, what was not unfolded with me. But by the consideration of what things and causes I am moved, so that my assent inclines to neither assertion, but I still debate between both, it is too long to bring out in this letter; nor so necessary that, this being omitted, what is enough cannot be disputed about this question—if not for removing hesitation, certainly for avoiding rashness.

**1. 3.** For that in which the Christian faith especially consists—that *through a man death, and through a man the resurrection of the dead: for as in Adam all die, so also in Christ all will be made alive*[^1]; and that *through one man sin entered into this world, and through sin death: and so death passed to all men, in whom all sinned*[^2]; and that *the judgment indeed was from one unto condemnation: but grace from many offenses unto justification of life*[^3]; and that *through the offense of one upon all men unto condemnation, and through the righteousness of one upon all men unto justification of life*[^4]: and any other testimonies which declare that no one is born of Adam except bound by the chain of offense and damnation, and that no one is freed thence except by being reborn through Christ—we ought so unshakenly to hold, that we may know that one who has denied this in no way pertains to the faith of Christ, and to that grace of God which is given through Christ to little and great. Whence, if the origin of the soul lies hidden, provided that the redemption be clear, there is no danger: for we do not believe in Christ that we may be born, but that we may be reborn, in whatever way we have been born.

**1. 4.** But we say that the origin of the soul lies hidden without danger to this extent: that we nevertheless do not believe it to be a part of God, but a creature. Nor born of God, but made by him, and to be adopted into his family by a marvelous condescension of grace, not by an equal dignity of nature. Nor that it is a body, but a spirit; not the Creator, surely, but created. Nor that it came into this corruptible body by which it is weighed down for this reason, that the merits of a life lived badly before, in the heavenly or in any other parts of the world, compelled it to that. For the Apostle says, when he spoke of Rebecca's twins, that, not yet born, they had done nothing good or evil; so that it was said, not of works—by which none was distinguished one from the other—but of him who calls, that the elder was going to serve the younger.

**2. 5.** These things, then, being most firmly established, if it is so recondite and set in the hidden works of God, that it is not declared even by the manifest utterance of the divine Scriptures, whether for this reason the not-yet-born are to be believed to have done nothing good or evil, because individuals receive souls not propagated from others, but created from nothing immediately; or because, when they were originally in their parents, they themselves were as yet none who would lead their own and proper lives: yet let that faith be safe, by which we believe that no man—whether of a greater, or of however young and recent an age—is freed from the contagion of the ancient death and the bond of the sin which he contracted by his first nativity, except through the one mediator of God and men, the man Christ Jesus.

**2. 6.** By the most healthful faith in which man, and the same God, even those just men were saved, who, before he came in the flesh, believed that he would come in the flesh. For the faith is the same, both ours and theirs, since they believed that to be future which we believe accomplished: whence the apostle Paul says: *But having the same Spirit of faith, according to what is written, I believed, therefore I have spoken, we also believe, therefore we also speak*[^5]. If, then, those too who foretold that Christ would come in the flesh had the same Spirit of faith as those who announced that he had come; the sacraments could be diverse according to the diversity of the times, yet most concordantly recurring to the unity of the same faith. It is written in the Acts of the Apostles, the apostle Peter speaking: *Now therefore why do you tempt God, to impose a yoke upon the neck of the disciples, which neither our fathers nor we were able to bear? But by the grace of the Lord Jesus we believe that we are saved, just as they too*[^6]. If, then, they too, that is, the fathers, not able to bear the yoke of the old law, believed that they were saved by the grace of the Lord Jesus; it is manifest that this grace made even the ancient just men live by faith: *For the just lives by faith*[^7].

**2. 7.** But the law came in that the offense might abound; that grace might superabound, by which the abundance of offense might be healed. *For if a law had been given which could give life, righteousness would altogether be from the law*. For what good the law was given, he consequently added, saying: *But Scripture has shut up all things under sin, that the promise by faith in Jesus Christ might be given to those who believe*[^8]. The law, then, had to be given which would show the man himself to himself more manifestly, lest the proud human mind should suppose that it could be just from itself, and, being ignorant of God's righteousness—that is, that which is to man from God—and wishing to establish its own—that is, as it were gained by its own strength—should not be made subject to the righteousness of God. It behooved, then, that, the commandment being added whose voice is, *You shall not covet*, even the crime of transgression should accrue to the proud sinner; and so the weakness, not healed by the law, but convicted, might seek the medicine of grace.

**2. 8.** Accordingly, since all the just—that is, the truthful worshipers of God, whether before the incarnation, or after the incarnation of Christ—neither lived nor live except by faith in the incarnation of Christ, in whom is the fullness of grace; surely that which is written, *that there is no other name under heaven in which we must be saved*[^9], avails for the saving of the human race from that time in which in Adam the human race was vitiated. For as in Adam all die, so also in Christ all will be made alive. Because as in the kingdom of death no one is without Adam, so in the kingdom of life no one is without Christ. As through Adam all men are unjust, so through Christ all are just men. As through Adam all, mortal in punishment, were made sons of the age, so too through Christ all, immortal in grace, are made sons of God.

**3. 9.** But why even those are created whom the Creator foreknew to pertain to damnation, not to grace, the blessed Apostle recalls with so much more succinct brevity, as with the greater authority. For he says that God, *willing to show his wrath, and to demonstrate his power, brought in much patience the vessels of wrath, which have been perfected for perdition, and that he might make known the riches of his glory upon the vessels of mercy*[^10]; whom above he had said, as a potter of clay, *from the same mass to make one vessel for honor, another for dishonor*[^11]. But it would deservedly seem unjust that vessels of wrath are made for perdition, if that whole mass from Adam were not damned. That, then, vessels of wrath are made thence by being born, pertains to owed punishment: but that vessels of mercy are made by being reborn, pertains to undue grace.

**3. 10.** God, then, shows his wrath; not, surely, a perturbation of mind, such as is that which is called the wrath of man, but a just and fixed vengeance, because from the stock of disobedience is drawn the propagation of sin and punishment. And *man born of woman*, as it is written in the book of Job, *is of short life and full of wrath*[^12]. For he is a vessel of that thing with which he is full: whence they are called vessels of wrath. He shows also his power, by which he uses even evils well, bestowing on them many natural and temporal goods, and accommodating their malice for exercising and, by comparison, admonishing the good, that in them they may learn to give thanks to God that they have been distinguished from them, not by their own merits, which in the same mass were equal, but by his mercy. Which appears most of all in little ones, of whom, when through the grace of Christ they are reborn, and, ending this life at that tender age, pass into the eternal and blessed life, it cannot be said that they are distinguished by free choice from other infants who, without this grace, die in the damnation of that same mass.

**3. 11.** But if only those were created from Adam who were to be recreated by grace, and besides those who are adopted into the sons of God, no other men were born, the benefit which is given to the unworthy would lie hidden; because to none coming from the same damnable stock would the owed punishment be rendered. But since he brought in much patience the vessels of wrath which have been perfected for perdition, he not only showed his wrath and demonstrated his power by rendering vengeance, and by using well those who are not good, but he also made known the riches of his glory upon the vessels of mercy. For thus one freely justified says what is bestowed on him, while, not by his own merit, but by the glory of the most bounteous mercy of God, he is distinguished from one damned, with whom he himself too, by the same justice, was to be damned.

**3. 12.** But he willed that so many be created and born, whom he foreknew not to pertain to his grace, that by an incomparable multitude there should be more of them than of those whom he deigned to predestine, as sons of promise, into the glory of his kingdom; that even by the very multitude of the rejected it might be shown how of no moment, with the just God, is the multitude, however great, of the most justly damned. And that hence too those who are redeemed from that very damnation may understand that this was owed to that whole mass, which they saw rendered to so great a part of it—not only in those who add to original sin many things by the choice of an evil will, but even in so many little ones who, bound only by the chain of original sin, without the grace of the Mediator are snatched from this light. For that whole mass of just damnation would receive what was owed, unless from it the potter, not only just, but even merciful, made other vessels for honor according to grace, not according to debt; while he both comes to the aid of little ones, of whom no merits can be spoken, and goes before older ones, that they may be able to have some merits.

**4. 13.** Since these things are so, if your assertion does not tend to this, that you say that new souls cannot, on account of the innocence of their newness, before they use free choice for sinning, be liable to original damnation; but you confess by the catholic faith that they, even if at that tender age they go out of the body, are going into the damnation of perdition, unless they be freed by the sacrament of the Mediator, who came to seek and to save what had perished: inquire where, or whence, or when they began to have the merit of damnation, if they are new, so indeed that you make neither God, nor any nature which God did not found, the author either of their sin or of the damnation of the innocent. And if you find what I have admonished you to seek—which I myself, I confess, have not yet found—defend it as much as you can, and assert that the newness of the souls of infants is of such a kind that they are drawn by no propagation; and what you have found, communicate with us by brotherly love.

**4. 14.** But if you do not find why or how the souls of infants become sinful, and, having nothing of malice in themselves, by what they are compelled to draw from Adam that for which they are damned, since you believe them not propagated from that first sinful one, but new and innocent, to be shut up in the flesh of sin; let not your assent, even so, be rashly bent to the other opinion, so that you believe them to be drawn by propagation from that one; lest perhaps another may be able to find what you yourself cannot, or you may at some time find what now you could not. For even those who assert that souls are propagated from the one which God gave to the first man, and so say that they are drawn from the parents, if they follow Tertullian's opinion, surely contend that they are not spirits, but bodies, and arise from corporeal seeds. Than which what can be said more perversely? Nor is it to be wondered that Tertullian dreamed this, who supposes even God the creator himself to be nothing but a body.

**4. 15.** This dementia being repelled from the Christian heart and mouth, whoever confesses that the soul, as it is, is not a body but a spirit, and yet is led into the sons from the parents; in this indeed he is hemmed in by no straits, that the true faith proclaims that all souls, even of little ones, whom the Church baptizes surely not into a false, but into a true remission of sins, draw original sin, committed by the proper will of the first man, and transmitted to all posterity by generation, to be purged by regeneration alone: but when it begins to be considered and treated what is said, it is a wonder if any human sense comprehends in what way, as a lamp is kindled from a lamp and, without detriment to the one, another fire arises thence, so a soul comes to be from the soul of the parent in the offspring, or is transmitted into the offspring; whether the incorporeal seed of the soul, by some hidden and invisible way of its own, runs separately from the father into the mother when conception comes about in the woman; or—what is more incredible—lies hidden in the seed of the body. But when the seeds flow forth in vain without any conceptions, whether the seed of the soul does not go out at the same time, or, with the highest speed and in an atom of time, runs back whence it had gone out, or perishes: and if it perishes, how the soul itself, whose seed is mortal, is immortal; or whether it receives immortality then, when it is formed to live, as righteousness when it is formed to be wise. And by what pact God forms it in the man, even if the soul is drawn seminally from the soul, as he forms in the man the members of the body, although the body is drawn seminally from the body. For if a spiritual creature too were not formed by God, it would not be written: *Who forms the spirit of man within him*[^13]: and in that which is read, *Who forms individually the hearts of men*[^14]; if by hearts souls are signified, who would doubt that they can be formed? but it is asked whether from the one soul of the first man; as he forms individually the faces of men, from the one body, nevertheless, of the first man.

**5. 16.** When these and many such things are asked about this matter, which can be explored by no sense of the flesh, and are far removed from our experience, and lie hidden in the most secret bosoms of nature; a man ought not to blush to confess that he does not know what he does not know, lest, while he lies that he knows, he never deserve to know. But who denies that God is the creator and maker not only of the one, but of every soul, except one who most openly opposes his words? For without any ambiguity he says through the prophet: *Every breath I made*[^15]; wishing souls, namely, to be understood, which the following words make manifest. Not, then, the one which he breathed into the first man made from earth, but he himself made every breath, he himself still makes it. It is asked, however, whether he makes every breath from that one breath, as every body of man from that one body: or whether he makes new bodies indeed from the one, but new souls from nothing. For who makes the kinds of things, congruous to their origins, even from seeds, except he who made the seeds themselves without seeds? But where a thing naturally obscure conquers our small measure, and the open divine Scripture does not come to aid, human conjecture rashly presumes to define anything from this. But according to the lives which they begin to have as their own, we say that new men are born, whether in soul or in body. But according to original sin, they are born old; therefore by baptism they are renewed.

**5. 17.** Something certain, then, about the origin of the soul I have not yet found in the canonical Scriptures. For those who assert that new souls come to be without any propagation, among the testimonies by which they strive to show this, set down also those two which I recalled a little before: *Who forms the spirit of man within him*; and: *Who forms individually the hearts of men*; about which you see how it can be answered by those resisting: for whether, when he forms, he forms from another, or from nothing, is uncertain. But that testimony among the rest seems to be chief, which is read in the book of Solomon, Ecclesiastes: *And the dust will return to the earth, as it was; and the spirit will return to God, who gave it*[^16]. But it is most easily answered: "The body to the earth from which the first body of man was made, and the spirit to God by whom the first soul of man was made: for just as our body," they say, "although it is propagated from that first body, yet returns thither whence that first body was made; so our soul, although propagated from that soul, returns not to nothing, because it is immortal, but to him by whom that first soul itself was made." And through this, that which is written of the spirit of each man, that it returns to God who gave it, does not resolve that most obscure question; because, whether from that one, or from no other, God surely gave it.

**5. 18.** Likewise those who, by inconsiderate rashness, defend the propagation of souls, among the other testimonies which they think favor their cause, can think none to be brought forward, as it were more manifest and express, for themselves, than that which is written in Genesis: *But all the souls which entered with Jacob into Egypt, who came out of his loins*[^17]. For by this, as it were a most evident testimony, souls can be believed to be propagated into the sons from the parents; since it seems said openly enough that souls too came out of Jacob's loins, not the bodies of the sons alone. So that in that way too they would have understood, as the whole from the part, what Adam said when his wife was shown to him: *This now is bone of my bones, and flesh of my flesh*[^18]: for he did not say, And soul of my soul; but so it can come about that, the flesh being named, both are understood, just as here he named souls, and yet wished bodies too in the sons to be understood.

**5. 19.** But this testimony, surely, as it were so clear and manifest, would not suffice to unravel this question, not even if it were read in the feminine gender, who came out of his loins, so that we should understand souls which came out. But for this reason it would not suffice, since by the name of soul too only the body can be signified, by a certain manner of speech in which what contains is signified by that which is contained; as a certain poet says: *The wines crown*, when the wine vessels were crowned: for the wine is contained, the vessel contains. As, then, we call the church a basilica, by which is contained the people who is truly called the Church; so that by the name of church—that is, of the people who is contained—we signify the place which contains: so, because souls are contained by bodies, the bodies of the sons can be understood by the named souls. For so too is that better taken which the Law says, that one is defiled who has entered over a dead soul—that is, over the corpse of one deceased—so that by the name of dead soul the dead body is understood, which contained the soul; because even when the people, that is, the Church, is absent, that place is nonetheless called a church. These things would be answered, if, as I said, it had been set down in the feminine gender, "which came out of Jacob's loins," that is, which souls. But now, since the masculine gender is set down, that is, "who came out of Jacob's loins"; who would not rather so understand: "All the souls of those who came out of his loins," that is, the souls of the men: so that even thus they can be understood to have come out of the father's loins according to the body only, the men whose those souls were, by whose number so many men are understood?

**6. 20.** But I would wish to read that little book of yours which you mentioned in your letter, lest perhaps you set down there some testimonies not ambiguous. But when a friend most dear to me and most zealous of the divine Letters had asked me what I thought about this question, and I, modesty laid aside, had confessed to him from this my fevers and ignorance, he wrote thence, far across the sea, to a most learned man: to whom that man wrote back that he should rather consult me, not knowing that he had already done so, nor had he been able to hear anything certain and defined from me. He intimated, however, in that same brief letter of his, that he rather believes souls to come to be than to be propagated: at the same time admonishing too that (since he himself is in the East) the western Church is wont to think the propagation of souls. By this greater occasion found, I wrote to him no brief book, consulting him, and asking that he first teach me, and then send to me those whom I may teach.

**6. 21.** Which book of mine can be read with me as that of an inquirer, not a teacher, and rather of one desiring to learn: but it ought not to be sent anywhere, or given to anyone outside, except when I shall have received a reply, with the Lord helping; that which he thinks I am going to defend most readily and most gladly, if he can teach me how souls come not from Adam, and yet from him find a just lot of damnation, unless they come, by being reborn, to the remission of sins. For far be it that we should believe either that the souls of little ones receive in the washing of regeneration a false cleansing of sins, or that God, or any nature which God did not found, is the author of that defilement from which they are cleansed. Until, then, either he writes back, or I, if God wills, in some way learn, if it draws no origin from that sinful one, what cause there is for the soul's undergoing original sin, which it is necessary be in all little ones; and how neither does God compel it, innocent, since he is not the author of sinning, nor any nature of evil, since there is none; I dare to proclaim nothing of the kind.

**6. 22.** But you, dearest brother, if you willingly or patiently admit it, I admonish lest, less cautious, you run into a new heresy, which strives to tear up the established foundations of the most ancient faith by disputing against the grace of God, which the Lord Christ bestows on little and great with ineffable goodness: of which heresy, since Pelagius and Celestius had stood forth as the authors, or at least the keenest and best-known persuaders, by the vigilance of episcopal councils, with the help of the Savior who protects his Church, they have been condemned throughout the whole Christian world, even by two venerable prelates of the apostolic See, pope Innocent and pope Zosimus, unless, corrected, they also do penance. Of which condemnation copies of recent letters—whether those which specially flowed to the Africans, or those which universally to all bishops, from the said See—lest perhaps they have not yet reached your holiness, we have arranged to be sent to you by those brothers to whom we gave these letters too, that they might direct them to your veneration. But these people are not heretics for this reason, that they say souls do not draw their origin from that first sinful one, which can either perhaps by some reasoning be said true, or be unknown without a lapse of faith: but from this they try to effect (whence they are judged altogether the most open heretics) that the souls of little ones draw nothing of evil from Adam, which is to be expiated by the washing of regeneration. For Pelagius's argument about this matter, which among his other damnable things has even been joined to the letters of the apostolic See, is thus: "If the soul," he says, "is not from transmission, but the flesh alone has the transmission of sin; the flesh alone, then, deserves punishment. For it is unjust that a soul born today, not from the mass of Adam, should bear so ancient another's sin; because by no reasoning is it granted that God, who forgives proper sins, should impute another's."

**6. 23.** If, then, you can so assert the newness of souls without any propagation, that, by a just reasoning and not alien from the catholic faith, they be shown, even so, liable to the sin of the first man; assert what you think, as you can. But if you cannot make them alien from propagation otherwise than by at the same time making them free from every bond of sin; restrain yourself altogether from a disputation of this kind. For the remission of sins is not false even in the baptism of little ones, nor is it said in word only, but is truly done. For, that I may now use the words which are read in the very letter of the most blessed prelate Zosimus: "The Lord is faithful in his words, and his baptism holds the same fullness in fact and words—that is, in deed, confession, and true remission of sins—in every sex, age, condition of the human race. For no one but he who is a slave of sin is made free, nor can be called redeemed, except one who was truly before captive through sin, as it is written: *If the Son frees you, you will be truly free*[^19]. For through him we are reborn spiritually, through him we are crucified to the world. By his death the bond contracted by propagation of that death introduced and transmitted from Adam to all of us, the whole soul, is broken, in which no one at all of those born, before he is freed through baptism, is not held liable." In these words of the apostolic See the catholic faith is so ancient and founded, so certain and clear, that it is wicked for a Christian to doubt about it.

**6. 24.** Since, then, by the death of Christ the bond of death, contracted by the propagation not of one or of some, but of the whole soul, is broken; if you can so defend souls as alien from propagation, that nevertheless they be shown, by a most right reasoning, bound by this bond, which is to be broken by the death of Christ alone, and appear justly obligated, not by their own propagation, but by this debt of the flesh: not only defend it, no one prohibiting, but even show us how we can defend it with you. But if what you think about the newness of souls cannot be asserted otherwise than that you say they are not held by the sin of the first man; or that, through propagation not their own, but of the flesh, with God as author, or with I know not what nature of evil, the most innocent are made sinful: better does the origin of the soul lie hidden, provided that we do not doubt it to be a creature of God, than that either God be called the author of sin, or against God an alien nature of evil be brought in, or the baptism of little ones be reckoned void.

**6. 25.** But that your charity may hear from me too something defined about this question—to be esteemed of no small value, nay rather especially necessary and to be retained—however the origin of souls may stand, whether they are propagated from that one, or from no other; that the soul of the Mediator drew no sin from Adam it is not lawful to doubt. For if none is propagated from another, where all are held by the propagated flesh of sin: how much less is it to be believed that a soul could have come from the propagation of a sinful one, whose flesh came from the Virgin, conceived not by lust, but by faith, that it might be in the likeness of the flesh of sin, not in the flesh of sin! But if the rest are held liable to the sin of the first sinful soul for this reason, that they are propagated from it; surely that one which the Only-begotten fitted to himself either drew no sin thence, or was altogether not drawn thence. For he was surely able to draw to himself a soul without sin, who loosed our sins; or he who created a new soul for that flesh which he made from earth without a parent, was able to create a new one for the flesh which he took from a woman without a man.

**6. 26.** These things, as I could—not indeed to letters given to me, but yet to letters of your holiness given to those most dear to us—I have answered, not with the skill which you desired, but with anxious love. If you receive it gratefully, and keep both a brotherly and useful admonition, not by erring, but by prudently considering, with the peace of the Church—thanks be to God. But if you wonder that I do not yet know these things, or even do not wonder, and do not refuse to teach me, by mutual love, anything certain about the origin of souls, saving this faith which is most certain and most clear—much more abundantly, thanks be to God. Mindful of us, may you live always to the Lord, most blessed lord, and by sincere love longed-for brother.
