# Letter 173

To Donatus, presbyter of the party of Donatus, from Augustine, bishop of the catholic Church.

**1.** If you could see the grief of my heart, and my anxiety for your salvation, perhaps you would pity your own soul, pleasing God in hearing the word, not ours but his own; nor would you so fix his Scriptures in your memory as to shut your heart against them. It displeases you that you are drawn to salvation, though you have drawn so many of ours to perdition. For what did we wish, except that you be seized, and presented, and saved that you might not perish? But that you were injured in your body, you did this to yourself, who would not use the beast soon brought to you, and dashed yourself heavily to the ground: for surely the other who was brought with you, your colleague, came uninjured, who did not do such things to himself.

**2.** But neither do you think this ought to have been done to you, because you suppose that no one should be compelled to the good. Consider what the Apostle said: *One who desires the episcopate desires a good work*[^1]; and yet so many, that they may take up the episcopate, are held unwilling, led, shut in, kept, suffer so many things they do not wish, until the will to take up the good work is present in them: how much more are you to be drawn from the pernicious error in which you are enemies to yourselves, and to be led to the truth, either to be known or to be chosen—not only that you may salutarily have honor, but also that you may not perish most wretchedly! You say that God gave free choice; that therefore a man ought not to be compelled even to the good. Why, then, are those of whom I spoke above compelled to the good? Attend, then, to what you do not wish to consider. A good will is mercifully bestowed for this reason: that the evil will of man may be directed. For who does not know that a man is neither damned except by the merit of an evil will, nor freed unless he has a good will? Yet not on that account are those who are loved to be left, with impunity and cruelly, to their evil will; but where power is given, they are both to be prohibited from evil and compelled to the good.

**3.** For if an evil will is always to be left to its liberty, why were the Israelites, refusing and murmuring, prohibited from evil by such hard scourges, and compelled toward the land of promise? If an evil will is always to be left to its liberty, why was Paul not permitted to use the most evil will by which he persecuted the Church, but was prostrated that he might be blinded, and blinded that he might be changed, changed that he might be sent, sent that he might suffer for the truth such things as he had done in error? If an evil will is always to be left to its liberty, why is the father admonished in the holy Scriptures to correct his hard son not only with words, but even to beat his sides, that, compelled and tamed, he may be directed to good discipline? Whence the same says: *You indeed strike him with the rod; but you free his soul from death*[^2]. If an evil will is always to be left to its liberty, why are the negligent shepherds corrected, and it is said to them: *The straying sheep you have not called back, the lost one you have not sought*[^3]? And you are sheep of Christ, you carry the Lord's mark in the sacrament which you received; but you stray and perish. Let us not displease you, therefore, because we call back the straying and seek the lost. For we do better to follow the will of the Lord admonishing us to compel you to return to his fold, than to consent to the will of the straying sheep, so as to permit you to perish. Do not, then, any longer say what I constantly hear you saying: I wish to err thus, I wish to perish thus. For we do better not to permit this at all, as far as we can.

**4.** Just now, that you cast yourself into the well to die, you surely did by free will. But how cruel the servants of God would be, if they permitted you to this evil will of yours, and did not free you from that death! Who would not deservedly blame them? Who would not rightly judge them impious? And yet you willingly cast yourself into the water to die; they unwillingly lifted you from the water, that you might not die: you acted according to your will, but to your destruction; they against your will, but for your salvation. If, then, this corporeal safety is so to be guarded that it is preserved even in the unwilling by those who love them, how much more that spiritual safety, in the desertion of which eternal death is feared! Although in this death which you wished to bring upon yourself, you would die not only for a time, but even forever; because even if you were compelled not to salvation, not to the peace of the Church, not to the unity of Christ's body, not to holy and undivided love, but to some evils, not even so ought you to bring death upon yourself.

**5.** Consider the divine Scriptures, and examine as much as you can, and see whether any of the just and faithful ever did this, when they suffered such great evils from those who were driving them to eternal destruction, not to eternal life, to which you are compelled. I have heard that you said the apostle Paul signified that this ought to be done, where he says: *Even if I deliver my body to be burned*[^4]; because, namely, he was calling all things good which without love profit nothing, such as are the tongues of men and angels, and all sacraments, and all knowledge, and all prophecy, and all faith so that mountains be moved, and the distribution of one's goods to the poor; and so it seems to you that he numbered this too among good things—that each should bring death upon himself. But attend diligently and acknowledge how the Scripture says that one delivers his body to be burned. Not, surely, that he should cast himself into the fire when he suffers a persecuting enemy; but when it is set before him that he should either do some evil or suffer some evil, let him choose to do no evil rather than not to suffer evil: and so deliver his body into the power of the slayer, as those three men did who were compelled to adore the golden statue, and unless they did so, the one who compelled them threatened them with a burning fiery furnace. They were unwilling to adore the idol; they did not cast themselves into the fire: and yet even of them it is so written, that they delivered their bodies, so that they would neither serve nor adore any god, but their God. Behold how the Apostle said: *If I deliver my body to be burned*.

**6.** But see what follows: *If I have not love, it profits me nothing*[^5]. To this love you are called, from this love you are not allowed to perish; and you think it profits you something if you hurl yourself into destruction, when it would profit you nothing even if another killed you, an enemy of love! But set outside the Church, and separated from the framework of unity and the bond of love, you would be punished with eternal torment, even if for the name of Christ you were burned alive: for this is what the Apostle says: *Even if I deliver my body to be burned, but have not love, it profits me nothing*[^6]. Recall your mind, then, to sound consideration and sober thought; attend diligently whether you are called to error and impiety, and suffer any troubles whatever for the truth. But if you rather are engaged in error and impiety, while where you are called there is truth and piety, since there is Christian unity and the love of the Holy Spirit; why do you still try to be an enemy to yourself?

**7.** Therefore the mercy of God brought it about that both we and your bishops came to Carthage in so frequent and numerous a gathering, and most orderly conferred among ourselves about that very dissension. The proceedings were written down, our subscriptions too are kept; read them, or suffer them to be read to you, and then choose what you will. I have heard that you said you could do something with us about those proceedings, if we omit the words of your bishops, where they said: "Neither does one cause prejudge another cause, nor one person another person." You wish us to omit these words, where through them, unknowing, truth itself spoke. But you are going to say that they erred here, and fell too incautiously into a false opinion. But we say that they said this truly, and we prove this most easily through you yourself. For if your bishops, chosen by the whole party of Donatus, who would sustain the cause of all, and if they had done anything pleasing and acceptable the rest would hold it—yet in that which you think they said rashly and not rightly, you do not wish them to prejudge you; therefore they said truly, that neither does one cause prejudge another cause, nor one person another person. And there you ought to acknowledge that, if you do not wish the person of so many of your bishops, set among those seven, to prejudge the person of Donatus, presbyter of Mutugenna, how much less ought the person of Caecilian, even if something evil were found in him, to prejudge the whole unity of Christ, which is not shut up in one villa of Mutugenna, but diffused throughout the whole world?

**8.** But behold, we do what you wished; we deal with you as if your bishops had not said: "Neither does one cause prejudge another cause, nor one person another person." Find you what they ought to have said, when there was set against them the cause and person of Primian, who both condemned his condemners along with the rest, and received the condemned and detested back again into their honors, and chose rather to acknowledge and accept the baptism which the dead had given (for of those very ones it had been said in that famous sentence that "the shores are full of the corpses of the dead" [in the Bagai council]) than to blow it away and rescind it, and dissolved entirely what you, understanding badly, are wont to say: One who is baptized by a dead man, what does his washing profit him? If, then, they did not say, "Neither does one cause prejudge another cause, nor one person another person," they would be held guilty in the cause of Primian: but since they said this, they made the catholic Church immune, as we asserted, from the cause of Caecilian.

**9.** But read the rest, examine the rest. See whether against Caecilian himself, of whose person they tried to prejudge the Church, they could prove anything evil. See whether they did not rather do much even for him, and, by the more numerous readings which they brought forward and recited against themselves, wholly confirm that his cause was good. Read these things, or let them be read to you. Consider all, review diligently, and choose what you will follow: whether to rejoice with us in the peace of Christ, in the unity of the catholic Church, in brotherly love; or, for a wicked dissension, for the party of Donatus, for a sacrilegious division, longer to endure the importunity of our love toward you.

**10.** For you attend and often repeat, as I hear, that it is written in the Gospel that seventy disciples withdrew from the Lord, and were permitted to the choice of their evil and impious withdrawal, and that to the other twelve who remained it was answered: *Do you too wish to go?*[^7] And you do not attend that then for the first time the Church was sprouting in fresh shoots, and that prophecy was not yet fulfilled in her: *And all the kings of the earth shall adore him; all nations shall serve him*[^8]: which surely, the more it is fulfilled, the greater power the Church uses, so that it not only invites, but even compels to the good. This the Lord then wished to signify, who, although he had great power, yet first chose to commend humility. This he showed quite evidently also in that likeness of the banquet, where he sent to those invited, and they would not come, and he said to the servant: *Go out into the streets and lanes of the city, and bring in here the poor, and the weak, and the blind, and the lame. And the servant said to the master, It is done as you commanded, and still there is room. And the master said to the servant, Go out into the roads and hedges, and compel them to enter, that my house may be filled*[^9]. See now how of those who came first it was said, *Bring in here*; it was not said, compel: so were signified the beginnings of the Church growing to this, that there should be strength even to compel. Accordingly, because it behooved that, the Church now strengthened in its powers and greatness, men be compelled even to the banquet of eternal salvation, after it was said, *It is done as you commanded, and still there is room*; *Go out*, he says, *into the roads and hedges, and compel them to enter*. Wherefore, if you were walking quietly outside this banquet of the holy unity of the Church, we would find you as if in the roads; but now, because through the many evil and savage things which you commit against ours, you are as it were full of thorns and roughness, we find you as if in the hedges, and we compel you to enter. He who is compelled is forced where he does not wish; but when he has entered, he now willingly feeds. Restrain, then, so iniquitous and unquiet a mind, that in the true Church of Christ you may find the saving banquet.
