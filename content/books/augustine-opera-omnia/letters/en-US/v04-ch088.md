# Letter 23/A\*

Here are reported the things that Alypius had announced in his memorandum.

**1.** [...] About brother Alypius I have found nothing certain except what I read in his memorandum sent to Thagaste through the son of Severianus Longus, who put in at our shore. For it so happened that I read what it carried, much wondering that he brought no letter to me. There, then, I read that they were awaiting the arrival of the senior count on account of those who had taken refuge in the Church. For as to the Carthaginians, about whose case we had been especially anxious, he said that on the day on which he wrote, an usher had been sent with their pardon, which they were able, even absent, to obtain by letter. For as to those who come from Carthage, they answer that they do not know whether it has arrived, but only that they have heard that a pardon was sent for Largus and that they are at Rome, where they were sent.

**2.** There came also just now a certain Josias, priest of the parish of Rusicade, who reported to us that on the day on which he set out from there they met the count (patricius), already drawing near. But since he departed from us [...].

**3.** [...] what of our little works you left while we were dictating I do not know. And so I recall all that I have dictated since we returned from Carthage: I wrote back to Optatus, the Spanish bishop, again about the question of the origin of the soul; I wrote back to Gaudentius of Thamugadi, the Donatist bishop, as he was replying to what I had earlier answered him; I dictated against the Arians a reply to what our Dionysius of Vicus Iuliani had sent me, and three sermons to be sent to Carthage; and amid these, when I was now preparing to return to the books *The City of God*, suddenly I received a letter from the holy Renatus, sending me from Caesarea two books of I-know-not-what Victor—who, from a Donatist, or rather a Rogatist, a former disciple of that Vincentius to whom I once wrote back, had become a Catholic. And he wished to write about the soul, rebuking our hesitation in not daring to define whether it is from propagation or whether a new one is breathed into each one at birth; and he himself affirmed that it is not drawn from propagation but given to us. In these two books of his he said very many false and absurd things, and against the catholic faith; and because our aforesaid friend earnestly asked me to refute them, since some of them, by their pleasant eloquence, were perverting many, I wrote one book about this to our same most beloved friend, and to Victor himself I wish to write, since it is very necessary. And so that I may do, regarding the Gospel of John too, what remains, I have now begun to dictate popular homilies, not lengthy, to be sent to Carthage on this condition: that, if our same old man wishes the rest to be sent to him, [he should say so], and when he has said so, not delay to publish them. I have already dictated six; for I have assigned to them properly the nights of Saturday and Sunday. So I have dictated, from when I came—that is, from the third day before the Ides of September up to the Kalends of December—nearly six thousand lines.

**4.** As to what your Holiness wrote to me about Donatianus, very dear both to you and to us, I very much wish it to be done. May the Lord grant that it be done. For I do not know what those brothers have added to the same question that they had not said before, to which we ought to reply; which I would have inserted into that work, had it not already been published to the Spanish bishops, our most beloved brothers, and on this account it seems it must be dictated in another work. But I take it ill that these things, which are thrust upon me from this side and that to be dictated crosswise, hinder the things we have in hand as arranged, and neither rest nor can be put off. The ecclesiastical records in the case of brother Maurentius—as soon as I read the memorandum of your Charity, I immediately ordered them to be copied out, so that they could be sent to you. And when you have received them, I too ask that you deign, with the Lord's help, to fulfill without delay what you have arranged. As to what they may have written to us through the priest Numedius, either I do not recall it or I did not receive it. We had sent, unless I am mistaken, to your Venerableness a copy of the letter of the bishop Boniface in which he replied to our report; I wish to know whether it reached you. We ought, moreover, to write jointly to those same brothers of ours, the people of Caesarea.

**5.** The deacon who brought the same letter, ever since he came from Rome, is here with us. I have detained him until he learns who has been ordained for the people of Caesarea in place of brother Deuterius. For it makes a very great difference; and let it be written, so that we may know how it is to be written. We have indeed heard that the bishop Honorius is vehemently striving to hold that same cathedral seat and—what would astonish you—the hearts of many are said to be inclined toward him; but the bishops were unwilling to consent and, to escape the force of those compelling them, answered them that we were first to be consulted, or even the bishop Boniface himself. This rumor has brought to us. Meanwhile that Church, as is evident, is agitated by seditions.

**6.** Here too is the bishop Priscillianus, who came earlier from Rome to Carthage and there first began to be in communion in Africa; thence he deigned to pass through us to you, but ever since he came it has seemed good to wait until we know what has been done there about the ordination of the new bishop, since it is very much his case, about which we too ought to write.
