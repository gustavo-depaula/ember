# Letter 36

To his most beloved and most longed-for brother and fellow presbyter Casulanus, Augustine sends greeting in the Lord

**1. 1.** I do not know how it came about that I did not return mine for your first letter: yet I know that I did not do this out of contempt for you. For I am delighted both by your studies and by your very discourse, and I both wish and exhort that in this youthful age you make progress in the word of God and abound to the edification of the Church. But now, having received your second writings, in which you demand, with a brotherly and most fair right of the love in which we are one, that at length some time you be answered, I have thought that the desire of your affection should be put off no longer, and amid my most pressing occupations I have undertaken to discharge this debt to you.

**1. 2.** As to what you consult me about, then—whether it is permitted to fast on the Sabbath: I answer that, if it were in no way permitted, surely neither Moses, nor Elijah, nor the Lord himself would have fasted forty continuous days. But by this reasoning it is concluded that fasting is not unlawful even on the Lord's day. And yet whoever shall think that this day must be decreed for fasting, as certain people who fast observe the Sabbath, will be a not small scandal to the Church, and not undeservedly. For in those matters about which the divine Scripture has established nothing certain, the custom of the people of God, or the institutions of our forefathers, must be held as law. About which, if we should wish to dispute, and from the custom of some to disapprove others, an unending wrestling will arise, which, when by the labor of discoursing it conveys no sure documents of truth, must surely be guarded against, lest by the storm of contention it cloud over the serenity of love. Which danger he neglected to avoid, whose lengthy disputation you judged should be sent with your first letter, that I might answer him.

**2. 3.** But the spaces of time are not so large for me that I should spend on refuting his individual opinions what I have necessary for unfolding other, more pressing works. But with that talent by which you show yourself to me in your letters—which gift of God in you I greatly love—consider yourself, a little more diligently, the same discourse of a certain Urbicus, as you write, and you will see that he has by no means feared to lacerate almost the whole Church of Christ, from the rising of the sun to its setting, with most injurious words. Nor would I say almost the whole, but plainly the whole. For he is found not even to have spared the Romans themselves, whose custom he seems to himself to be defending; but how the onslaught of his abuse overflows onto them too he does not know, since he does not notice. For when arguments fail him, by which to prove that one must fast on the Sabbath, he inveighs insultingly against the luxuries of feasts and drunken banquets and the most worthless drunkennesses, as though not to fast were the same as to be drunk. But if this is so, what then does it profit the Romans to fast on the Sabbath? since on the other days on which they do not fast, it is necessary, according to this man's disputation, for them to be judged drunkards and belly-worshippers. Moreover, if it is one thing to weigh down the heart with gluttony and drunkenness[^1], which is always evil, but another to relax a fast with modesty and temperance preserved—which surely, when it is done on the Lord's day, has no Christian reprover; let this man first distinguish the luncheons of the saints from the voracity and drunkenness of belly-worshippers, lest he make the Romans themselves belly-worshippers when they do not fast, and then let him inquire, not whether it is permitted to be drunk on the Sabbath—which is not permitted even on the Lord's day—but whether one ought not even to fast on the Sabbath, as one is not wont to do on the Lord's day.

**2. 4.** Would that he would so inquire, or so affirm, that he might not most openly blaspheme the Church spread through the whole world, except the Romans and as yet a few Westerners. But now, who could bear that, throughout all the eastern, and many western, Christian peoples, of so many and so great menservants and maidservants of Christ who lunch soberly and modestly on the Sabbath, it should be said by this man that they are in the flesh and cannot please God, and that of them it is written: *Let the wicked depart from me, I do not wish to know their way*[^2]; and that they are belly-worshippers, preferring the Judea to the Church, and the sons of the bondwoman; and consulting, by a law not just but voluptuary, the belly, not submitting to discipline; and that they are flesh, and savor death[^3], and other things of this kind: which, if he said them of any one servant of God, who ought to hear him, who not to shun him? But since with these reproaches and curses he assails the Church fructifying and growing throughout the whole world and lunching almost everywhere on the Sabbath day, I admonish whoever he is to restrain himself. For whose name you wished me not to know, surely about him you wished me not to judge.

**3. 5.** *The Son of man*, he says, *is Lord of the Sabbath*: on which it is most of all permitted *to do good* rather than evil[^4]. If, then, we do evil when we lunch, on no Lord's day do we live well. But that he confesses the Apostles to have eaten on the Sabbath, and says that it was not the time for fasting then, on account of which the Lord says: *The days will come when the bridegroom shall be taken away from them, and then the sons of the bridegroom will fast*[^5], because there is a time of joy and a time of mourning[^6]—he ought first to have attended to this, that there the Lord was speaking about fasting, not about fasting on the Sabbath. Then, since he wants mourning to be understood by fasting, and joy to be reckoned to food, why does he not consider that, whatever it is that God wished to signify in that which is written, that he *rested on the seventh day from all his works*[^7], not mourning was signified there, but joy? Unless perhaps he is going to say that in that rest of God and the sanctification of the Sabbath, joy was signified to the Jews, mourning to the Christians. And yet neither when God sanctified the seventh day, because in it he rested from all his works, did he express anything about fasting or lunching on the Sabbath; nor when he afterward commanded the Hebrew people about the observance of that day, did he say anything about foods either to be taken or not to be taken. Only a rest from one's own, or from servile, works is commanded for man: which the former people, receiving in a *shadow of things to come*[^8], so rested from works as we now see the Jews rest; not, as is supposed, the carnal Jews not rightly understanding what the Christians rightly understand. For we do not understand these things better than the Prophets, who nevertheless, at that time when it was right that it should be so done, kept this rest of the Sabbath which the Jews think must still be kept. Whence it is that God ordered a man to be stoned who had gathered wood on the Sabbath[^9]; but nowhere do we read that anyone, whether fasting on the Sabbath or lunching, was stoned or judged worthy of any punishment. But which of these two suits rest and which labor, let him himself see, who reckoned joy to those eating, mourning to those fasting, or understood that it was reckoned by the Lord, where, answering about fasting, he says: *The sons of the bridegroom cannot mourn so long as the bridegroom is with them*[^10].

**3. 6.** But as for his saying that the Apostles ate on the Sabbath because it was not yet the time for fasting on the Sabbath—which, namely, the tradition of the ancients forbade—was it then already the time for not resting on the Sabbath? Did not the tradition of the ancients forbid this too, and compel resting? And yet on that very Sabbath day, on which we read that Christ's disciples ate, they surely plucked the ears of grain[^11]; which was not permitted on the Sabbath, because the tradition of the ancients forbade it. Let him see, then, lest perhaps it be more fittingly answered to him that the Lord on that day willed these two things to be done by the disciples—one about plucking the ears, the other about taking food—so that the former might be against those who wish to rest on the Sabbath, but the latter against those who compel fasting on the Sabbath: since he had signified that the former, the time being changed, was now superstitious, but had willed the latter to be free at either time. Nor would I say this by way of confirming it, but I would show what could be answered to him much more aptly than are the things he says.

**4. 7.** "How," he says, "shall we not be condemned with the Pharisee, fasting twice in the Sabbath?"—as though the Pharisee were condemned because he fasted twice in the week, and not because, swollen, he exalted himself above the publican[^12]. But this man can say that even those who give the tithes of all their fruits to the poor are condemned with the Pharisee, because he too proclaimed this among his works; which we desire to be done by many Christians, and we scarcely find the very fewest: or indeed that whoever has not been unjust, an adulterer, and a robber will be condemned with the Pharisee, because that man boasted that he was not such; which surely whoever thinks, is mad. Moreover, if these things—without doubt good—which the Pharisee recalled to be in him, are not to be had with the proud boasting which appeared in him, but yet are to be had with the humble piety which was not in him: so too to fast twice in the week, in a man such as that Pharisee was, is fruitless; but in a man humbly faithful, or faithfully humble, it is religious: although the gospel Scripture did not say that the Pharisee was condemned, but rather said that the publican was justified[^13].

**4. 8.** But if this man thinks that what the Lord says—*Unless your righteousness shall abound more than that of the Scribes and Pharisees, you will not enter the kingdom of heaven*[^14]—must be understood in such a way that, unless we fast more than twice in the week, we cannot fulfill this precept; it is well that there are seven days which are recalled by their own traces in the rolling of times. When, therefore, anyone has subtracted two days out of the seven, that he not fast on the Sabbath and the Lord's day, there remain five days by which he can surpass the Pharisee fasting twice in the week. For I think that if anyone fasts three times in the week, he already surpasses the Pharisee, who fasted twice in the week. But if four times, or even so that no day be passed over except the Sabbath and the Lord's day, so that one fasts five times in the week, which many do their whole life, especially those settled in monasteries; not only the Pharisee who fasted twice in the week, but even the Christian who is accustomed to fast on the fourth day, the sixth day, and the Sabbath itself, which the Roman people frequently do, will be surpassed in the labor of fasting: and yet this man, whoever he is, this disputer Urbicus, as you say, even if someone fast five continuous days besides the Sabbath and the Lord's day so that on no day at all does he refresh his body, calls him carnal, as though food and drink on the other days did not pertain to the flesh; and judges him a belly-worshipper, as though the lunch of the Sabbath alone went down into the belly.

**5. 9.** For this man, indeed, it does not suffice that for surpassing the Pharisee it already suffices to fast three times in the week; but, excepting the Lord's day, he compels fasting on the six other days, so that he says: "The old stain removed, two in one flesh, now abiding under the discipline of Christ, ought not, with the sons without law and with the princes of Sodom and with the people of Gomorrah, to hold the voluptuary banquets of the Sabbath[^15]; but, with the dwellers in holiness and those devoted to God, to fast more and more lawfully by solemn and ecclesiastical right, that the error, even slight, of the six days may be washed away by the fountains of fasting, prayer, and almsgiving, so that, refreshed by the Lord's good fare, we may all with equal heart worthily sing: *You have filled, Lord, the empty soul, and given drink to the thirsting soul*[^16]." Saying these things, and excepting from frequent fasting only the Lord's day, he, improvident and incautious, accuses not only the Christian peoples of the East and the West, among whom no one fasts on the Sabbath, but even the Roman Church itself. For when he says: "Those abiding under the discipline of Christ ought not, with the sons without law, with the princes of Sodom, with the people of Gomorrah, to hold the voluptuary banquets of the Sabbath, but, with the dwellers in holiness and those devoted to God, to fast more and more lawfully by solemn and ecclesiastical right"; and then, defining what it is to fast lawfully, he adds and says: "that the error, even slight, of the six days may be washed away by the fountains of fasting, prayer, and almsgiving": surely he thinks that those who fast fewer than six days in the week do not lawfully practice fasting, nor are devoted to God, nor wash away the stains of error which are contracted from this mortal life. Let the Romans, then, see what they do, because they too are treated all too insultingly by this man's disputation; among whom, on all these six days, except for the very few clergy or monks, how rare is anyone found who frequents daily fasts? especially because there it does not seem right to fast on the fifth day of the week.

**5. 10.** Then I ask: if the error, even slight, of each day is loosed or washed away by the fasting of that day—for thus he says: "that the error, even slight, of the six days may be washed away also by the fountains of fasting"—what shall we do about that error which has crept in on the Lord's day, on which it is a scandal to fast? Or if no error creeps upon Christians on that day, let this man, who accuses belly-worshippers as a great faster, see how much honor and usefulness he attributes to bellies, if there is no erring then, when one lunches. Or does he perhaps establish so great a good in the fasting of the Sabbath that the fasting of the Sabbath alone can abolish the error, even slight, as he says, of the other six days—that is, even of the Lord's day itself—and that there is no erring only on that very day on which the whole is surely fasted? What is it, then, that he prefers the Lord's day to the Sabbath, as though by a Christian right? Behold, according to him the Sabbath day is found much holier, on which both there is no erring, when one fasts through its whole span, and by the same fasting the error of the other six days, and thereby of the Lord's day itself, is washed away: I think this presumption does not please you.

**5. 11.** But now, since he wishes himself to seem a spiritual man, and accuses the Sabbath-lunchers as carnal, attend to how he is refreshed on the Lord's day not with a sparing lunch, but is delighted with feasting. Now what is *alogia*—a word borrowed from the Greek tongue—except when one indulges in feasts so that one is led astray from the path of reason? Whence the animals lacking reason are called *aloga*, to which those given over to the belly are like: for which reason an immoderate banquet, by which the mind, in which reason rules, is in a manner overwhelmed by the gorging of eating and drinking, is named *alogia*. Moreover, on account of food and drink, he says that the *alogia*, not of the mind but of the belly, of the Lord's day must be sung: *You have filled, Lord, the empty soul, and given drink to the thirsting soul*[^17]. O spiritual man! O reprover of the carnal! O great faster, and no belly-worshipper! Behold, he who admonishes us not to corrupt the law of the Lord by the law of the belly, not to sell the bread of heaven for earthly food, and adds: "Because by food Adam perished from paradise, by food Esau lost his birthright." Behold, he who says: "For it is Satan's usual calumny, the temptation of the belly, to which he persuades a little, that he may take away the whole. And the interpretation of these precepts," he says, "bends the belly-worshippers less."

**5. 12.** Does he not seem, by these words of his, to be aiming at this: that one should fast even on the Lord's day? Otherwise the Sabbath day, on which the Lord rested in the tomb, will be holier than the Lord's day, on which he rose from the dead. For surely the Sabbath is holier, if, according to this man's words, on the Sabbath every sin is avoided by fasting, and what was contracted on the other days is abolished: but on the Lord's day, by the food of the belly, the temptation is not guarded against, and place is given to diabolical calumny, and one perishes from paradise, and loses the birthright. What is it, then, that, again contrary to himself, he admonishes that we should not be refreshed with a modest, sober, Christian lunch on the Lord's day, but, rejoicing and clapping in *alogia*, should sing: *You have filled, Lord, the empty soul, and given drink to the thirsting soul*[^18]? For surely, if we do not err then, when we fast, and we wash away the errors of the other six days when we fast on the Sabbath, none will be worse than the Lord's day, none better than the Sabbath. Believe, most beloved brother, no one understands the law as this man does, except one who does not understand it. For if it was not food, but forbidden food, that destroyed Adam[^19], and Esau, the grandson of holy Abraham, was condemned not by food, but by food coveted even to the contempt of the sacrament which he had in his birthright[^20]: so by the holy and faithful one lunches piously, just as by the sacrilegious and unbelieving one fasts impiously. But the Lord's day is preferred to the Sabbath by the faith of the resurrection, not by the custom of refreshment, or even by the license of wine-drinking song.

**6. 13.** "Moses," he says, "for forty days ate no bread, nor drank water[^21]." But why he said this, he adds, saying: "Behold Moses, the friend of God, dweller in the cloud, deliverer of the Law, and leader of the people, celebrating twice-three Sabbaths with fasting, gained not offense but merit." Does he not notice what can consequently be objected to this? For surely, if he sets forth the example of Moses fasting because in those forty days he fasted twice-three Sabbaths, as he says, and from this wishes to persuade that one should fast on the Sabbath; then from this let him persuade that one should fast on the Lord's day too, because in those forty days Moses no less fasted twice-three Lord's days. But he adds and says: "And still, with Christ, the Lord's day was being kept for the imminent Church": which, why he said it, I do not know. For if it is because one must fast much more, after the Lord's day came with Christ; then, God forbid, one should fast even on the Lord's day itself. But if he feared lest, on account of the forty-day fast, it should be objected that one must fast on the Lord's day too, and therefore added that "still, with Christ, the Lord's day was being kept by God for the imminent Church," so that it may be understood that Moses fasted for this reason even on the day that follows the Sabbath, because Christ had not yet come, through whom that Lord's day itself was made, on which it is not expedient to fast; why did Christ himself likewise fast for forty days[^22]? Why did he not, in those forty days, on the twice-three days that followed the Sabbath, break his fast, so as to commend the lunch of the Lord's day even before his resurrection, just as he gave his blood to be drunk before his passion? You surely see that the forty-day fast, which this man recalls, no more pertains to the point that we should fast on the Sabbath than it pertains to the point that we should fast on the Lord's day.

**6. 14.** He utterly fails to notice what can be objected to him about the Lord's day, when, just as drunken banquets and all voracious and intoxicated luxury are to be accused, so he accuses the lunches of the Sabbath, although these too can be the lunches of the modest and sober. And therefore he is not to be answered point by point, since, in reproving the vices of luxury for the lunch of the Sabbath, he says the same things over and over; finding nothing else to say, except what he says emptily and not to the point. The question is whether one ought not to fast on the Sabbath, not whether one ought not to indulge in luxury on the Sabbath; which those who fear God do not do even on the Lord's day, although on that day they surely do not fast. But who would say what this man has dared to say? "How," he says, "shall things be ratified to God for us, or through us, or worthy, which on a sanctified day force us to sin?" He confesses the Sabbath day to be sanctified, and says that men are forced to sin, because one lunches. And thereby, according to this man, either the Lord's day is not sanctified, and the Sabbath begins to be better; or, if the Lord's day too is sanctified, we are forced to sin, because we lunch.

**7. 15.** And he tries to prove by divine testimonies that one must fast on the Sabbath: but whence he might prove this he finds not at all. "Jacob," he says, "ate and drank wine, and was filled, and departed from God his Savior, and there fell on one day twenty-three thousand"[^23]: as though it were said: Jacob lunched on the Sabbath, and departed from God his Savior. And the Apostle, when he recalled that so many thousands fell, did not say: Nor let us lunch on the Sabbath, as they lunched; but said: *Nor let us commit fornication, as some of them committed fornication, and there fell on one day twenty-three thousand*[^24]. What also does he mean by saying: *But the people sat down to eat and drink, and rose up to play*[^25]? The Apostle indeed set down this testimony too, but in order to forbid the service of idols, not the Sabbath lunch. But that this happened on the Sabbath, this man does not prove, but surmises as he pleases. For just as it can come about that one fasts, and, when the fast is broken, if anyone is a drunkard, he then gets drunk; so it can come about that one does not fast, and, if men are temperate, one lunches most modestly. What is it, then, that, wishing to persuade the fasting of the Sabbath, he brings the Apostle as witness, saying: *Do not be drunk with wine, in which is all luxury*[^26]; as though he said: Do not lunch on the Sabbath, because therein is all luxury. But just as this apostolic precept that they not be drunk with wine, in which is all luxury, is observed by God-fearing Christians when one lunches on the Lord's day, so it is observed when one lunches on the Sabbath too.

**7. 16.** "That I may more expressly contradict those who err," he says, "no one offends God by fasting, even if he does not earn merit; moreover, even not to offend is to earn merit." Who would say this, except one who would not consider what he was saying? Therefore the Pagans, when they fast, do not on that account offend God more: or if he wished what he said to be understood of Christians, who will not offend God, if he should wish to fast on the Lord's day, with scandal to the whole Church which is spread everywhere? Then he subjoins testimonies from the Scriptures availing nothing for the cause he has undertaken. "By fasting," he says, "Elijah, granted to paradise, reigns in the body"[^27]: as though those who do not fast on the Sabbath did not preach fasting, just as those preach fasting who nevertheless do not fast on the Lord's day; or as though Elijah fasted at that time, when the people of God fasted even on the Sabbath. But what we have answered about the forty days of Moses' fast, this I reckon must be answered also about the forty days of Elijah. "By fasting," he says, "Daniel, unharmed, escaped the dry rage of the lions' mouths"[^28]: as though he had read that he fasted on the Sabbath, or even that he was among those very lions on the Sabbath; where, however, we read that he also lunched[^29]. "By fasting," he says, "the faithful brotherhood of three, lording it over the fire-flashing prison, adored the Lord received into the hospitality of the pyre"[^30]. These examples of the saints do not avail even to persuade the fasting of any day whatever; how much less of the Sabbath? Since not only do we not read that the three men were cast on the Sabbath into the burning fiery furnace; but we do not even read that they were there so long that anyone could say they fasted—nay rather, there is scarcely the space of one hour, in which their confession and hymn is sung: nor did they walk among those harmless flames longer than they finished that canticle. Unless perhaps even the space of one hour is reckoned by this man as fasting. But if it is so, he has no grounds to be angry at the Sabbath-lunchers: for until the hour of lunch, one fasts much longer than in that furnace.

**7. 17.** He brings forward also that testimony of the Apostle, where he says: *The kingdom of God is not food and drink, but righteousness and peace, and joy in the Holy Spirit*[^31]; and by *the kingdom of God* he wishes the Church to be understood, in which God reigns. I beseech you, was the Apostle, when he spoke these things, aiming at this, that Christians should fast on the Sabbath? But he was not even speaking about the fasting of any day whatever, when he said these things. For it was said against those who, in the manner of the Jews, according to the old Law, thought there was cleanness in the observance of certain foods, and for the admonition of those brothers by whose food and drink, indifferently received, the weak were scandalized. Therefore, when he had said: *Do not by your food destroy him for whom Christ died*; and: *Let not our good be blasphemed*[^32]; then he added: *For the kingdom of God is not food and drink*. For according as this man understands these words of the Apostle, that the kingdom of God, which is the Church, is not in food and drink, but in fasting; we ought, I do not say to fast on Sabbaths, but never at all to take food and drink, lest we ever depart from this kingdom of God. But I think that, by this man's own confession, we belong somewhat more religiously to the Church on the Lord's day, when nevertheless, even by his own concession, we lunch.

**8. 18.** "Why," he says, "do we murmur to offer to the more powerful Lord a sacrifice dear to him, which the spirit desires and the angel praises?" then he adds the testimony of the angel saying: *Good is prayer with fasting and almsgiving*[^33]. What he meant by *the more powerful Lord*, I do not know, unless perhaps the scribe erred, and it escaped you, so that you did not correct what you sent me to read. By the sacrifice dear to the Lord, then, he wishes fasting to be understood, as though this question turned on fasting, and not on fasting on the Sabbath. For neither is the Lord's day passed without a sacrifice, which is dear to God, because one does not fast. He goes on still and heaps up testimonies utterly foreign to the cause which he undertook to defend. *Offer to God a sacrifice of praise*[^34], he says; and, wishing somehow to connect this voice of the divine psalm to what is at issue: "Surely," he says, "not a banquet of blood or of drunkenness, in which not the praises due to God, but blasphemies, with the devil seconding, run wild." O improvident presumption! Therefore a *sacrifice of praise* is not offered on the Lord's day, because one does not fast; but a *banquet of drunkenness* is held, and *blasphemies, with the devil seconding, run wild*. But if it is wicked to say this, let him understand that fasting is not signified in what is written: *Offer to God a sacrifice of praise*. For fasting is not practiced on certain days, and especially on feast days. But the sacrifice of praise is offered by the Church spread through the whole world on all days. Otherwise—what no one, I do not say Christian, but not even a madman, would dare to say—those fifty days after Easter until Pentecost, on which one does not fast, will, according to this man, be foreign to the sacrifice of praise; on which days only, in many churches, but in all most of all, Alleluia is sung; which voice no Christian, however unskilled, is ignorant to be a voice of praise.

**8. 19.** Yet he himself confesses that on the Lord's day one lunches not in drunkenness but in cheerfulness, when he says that we ought, from Jews and gentiles, many of the Christian name, few elect by faith, at the evening kindling of the Sabbath, in place of the victims of cattle, to offer to God by praises a fasting pleasing to him, by whose fervor, burned up, the works of sins fail. "And in the morning," he says, "a hearing heard by us will hear us, and there will be houses for us to eat and drink in, not in drunkenness, but in cheerfulness, the Lord's celebration accomplished." Then, therefore, *eulogia* is celebrated, not, as he says above, *alogia*. But what offends him about the Sabbath day, which the Lord sanctified, I do not know, that on it he should think one cannot eat and drink with such cheerfulness as lacks drunkenness; since we can fast before the Sabbath, just as he says one must fast on the Sabbath before the Lord's day: or does he judge it wicked to lunch two days continuously? Let him see, then, with how great insult he affects the Roman Church itself, where even in those weeks in which one fasts on the fourth day, the sixth day, and the Sabbath, one nevertheless lunches on three continuous days, namely the Lord's day and then the second and the third.

**8. 20.** "It is certain," he says, "that the life of the sheep hangs on the judgment of the shepherds; but: *Woe to those who call good evil, and darkness light, and light darkness, and bitter sweet, and sweet bitter*[^35]." What these words of his mean, I do not sufficiently understand. For if, as you write, Urbicus says these things; in the City the people, hanging on the judgment of the shepherd, fast on the Sabbath with their bishop. But if he wrote these things to you because in your letter you yourself wrote something of the sort; let him not persuade you to praise the Christian city fasting on the Sabbath in such a way that you are compelled to condemn the Christian world lunching. For when he says: "Woe to those who call good evil, and darkness light, and light darkness, and bitter sweet, and sweet bitter," wishing the fasting of the Sabbath to be understood as good and light and sweet, but the lunch as evil and darkness and bitter; who doubts that he condemns the whole world of lands in all the Christians lunching on the Sabbath? He neither regards himself nor attends to what he says, so as to be restrained by his own writings from this headlong audacity. For immediately he subjoined: *Let no one therefore judge you in food or in drink*[^36]: which he himself surely does, who so accuses those taking food and drink on the Sabbath. How fitting it would have been that there should have come into his mind from this also that which the same Apostle says elsewhere: *He who eats, let him not despise him who does not eat; and he who does not eat, let him not judge him who eats*[^37]? Let him keep this measure, this temper, by which he might avoid scandals between those fasting on the Sabbath and those eating, so that one eating might not despise him who does not eat, and one not eating might not judge him who eats.

**9. 21.** "Peter too," he says, "the head of the Apostles, the doorkeeper of heaven, and the foundation of the Church, having extinguished Simon—who had been a figure of the devil, to be conquered only by fasting—taught this same thing to the Romans, whose faith is announced to the whole world[^38]." Did the other Apostles, then, teach Christians, against Peter, to lunch throughout the whole world of lands? Just, then, as Peter and his fellow disciples lived concordantly among themselves, so let those whom Peter planted, fasting on the Sabbath, and those whom his fellow disciples planted, lunching on the Sabbath, live concordantly among themselves. There is indeed this opinion too among very many—although most Romans declare it to be false—that the apostle Peter, being about to contend with Simon Magus on the Lord's day, on account of the very peril of that great temptation, fasted the day before with the Church of that city, and, after so prosperous and glorious a success had followed, kept the same custom, and some Churches of the West imitated him. But if, as this man says, Simon Magus was a figure of the devil, that tempter is plainly neither a Sabbatarian nor a Lord's-day man, but a daily one; nor yet is there fasting against him daily, since on all the Lord's days, on the fifty days after Easter, and in various places on the solemn days of the martyrs and on every feast, one lunches: and yet the devil is conquered, if *our eyes* are *always to the Lord*, that he himself may *pluck our feet out of the snare*[^39]; and if *whether we eat or drink, or whatever we do, we do all to the glory of God*[^40]; and, as far as is in us, we are *without offense to Jews and Greeks and to the Church of God*[^41]. Which those consider too little who eat with offense, or fast with offense, and through either intemperance stir up scandals, by which the devil is not overcome, but gladdened.

**9. 22.** But if it is answered that James taught this at Jerusalem, John at Ephesus, and the rest in other places, what Peter taught at Rome, that is, that one should fast on the Sabbath, but that the other lands deviated from this teaching, and Rome stood firm in it: and on the contrary it is replied that rather some places of the West, in which Rome is, did not keep what the Apostles handed down; but that the lands of the East, whence the Gospel itself began to be preached, remained, without any variation, in that which was handed down by all the Apostles together with Peter himself, namely that one should not fast on the Sabbath: this is an interminable contention, begetting quarrels, not ending questions. Let there be, then, one faith of the universal Church, which is everywhere spread, as it were within in the members, even if that very unity of faith is celebrated by certain diverse observances, by which in no way is that which is true in the faith hindered. For *all the beauty of the king's daughter is within*: but those observances which are variously celebrated are understood to be in her garment; whence it is there said: *In golden fringes, clothed around with variety*[^42]. But let that garment too be varied by diverse celebrations in such a way that it is not torn apart by adverse contentions.

**10. 23.** "Finally," he says, "if the Jew, by keeping the Sabbath, denies the Lord's day, how does the Christian observe the Sabbath? Either let us be Christians, and keep the Lord's day; or let us be Jews, and observe the Sabbath: for *no one can serve two lords*[^43]." Does he not speak as though the lord of the Sabbath were one, and of the Lord's day another? Nor does he hear that which he himself also recalled: *For the Son of man is Lord of the Sabbath*[^44]. But that he wishes us to be so foreign to the Sabbath as the Jews are foreign to the Lord's day, does he not err so much that he could even say that we ought not to receive the Law or the Prophets, just as the Jews do not receive the Gospel or the Apostles? Which whoever savors, you surely understand what evil he savors. But "the old things," he says, "have all passed away, and in Christ they have been made new[^45]": this is true. For therefore we do not, like the Jews, keep idle on Sabbaths, even if, to signify the rest which was signified on that day, with Christian sobriety and frugality preserved, we relax the bond of fasting. And if some of our brothers do not think that the rest of the Sabbath should be signified by the relaxation of fasting, we by no means quarrel about the variety of the royal garment, lest we vex the inner members of the queen herself, where we retain one faith even about that rest. For although, since the old things have passed away, with them the carnal rest of the Sabbath too has passed away; yet, because we lunch on the Sabbath and the Lord's day without superstitious idleness, we do not on that account serve two lords, because the lord of the Sabbath and of the Lord's day is one.

**10. 24.** But this man, who says that the old things have so passed away that "in Christ the altar of sacrifice gave way to the altar, the sword to fasting, the fire to prayers, the cattle to bread, the blood to the cup," does not know that the name "altar" is more frequented in the writings of the Law and the Prophets, and that an altar was first set up for God in the tabernacle, which was made through Moses[^46]; and that the altar of sacrifice too is found in the apostolic writings, where the Martyrs cry out under the altar of God[^47]. He says that the sword gave way to fasting, not recalling that with which the evangelical soldiers are armed from both Testaments, the twice-sharp sword[^48]. He says that the fire gave way to prayers, as though prayers were not then offered in the temple too, and now fire has been sent by Christ into the world[^49]. He says that the cattle gave way to bread, as though not knowing that then too the loaves of proposition used to be placed on the Lord's table[^50], and that now he takes a part of the body of the immaculate Lamb. He says that the blood gave way to the cup, not considering that even now he receives blood in the cup. How much better and more fittingly, then, would he say that the old things have passed away and the new have been made in Christ, in such a way that the altar gave way to the altar, the sword to the sword, the fire to the fire, the bread to the bread, the cattle to the cattle, the blood to the blood. For we see in all these the carnal oldness giving way to the spiritual newness. So, then, it must be understood, whether on this revolving seventh day one lunches, or whether by some one even fasts, yet that the carnal Sabbath has given way to the spiritual Sabbath; since on the latter the everlasting and true rest is desired, while on the former the now-superstitious temporal idleness is despised.

**11. 25.** The rest that follows, by which this man concludes his disputation, like certain other things which I have not thought worth recalling thence, pertain much more not to the cause, in which there is dispute about fasting or lunching on the Sabbath. But these I leave to you yourself to attend to and judge, especially if you are helped in anything by the things said by me. But if, since I think I have answered this man sufficiently, as far as I could, you ask my opinion on this matter, I, turning it over in mind in the evangelical and apostolic writings, and the whole instrument which is called the New Testament, see that fasting is commanded. But on which days one ought not to fast, and on which one ought, I do not find defined by a precept of the Lord or of the Apostles. And thereby I judge that—not indeed for obtaining the rest which faith obtains, and the righteousness in which is *the beauty of the king's daughter within*[^51], but for signifying the everlasting rest where the true Sabbath is—the relaxation rather than the constriction of fasting more aptly suits.

**11. 26.** Nevertheless, in the fasting or lunching of this Sabbath, nothing seems to me to be more safely and peaceably observed than that *he who eats, let him not despise him who does not eat, and he who does not eat, let him not judge him who eats; because neither if we eat shall we abound, nor if we do not eat shall we be in want*[^52]: the unoffending fellowship of those among whom we live, and with whom we live to God, being preserved in these matters. For just as what the Apostle says is true, *that it is evil for the man who eats with offense*[^53]; so it is evil for the man who fasts with offense. Let us therefore not be like those who, seeing John neither eating nor drinking, said: *He has a demon*[^54]. But neither, again, like those who, seeing Christ eating and drinking, said: *Behold a man voracious and a wine-drinker, a friend of publicans and sinners*[^55]. For the Lord himself subjoined to these words a very necessary thing, saying: *And wisdom is justified by her children*[^56]. But who these are, if you ask, read what is written: *The children of wisdom are the Church of the just*[^57]: these are they who, when they eat, do not despise those who do not eat; when they do not eat, do not judge those who eat; but they plainly either despise or judge those who, with offense, do not eat or eat.

**12. 27.** And about the Sabbath day, indeed, the case is easier, because both the Roman Church fasts, and some others, even if few, whether near it or far off: but to fast on the Lord's day is a great scandal, especially after the heresy of the Manichees, detestable and most openly contrary to the catholic faith and the divine Scriptures, became known—who appointed for their hearers this day, as it were, as a lawful day for fasting; through which it came about that fasting on the Lord's day was held the more horrible. Unless perhaps someone is fit to continue a fast beyond a week with no refreshment interposed, so as to approach, as far as he can, the fasting of forty days, as we have known some to have done. For that someone even reached that very forty-day number has been affirmed to us by brothers most worthy of trust. For just as in the times of the ancient fathers, Moses and Elijah did nothing against the lunches of the Sabbath, when they fasted for forty days; so he who has been able to pass seven days by fasting did not choose the Lord's day for fasting, but found it among those very many days which he vowed to fast. Yet a fast, even a continued one, if it must be broken within a week, is broken on no day more fittingly than the Lord's day. But if the body is refreshed after a week, the Lord's day is surely not chosen for fasting; but it is found in the number which it was resolved to vow.

**12. 28.** Nor let it move you that the Priscillianists, most like the Manichees, are wont to bring forward, for fasting on the Lord's day, a testimony from the Acts of the Apostles, when the apostle Paul was at Troas. For thus it is written: *On the first day of the week, when we had gathered to break bread, Paul discoursed to them, being about to depart the next day, and he prolonged his discourse until the middle of the night*[^58]. Then, when he had come down from the upper room, where they had gathered, to raise up the young man who, weighed down by sleep, had fallen from the window and was being carried as dead, the Scripture speaks thus of the Apostle himself: *But having gone up*, it says, *when he had broken bread and tasted, and had spoken enough until daybreak, he so set out*[^59]. Far be it that this should be so taken, as though the Apostles were wont to fast solemnly on the Lord's day. For "the first of the week" was then called the day which is now called the Lord's day, which is found more openly in the Gospels. For the day of the Lord's resurrection is called by Matthew *the first of the Sabbath*[^60], but by the other three *the first of the week*[^61]; which it is agreed is that day which was afterward called the Lord's day. Either, then, after the Sabbath day was finished, they had gathered at the beginning of the night, which night surely now pertained to the Lord's day, that is, to the first of the week; and so on the same night, about to break bread, as it is broken in the sacrament of the body of Christ, he prolonged the discourse until the middle of the night, so that, after the sacraments were celebrated, again addressing those gathered until daybreak, since he was in much haste, he might set out as the Lord's day was dawning: or surely, if on the first of the week they had gathered not through the night, but through the day at the hour of the Lord's day; by the very fact that it was said, *Paul discoursed to them, being about to depart the next day*, the cause of prolonging the discourse is expressed, because he was about to depart, and desired to instruct them sufficiently. They did not therefore solemnly fast on the Lord's day, but the necessary discourse, which was heard with the ardor of most fervent zeal, seemed not to need to be interrupted for the sake of refreshing the body, by the Apostle about to set out, who, on account of his various other journeys here and there, either never or most rarely visited them; especially because then, as the sequel teaches, he was about to depart from those lands in such a way that he would no longer see them in the flesh. And thereby it is rather shown that fasts were not customary for them on the Lord's days, because, lest this be believed, the writer of the book took care to set forth the cause of prolonging the discourse; so that we might know that, if some necessity arises, lunch is not to be preferred to a more urgent action: although by those listening most avidly, and thinking of the very fountain about to depart, and therefore drinking with great thirst, not of water but of the word, whatever flowed in, without satiety, not only the carnal lunch but even the supper was despised.

**12. 29.** But then, although fasts were not customary for them on the Lord's day, there was not yet so signal an offense to the Church, if, by some such necessity as the apostle Paul had, they did not take care to refresh their bodies for a whole Lord's day until the middle of the night, or even until daybreak. But now, after the heretics, especially the most impious Manichees, began to dogmatize and became known to the Christian peoples for practicing fasts on the Lord's day, not when some necessity occurred, but as if by an appointed sacred solemnity; surely I judge that not even by such a necessity as the Apostle had ought one to do what he did, lest a greater evil be incurred in the scandal than good be gained from the word. Yet whatever cause or necessity should arise, why a Christian man should be compelled to fast on the Lord's day—as we find also that case in the Acts of the Apostles, in the peril of shipwreck, where the Apostle himself was sailing, when there was fasting for fourteen days, and thereby for two Lord's days[^62]—we ought in no way to doubt that the Lord's day, when not several days to be continued without any refreshment are vowed, is not to be placed among the days of fasting.

**13. 30.** But why the Church fasts especially on the fourth and sixth days, this reason seems to be given: that, considering the Gospel, on that very fourth day of the week, which is commonly called the fourth feria, the Jews are found to have taken counsel to kill the Lord. But, one day having been passed—on the evening of which the Lord ate the Passover with his disciples, which was the end of that day which we call the fifth of the week—he was then betrayed on that night which already pertained to the sixth of the week, which is the manifest day of his passion. This was the first day of unleavened bread, beginning from the evening. But the evangelist Matthew says that the fifth of the week was the first day of unleavened bread; because, on its following evening, the paschal supper was to be, at which supper the unleavened bread and the sacrifice of the lamb began to be eaten. From which it is gathered that it was the fourth of the week, when the Lord said: *You know that after two days the Passover will be, and the Son of man will be betrayed to be crucified*[^63]: and thereby that day itself is reckoned for fasting, because, as the Evangelist continues and says: *Then the chief priests and elders of the people gathered in the court of the high priest, who was called Caiaphas, and took counsel to take Jesus by deceit and to kill him*[^64]. But, one day having been passed, of which the Gospel says: *On the first day of unleavened bread the disciples came to Jesus, saying: Where do you wish that we prepare for you to eat the Passover?*[^65]—on this day, then, being passed, the Lord suffered, which no one doubts, on the sixth of the week; wherefore the sixth too is rightly reckoned for fasting: for fasts signify humility. Whence it is said: *And I humbled my soul in fasting*[^66].

**13. 31.** There follows the Sabbath, on which day the flesh of Christ rested in the tomb, just as in the first works of the world *God rested* on that day *from all his works*[^67]. Hence arose this variety in the garment of that queen, so that some—as especially the peoples of the East—preferred, in order to signify the rest, to relax the fast, while others, on account of the humility of the Lord's death, preferred to fast, as the Roman and some Western Churches. Which indeed, on the one day on which Easter is celebrated, on account of renewing the memory of the deed by which the disciples humanly mourned the death of the Lord, is fasted by all in such a way that even those who lunch on the other Sabbaths through the whole year most devoutly celebrate the fast of that Sabbath; signifying, of course, both things—both, on the one anniversary day, the mourning of the disciples, and, on the other Sabbaths, the good of rest. For there are two things which cause the blessedness of the just, and the end of all misery, to be hoped for: the death and the resurrection of the dead. In death there is rest, of which it is said through the Prophet: *My people, enter into your chambers; hide yourself for a little while until the wrath of the Lord pass over*[^68]. But in the resurrection there is, in the whole man, that is, in flesh and spirit, perfect happiness. Hence it came about that each of these two was thought to be signified not by the labor of fasting, but rather by the cheerfulness of refreshment, except for the one paschal Sabbath, on which, as we have said, the mourning of the disciples, on account of the memory of the deed, was to be signified by a more prolonged fast.

**14. 32.** But since we do not find, as I have already recalled above, in the evangelical and apostolic writings, which properly pertain to the revelation of the New Testament, that fasts are evidently commanded to be observed on any certain days, and therefore this matter too, like very many others which it is difficult to enumerate, has found in the garment of that king's daughter, that is, of the Church, a place of variety; I will indicate to you what the venerable Ambrose, by whom I was baptized, bishop of Milan, answered me when I inquired about this. For when my mother was with me in that same city, and we, still catechumens, caring little about these things, she bore a solicitude whether, according to the custom of our city, she ought to fast on the Sabbath, or, according to the custom of the Church of Milan, to lunch, that I might free her from this hesitation, I asked the aforesaid man of God. But he: "What can I teach you herein," he said, "more than I myself do?" Where I had thought that by this answer he had prescribed nothing except that we should lunch on the Sabbath; for I knew that he himself did this: but he went on and added: "When I am here, I do not fast on the Sabbath; when I am at Rome, I fast on the Sabbath: and to whatever Church you come," he said, "keep its custom, if you do not wish to suffer scandal or to cause it." This answer I reported to my mother, and it sufficed her, nor did she doubt that it must be obeyed: this we too followed. But since it happens, especially in Africa, that one Church, or the Churches of one region, have some who lunch on the Sabbath, others who fast; the custom of those to whom the governing of the congregation of those peoples has been committed seems to me to be the one I must follow. Wherefore, if you gladly acquiesce in my counsel—especially because in this cause I have perhaps spoken more than was enough, at your asking and urging—do not resist your bishop in this matter, and what he himself does, follow without any scruple or dispute.
