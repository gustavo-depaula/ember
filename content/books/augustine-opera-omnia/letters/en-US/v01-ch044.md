# Letter 44

To the most genuine, most beloved, and in Christ praiseworthy lords and brothers Eleusius, Glorius, and the two Felixes, Augustine

**1. 1.** Fortunius, whom you have as bishop of Tubursicum, we experienced—through that same city, though in greatest haste, when we were going to the Church of Cirta—altogether as you are wont most kindly to promise concerning him. For when we reported to him that very conversation of yours about him, wishing to see him, he did not refuse. And so we came to him; because it seemed to us that this ought to be deferred to his age, rather than that it should be exacted that he himself come to us first. We therefore set out, accompanied by not a few, whom that time had found gathered to us by chance. But when we had sat down with him, the rumor being spread abroad, no small crowd besides flowed together; but to us, in all that multitude, very few appeared who desired that that cause be conducted usefully and wholesomely, and that so great a question of so great a matter be discussed prudently and piously. But the rest had gathered rather for a spectacle, as it were of our altercation, after the almost theatrical custom, than for the instruction of salvation with Christian devotion. Wherefore they could neither afford us silence, nor converse intently and with us at least modestly and orderly, except, as I said, a few, whose religious and simple intent appeared. And so, with all freely speaking immoderately according to each one's impulse of mind, everything was disturbed by the din, nor could we, whether by asking, sometimes even by chiding, prevail, that a modest silence be afforded us.

**1. 2.** The matter, however, began somehow to be conducted, and we drew out several hours in alternate discourse, as much as we were allowed, the voices of the variously tumultuous ones resting in between. But at the very beginning of the disputation, when we saw that the things which were said slipped from time to time from the memory either of ours, or of those whose salvation we most cared for; and that our treatment might be more cautious and more modest, and at the same time that you and other brothers who were absent might learn by reading what had been done between us, we asked that our words be taken down by notaries. For a long time it was resisted by him or by those agreeing with him; afterward, however, he himself granted it. But the notaries who were present, and could do this vigorously, for some reason I do not know were unwilling to take it down. We at least arranged that the brothers who were with us should take it down, although in this matter they could do it more slowly, promising that we would leave the same tablets there. It was agreed. Our words began to be taken down, and some things were said to one another for the tablets. Afterward, the notaries, unable to sustain the disorderly interruptions of the clamorous ones, and on that account our too tumultuous disputation, gave up; we, indeed, not desisting, and, as opportunity was given to each, saying many things. From all which words of ours, as far as I could recall, I have been unwilling to defraud your Love of the conduct of the whole cause. For you can read my letter to him, that either he may approve that I have written true things; or that he himself may, without hesitation, indicate to you if he recalls anything better.

**2. 3.** For first he deigned to proclaim our life, which he said he had learned from you—relating it more benevolently perhaps than truly—adding that he had said to you that we could do well all the things which you had told about us, if we did them in the Church. Then we began to ask what that Church was, where one ought so to live; whether that one which, as the holy Scripture so long ago foretold, would spread itself through the world of lands, or that one which a small part either of the Africans or of Africa contained. Here first he tried to assert that his communion was everywhere on earth. I asked whether he could give letters of communion, which we call "formed," to wherever I wished, and I affirmed, what was manifest to all, that in this way that question could most easily be ended. But I was preparing that, if he should consent, such letters should be sent by us to those churches which, in the apostolic authorities, we would read alike to have been founded at that time.

**2. 4.** But because the matter was openly false, with mixed words it was quickly departed from there; among which words he recalled that gospel admonition of the Lord, by which he said: *Beware of false prophets; many will come to you in the clothing of sheep, but within they are ravening wolves: by their fruits you will know them*[^1]. Which words of the Lord, when we said the same could be recited by us against them, it was thence come to the exaggeration of persecution, which he said his party had often endured; hence wishing to show that his were Christians, because they suffered persecution. Among which words, when I was preparing to answer from the Gospel, he recalled the chapter first, where the Lord says: *Blessed are those who suffer persecution for righteousness' sake, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven*[^2]. At which place I, rejoicing, immediately subjoined that this, then, was to be inquired into: whether they suffered persecution for righteousness' sake. In which question I desired it to be discussed—which indeed was clear to all—whether the times of Macarius found them established in the unity of the Church, or already divided in schism; so that those who wished to see whether they suffered persecution for righteousness might rather attend to this, whether they rightly cut themselves off from the unity of the whole world. But if they were found to have done unjustly, it would be manifest that they suffered persecution rather for injustice than for righteousness; and therefore could not be added to the number of the blessed, of whom it is said: *Blessed are those who suffer persecution for righteousness' sake*. There the more famous than certain betrayal of the Codices was recalled. But it was answered by our party that their chief men had rather been the traitors: but that, if they would not believe our letters about this matter, neither ought we to be compelled to believe their letters.

**3. 5.** But yet, that doubtful question set aside, I asked how these men had justly separated themselves from the innocence of the other Christians, who, throughout the world of lands keeping the order of succession, established in the most ancient Churches, were utterly ignorant who had been traitors in Africa; who surely could not communicate except with those whom they heard sitting in the episcopal sees. He answered that the Churches of the parts across the sea had remained innocent so long, until they consented to the blood of those whom he said had endured the Macarian persecution. Whereupon I could indeed say that the innocence of the Churches across the sea could not have been contaminated even by the odium of the Macarian time; since it would in no way be proved that those men had done even what Macarius had done at their authorship. But I preferred to ask by way of a shortcut, if the savagery of Macarius, from which the Churches across the sea were said to have consented to it, made them lose their innocence, whether it could at least be proved that until those times the Donatists remained in unity with the eastern Churches and the other parts of the world.

**3. 6.** Then he brought forward a certain volume, where he wished to show that the Council of Sardica had given a letter to the African bishops who were of the communion of Donatus. Which, when it was read, we heard the name of Donatus among the other bishops to whom they had written. And so we began to demand that it be said whether this was the Donatus from whose party these men are named: for it could have come about that they wrote to some Donatus, a bishop of another heresy, since especially in those names not even mention of Africa was made. How then could he prove that Donatus, the bishop of the party of Donatus, was to be taken by that name, when he could not even prove whether those letters had been sent specifically to the bishops of the African Churches? For although the name of Donatus is wont to be African, yet it would not be repugnant to the truth that either someone of those parts be called by an African name, or that some African be established a bishop in those parts. For neither in them do we find either the day or the consul, so that at least, the times being considered, something certain might shine forth. Indeed, since at some time we had heard that the Arians, when they had differed from catholic communion, had tried to ally the Donatists in Africa to themselves: brother Alypius suggested this very thing to me in my ear. Then, the volume itself being taken, considering the statutes of the same council, I read that Athanasius, the catholic bishop of Alexandria, whose conflict of most fierce disputations against the Arians especially stood out, and Julius, bishop of the Roman Church, no less catholic, had been disapproved by that Council of Sardica. Whence it was established with us that it had been a council of the Arians, whom these catholic bishops most vehemently resisted. And so, for an even more diligent discussion of the times, we wished to take the volume itself and carry it off with us. He was unwilling to give it, saying that we had it there, when we wished to consider anything in it. I asked also that he permit it to be noted by my hand—fearing, I confess, lest perhaps, the cause requiring it, when it should be asked for, another should be brought forward in its place; but neither did he wish this.

**4. 7.** Then he began to press me to answer briefly to his question, asking of me whom I thought just, him who persecuted, or him who suffered persecution. To whom I answered that it was not rightly so asked: for it could come about that both be wicked, it could even come about that the more just persecute the more wicked. It is not therefore consequent that anyone is the more just because he suffers persecution, although this often happens. Then, when I saw that he dwelt much on this, that he wished the righteousness of his party to be understood as certain from the fact that it had suffered persecution; I asked of him whether he thought Ambrose, bishop of the Church of Milan, just and a Christian. He was surely compelled to deny that that man was a Christian and just; because, if he confessed it, we would immediately object that he judged him to be rebaptized. When, therefore, he was compelled to say those things by which that man would not be held a Christian and just, I recalled how great a persecution he had endured, the church even surrounded by armed soldiers. I asked also whether he thought Maximian, who had made a schism from them at Carthage, both just and a Christian. He could only deny it. I recalled, then, that he too had endured such persecution that his church was destroyed to its foundations. By these examples, then, I strove, if I could, to persuade him that he should now cease to say that the endurance of persecution is a most certain document of Christian righteousness.

**4. 8.** He also related that, in the very newness of the schism, his elders, when they were considering the fault of Caecilian, in order that a schism might not be made, wishing in some way to lull it, gave a certain interventor to the people of his communion established at Carthage, before Majorinus was ordained against Caecilian. This interventor, then, he said had been killed by our people in his own little assembly. Which, I confess, I had never before at all heard, although so many crimes objected by them were refuted and confuted by our people, and more and greater ones hurled against them. But yet, after he related this, he again began insistently to ask of me whom I thought just, him who killed, or him who had been killed; as though he had now proved to me that it had been committed as he had related. I said, therefore, that first it was to be inquired whether it were true; for one ought not rashly to believe whatever is said: and yet it could have come about, that either both were equally evil, or even that some evil man killed a worse one. For in truth it can come about that the rebaptizer of the whole man is more wicked than the slayer of the body only.

**4. 9.** Whence that too which he afterward asked of me was now not to be asked. For he said also that an evil man ought not to have been killed by Christians and just men; as though we call those just who do these things in the Catholic Church: which, however, are wont to be said by these men against us more easily than proved, when so many most violent slaughters and massacres very many of them—both bishops and presbyters and any clergy whatever—cease not to inflict, crowds of most furious men being gathered, not only on catholics, but sometimes even on their own, where they can. Since this is so, yet dissembling about the most wicked deeds of his own, which he himself knows better, he urged that I say which of the just had killed even some evil man. Which, even if it now did not pertain to the cause; for we confessed that these things, wherever they were done under the Christian name, were not done by good men; yet, that he might be admonished what was to be inquired into, we answered, asking whether Elijah seemed to him to have been just: which he could not deny. Then we subjoined how many false prophets he slew with his own hand[^3]. Here in truth he saw what was to be seen, that such things had been permitted to the just. For these things they did by a prophetic spirit, by the authority of God, who without doubt knows for whom it is even profitable to be killed. He demanded, then, that I show, now in the times of the New Testament, which of the just had killed anyone, even a wicked and impious man.

**5. 10.** Then it was returned to the earlier treatment, by which we wished to show that neither ought we to object to them the crimes of their own, nor they to us, if any such deeds of ours were found. For I showed that from the New Testament it could not indeed be shown that any just man had killed anyone; but yet that this could be proved by the very example of the Lord, that the wicked were tolerated by the innocent. For he endured his betrayer, who had now received his price, among the innocent with him even to the last kiss of peace: to whom he did not keep silent that there was among them a man of so great a crime; and yet the first sacrament of his body and his blood, that man not yet excluded, he gave in common to all[^4]. By which example, when nearly all were moved, he tried to say that, before the Lord's passion, that communion with the wicked man did not harm the Apostles, because they did not yet have the baptism of Christ, but the baptism of John. After he said this, I began to ask of him how, then, it was written that Jesus baptized more than John, since he himself did not baptize, but his disciples—that is, that he baptized through his disciples[^5]. How then did they give what they had not received? which they themselves are especially wont to say. Or did Christ perhaps baptize with the baptism of John? Then in this opinion I was about to ask many things: how it was then asked of John himself about the Lord's baptism, and he answered that the one had the bride, and the other was the bridegroom[^6]. Was it then right that the bridegroom baptize with the baptism of John—that is, with the baptism of a friend or a servant? Then, how could they receive the Eucharist not yet baptized? Or how did he answer Peter wishing to be wholly washed: *He who is once washed need not, when it is right, be washed again, but is wholly clean*[^7]? For perfect cleansing is baptism not in the name of John, but of the Lord, if he who receives it shows himself worthy of it; but if unworthy, the sacraments will remain in him not for salvation, but for ruin. When, therefore, I was about to ask these things, he himself too saw that he ought not to have raised the question about the baptism of the Lord's disciples.

**5. 11.** Thence it went to another matter, many speaking on both sides as they could: among which it was said that our people would still persecute them; and he said to us that he wished to see what sort of men we would show ourselves in that persecution, whether we would consent to such savagery, or would lend no consent. We said that God sees our hearts, which they themselves could not: and that they rashly feared for themselves these things, which, if they should happen, happen from evil men, than whom they hold worse men themselves; nor yet ought we on that account to separate ourselves from catholic communion, if anything should perhaps be done with us unwilling, or even, if we should be able, contradicting, since we have learned a peaceful toleration, the Apostle saying: *Bearing with one another in love, endeavoring to keep the unity of the spirit in the bond of peace*[^8]. Which peace and toleration we said those had not kept who made the schism, so that now among their own, who are gentler than they, they tolerate graver things, lest what is torn be torn further; when they were unwilling to tolerate lighter things for unity itself. We said also that in the times of the ancients the peace of unity and toleration had not yet been proclaimed with so great a commendation as by the example of the Lord and the love of the New Testament; and yet those Prophets and holy men were wont to speak the crimes of their people against the people, when nevertheless they had not attempted to tear themselves from the unity of that people and from the communion of the sacraments to be received alike which then existed.

**5. 12.** Thence, I know not how, it came to the mention of Genethlius of blessed memory, the bishop of Carthage before Aurelius, that he suppressed some ordinance given against them, and did not allow it to have effect. All praised him, and most kindly preferred him. Among which praises it was subjoined by us that yet that very Genethlius too, if he had fallen into their hands, would be judged to be rebaptized. And these things we spoke now standing, because the time of departing pressed. There plainly that old man said that a form had now been made, that whoever of the faithful came to them from us should be baptized, which he, unwilling and with grief of mind, said, as much as he could, it appeared. Indeed, since he himself too most openly groaned over and showed many evils of his own, which was proved by the testimony of his whole city, how removed he was from such deeds, and brought forth, with a modest complaint, the things he was wont to say to his own people, and we recalled that passage of the prophet Ezekiel, where it is openly written that neither the fault of the son is to be imputed to the father, nor the fault of the father to his son, where it is said: *For as the soul of the father is mine, so also the soul of the son is mine; for the soul which sins, it alone shall die*[^9], it pleased all that in such disputations the violent deeds of evil men ought not to be objected to one another by us. There remained, then, the question of schism. And so we exhorted him to strive with us again and again, with a placid and peaceful mind, that so great an inquiry might take an end by a diligent examination. When he kindly said that we alone sought these things, but that our people were unwilling that these be inquired into: with this promise made, we departed, that we would produce for him more of our colleagues—at least ten—who would wish this to be inquired into with as much benevolence and gentleness and as pious a zeal as we now felt that he himself had noticed and approved in us. This too he himself promised, from the number of his own.

**6. 13.** Whence I exhort and beseech you, by the blood of the Lord, that you remind him of his promise, and gravely insist, that the matter begun be carried through, which you see has now come almost to its end. For, as I judge, you can most hardly find in your bishops so useful a mind and will as we perceived in this old man. For on the next day he himself came to us, and we had begun again to inquire into these things. But because the necessity of ordaining a bishop was now snatching us away from there, we could not be with him longer. For we had already sent to the Elder of the Coelicolae, whom we had heard had arisen among them as the founder of a new baptism, and had seduced many by that sacrilege, that we might speak something with him, as much as the straits of that very time allowed. After he learned that the latter would come, seeing that we had undertaken another business, since some necessity of departure pressed him too, he departed from us kindly and peacefully.

**6. 14.** But it seems to me that, in order that we may altogether avoid turbulent crowds, which bring an impediment rather than a help, and may truly, from a friendly and tranquil mind, carry through, with the Lord's aid, so great a business undertaken, we ought to come together at some not large country estate, where there would be a church of none of us: which estate, however, men both of our communion and of his hold in common, as is the estate of Titiana. Whether, then, in the territory of Tubursicum, or of Thagaste, such a place, either the one I have recalled, or some other, be found, let us make the canonical Codices to be at hand. And if any documents can be brought forward from either party: so that, the rest being set aside, with no trouble interrupting, if it please the Lord, on however many days we can have leisure for this, and each of us, before his own host, beseeching the Lord—him aiding to whom Christian peace is most pleasing—we may carry through a matter so great and begun with good mind to the end of the inquiry. Write back, indeed, what seems about this matter either to you or to him.
