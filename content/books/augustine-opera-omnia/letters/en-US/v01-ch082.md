# Letter 82

TO HIS MOST BELOVED LORD, AND TO BE HONORED IN THE BOWELS OF CHRIST, HIS HOLY BROTHER AND FELLOW-PRIEST JEROME, AUGUSTINE SENDS GREETING IN THE LORD.

**1. 1.** Long ago I sent your Charity a lengthy letter, answering that one of yours which you recall having sent through your holy son Asterius, now not only my brother but also my colleague. Whether it deserved to come into your hands, I still do not know; except that, since you write through the most sincere brother Firmus, that if the one who first attacked you with the sword has been repelled with the pen, it should be the part of my humanity and justice to rebuke the accuser, not the one who answers—by this one slenderest indication I somehow conjecture that you have read that letter of mine. For in it I lamented that so great a discord had arisen between you—you of whose great friendship, wherever fame had spread it, brotherly love rejoiced. This I did not do by way of reproaching your Brotherliness, of whom I would not dare to say that I had known any fault in that matter; but by grieving over human misery, whose continuance in friendships, in retaining them by mutual love—however great that love may be—is uncertain. But I had rather have learned from your reply whether you had given me the pardon I had asked: which I desire to be made known to me more openly; although a certain more cheerful look of your letter seems to signify that I have obtained even this—if, however, it was sent after that letter was read, which by no means appears in it.

**1. 2.** You ask, or rather with the confidence of love you bid, that in the field of the Scriptures we sport without mutual hurt to one another. For my part, as far as it concerns me, I would rather we did these things in earnest than in sport. But if you were pleased to use this word on account of its easiness, I confess, I look for something greater from the goodness of your powers, and from a prudence so learned and unhurried, mature in years, studious, and ingenious in diligence—these things being given you, indeed even dictated, by the Holy Spirit—namely, that in great and laborious questions you would help me, not as one sporting in the field of the Scriptures, but as one panting on the mountains. But if, on account of the cheerfulness that befits the dearest friends discussing together, you thought it should be said, "Let us sport": whether what we discuss be open and plain, or arduous and difficult, teach me this very thing, I beg you, in what way we may be able to attain it: so that when perhaps something moves us which has not been proved to us—even if we attend not too cautiously, at least are slower to understand—and we try on the contrary to assert what seems to us, if we say this with somewhat more secure freedom, we may not fall into the suspicion of childish boastfulness, as though we sought fame for our own name by accusing illustrious men; and if anything harsh is brought forth out of the necessity of refuting, we may, so that it become tolerable, surround it with gentler eloquence, lest we be judged to draw a sword smeared with honey. Unless perhaps that is the way by which we may avoid both this fault, or the suspicion of fault—if we dispute with a more learned friend in such a way that whatever he says must be approved, nor is it permitted, even for the sake of inquiry, to resist a little.

**1. 3.** Then indeed, without any fear of giving offense, one sports as in a field; but it would be a wonder if one were not mocked. For I confess to your Charity that I have learned to render this fear and honor only to those books of the Scriptures that are now called canonical, so that I most firmly believe that none of their authors erred in anything in writing. And if I come upon anything in those Letters which seems contrary to the truth, I will not doubt that either the codex is faulty, or the translator did not attain what was said, or that I have by no means understood it. But others I so read that, however much they excel in holiness and learning, I do not therefore think it true because they themselves thought so; but because they were able to persuade me, either through those canonical authors, or by probable reason, that it does not depart from the truth. Nor do I think, my brother, that you feel otherwise: indeed, I say, I do not suppose that you wish your books to be read as though they were the books of the Prophets or the Apostles, of whose writings—that they are free from all error—it is wicked to doubt. Far be this from your pious humility and truthful thinking about yourself; for unless you were endowed with it, you would surely not say: "Would that we deserved your embraces, and by mutual conference might either teach something or learn."

**2. 4.** But if I believe that you said this about yourself—from a consideration of your life and morals—not feignedly nor deceitfully, how much more just is it for me to believe that the apostle Paul felt nothing other than what he wrote, where he says of Peter and Barnabas: *When I saw that they were not walking rightly toward the truth of the Gospel, I said to Peter before them all: If you, being a Jew, live like the Gentiles and not like the Jews, how do you compel the Gentiles to live like Jews*[^1]? For of whom shall I be certain that he does not deceive me in writing or speaking, if the Apostle deceived his sons, whom he was bringing forth again in travail, until Christ, that is, the truth, should be formed in them[^2]? To whom, when he had said first: *What I write to you, behold, before God, that I do not lie*[^3]—yet he was not writing truthfully, but by some dispensational dissimulation was deceiving them, that he had seen Peter and Barnabas not walking rightly toward the truth of the Gospel, and had withstood Peter to his face, for no other reason than that he was compelling the Gentiles to live like Jews!

**2. 5.** But indeed it is better to believe that the apostle Paul wrote something not truthfully, than that the apostle Peter did something not rightly. If it is so, let us say—which God forbid—that it is better to believe that the Gospel lies, than that Christ was denied by Peter[^4]; and that the book of Kingdoms lies, than that so great a prophet, so excellently chosen by the Lord God, committed adultery in coveting and carrying off another man's wife, and so dreadful a homicide in slaying her husband[^5]. No: rather let me read holy Scripture, placed on the highest and heavenly summit of authority, certain and secure of its truth, and let me there truthfully learn that men were either approved, or corrected, or condemned—rather than, while I sometimes fear to believe that things to be reproached were done by certain persons of praiseworthy excellence, let the divine words themselves be everywhere suspect to me.

**2. 6.** The Manicheans contend that very many of the divine Scriptures—by which their wicked error is convicted with the clearest perspicuity of statements—are false, because they cannot twist them into another sense; in such a way, however, that they attribute that same falsity not to the Apostles who wrote them, but to certain corrupters of the codices, I know not whom. Yet because they could never prove this either by more or more ancient copies, or by the authority of the preceding language from which the Latin books were translated, they depart overcome and confounded by the truth most well known to all. Does not your holy prudence understand how great an occasion for their malice lies open, if we say that the apostolic Letters were falsified not by others, but that the Apostles themselves wrote false things?

**2. 7.** It is not credible, you say, that Paul reproved in Peter what Paul himself had done. I do not now inquire what he did; I ask what he wrote: this pertains most of all to the question I have undertaken—that the truth of the divine Scriptures, committed to memory for the building up of our faith, written not by just anyone, but by the Apostles themselves, and through this received into the canonical summit of authority, should remain in every part truthful and beyond doubt. For if Peter did what he ought to have done, Paul lied in saying that he saw him not walking rightly toward the truth of the Gospel. For whoever does what he ought to do, surely does rightly. And therefore he says a falsehood about him who says that he did not do rightly what he knew he ought to have done. But if Paul wrote the truth, it is true that Peter was then not walking rightly toward the truth of the Gospel. He was therefore doing what he ought not to have done; and if Paul himself had already done some such thing, I will rather believe that he too, corrected, could not have neglected the correction of his fellow-apostle, than that he set down something mendaciously in his Epistle—and in any Epistle whatever: how much more in that one in which he had said beforehand: *What I write to you, behold, before God, that I do not lie*[^6]?

**2. 8.** I indeed believe that Peter acted thus, so as to compel the Gentiles to live like Jews. For this I read that Paul wrote, whom I do not believe to have lied. And therefore Peter was not acting rightly in this. For it was against the truth of the Gospel that those who believed in Christ should think they could not be saved without those old sacraments. For this is what those at Antioch who had believed from the circumcision were contending; against whom Paul perseveringly and sharply fought. But Paul himself did not do this—whether that he circumcised Timothy[^7], or paid a vow at Cenchreae[^8], or at Jerusalem, admonished by James, took up with those who knew them the celebration of those legal observances[^9]—in order that it might be thought that through those sacraments Christian salvation also is given; but lest those things which God had bidden be done in former times, as was fitting, in the shadows of things to come, be believed to be condemned, like the idolatry of the Gentiles. For this is what James said to him: that it had been heard of him that he teaches a falling-away from Moses[^10]. And this is surely wicked: that those who believe in Christ should fall away from the prophet of Christ, as though detesting and condemning his teaching; of whom Christ himself says: *If you believed Moses, you would also believe me; for he wrote of me*[^11].

**2. 9.** For attend, I beg, to the very words of James: *You see,* he says, *brother, how many thousands there are in Judea who have believed in Christ; and these are all zealous for the Law. Now they have heard of you that you teach a falling-away from Moses to those Jews who are among the Gentiles, telling them not to circumcise their sons, nor to walk according to the custom. What then is it? Surely the multitude must come together; for they have heard that you have arrived. Do this, then, which we tell you. We have four men who have a vow upon them; take them, sanctify yourself with them, and pay for them, that they may shave their heads; and all will know that the things they have heard about you are false, but that you yourself also follow, keeping the law. But concerning the Gentiles who have believed, we have given judgment that they should observe nothing of this kind, except that they keep themselves from what is sacrificed to idols, and from blood, and from fornication*[^12]. It is not, I think, obscure that James gave this advice for this reason: that those who, though they had believed in Christ from among the Jews, were nevertheless zealous for the Law, might know that the things they had heard about him were false—lest, through the teaching of Christ, the things which had been ministered through Moses to the fathers should be thought condemned as sacrilegious and not written down at God's command. For this had been spread abroad about Paul—not by those who understood with what mind these things ought then to be observed by the faithful Jews, namely for the sake of commending the divine authority and the prophetic holiness of those sacraments, not for the sake of obtaining salvation, which was now revealed in Christ and ministered through the sacrament of Baptism; but those had spread this about Paul who wished them to be observed in such a way as though without them there could be no salvation for believers in the Gospel. For they had perceived him to be a most vehement preacher of grace, and most opposed to their intention, teaching that man is justified not through those things, but through the grace of Jesus Christ, for the foretelling of which those shadows were commanded in the Law. And therefore they, contriving to stir up ill will and persecution, accused him as an enemy of the Law and of the divine commandments; the ill will of which false accusation he could not more fittingly avoid than by himself celebrating those very things which he was thought to condemn as sacrilegious, and so showing that neither were the Jews then to be forbidden them as wicked things, nor were the Gentiles to be compelled to them as necessary.

**2. 10.** For if he had really reproved them in the way it had been heard of him, and had therefore undertaken to celebrate them so that by a feigned action he might be able to conceal his own opinion, James would not say to him: *And all will know;* but he would say, And all will think, *that the things they have heard about you are false*[^13]—especially since in Jerusalem itself the Apostles had already decreed that no one should compel the Gentiles to live like Jews[^14]; but they had not decreed that no one should then forbid the Jews to live like Jews, although Christian teaching no longer compelled even them. Accordingly, if after the decree of the Apostles Peter had that dissimulation at Antioch, by which he compelled the Gentiles to live like Jews—which he himself was no longer compelled to do, though, for the sake of commending the words of God which were entrusted to the Jews[^15], he was not forbidden—what wonder is it if Paul constrained him to assert freely what he remembered he had decreed at Jerusalem along with the other Apostles?

**2. 11.** But if Peter did this—which I rather think—before that Jerusalem council, neither so is it a wonder that Paul wished him not timidly to cover up, but confidently to assert, what he knew Peter already felt alike with himself: whether because he had conferred the Gospel with him; or because in the calling of the centurion Cornelius he had received that Peter had been divinely admonished about this matter[^16]; or because, before those whom he had feared came to Antioch, he had seen him eat together with the Gentiles. For we do not deny that Peter was already of this opinion in which Paul also was. Paul was therefore not then teaching him what was true in that matter; but he was reproving his dissimulation, by which the Gentiles were being compelled to live like Jews—for no other reason than that all those things were being conducted by way of pretense, as though it were true what those said who thought that believers could not be saved without the circumcision of the foreskin and the other observances which were shadows of things to come[^17].

**2. 12.** Therefore he both circumcised Timothy[^18], for this reason: lest to the Jews, and especially to his mother's kindred, those who had believed in Christ from among the Gentiles should seem to detest circumcision, as idolatry is to be detested—since God had commanded the former to be done, while Satan persuaded the latter. And Titus he did not circumcise, for this reason: lest he give occasion to those who said that believers could not be saved without that circumcision, and, to the deception of the Gentiles, boast that Paul too held this view. This he himself sufficiently signifies where he says: *But not even Titus, who was with me, although he was a Greek, was compelled to be circumcised; but on account of false brothers secretly brought in, who had slipped in to spy out our liberty, that they might bring us into slavery, to whom we did not yield in subjection even for an hour, that the truth of the Gospel might remain among you*[^19]. Here it appears what he understood them to be aiming at, so that he would not do what he had done in the case of Timothy, and what he was able to do by that liberty by which he showed that those sacraments ought neither to be sought after as necessary, nor condemned as sacrilegious.

**2. 13.** But we must beware, in this disputation, lest, like the philosophers, we call certain deeds of men things in between right action and sin, which are reckoned neither among right actions nor among sins; and lest we be pressed by the fact that to observe the ceremonies of the Law cannot be indifferent, but must be either good or evil—so that, if we call them good, we too are compelled to observe them; but if evil, we believe that they were observed by the Apostles not truly, but by pretense. For my part, I do not so much fear for the Apostles the example of the philosophers, since they too in their disputation say something true, as that of forensic advocates, when in the conduct of others' cases they lie. If their likeness has been thought fittingly introduced, in the very exposition of the Epistle to the Galatians, to confirm the pretense of Peter and Paul—why should I fear before you the name of the philosophers, who are vain not because they say all things false, but because they both trust in most things that are false, and where they are found to say true things, are estranged from the grace of Christ, who is truth itself?

**2. 14.** But why should I not say that those precepts of the old sacraments were neither good, because men are not justified by them—for they are shadows foretelling the grace by which we are justified—nor yet evil, because they were divinely commanded, suited to the time and to the persons: especially since even a prophetic statement helps me, where God says that he gave that people precepts that were not good[^20]? For perhaps for this reason he did not say "evil," but only "not good," that is, not such that by them men are made good, or without them are not made good. I would wish your kind Sincerity to teach me whether any holy man of the East, when he has come to Rome, fasts feignedly on the Sabbath, except on that day of the paschal vigil. For if we say that this is evil, we will condemn not only the Roman Church, but also many neighboring it, and some somewhat more remote, where the same custom is held and remains. But if we think that not to fast on the Sabbath is evil, with what rashness shall we accuse so many churches of the East, and a much greater part of the Christian world? Does it please you that we call it something in between, which is nevertheless acceptable to him who does it not feignedly, but with a fitting fellowship and observance? And yet we read nothing concerning this commanded to Christians in the canonical books. How much more do I not dare to call evil that which I cannot, by the Christian faith itself, deny that God commanded—by which faith I have learned that I am justified not by it, but by the grace of God through Jesus Christ our Lord?

**2. 15.** I say, therefore, that the circumcision of the foreskin and the other things of this kind were divinely given to the former people, through the Testament which is called Old, for the signification of things to come which had to be fulfilled through Christ; and that, when these had come, those things remained for Christians only to be read, for the understanding of the prophecy that had gone before, but not to be done as necessary, as though the revelation of the faith which was signified by them as to come were still to be awaited. But although they were not to be imposed on the Gentiles, yet they ought not to have been taken away from the custom of the Jews in such a way as to be detested and condemned. Accordingly, gradually and little by little, as the sound preaching of the grace of Christ grew warm—by which alone believers might know themselves to be justified and saved, not by those shadows of things formerly to come, now coming and present—so that, in the calling of those Jews whom the presence of the Lord's flesh and the apostolic times had found in this state, all that action of the shadows might be consumed. This sufficed them for commendation: that it should not be avoided as a thing to be detested and like idolatry; but it should have no further progress, lest it be thought necessary, as though either salvation were from it, or could not be without it. This the heretics thought, who, while they wish to be both Jews and Christians, could be neither Jews nor Christians. Whose opinion you have most kindly deigned to warn me to beware of, although I was never in it. Into the not the consent but the pretense of which opinion Peter had fallen through fear, so that of him Paul most truly wrote that he had seen him not walking rightly toward the truth of the Gospel, and most truly said to him that he was compelling the Gentiles to live like Jews. Which Paul surely was not compelling, for this reason truthfully observing those old things, where there was need, that he might show they were not to be condemned; yet preaching insistently that not by them, but by the revealed grace of faith, the faithful are saved, lest he compel anyone to take them up as though necessary. But I so believe that the apostle Paul did all those things truthfully, and yet I now neither compel nor allow anyone made a Christian out of a Jew to celebrate such things truthfully; just as neither do you, to whom Paul seems to have pretended them, compel or allow such a one to pretend them.

**2. 16.** Or do you wish that I too should say that this is the sum of the question, or rather of your opinion: that after the Gospel of Christ, believing Jews do well if they offer the sacrifices which Paul offered, if they circumcise their sons, if they observe the Sabbath, as Paul did in the case of Timothy, and as all the Jews observed—provided they do these things feignedly and deceitfully? If it is so, we slip not now into the heresy of Ebion, or of those whom they commonly call Nazarenes, or any other old one, but into some new one, I know not what, which is the more pernicious in that it is not by error, but by deliberate purpose and a deceitful will. But if you answer, in order to clear yourself of this opinion, that the Apostles then pretended these things laudably, lest the weak be scandalized, who had believed in great numbers from among the Jews and did not yet understand that they were to be rejected; but that now, with the teaching of Christian grace confirmed through so many nations, confirmed also through all the Churches of Christ by the reading of the Law and the Prophets—in such a way that these things are recited to be understood, not observed—whoever should wish to do them by pretense is mad: why is it not permitted me to say that the apostle Paul, and other Christians of right faith, ought then truthfully to commend those old sacraments by observing them a little while, lest those observances of prophetic signification, kept by the most pious fathers, be thought, like diabolical sacrileges, to be detested by their descendants? For now, when the faith had come which, foretold beforehand by those observances, was after the death and resurrection of the Lord revealed, they had lost, as it were, the life of their office. Nevertheless, just as the bodies of the dead must be conducted, by the offices of their kinsmen, in some sort to burial—and not by pretense, but religiously—and not deserted at once, or cast out to the disparagements of enemies like the bites of dogs: so now, whoever of the Christians, though he be from among the Jews, should wish to celebrate them in like manner, as though digging up ashes laid to rest, will not be a pious conductor or bearer of the body, but an impious violator of the burial.

**2. 17.** I confess indeed that in what my letter contains—that for this reason Paul had undertaken to celebrate the sacraments of the Jews, when he was already an apostle of Christ, so as to teach that they were not pernicious to those who wished to keep them, as they had received them from their parents through the Law—I put too little: *at that time only, when the grace of faith was first revealed;* for then this was not pernicious. But with the progress of time those observances were to be abandoned by all Christians; lest, if it were done then, what God commanded to his people through Moses should not be distinguished from what in the temples of demons the unclean spirit instituted. Accordingly, my negligence is rather to be blamed, because I did not add this, than your reproof. Nevertheless, long before I had received your letters—when writing against Faustus the Manichean, in what way I explained the same passage, though briefly, and that I did not pass over this point there—you will be able to read, if you do not disdain to, your Kindness; and from our dearest friends, through whom I now send these writings, you will, however you wish, be assured that I dictated that before; and believe me concerning my mind, which, speaking before God, I claim by the right of love: that it never seemed to me that even now Christians made out of Jews ought, with any disposition, with any mind, to celebrate those old sacraments, or that it is in any way permitted them—since I have always so held concerning Paul, from the time his Letters became known to me—just as it does not seem to you that anyone ought at this time to pretend these things, although you believe the Apostles did this.

**2. 18.** Accordingly, just as you, on the contrary, declare—and, as you write, though the world cry out against it, with free voice pronounce—that the ceremonies of the Jews are both pernicious and deadly to Christians, and that whoever observes them, whether from among the Jews or from among the Gentiles, has been cast headlong into the pit of the devil: so I altogether confirm this word of yours, and add: Whoever observes them, whether from among the Jews or from among the Gentiles, not only truthfully but even by pretense, has been cast headlong into the pit of the devil. What more do you seek? But just as you separate the pretense of the Apostles from the reckoning of this present time, so I separate the truthful conduct of the apostle Paul, then in all these things, from the observance of the Jewish ceremonies at this present time—an observance by no means pretended; since then it was to be approved, now to be detested. Thus, although we have read: *The Law and the Prophets were until John the Baptist*[^21]; and that *for this reason the Jews sought to kill Christ, because he not only broke the Sabbath, but also called God his Father, making himself equal to God*[^22]; and that *we have received grace for grace*[^23]; and that *the Law was given through Moses, but Grace and truth came through Jesus Christ*[^24]; and that through Jeremiah it was promised that God would give a new Testament to the house of Judah, not according to the Testament which he had arranged with their fathers[^25]: nevertheless I do not think that the Lord himself was deceitfully circumcised by his parents. Or if, on account of his age, he in no way forbade it, neither do I think that he said deceitfully to the leper—whom surely not that observance commanded through Moses, but he himself had commanded: *Go and offer for yourself the sacrifice which Moses commanded, for a testimony to them*[^26]. Nor did he deceitfully go up to the feast day, so far from doing so for the sake of ostentation before men that he did not go up openly, but secretly[^27].

**2. 19.** But, you say, the same Apostle said: *Behold, I, Paul, say to you that if you are circumcised, Christ will profit you nothing*[^28]. He deceived Timothy, then, and made Christ profit him nothing. Or is it that, because this was done deceitfully, it therefore did no harm? But he himself did not put it thus, nor did he say: If you are circumcised truthfully, as not deceitfully; but without any exception he said: *If you are circumcised, Christ will profit you nothing.* Just as, then, you wish to give place here to your opinion, so that you would have it understood as "unless deceitfully," so I, not without shame, demand that you allow us there to understand it said to those: *If you are circumcised,* who wished to be circumcised for this reason, that they thought they could not otherwise be saved in Christ. With this mind, then, with this will, with this intention, whoever was then circumcised, Christ profited him nothing at all—just as elsewhere he openly says: *For if righteousness is through the Law, then Christ died in vain*[^29]. This is declared also by what you yourself recalled: *You who are justified by the Law have been emptied of Christ; you have fallen from grace*[^30]. He therefore reproved those who believed they were justified in the Law, not those who observed those legal things in honor of him by whom they were commanded, understanding both by what reason of foretelling the truth they were commanded, and how long they ought to last. Hence is that which he says: *If you are led by the Spirit, you are no longer under the Law*[^31]—from which, as you gather, it appears that he who is under the Law has not the Holy Spirit, not in a dispensational sense, as you think our elders wished, but truly, as I understand.

**2. 20.** It seems to me a great question, what it is to be under the Law in the way the Apostle blames. For I do not think he says this on account of circumcision, or those sacrifices which were then performed by the fathers and are now not performed by Christians, and other things of this kind; but this very thing which the Law also says: *You shall not covet*[^32], which we certainly confess that Christians ought to observe, and which is preached above all by the illumination of the Gospel—he says the Law is holy, and the commandment holy, and just, and good[^33]; then he adds: *Has then what is good become death to me? God forbid; but sin, that it might appear sin, worked death in me through what is good, so that the sinner, or sin, might become exceedingly sinful through the commandment*[^34]. But what he here says, that sin becomes exceedingly so through the commandment, this he says elsewhere: *The Law crept in so that the offense might abound. But where the offense abounded, grace abounded all the more*[^35]. And elsewhere, when he had spoken above of the dispensation of grace, that it itself justifies, he says, as if asking: *Why then the Law?* and to this question he immediately answered: *It was set down because of transgression, until the offspring should come to whom the promise was made*[^36]. He therefore says that those are damnably under the Law whom the Law makes guilty, not fulfilling the Law, while—not understanding the benefit of grace for doing the precepts of God—they presume, as it were, upon their own powers with proud elation. For *the fullness of the Law is love*[^37]. And the *love of God is poured out in our hearts,* not through ourselves, but *through the Holy Spirit who has been given to us*[^38]. But to explain this matter as much as is sufficient, a longer discourse, perhaps, of its own proper volume is owed. If, then, that which the Law says, *You shall not covet,* if human weakness has not been helped by the grace of God, holds the man guilty under itself, and condemns him as a transgressor rather than frees him as a sinner, how much more could those things which were commanded for the sake of signification—circumcision, and the rest, which it had been necessary to abolish as the revelation of grace became more widely known—justify no one? Yet they were not for that reason to be fled, like the diabolical sacrileges of the Gentiles, even when grace itself had now begun to be revealed, which had been foretold by such shadows; but they were to be permitted a little while, especially to those who had come from that people to whom they had been given. Afterward, however, having as it were been buried with honor, they were to be irreparably abandoned by all Christians.

**2. 21.** But this which you say: *not in a dispensational sense, as our elders wished;* what does it mean, I ask you? For either it is what I call a dutiful lie, so that this dispensation is a kind of office, as it were, of lying honorably; or what else it may be, I altogether do not see—unless perhaps, with the name of "dispensation" added, it comes about that a lie is not a lie; and if this is absurd, why then do you not openly say that the dutiful lie is to be defended? Unless perhaps the word troubles you, because the term "office" is not so customary in ecclesiastical books—a word which our Ambrose did not fear, who wished to call certain books of his, full of useful precepts, On Offices. Or is it that, if anyone lies dutifully, he is to be blamed; but if dispensationally, he is to be approved? I ask you: let him who thinks this lie where he chooses; for in this too there is a great question, whether it is ever permitted for a good man—indeed, a Christian man—to lie, to whom it was said: *Let it be in your mouth, Yes, yes, No, no, so that you do not fall under judgment*[^39]; and who with faith hear: *You will destroy all who speak a lie*[^40].

**2. 22.** But this, as I said, both this and other great questions; let him who thinks this choose where he will lie—provided, however, that of the writing authors of the holy Scriptures, and especially the canonical ones, it be believed without shaking, and defended, that a lie is altogether absent; lest the stewards of Christ, of whom it was said: *Here now it is sought among stewards that one be found faithful*[^41], should seem to have faithfully learned something great for themselves—to lie for the sake of dispensing the truth—since faith itself is so called in the Latin tongue, because what is said is done. But where what is said is done, there is surely no place for lying. The faithful steward, the apostle Paul, therefore shows us beyond doubt his faith in writing, because he was a steward of truth, not of falsehood. And through this he wrote the truth, that he had seen Peter not walking rightly toward the truth of the Gospel, and had withstood him to his face, because he was compelling the Gentiles to live like Jews[^42]. But Peter himself accepted what Paul was doing usefully by the liberty of love, with the holy and kindly piety of humility; and so he furnished posterity a rarer and holier example, by which they would not disdain, if perhaps anywhere they had left the path of rectitude, to be corrected even by those who came after, than Paul, by which lesser men would confidently dare to resist greater ones for the sake of defending the truth of the Gospel, with brotherly love preserved. For although it is better to deviate from the road being kept in nothing than in anything, it is nevertheless far more marvelous and praiseworthy to accept the corrector willingly than to correct the one who strays boldly. There is therefore praise of just liberty in Paul, and of holy humility in Peter: which—as far as it seems to me according to my small measure—ought rather to have been defended against the slandering Porphyry, than that a greater occasion of disparagement be given him, by which he would much more bitingly accuse Christians of either writing their letters or carrying the sacraments of their God deceitfully.

**3. 23.** You demand of me that I show at least some one whose opinion I have followed in this matter, since you have recalled by name so many who preceded you in what you maintain; asking that, if I reprove you for erring in it, I allow you to err with such men, of whom, I confess, I have read none. But although there are about six or seven of them, the authority of four of these you yourself also weaken. For the man of Laodicea, whose name you keep silent, you say recently departed from the Church; Alexander you call an old heretic; and Origen and Didymus I read reproved by you in your more recent works, and not mildly, nor on mild questions—although you had previously praised Origen marvelously. With these, then, I think I would err, because you yourself would not allow it—although this is said just as if they had not erred in this opinion. For who is there who would wish to err with anyone at all? Three therefore remain: Eusebius of Emesa, Theodore of Heraclea, and the one you mention a little later, John, who not long ago governed the Church of Constantinople in the pontifical rank.

**3. 24.** Further, if you seek out or recall what our Ambrose thought on this, and likewise our Cyprian, you will perhaps find that we too were not without men whom we might follow in what we assert. Although, as I said a little before, I owe this ingenuous servitude only to the canonical Scriptures, by which I so follow them alone that I do not doubt that their authors erred in nothing at all in them, set down nothing deceitfully. Accordingly, when I seek a third, so that I too may oppose three to three, I could indeed, I think, easily find one, if I had read much; nevertheless, the apostle Paul himself meets me in place of all these—indeed, above all these. To him I flee; to him I appeal from all who think otherwise, the expositors of his letters; questioning him, I call upon him and inquire in what he wrote to the Galatians[^43], that he had seen Peter not walking rightly toward the truth of the Gospel, and had withstood him to his face for this reason, that by that pretense he was compelling the Gentiles to live like Jews—whether he wrote the truth, or perhaps lied by some dispensational falsity, I know not what. And I hear him, a little above, at the beginning of that same narration, crying out to me with a religious voice: *What I write to you, behold, before God, that I do not lie*[^44].

**3. 25.** Let whoever thinks otherwise grant pardon; I rather believe so great an apostle swearing in his own letters and for his own letters, than anyone most learned disputing about another's letters. Nor do I fear to be told that I so defend Paul that he did not pretend the error of the Jews, but truly was in error. For neither did he pretend an error who, by apostolic liberty, as befitted that time, by performing those old sacraments where there was need, commended them—established prophetically not by Satan's cunning to deceive men, but by God's providence to foretell things to come; nor had he truly been in the error of the Jews, who not only knew, but also insistently and sharply preached, that those erred who thought they were to be imposed on the Gentiles, or were necessary for the justification of any believers whatever.

**3. 26.** But as for my saying that he became to the Jews as a Jew, and to the Gentiles as a Gentile, not by the cunning of one lying, but by the affection of one compassionate: in what way I said it, you seem to me to have attended too little; or rather, perhaps I could not explain this sufficiently. For I did not say this because he mercifully pretended those things; but because he so did not pretend the things he did that were like the Jews, just as neither those that he did that were like the Gentiles, which you too have recalled; and in this you have helped me, which I confess not ungratefully. For when I had asked you in my letter how he can be thought to have become to the Jews as a Jew because he deceitfully took up the sacraments of the Jews, when to the Gentiles also he became as a Gentile, and yet did not deceitfully take up the sacrifices of the Gentiles, you answered that he became to the Gentiles as a Gentile in that he received the uncircumcision, in that he indifferently permitted himself to eat foods which the Jews condemn; whereupon I ask whether he also did this by pretense; and if that is most absurd and most false, then likewise also those things in which he conformed to the custom of the Jews were done by prudent liberty, not by servile necessity, or, what is more unworthy, by a deceitful rather than a faithful dispensation.

**3. 27.** For to the faithful, and to those who have known the truth, as he himself testifies—unless perhaps here too he deceives—*every creation of God is good, and nothing is to be rejected which is received with thanksgiving*[^45]. Therefore to Paul himself—not only a man, but also a most faithful steward, not only a knower, but also a teacher of truth—surely every creation of God in foods was good not by pretense, but truly. Why, then, by taking up nothing of the sacred rites and ceremonies of the Gentiles by pretense, but by feeling and teaching the truth about foods and uncircumcision, did he nevertheless become to the Gentiles as a Gentile, and could not become to the Jews as a Jew, except by deceitfully taking up the sacraments of the Jews? Why, with the wild olive grafted in, did he preserve the truthful faith of his dispensation[^46], while over the natural branches—set not outside, but in the tree—he drew some veil of dispensational pretense, I know not what? Why, having become to the Gentiles as a Gentile, does he teach what he feels, and feel what he does; but having become to the Jews as a Jew, does he shut up one thing in his breast and bring forth another in words, in deeds, in writings? But far be it to think this. For to both he owed love from a pure heart and a good conscience and unfeigned faith[^47]. And through this he became all things to all, that he might gain all[^48]—not by the cunning of one lying, but by the affection of one compassionate; that is, not by deceitfully doing all the evils of men, but by procuring, for all the evils of all others as if they were his own, the diligence of a merciful medicine.

**3. 28.** And so, since he by no means refused that those sacraments of the Old Testament should be performed even by himself, he was not mercifully deceiving, but, deceiving not at all, and in this way commending that they had been commanded by the Lord God for a dispensation up to a certain time, he distinguished them from the sacrilegious rites of the Gentiles. But then, not by the cunning of one lying, but by the affection of one compassionate, he became to the Jews as a Jew, when he desired so to free them from that error—by which they either were unwilling to believe in Christ, or thought that through their old priesthoods and observances of ceremonies they could be cleansed from sins and made saved—as if he himself were held by that error; loving, surely, his neighbor as himself, and doing to others these things which he would wish to be done to him by others, if he had this need: which, when the Lord had advised, he added: *For this is the Law and the Prophets*[^49].

**3. 29.** This affection of one compassionate he commands in the same Epistle to the Galatians, saying: *If a man be overtaken in any fault, you who are spiritual instruct such a one in the spirit of gentleness, looking to yourself, lest you also be tempted*[^50]. See whether he did not say: Become as he is, so that you may gain him. Not, surely, that he should deceitfully act the beloved one, or pretend to have what he had; but that in another's fault he should attend to what could also happen to himself, and so come mercifully to the aid of the other, as he would wish to be aided by another himself: this is not by the cunning of one lying, but by the affection of one compassionate. Thus to the Jew, thus to the Gentile, thus to any man at all set in some error or sin, Paul, not by pretending what he was not, but by being compassionate, because he could have been such—as one who thought of himself as a man—became all things to all, that he might gain all[^51].

**4. 30.** Look, if you please, I beg you, a little at yourself; at yourself, I say, in relation to me; and recall—or, if you have them written down, reread—your words in that shorter letter which you sent me through our brother, now my colleague, Cyprian, with how truthful, how genuine, how full an affection of love, when you had gravely expostulated that I had committed certain things against you, you added: *In this friendship is wounded, in this the rights of intimacy are violated, lest we seem to contend childishly, and to give material for contending to those who favor us, or to those who disparage us, in turn.* I perceive these words to have been said by you not only from the heart, but also with a kindly mind to counsel me. Finally you add—what would be apparent even if you did not add it—and you say: *I write this because I desire to love you purely and as a Christian, and to retain nothing in my mind that differs from my lips.* O holy man, and beloved by me, as God sees my soul, with a truthful heart, this very thing which you set down in your letter, which I do not doubt you displayed toward me, this very thing I believe the apostle Paul altogether displayed in his Letters—not to any one man, but to the Jews, and the Greeks, and all the Gentiles, his sons, whom he had begotten in the Gospel, and whom he was bringing forth in travail to be born[^52]; and then to so many thousands of later faithful Christians, for whose sake that Epistle was committed to memory—so that he retained nothing in his mind that differed from his lips.

**4. 31.** Surely you too became, as I did, not by the cunning of one lying, but by the affection of one compassionate, when you thought that I was as little to be left in that fault into which you supposed me to have slipped, as you would wish to be yourself, if you had slipped in that way. Hence, giving thanks to your kindly mind toward me, I at the same time ask that you also not be angry with me, that, when certain things in your works moved me, I made my being moved known to you—wishing this to be preserved toward me by all, which I myself preserved toward you: that whatever they think should be disapproved in my writings, they neither shut up in a crafty breast, nor so reprove before others as to be silent before me; thinking that hence rather friendship is wounded and the rights of intimacy violated. For I do not know whether those are to be thought Christian friendships in which the common proverb prevails more: *Compliance begets friends, truth begets hatred*, than the ecclesiastical one: *More faithful are the wounds of a friend than the voluntary kisses of an enemy*[^53].

**4. 32.** Accordingly, let us rather teach our dearest friends, who most sincerely favor our labors, with as much insistence as we can, so that they may know that it can come to pass that among the dearest something be contradicted by one or the other in speech, and yet love itself not be diminished, nor truth, which is owed to friendship, beget hatred—whether what is contradicted be true, or whatever it be, be said with a truthful heart, retaining nothing in the mind that differs from the lips. Let our brothers, then, your intimates, to whom you bear witness that they are vessels of Christ, believe that it was done against my will, and that no small grief about this matter is in my heart, that my letters came into the hands of many before they could reach you, to whom they were written. But in what way this happened, it is both long to tell, and, unless I am mistaken, superfluous: since it suffices, if anything is credited to me in this, that it was not done with the mind that is supposed; nor was it at all of my will, or arrangement, or consent, or even thought, that it should be done. If they do not believe this, which I say with God as witness, I have nothing more to do. Yet far be it that I should believe that they suggest these things to your Holiness with a malevolent mind, to stir up enmities between us—which may the mercy of the Lord our God turn away from us—but that, without any mind to harm, they easily suspect human faults of a man. For it is fair for me to believe this about them: if they are vessels of Christ, made not for dishonor, but for honor, and set in a great house by God, for a good work[^54]. But if, after this attestation of mine, if it shall have come to their notice, they should wish to do it, how wrongly they would act, you too see.

**4. 33.** What indeed I had written, that I had sent no book against you to Rome, I had written for this reason: because I distinguished the name "book" from that letter—from which I altogether did not know what else you had heard; and because I had sent to Rome not even the letter itself, but to you; and I did not think it was against you, since I knew I had done it in the sincerity of friendship, either to admonish, or to correct you, or to be corrected by you. But, apart from your intimates, I beg you yourself, through the grace by which we were redeemed, not to think that whatever good things of yours—which were granted you by the goodness of the Lord—I set down in my letters, I set down by insidious flattery. But if I have sinned against you in anything, forgive me. Nor draw to yourself, more than I said, that which was recalled by me, perhaps more ineptly than learnedly, about the deed of some poet, I know not whom: since I immediately added that I did not say this so that you might recover the eyes of your heart, which far be it, I say, that you have lost; but so that you might attend to how sound and watchful you have them. For the sake, then, of imitating the palinode alone—if we have written anything which we ought to destroy by a later writing—not for the sake of the blindness of Stesichorus, which I neither attributed to your heart nor feared, I thought that should be touched on; and again and again I ask that you confidently correct me, where you perceive that I have need of this. For although, according to the titles of honors which the usage of the Church has now obtained, the episcopate is greater than the priesthood, yet in many things Augustine is less than Jerome; though correction is not to be shunned or disdained even from any lesser one whatever.

**5. 34.** Concerning your translation, you have now persuaded me with what usefulness you wished to translate the Scriptures from the Hebrew—namely, that you might bring into the open those things which were passed over or corrupted by the Jews. But I beg you to deign to indicate by which Jews: whether by those very ones who translated before the coming of the Lord; and if so, by which, or where, of them; or by those later ones, who can for this reason be thought either to have subtracted certain things from the Greek codices, or to have corrupted them in them, lest by those testimonies they be convicted concerning the Christian faith. But those earlier ones—why they would have wished to do this, I do not find. Next, send us, I beg, your translation of the Septuagint, which I did not know you had published. Your book also, of which you made mention, On the Best Kind of Translating, I desire to read, and still to know how the skill of languages in a translator is to be made equal to the conjectures of those who treat the Scriptures by expounding them—who must, even if they are of a right and single faith, beget various opinions in the obscurity of many passages; although that variety by no means discords from the unity of the same faith; just as even one expositor, according to the same faith, can expound the same passage in one way and another, because its obscurity allows this.

**5. 35.** But for this reason I desire your translation of the Septuagint: both that we may, as much as we can, be free of the great inexperience of Latin translators—whoever they may have been—who dared this; and that those who think I envy your useful labors may at last, sometime, if it can be done, understand that I am unwilling that your translation from the Hebrew be read in the churches for this reason: lest, against the authority of the Septuagint, as though bringing forth something new, we disturb with great scandal the people of Christ, whose ears and hearts have grown accustomed to hear that translation which was also approved by the Apostles. Hence too, as for that shrub in Jonah, if in the Hebrew it is neither ivy nor gourd, but something else, I know not what, which, resting on its own trunk, is raised up to be supported by no props, I would rather that now in all Latin copies "gourd" be read. For I do not think the Septuagint set this down in vain, except that they knew something similar to this also.

**5. 36.** I think I have answered enough—indeed, perhaps more than enough—to your three letters; of which I received two through Cyprian, one through Firmus. Write back what seems good, for instructing us or others. But I will take more diligent care, as much as the Lord helps me, that the letters I write to you reach you before anyone from whom they might be more widely scattered. For I confess that I do not wish this to be done to me with your letters to me, which you most justly expostulate was done with mine to you. Nevertheless, let not only love, but also the liberty of friendship, please us in turn: that you not be silent before me, nor I before you, about what in our letters moves us in turn—with that mind, namely, which does not displease the eyes of God in brotherly love. But if you do not think this can be done between us without a ruinous offense to that very love, let it not be done. For that love which I would wish to have with you is surely greater; but better this lesser than none at all.
