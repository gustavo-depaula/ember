# Letter 101

To the most blessed lord, reverently most dear and sincerely most longed-for brother and fellow bishop Memorius, Augustine sends greetings in the Lord

**1.** I ought not now to have sent any letter to your holy Charity without these books, which you demanded of me by the most forceful right of holy love, so that by this obedience at least I might answer your letters, in which you have seen fit to burden me rather than to honor me. And yet where I sink down because I am burdened, there too I am lifted up because I am loved. For I am not loved, lifted up, and chosen by just anyone, but by that man and priest of the Lord whom I perceive to be so acceptable to God that, when you raise your soul, so good a soul, to the Lord, then, since you hold me within it, you raise me too. I ought, then, now to have sent the books that I had promised to revise; and the reason I have not sent them is that I have not revised them, not because I was unwilling but because I was unable, occupied as I am with many cares, and very pressing ones. But it would have been too ungrateful and hard of me if this holy brother and colleague of ours, Possidius, in whom you will find no small part of myself present, were either not to learn from you, or to learn from you without a letter from me. For through our ministry he has been nourished, not on those letters which the slaves of various lusts call liberal, but on the Lord's bread, as much as could be dispensed to him through our straitened means.

**2.** For what else is to be said to those who, though they are unjust and impious, think themselves liberally educated, except what we read in the truly liberal letters: *If the Son makes you free, then you will be free indeed*[^1]? For it is through him that even what are called the liberal disciplines, by those who are not called into freedom, are known to have whatever of the liberal is in them. For they have nothing that fits freedom except what fits truth; whence that same Son says, *And the truth will make you free*[^2]. Those countless and impious fables, then, with which the songs of empty poets are full, in no way accord with our freedom; nor do the inflated and polished lies of the orators; nor, finally, the garrulous subtleties of the philosophers themselves, who either did not know God at all, or, *when they knew God, did not glorify him as God or give thanks, but became futile in their thinking, and their senseless hearts were darkened; and claiming to be wise, they became fools; and they exchanged the glory of the immortal God for the likeness of an image of corruptible man and of birds and four-footed animals and serpents*[^3]; or who, though not given over to these images, or not too much given over to them, nevertheless *worshiped and served the creature rather than the Creator*[^4]. Far be it that the vanities and lying follies of these men, their windy trifles and proud error, should rightly be named liberal letters, the letters, that is, of unhappy men who have not known the grace of God through Jesus Christ our Lord, by which alone we are freed from the body of this death, not even in those very things which they perceived to be true. History indeed, whose writers profess that they owe trustworthiness above all to their narratives, may perhaps have something worthy of the knowledge of free men, since it relates true things, whether good or evil, of men. Yet in coming to know these things, I do not at all see how those who were not helped by the Holy Spirit, and who were compelled by the very condition of human weakness to gather rumors, could fail to be deceived in very many matters; nevertheless there is in them some nearness to freedom, if they have no will to lie, and do not deceive men except when they are themselves deceived by men through human weakness.

**3.** But because what the numbers are worth in all the movements of things is more easily considered in sounds, and that consideration strives by certain steplike paths, as it were, toward the inmost heights of truth, on which roads wisdom shows herself cheerfully and meets those who love her in all her providence: at the beginning of my leisure, when my mind was free from greater and more necessary cares, I wished by these writings, which you have desired of me, to make a kind of prelude, when I wrote six books on rhythm alone, and was disposing myself, I confess, to write perhaps another six on melody, when I hoped that I would have leisure in the future. But after the burden of ecclesiastical cares was laid upon me, all those delights fled from my hands, so that I can now scarcely find the manuscript itself, since I cannot disregard your wish, indeed not a petition but a command. If I can manage to send this little work, you will indeed regret, not that I obeyed you, but that you demanded it of me so insistently. For the five books in it are most difficult to understand unless there is present someone who can not only distinguish the persons of those disputing, but also so sound out, in delivery, the brief pauses of the syllables that the kinds of measures are expressed by them and strike the sense of the ears, especially because in some places even measured intervals of silence are mingled in, which cannot at all be perceived unless a reciter instructs the hearer.

**4.** The sixth book, however, which I have found revised, and in which lies all the fruit of the rest, I have not put off sending to your Charity: perhaps it will not too much shrink from your seriousness. For the earlier five will scarcely seem worthy of being read and studied by anyone but our son and fellow deacon Julian, since he too now serves alongside us. Of him indeed I do not dare to say that I love him more than you, because I would not be speaking truly; but I do dare to say that I long for him more than for you. It may seem strange how I long more for one whom I love equally; but this comes from a greater hope of seeing him: for I think that if he comes to us at your bidding or sending, he will do what befits a young man, especially since he is not yet held by greater cares, and he will bring you yourself to me more readily. By what numbers David's verses are constituted I have not written, because I do not know. For the translator could not express the numbers from the Hebrew tongue, which I do not know, lest by the necessity of meter he be compelled to depart from the truth of translation more than the sense of the thoughts allowed; yet that they consist of fixed numbers I believe those who know that tongue well. For that holy man loved devout music and kindled us to these studies more than any other author. May you all dwell forever in the help of the Most High, you who dwell of one mind in the house, father and mother, brothers of sons, and all sons of one Father, mindful of us.

[^1]: John 8:36
[^2]: John 8:32
[^3]: Rom 1:21-23
[^4]: Rom 1:25
