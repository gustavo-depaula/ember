# Letter 43

To the most beloved and deservedly to be proclaimed lords and brothers Glorius, Eleusius, the two Felixes, Grammaticus, and all the rest to whom this is welcome, Augustine

**1. 1.** The apostle Paul indeed said: *A heretical man, after one correction, avoid, knowing that such a one is subverted, and sins, and is condemned by his own judgment*[^1]. But those who defend their opinion, however false and perverse, with no obstinate animosity—especially one which they did not beget by the audacity of their own presumption, but received from parents who had been seduced and fallen into error—and who seek the truth with cautious solicitude, ready to be corrected when they have found it, are by no means to be reckoned among heretics. Unless I believed that you were such, I would perhaps send you no letter. Although even the heretic himself, however swollen with hateful pride and mad with the obstinacy of evil contention—just as we are admonished that he is to be avoided, lest he deceive the weak and the little ones—so we do not refuse that he is to be corrected, by whatever means we can. Whence it came about that we wrote even to some of the chief Donatists, not letters of communion, which they have long since, on account of their perversity, not accepted from the catholic unity which is spread throughout the whole world; but such private letters as it is permitted us to use even toward Pagans, which they, even if they sometimes read, yet either would not, or, as is rather to be believed, could not answer. Wherein it seemed to us enough that we had fulfilled the duty of love, which the Holy Spirit teaches us we owe not only to our own but to all, who says to us through the Apostle: *But may the Lord multiply you, and make you abound in love, toward one another and toward all*[^2]. He also admonishes, in another place, that those who think differently are to be corrected in moderation; *Lest perhaps*, he says, *God give them repentance to know the truth, and they come to their senses out of the snares of the devil, captured by him into his own will*[^3].

**1. 2.** I have said this beforehand, lest anyone think that I have sent you writings more impudently than prudently, and that in this way I wished to deal with you about the business of your soul, because you are not of our communion: when nevertheless, if I were writing you something about the business of an estate, or of settling some monetary lawsuit, no one perhaps would reproach me. So dear is this world to men, and they have grown cheap to themselves! Therefore this letter will be to me, for my defense, a witness in the judgment of God, who knows with what mind I have done it, and who said: *Blessed are the peacemakers, for they shall be called the sons of God*[^4].

**2. 3.** Therefore, that you may deign to remember, when we were in your city, and were dealing with you about the communion of Christian unity, certain Records were brought forward by your party, by which it was recited that about seventy bishops condemned Caecilian, formerly bishop of the Church of Carthage, of our communion, with his colleagues and ordainers. There also the case of Felix of Aptungi was bandied about far more invidiously and accusingly than the rest. And when all these had been read through, we answered that it was not to be wondered at if men who then made that schism—not without the drawing up of Records—thought that those against whom they had been stirred up by jealous and abandoned men ought to be rashly condemned, absent, the case unheard. But that we have other ecclesiastical Records, in which Secundus of Tigisi, who then held the primacy in Numidia, left to God to be judged those who were present and confessed traitors, and permitted them to remain in their episcopal sees, as they were, whose names are numbered among the condemners of Caecilian; whereas Secundus himself also held the headship of that same council, where he condemned absent men, as traitors, by their sentences, men whom, when present and confessed, he had pardoned.

**2. 4.** Then we said that, some time after the ordination of Majorinus, whom by a wicked crime they raised up against Caecilian, erecting altar against altar, and dissipating the unity of Christ by furious discords, they had asked of Constantine, then emperor, bishops as judges, who, with a mediating arbiter, should judge concerning their questions which, having arisen in Africa, were tearing apart the bond of peace. And after this was done, with Caecilian present and those who had sailed against him, Melchiades, then bishop of the city of Rome, judging, with his colleagues whom the Emperor had sent at the prayers of the Donatists, nothing could be proved against Caecilian, and thereby, with him confirmed in his episcopate, Donatus, who was then present against him, was disapproved. These things being done, since all those persevered in the obstinacy of a most wicked schism, the said emperor afterward, at Arles, took care that the same cause be more diligently examined and ended. But they appealed from the ecclesiastical judgment, that Constantine should hear the cause. After this was reached, with both parties present, Caecilian was judged innocent, and they withdrew, defeated, and yet remained in the same perversity. Nor did negligence follow in the case of Felix of Aptungi, but at the order of the same prince, by proconsular Records, he too was cleared.

**2. 5.** But because we said all these things only, and did not also read them, we surely seemed to you to be doing less than you expected from our insistence. When we sensed this, we did not delay to send for those things which we had promised to read. All which, while we ran out to the Church of Geliza, intending to return thence to your town, came with an interval of not a full two days, and, as you know, as much as time allowed, were recited to you in one day. First, where Secundus of Tigisi did not dare to remove confessed traitors from his own college, with whom he afterward dared to condemn Caecilian, not confessed and absent, and his other colleagues: then the proconsular Records, where Felix, by the most diligent examination, was proved innocent. These you remember were read to you before noon. But after noon we recited their petitions to Constantine, and the ecclesiastical Records held in the city of Rome, judges having been given by him, by which they were disapproved, but Caecilian was confirmed in episcopal honor. Finally the letters of the emperor Constantine, by which all things shone forth most fully attested.

**3. 6.** What do you want more, men; what do you want more? It is not about your gold and silver that the matter turns; not land, not estates, not finally the health of your body is called into peril: we urge your souls about attaining eternal life and fleeing eternal death. Wake up at last: we are not engaged in any obscure question, we are not searching out hidden secrets, for the penetrating of which no, or rare, human hearts suffice; the matter is in the open. What stands out more clearly? what is discerned more quickly? We say that by a rash council, however most numerous, innocent and absent men were condemned. We prove this by the proconsular Records, by which that man was judged free of all crime of betrayal whom the Records of your council, brought forward by your own people, proclaimed most criminal. We say that by confessed traitors sentences were pronounced against those who were called traitors. We prove this by the ecclesiastical Records, where they are declared by name, among whom Secundus of Tigisi pardoned, as if with a view to peace, the things he knew, and with whom he afterward, the peace torn apart, condemned what he did not know. Whence it appeared that he too, even at first, did not consult peace, but feared for himself. For Purpurius of Limata had objected to him that he himself too, when he had been detained by the curator and council that he might hand over the Scriptures, was released—surely not for nothing, except because he handed over, or ordered something to be handed over. Fearing this sufficiently probable suspicion, having received counsel from the younger Secundus his kinsman, and having consulted the other bishops who were with him, he left the most manifest crimes to God to be judged, and so seemed to have looked to peace; which was false, since he looked to himself.

**3. 7.** For if the thought of peace dwelt in his heart, he would not afterward at Carthage condemn, with the crime of betrayal, those whom no one had convicted before him, absent, while with the traitors whom, present and confessed, he had left to God. For he ought to have feared so much the more lest the peace of unity be violated, the more Carthage was an ample and illustrious city, whence the evil that arose there could pour itself, as from a summit, through the whole body of Africa. It was also near to the regions across the sea, and noble with a most celebrated fame: whence it surely had a bishop of no mediocre authority, who could disregard a conspiring multitude of enemies, when he saw himself joined, by letters of communion, both to the Roman Church, in which the primacy of the apostolic chair has always flourished, and to the other lands whence the Gospel came to Africa itself, where he would be ready to plead his cause, if his adversaries tried to alienate those Churches from him. Because, then, he was unwilling to come to the lodging of colleagues whom he felt or suspected, or, as they themselves assert, pretended, to have been perverted by his enemies against the truth of his cause, so much the more ought Secundus, if he had wished to be the guardian of true peace, to have guarded against the absent being condemned, who were altogether unwilling to be present at their judgment. For it was not about presbyters or deacons or clergy of lower order, but about colleagues, who could keep their cause intact for the judgment of other colleagues, especially of the apostolic Churches; where sentences pronounced against the absent would in no way avail anything, since they did not desert their judgment after first approaching it, but, always holding it suspect, never wished to approach it.

**3. 8.** This matter ought to have most greatly troubled Secundus, who was then primate, if he governed the council for this purpose, that he might consult peace; for he could perhaps easily have repressed, calmed, or bridled the rabid mouths against the absent, if he said: You see, brothers, after so great a slaughter of persecution, that by the mercy of God peace has been granted by the princes of the world; we Christians and bishops ought not to break the Christian unity which now no pagan enemy assails. Therefore either let us leave all these cases, which the disaster of a most turbulent time inflicted on the Church, to God the judge: or, if there are any among you who know the certain crimes of these men so well that they could easily teach them and convict those who deny them, and who dread to communicate with such men, let them go to our brothers and colleagues, the bishops of the Churches across the sea, and there first complain of these men's deeds and contumacy, that, being conscious of evil in themselves, they were unwilling to come to the judgment of their African colleagues, so that it may be announced to them from there that they are to come, and there answer the charges. But if they do not do this, there too their depravity and perversity will become known, and, a circular letter being sent concerning their name throughout the whole world of lands, wherever the Church of Christ is now spread, their communion will be cut off by all the Churches, lest any error arise in the Chair of the Church of Carthage. Then at last, secure, we will ordain another bishop for the people of Carthage, when these men have been separated from the whole Church: lest perhaps, when another has just been ordained, he be not communicated with by the Church across the sea, because this one will not seem to have been deposed from honor, whom, already ordained, fame has celebrated, and to whom it has caused letters of communion to come; and so a great scandal of schism arise in the unity of Christ, in times now at peace, while we wish to rush our sentences too hastily, and dare to erect another altar, not against Caecilian, but against the world of lands, which communicates with him through ignorance.

**3. 9.** If anyone unbridled had been unwilling to obey this so sound and right counsel, what would he have done? or how would he condemn any of the absent colleagues, when he did not have the Acts of the council in his power, the primate contradicting? But if so great a sedition arose even against the first see, that some now wished to condemn those whom he wished to be deferred, how much better to dissent from such men, plotting unquiet and unpeaceful things, than from the communion of the whole world? But because there was nothing in Caecilian and his ordainers which could be proved by a judgment across the sea, therefore neither, before they pronounced sentences against him, did they wish to refer it, nor, after they pronounced them, did they perseveringly act to have it brought to the notice of the Churches across the sea, the communion of which traitors, condemned in Africa, they ought to avoid. For if they had attempted to do this, Caecilian and the rest would have been present to themselves, and would have cleared their cause against the deceitful accusers, before the ecclesiastical judges across the sea, by a most diligent discussion.

**3. 10.** And so that council, perverse and wicked, was, as it is believed, chiefly of traitors, whom, confessed, Secundus of Tigisi had pardoned: so that, since the report about betrayal had grown frequent, by defaming others they might turn suspicion from themselves, and, while men throughout all Africa, believing the bishops, spoke false things about innocent men—that traitors had been condemned at Carthage—they themselves, who had truly betrayed, might lie hidden, as it were, in the fog of a most lying rumor. Whence you see, dearest ones, that it could have come about—which some of you said was not verisimilar—that those who had confessed their own betrayal, and had obtained that their cause ought to be left to God, the same afterward sat as judges and condemners, as it were, of absent traitors. For they rather embraced the occasion by which they could spread a false accusation upon others, and in this way turn aside the tongues of men, turned against them, from the inquiry into their own crimes. Otherwise, if it could not come about that anyone judges in another the evil which he himself commits, the apostle Paul would not say to certain men: *Therefore you are inexcusable, O man, everyone who judges. For wherein you judge another, you condemn yourself; for you do the same things which you judge*[^5]. Which they did altogether, so that these apostolic words apply to them wholly and aptly.

**3. 11.** Not then did Secundus, when he left their crimes to God, consult peace and unity: otherwise he would rather have provided for this at Carthage, that no schism be made, where no one was present to whom, confessed, he would be compelled to forgive a crime; but, what was easiest, the whole preservation of peace would be to be unwilling to condemn the absent. And so they would do injury to the innocent, even if they had been willing to pardon them, not convicted, nor confessed, nor at all present. For he receives pardon whose fault is most certain. How much more monstrous and blind, then, were they who thought they could condemn things which, unknown, they could not even have pardoned? But there things known were left to God, lest others be inquired into: here things unknown were condemned, that those might be covered. But someone will say: They knew them. Which even if I grant, even so the absent surely ought to have been spared. For neither had they deserted a judgment where they had never at all stood; nor was the Church in those African bishops alone, so that those who had been unwilling to present themselves to their judgment would seem to have avoided every ecclesiastical judgment. For thousands of colleagues across the sea remained, where it appeared that those could be judged who seemed to hold their African or Numidian colleagues suspect. For where is that which the Scripture cries: *Before you ask, do not blame anyone; and when you have asked, rebuke justly*[^6]? If, then, the Holy Spirit wished no one to be blamed or rebuked except when asked; how much more wickedly were those condemned outright—not blamed or rebuked—who, absent, could be asked nothing about their crimes?

**3. 12.** But yet these men—who say that, although of absent men, and men by no means deserting a judgment, since they were never present, and always declared that wedge to be suspect to them, yet condemned crimes that were known—I ask you, my brothers, how did they know them? You answer: We do not know, since the very knowledge is not set forth in those Records. But I will show you how they knew. Attend to the case of Felix of Aptungi, and first read how much more vehement they were against him. So, then, they knew the cause of the rest as they knew that of this man, who was afterward proved most innocent by a diligent and terrible inquiry. How much more justly, then, and more safely and quickly, ought we to esteem those innocent whose crimes were more lightly accused by these men, and condemned with a more sparing reproof, when that man was found innocent against whom they raged much more monstrously!

**4. 13.** Or perhaps, as a certain man said—which, when it was said to you, displeased, but yet is not to be passed over; for a certain man said: A bishop ought not to have been cleared by a proconsular judgment: as though indeed he himself procured this for himself, and not the emperor ordered it to be so inquired into, to whose care, of which he would render an account to God, that matter most especially pertained. For they had made him the arbiter and judge of the cause of betrayal and schism, who had even sent prayers to him, to whom they afterward appealed; and yet they were unwilling to acquiesce in his judgment. And so, if he is to be blamed whom an earthly judge absolved, when he himself had not asked this for himself; how much more are they to be blamed who wished an earthly king to be the judge of their cause? But if it is no crime to appeal to the emperor, it is no crime to be heard by the emperor: therefore neither by him to whom the emperor has delegated the cause. That friend wished even to accuse a certain man, hung on the rack in the case of bishop Felix, so that someone might even be tortured with the claws. Could Felix contradict, lest the matter be inquired into with so great diligence or severity, when the examiner was conducting the finding of his cause? For what else was it to be unwilling to be so inquired into than to confess the crime? And yet that proconsul himself, amid the terrible voices of the heralds and the bloody hands of the torturers, would never condemn an absent colleague who had been unwilling to present himself to his judgment, when he had another means by which he could be heard. Or if he should condemn, surely even by the secular laws themselves he would pay just and due penalties.

**5. 14.** But if the proconsular Records displease, yield to the ecclesiastical ones. All were recited to you in order. Or perhaps Melchiades, bishop of the Roman Church, with his colleagues the bishops across the sea, ought not to have usurped to himself that judgment which had been ended by the seventy Africans, where the primate of Tigisi presided? What of the fact that he himself did not usurp it? For, being asked, he sent to the emperor bishops as judges who should sit with him, and decide concerning that whole cause what seemed just. This we prove both by the prayers of the Donatists, and by the words of the emperor himself: for you remember that both were read to you, and you now have license to inspect and copy them. Read and consider all. See with how great care for preserving or restoring peace and unity all things were discussed; how the person of the accusers was treated, and with what stains some of them were disapproved, and how, by the voices of those present, it clearly was established that they had nothing to say against Caecilian, but wished to transfer the whole cause to the people of the party of Majorinus—that is, to a seditious multitude estranged from the peace of the Church—so that by that crowd, of course, Caecilian might be accused, which they judged could, by tumultuous cries alone, with no attestation of documents, with no examination of truth, twist the minds of the judges to their will; unless perhaps a furious multitude, drunk with the cup of error and corruption, would speak true crimes against Caecilian, where seventy bishops, as was established about Felix of Aptungi, condemned absent and innocent colleagues with so insane a rashness. For to such a crowd they had consented, that they pronounced sentences against innocent men not interrogated; by such a crowd they wished Caecilian to be accused again. But plainly they had found such judges, to whom they might persuade that madness.

**5. 15.** For you can, by your prudence, both attend there to their perversity, and to the gravity of the judges, how in the end they could not be persuaded that Caecilian should be accused by the people of the party of Majorinus, which had no certain person; and the accusers or witnesses or those in any way necessary to the cause were demanded of them, who had come with them from Africa, and who, it was said, had been present and had been withdrawn by Donatus. The same Donatus promised that he would produce them: which, when he had promised not once but often, he was unwilling to approach that judgment further, where he had now confessed so much that, by not approaching, he seemed to wish nothing else than not to be condemned when present; although the things which were to be condemned had been made manifest with him present and interrogated. Another thing too occurred, that by certain men a libel of denunciation against Caecilian was given: after which deed, how the inquiry was again stirred, and what persons gave that libel, and how nothing could be proved against Caecilian, why should I say, when you have both heard all things, and can read them as often as you wish?

**5. 16.** But about the number of seventy bishops, when it was set forth as if the weightiest authority, you remember what was said; and yet the most weighty men preferred to temper their judgment, away from infinite questions binding themselves to one another by a certain inexplicable chain, by no means caring how many those bishops were, or whence collected, whom they saw blinded by so great rashness that, against absent and uninterrogated colleagues, they dared to pronounce sentences so headlong. And yet how innocent, how upright, how provident and peaceful is the final sentence pronounced of the blessed Melchiades himself, by which he neither dared to remove from his college colleagues in whom nothing had been established, and, Donatus alone, whom he had found the chief of all the evil, being especially blamed, made free for the rest the option of recovering soundness, ready to send letters of communion even to those whom it was established had been ordained by Majorinus: so that in whatever places there were two bishops, whom dissension had doubled, he wished him to be confirmed who had been ordained first, but for the other of them another people would be provided to be governed. O most excellent man! O son of Christian peace, and father of the Christian people! Compare now this fewness with that multitude of bishops, and compare not number with number, but weight with weight: here modesty, there rashness, here vigilance, there blindness. Here neither did gentleness corrupt integrity, nor integrity fight against gentleness; but there both fear was covered by fury, and fury was incited by fear. For these had gathered to reject false things by the knowledge of true crimes; those, to cover true things by the condemnation of false ones.

**6. 17.** Would Caecilian at last commit himself to be heard and judged by those men, when he had such men before whom, if a cause were moved against him, he could most easily show his innocence? He would in no way commit himself to them—not even if, as a stranger, he had suddenly been ordained bishop of the Church of Carthage, and did not know what a certain most wealthy woman, Lucilla, could then do toward corrupting the minds of the wicked or the unskilled, whom he, when he was a deacon, had offended by rebuking her for ecclesiastical discipline: for this evil too had been added to the perfecting of that iniquity. For in that council, where absent and innocent men were condemned by confessed traitors, there were a certain few who desired to cover their own crimes by defaming others, that men might be turned away from the inquiry into the truth, called off by false rumors. Few, then, there were who most especially busied themselves with this affair, although among them there was greater authority, on account of the fellowship of Secundus himself, who, terrified, had spared them. But the rest are reported to have been bought and instigated against Caecilian chiefly by the money of Lucilla. There are extant Records before Zenophilus the consular, where a certain Nundinarius, a deacon degraded by Silvanus, bishop of Cirta—as far as is understood from the Records themselves—when he had tried in vain to satisfy them through the letters of other bishops, disclosed many things in anger, and brought them before a public court: among which it is read recalled this too, that, the bishops having been corrupted by the money of Lucilla, in the Church of Carthage, in the head of Africa, altar was raised against altar. I know that we did not read these Records to you, but you remember that there was not time. There was also some grief of mind coming from the swelling of pride, that they themselves had not ordained the bishop at Carthage.

**6. 18.** By all these things, since Caecilian recognized that they had come together not as true judges, but as enemies and corrupted men; how could it come about that either he himself would wish, or the people over whom he presided would permit, that, leaving the church, he should go into a private house, not to be examined by the scrutiny of colleagues, but to be slaughtered by the wedge of a faction and by womanly hatreds? especially when he saw that, before the Church across the sea, foreign to private enmities and to dissension on either side, an uncorrupted and intact examination of his cause remained for him. Where, if his adversaries should wish to do nothing, they themselves would cut themselves off from the most innocent communion of the world of lands. But if they should there attempt to accuse him, then he would be present to himself, then he would defend his innocence against their machinations, as you have learned was afterward done: when nevertheless they, too late, sought a judgment across the sea, being now guilty of schism, now stained with the horrendous crime of an altar raised. For they would do it first if they relied on the truth; but, false rumors being strengthened by length of time, they wished to come to judgment as if with old fame prejudging: or, what is rather to be believed, Caecilian having first been condemned, as it pleased them, they seemed secure to themselves, overconfident in their number, and not daring to move so evil a cause elsewhere, where, no corruption working, the truth could be found.

**7. 19.** But after they had experienced by the very facts that the communion of the world of lands remained with Caecilian, and that letters of communion were sent to him from the Churches across the sea, not to him whom they themselves had wickedly ordained, they were ashamed always to be silent, because it could be objected to them, why they suffered the Church, ignorant through so many nations, to communicate with the condemned, and why they themselves cut themselves off from the communion of the innocent world of lands, when by being silent they allowed the bishop whom they had ordained for the Carthaginians not to be communicated with by the whole world. They chose, as it is said, to plead their cause with Caecilian before the Churches across the sea on a double track, ready for either outcome: so that, if they could by any cunning of false accusation prevail against him, they would most fully satisfy their desire; but if they could not, they would indeed endure in the same perversity, but yet would now, as if they had something to say, have suffered evil judges; which is the voice of all evil litigants, when they have been overcome even by the most manifest truth: as though it could not be said to them, and most justly said: Behold, let us suppose those bishops who judged at Rome were not good judges; there yet remained a plenary council of the universal Church, where the cause could be agitated even with those judges themselves, so that, if they were convicted of having judged ill, their sentences might be loosed. Whether they did this, let them prove: for we easily prove it was not done, from the fact that the whole world does not communicate with them: or if it was done, there too they were defeated; which their very separation makes manifest.

**7. 20.** But yet what they did afterward is most sufficiently shown by the emperor's letters. For they dared to accuse the ecclesiastical judges, bishops of so great authority, by whose judgment both the innocence of Caecilian and their own wickedness was declared, not before other colleagues, but before the emperor, that they judged ill. He gave another judgment at Arles, of other bishops, namely: not because it was now necessary, but yielding to their perversities, and desiring in every way to check so great an impudence. For a Christian emperor did not dare so to take up their tumultuous and deceitful complaints as himself to judge concerning the judgment of the bishops who had sat at Rome; but he gave, as I said, other bishops: from whom, however, they preferred to appeal again to the emperor himself; for you have heard how he detests them in the matter. And would that at least by his judgment they had put an end to their most insane animosities, and, as he himself yielded to them, to judge concerning that cause after the bishops—being about afterward to ask pardon of the holy prelates, provided that they would have nothing further to say, if they did not obey the sentence of him to whom they themselves appealed—so they too would at some time yield to the truth. For he ordered that the parties should come to Rome to plead the cause. When Caecilian, for some reason I do not know, had not come there, being importuned by them, he ordered them to follow to Milan. Then some of them began to withdraw, perhaps indignant because Constantine did not imitate them, so as immediately and swiftly to condemn the absent Caecilian. When the provident emperor learned this, he had the rest, guarded by officers, brought to Milan. When Caecilian too had come there, he too, as he wrote, produced him, and, the cause being known—with what diligence, with what caution, with what foresight, as his letters indicate—he judged Caecilian most innocent, those men most wicked.

**8. 21.** And still they baptize outside the church, and, if they can, they rebaptize the Church; they sacrifice in dissension and schism, and greet the peoples in the name of peace, whom they banish from the peace of salvation. The unity of Christ is rent, the inheritance of Christ is blasphemed, the baptism of Christ is blown upon with contempt; and they are unwilling that these things in themselves be corrected by ordinary human powers with temporal scourges, lest they be destined to eternal punishments for so great sacrileges. We object to them the fury of schism, the madness of rebaptizing, the wicked separation from the inheritance of Christ which is spread through all nations. From the codices not only ours but even theirs, we recite the churches whose names they read today, and with whom today they do not communicate: which, when they are recited in their little assemblies, they say to their readers: Peace be with you; and with those very peoples to whom those letters were written, they do not have peace. And they themselves object to us either false crimes of the dead, or, even if true, yet another's; not understanding that in those things which we object to them, all of them are held; but in those things which they object to us, the chaff or the tares of the Lord's harvest are reproved, but the crime does not pertain to the grain: nor considering that those to whom the evil are pleasing in unity, themselves communicate with the evil; but those to whom they are displeasing, and who cannot correct them, nor dare before the time of harvest to root out the tares, *lest they root up the wheat too*[^7], communicate not with their deeds but with the altar of Christ: so that they are not only not stained by them, but even deserve to be praised and proclaimed by the divine words; since, lest the name of Christ be blasphemed through horrible schisms, for the good of unity they tolerate what for the good of equity they hate.

**8. 22.** *If they have ears, let them hear what the Spirit says to the churches*[^8]. For thus it is read in the Apocalypse of John: *To the angel*, it says, *of the Church of Ephesus write: These things says he who holds the seven stars in his right hand, who walks in the midst of the seven golden lampstands: I know your works and your labor and your patience, and that you cannot bear evil men; and you have tested those who say they are apostles, and are not, and you have found them liars: and you have patience, and you have borne them for my name's sake, and have not failed*[^9]. But if he wished it to be understood of an angel of the higher heavens, and not of the rulers of the Church, he would not consequently say: *But I have against you that you have left your first love. Be mindful therefore whence you have fallen, and do penance, and do the first works; but if not, I come to you and will move your lampstand out of its place, unless you do penance*[^10]. This cannot be said to the higher angels, who retain perpetual love, whence those who failed and fell are the devil and his angels[^11]. Therefore he says "first love," because he bore the false apostles for the name of Christ, which he bids him resume, and do his former works. And the crimes of evil men are objected to us, not ours, but another's; and those partly unknown, which, even if we saw them true and present, and, sparing the tares for the sake of the grain, tolerated them for unity, whoever heard the holy Scriptures not deaf in heart would say us worthy not only of no reproach, but even of no small praise.

**8. 23.** Aaron tolerates the multitude demanding and fashioning and adoring the idol[^12]. Moses tolerates so many thousands murmuring against God, and so often offending his holy name[^13]. David tolerates Saul his persecutor, deserting heavenly things by his wicked ways, seeking the things below by magic arts; he avenges him when slain, and calls him even the anointed of the Lord on account of the sacrament of the venerable anointing[^14]. Samuel tolerates the wicked sons of Eli, and his own perverse sons, whom, because the people would not tolerate, it was accused by divine truth and corrected by divine severity[^15]. He tolerates, finally, the proud people themselves, the despisers of God. Isaiah tolerates those against whom he hurls so many true accusations. Jeremiah tolerates those by whom he suffers so much. Zechariah tolerates the Pharisees and Scribes, such as the Scripture testifies they were at that time. I know that I have passed over many: let those who wish, let those who can, read the heavenly oracles, and they will find that all the holy servants and friends of God always had those whom they tolerated in their own people; with whom, nevertheless, communicating in the sacraments of that time, they were not only not defiled, but even praiseworthily endured them, *endeavoring*, as the Apostle says, *to keep the unity of the spirit in the bond of peace*[^16]. Let them attend also to the time after the Lord's coming, where we would find many more examples of this toleration throughout the whole world, if all could have been written and reduced to authority; yet attend to these very ones which we have. The Lord himself tolerates Judas, a devil, a thief, and his seller: he allows him to receive among the innocent disciples what the faithful know to be our price. The Apostles tolerate false apostles; and, among those seeking their own things, not the things of Jesus Christ, Paul, not seeking his own things but those of Jesus Christ[^17], conducts himself with most glorious toleration. Finally, what I recalled a little before, by a divine voice the ruler of the Church, under the name of an angel, is praised, because, although he hated the evil, he yet, when they had been tested and found out, tolerated them for the Lord's name.

**8. 24.** To sum up, let them question themselves: Are not the slaughters and burnings of the Circumcellions tolerated by them—worshippers of the corpses of men hurled down of their own accord—and, under the incredible evil of one Optatus, the groans of all Africa for so many years? I now spare to speak of the tyrannical powers and public brigandages of the several regions and cities and estates throughout Africa. For you yourselves say these things to yourselves better, whether in the ear or openly, as it pleases. For wherever you turn your eyes, what I say will meet you, or rather what I am silent about. Nor do we on this account accuse those whom you love there. For they do not displease us because they tolerate the evil; but because they are intolerably evil on account of the schism, on account of altar against altar, on account of the separation from the inheritance of Christ spread through the whole world, as it was so long ago promised. We bewail and mourn peace violated, unity torn, baptisms repeated, sacraments blown upon with contempt, which are holy even in wicked men. Which, if they hold of small account, let them behold the examples by which it is shown how greatly God held these things. Those who made the idol were killed by the customary death of the sword[^18]: but those who wished to make a schism, the princes were devoured by the gaping of the earth, and the consenting crowd was consumed by fire[^19]. By the diversity of the punishments, the diversity of the deserts is recognized.

**9. 25.** The holy Codices are handed over in persecution, the traitors confess, and are left to God. The innocent are not interrogated, and are condemned by rash men. He is proved upright by certain judgments who, among the absent condemned, was accused much more vehemently than the rest. The judgment of the bishops is appealed to the emperor: the emperor is chosen judge; the emperor, judging, is despised. What was done then you have read, what is being done now you see: if you doubt about those things in anything, behold now these. Surely let us not deal with old papers, not with public archives, not with forensic or ecclesiastical Records. Our greater book is the world of lands; in it I read fulfilled what in the Book of God I read promised: *The Lord*, it says, *said to me: You are my Son, today I have begotten you: ask of me, and I will give you the nations as your inheritance, and the ends of the earth as your possession*[^20]. Whoever does not communicate with this inheritance, let him hold whatever books he will, let him know that he is disinherited. Whoever assails this inheritance, sufficiently indicates that he is a stranger to the family of God. Surely the question turns on the betrayal of the divine Books, where this inheritance is promised. Let him, then, be believed to have handed over the testament to the flames, who litigates against the will of the testator. What did the Church of the Corinthians do to you, O party of Donatus, what did it do to you? But what I say about it, I wish to be understood of all such and so far-placed Churches. What did those do to you, who could not know at all either what you have done, or whom you have defamed? or, because Caecilian offended Lucilla in Africa, has the world lost the light of Christ?

**9. 26.** Let them at last perceive what they have done: deservedly, by a certain interval of years, their own deed has been rolled back before their eyes. Inquire through what woman Maximian, who is said to be a kinsman of Donatus, cut himself off from the communion of Primian, and how, a faction of bishops having been gathered, he condemned Primian absent, and a bishop was ordained against him; just as Majorinus, a faction of bishops having been gathered through Lucilla, condemned Caecilian absent, and a bishop was ordained against him. Or do you perhaps wish it to be valid that Primian was cleared by the other African bishops of his communion against the faction of Maximian; and not wish it to be valid that Caecilian was cleared by the bishops of unity across the sea against the faction of Majorinus? I ask, my brothers, what great thing do I seek? what difficult thing do I desire to be understood by you? There is indeed much difference, and incomparably distant whether in authority or in number, is the African Church, if it be compared with the other parts of the world: and it is far less—even if there were unity here, it is far, far less, compared with all the other Christian nations, than the party of Maximian compared with the party of Primian; yet I ask, and judge it just, that the council of Secundus of Tigisi, which Lucilla blew up against the absent Caecilian and the apostolic sees and the whole world communicating with Caecilian, be of as much validity as the council of the Maximianists, which similarly some woman I know not blew up against the absent Primian, and the rest of the multitude through Africa communicating with Primian. What is discerned more openly? what is demanded more fairly?

**9. 27.** You see all these things, and know them, and groan; and yet God too sees that no thing compels you to remain in so pestilent and sacrilegious a cutting-off, if for the sake of attaining the spiritual kingdom you overcome carnal affection, and do not fear to offend, for the sake of avoiding everlasting punishments, the friendships of men, which will profit nothing in the judgment of God. Behold, go, take counsel; learn what they can answer against these things of ours: if they bring forward papers, we bring forward papers; if they say ours are false, let them not be indignant that we say this of theirs. No one deletes from heaven the ordinance of God, no one deletes from the earth the Church of God: he promised the whole world, she has filled the whole world; and she has both evil and good, but she neither loses on earth any but the evil, nor admits into heaven any but the good. But this discourse will be for you—he himself knows from the gift of God with how great love both of peace and of you we have brought it forth—a correction if you will, but a witness even if you will not.
