# Letter 228

TO HONORATUS, A HOLY BROTHER AND FELLOW BISHOP, AUGUSTINE SENDS GREETINGS IN THE LORD.

**1.** Having sent your Charity a copy of the letter I wrote to brother Quodvultdeus, our fellow bishop, I thought I had been freed of this burden which you laid upon me, by asking counsel as to what you ought to do in these perils which our times have brought upon us. For although I wrote that letter briefly, I think I nonetheless omitted nothing that would suffice both for the one answering to say and for the one asking to hear: since I said both that those are not to be forbidden who desire to migrate, if they can, to fortified places; and that the bonds of our ministry, by which the love of Christ has bound us, are not to be broken, that we may not desert the churches we ought to serve. For these are the words I set down in that letter: "It remains, then," I said, "that we, whose ministry is so necessary to however small a people of God remaining where we are, that it is not right for it to remain without this ministry, should say to the Lord, *Be to us a protecting God, and a fortified place*[^1]."

**2.** But this counsel does not suffice for you, as you write, lest we strive to act against the precept or example of the Lord, where he warns that one must flee from city to city. For we recall the words of him who says: *But when they persecute you in this city, flee to another*[^2]; yet who would believe that the Lord willed this to be done in such a way that the flocks, which he purchased with his own blood, should be deserted by the ministry necessary to them, without which they cannot live? Did he himself do this, when, his parents carrying him, he fled as a little child into Egypt[^3]—he who had not yet gathered the churches that we might say were deserted by him? When the apostle Paul, lest the enemy seize him, was let down through a window in a basket and escaped his hands[^4], was the church that was there deserted by the necessary ministry, and was what was needful not fulfilled by other brothers established in the same place? For the Apostle had done this at their wish, that he might preserve himself for the Church, since the persecutor was seeking him in particular. Let the servants of Christ, then, the ministers of his word and Sacrament, do what he commanded or permitted. Let them flee altogether from city to city, when any one of them is specially sought by the persecutors, so that the Church may not be deserted by the others who are not so sought, but that these may furnish food to their fellow servants, whom they know cannot live otherwise. But when the peril is common to all, that is, to bishops, clergy, and laity, let those who need others not be deserted by those whom they need. Either, therefore, let all cross over to fortified places; or let those who have a necessity of remaining not be left by those through whom their ecclesiastical need is to be supplied, so that they may either live together, or together suffer what the householder will have them endure.

**3.** But if it should happen that, whether some suffer less and others more, or all suffer equally, it becomes clear which of them are those who suffer for others; namely those who, when they could have rescued themselves from such evils by fleeing, preferred to remain, lest they desert the need of others. By this most of all is proved that love which the apostle John commends, saying: *As Christ laid down his life for us, so we too ought to lay down our lives for our brothers*[^5]. For those who flee, or who, bound by their own necessities, cannot flee, if, caught, they suffer, surely suffer for themselves, not for their brothers: but those who suffer for this reason, that they were unwilling to desert the brothers who needed them for Christian salvation, without doubt lay down their lives for their brothers.

**4.** Hence that which we have heard a certain bishop said: "If the Lord commanded us to flee in those persecutions where there can be the fruit of martyrdom, how much more ought we to flee barren sufferings, when there is a barbarian and hostile incursion?"—this is indeed true and acceptable, but for those whom the bonds of ecclesiastical office do not hold. For he who, for this reason, does not flee a hostile attack, though he could escape, lest he desert the ministry of Christ, without which men can neither be made nor live as Christians, finds a greater fruit of love than he who, fleeing not for the sake of his brothers but for his own sake, and, when caught, does not deny Christ, and undergoes martyrdom.

**5.** What, then, is it that you set down in your earlier letter? For you say: "If we must persist in the churches, I do not see what we shall profit ourselves or the people, except that before our eyes men fall, women are ravished, churches are burned, and we ourselves fail under torments, when there is demanded of us what we do not have." God is indeed able to hear the prayers of his household, and to avert these things that are dreaded; nor, nevertheless, on account of these things, which are uncertain, ought there to be a certain desertion of our office, without which there is certain ruin for the people—not in the matters of this life, but of another, incomparably more diligently and anxiously to be cared for. For if these evils that are feared lest they perhaps befall the places where we are were certain, all those for whose sake we must remain there would flee from there first, and would render us free from the necessity of remaining: for there is no one who would say that ministers ought to remain where there are no longer those who need to be ministered to. So certain holy bishops fled from Spain, when the peoples had first partly slipped away in flight, partly perished, partly been consumed by siege, partly been scattered into captivity: but far more remained, those for whom they would remain remaining there, under the density of those same perils. And if some deserted their peoples, this is what we say ought not to be done: for such men were not taught by divine authority, but deceived by human error, or overcome by fear.

**6.** For why do they think the precept is to be obeyed indifferently, where they read that one must flee from city to city, and do not shudder at the hireling, who *sees the wolf coming and flees, because he has no care for the sheep*[^6]? Why do they not strive so to understand these two dominical and true sentences—the one, namely, where flight is permitted or commanded, the other where it is reproved and blamed—that they may be found not to be contrary to one another, as they are not? And how is this found, except by attending to what I have already argued above: that Christ's ministers must then flee, when persecution presses, from the places where they are, when either there is no people of Christ there to be ministered to, or there is, and the necessary ministry can be fulfilled through others who do not have the same cause for fleeing—just as the Apostle, let down in a basket, as I mentioned above, fled, when he himself in particular was sought by the persecutor, others surely not having a like necessity, by whom—far be it—the ministry to the Church there would be deserted; just as holy Athanasius, bishop of Alexandria, fled, when the emperor Constantius desired specially to seize him, the Catholic people who dwelt in Alexandria being in no way deserted by the rest of the ministers? But when the people remain and the ministers flee and the ministry is withdrawn, what will it be but that damnable flight of hirelings, who have no care for the sheep? For the wolf will come; not a man, but the devil, who has very often persuaded the faithful to be apostates, those to whom the daily ministry of the Lord's body was lacking; *and the weak brother, for whom Christ died, will perish—not through your knowledge, but through your ignorance*[^7].

**7.** But as for those who in this matter are not deceived by error, but are overcome by dread, why do they not rather fight bravely against their fear, by the Lord's mercy and help, lest evils incomparably more grievous, which are far more to be dreaded, befall? This comes about where the love of God burns, not where the desire of the world smolders. For love says: *Who is weak, and I am not weak? Who is scandalized, and I do not burn?*[^8] But love is from God. Let us pray, then, that it may be given by him by whom it is commanded; and through it let us fear more lest the sheep of Christ be slaughtered by the sword of spiritual wickedness in the heart, than by the steel in the body, where, whenever and by whatever kind of death, they are to die. Let us fear more lest, the inner sense being corrupted, the chastity of faith perish, than lest women be violently ravished in the flesh: because chastity is not violated by violence, if it is preserved in the mind; since not even in the flesh is it violated, when the will of the one suffering does not basely use her own flesh, but without consent endures what another does. Let us fear more lest the living stones be extinguished while we desert them, than lest the stones and wood of earthly buildings be burned while we are present. Let us fear more lest the members of the body of Christ, deprived of spiritual nourishment, be killed, than lest the members of our own body, overcome by hostile assault, be tortured. Not because these things are not to be avoided when they can be; but because they are rather to be borne, when they cannot be avoided without impiety: unless perhaps someone should contend that he is not an impious minister who then withdraws the ministry necessary to piety, when it is most necessary.

**8.** Or do we not consider, when one comes to the extreme of these perils, and there is no power of fleeing, how great a concourse is wont to be made in the church of both sexes and of every age; some demanding baptism, others reconciliation, others even the very performance of penance, all consolation and the confecting and dispensing of the sacraments? And if the ministers are lacking there, how great a ruin follows those who go forth from this world either not regenerated or bound! how great too the grief of their faithful, who will not have them with them in the rest of eternal life! how great, finally, the groaning of all, and how great the blasphemy of some, over the absence of the ministries and the ministers! See what the fear of temporal evils does, and how great is the acquisition of eternal evils in it. But if the ministers are present, by the strength which the Lord supplies them, all are helped: some are baptized, others reconciled, none are defrauded of the communion of the Lord's body, all are consoled, edified, exhorted to ask God, who is able to avert all the things that are feared; prepared for either outcome, so that, if this cup cannot pass from them, the will be done of him who can will nothing evil.

**9.** Surely you now see what you had written that you did not see—how much good Christian peoples obtain, if in present evils the presence of the ministers of Christ is not lacking to them: whose absence too you see how much it harms, while *they seek their own things, not the things that are of Jesus Christ*[^9]; nor do they have that of which it is said: *It does not seek its own things*[^10]; nor do they imitate him who said: *Not seeking what is useful to me, but what is useful to many, that they may be saved*[^11]. He too would not have fled the snares of that persecuting prince, had he not wished to preserve himself for others to whom he was necessary[^12]: on account of which he says: *But I am pressed between the two: having a desire to be dissolved and to be with Christ, which is by far the best; but to remain in the flesh is necessary on your account*[^13].

**10.** Here perhaps someone may say that the ministers of God ought for this reason to flee when such evils threaten, that they may preserve themselves for the advantage of the Church in more tranquil times. This is rightly done by certain ones, when there is no lack of others through whom the ecclesiastical ministry may be supplied, so that it not be deserted by all; which we said above that Athanasius did: for how necessary it was to the Church, and how much it profited, that that man remained in the flesh, the Catholic faith knows, which was defended against the Arian heretics by his mouth and his love. But when the peril is common, and it is rather to be feared lest anyone be believed to do this not from a will to take counsel but from a fear of dying, and lest he do more harm by the example of fleeing than good by the duty of living, it must by no means be done. Finally, holy David, lest he commit himself to the perils of battles, and perhaps *the lamp of Israel be extinguished*[^14], as it is there said, took this course at the asking of his own men, and did not himself presume it; otherwise he would have made many imitators of cowardice, who would believe he had done this not from consideration of the advantage of others, but from the disturbance of his own dread.

**11.** But another question occurs that we ought not to despise. For if this advantage is not to be neglected, that some ministers should flee when some devastation threatens, so that those may be preserved who will minister to those whom, after that disaster, they can find remaining; what shall be done where all seem about to perish, unless some flee? What if, indeed, that destruction rages so far that it persecutes only the ministers of the Church? What shall we say? Is the Church to be left by ministers fleeing, lest it be more miserably left by those dying? But if the laity are not sought for death, they can in some way conceal their bishops and clergy, just as he will help in whose power are all things, who can also preserve, by his wonderful power, even one not fleeing. But for this reason we ask what we ought to do, lest, by awaiting divine miracles in all things, we be judged to tempt the Lord. This storm, indeed, is not such that the peril of the laity and the clergy is common, as in one ship the peril of merchants and sailors is common. But far be it that this ship of ours should be reckoned of such value that the sailors, and especially the helmsman, ought to desert it in peril, even if by leaping into the skiff or even by swimming they could escape. For those whom we fear may perish by our desertion, we fear may perish not by temporal death, which is to come someday, but by eternal death, which can, if it is not guarded against, come, and can, if it is guarded against, also not come. But in the common peril of this life, why do we suppose that, wherever there is a hostile incursion, all the clergy and not also all the laity will die, so that they may end at the same time this life for which the clergy are necessary? Or why do we not hope that, as some laity, so also some clergy, will remain, by whom the necessary ministry may be furnished to them?

**12.** And yet—oh, if among the ministers of God there should be a dispute about it, which of them should remain, lest the Church be deserted by the flight of all, and which of them should flee, lest it be deserted by the death of all! For such a contest will be among them, where both burn with love, and both please love. If this dispute cannot be ended otherwise, then, as it seems to me, those who are to remain and those who are to flee must be chosen by lot. For those who say that they rather ought to flee will seem either timid, because they were unwilling to bear the impending evil, or arrogant, because they judged themselves the more necessary to the Church, as those who ought to be preserved. Then, perhaps those who are the better will choose to lay down their lives for their brothers; and those will be preserved by fleeing whose life is less useful, because of less skill in counseling and governing: who nevertheless, if they are devoutly wise, will contradict those whom they see both ought rather to live, and would rather die than flee. Therefore, as it is written: *The casting of lots settles disputes, and decides among the powerful*[^15]: for God judges better in ambiguities of this kind than men do; whether he deign to call the better to the fruit of suffering and to spare the weak, or to make the latter stronger for enduring evils, and to withdraw from this life those whose life cannot profit the Church of God as much as theirs. The matter will indeed be done less customarily, if this casting of lots is done: but if it is done, who will dare to reprehend it? Who, except the unskilled or the envious, will not praise it with fitting commendation? But if it does not please to do this, of the doing of which no example occurs, let no one's flight cause the ministry of the Church—especially in such great perils necessary and owed—to be lacking. Let no one except his own person, so that, if he seems to excel by some grace, he should for that reason say he is the more worthy of life, and on this account of flight. For whoever thinks this is too pleased with himself: and whoever even says this displeases all.

**13.** There are indeed some who think that bishops and clergy, by not fleeing in such perils but remaining, cause the peoples to be deceived, since the latter do not flee for this reason, that they see their own superiors remaining. But it is easy to avoid this objection, or this ill will, by addressing those same peoples and saying: "Do not let it deceive you that we do not flee from this place: for it is not for our own sake, but rather for yours, that we remain here, lest we fail to minister to you whatever we know necessary to your salvation, which is in Christ. If, therefore, you wish to flee, you have also loosed us from these bonds by which we are held." Which I think is then to be said, when it truly seems useful to migrate to safer places. Which having been heard, if either all or some say: "We are in the power of him whose wrath no one escapes wherever he goes; whose mercy, wherever it be, he can find who wishes to go nowhere, whether hindered by certain necessities, or unwilling to labor toward uncertain refuges, and not to ending but to changing perils": without doubt these are not to be deserted by Christian ministry. But if, this having been heard, they prefer to depart, neither must those remain who were remaining on their account; because there are no longer there those for whose sake they ought still to remain.

**14.** Whoever, therefore, flees in such a way that the ministry necessary to the Church is not lacking by his fleeing, does what the Lord commanded or permitted. But he who flees in such a way that from the flock of Christ are withdrawn those nourishments by which it lives spiritually, that man is the hireling who *sees the wolf coming and flees, because he has no care for the sheep*[^16]. These things, because you consulted me, most beloved brother, I have written back with what truth I judged and with sure love; but I have not prescribed that you should not follow a better opinion, if you find one. Yet we cannot find anything better to do in these perils than prayers to the Lord our God, that he may have mercy on us. And this very thing—namely, not to desert the churches of God—by the gift of God certain prudent and holy men have merited both to will and to do, and amid the teeth of detractors have in no way fallen away from the intention of their purpose.
