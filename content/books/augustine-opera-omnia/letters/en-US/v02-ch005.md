# Letter 104

To the most excellent lord, deservedly honorable and worthy to be received brother Nectarius, Augustine sends greetings in the Lord.

**1. 1.** I have read the letter of Your Kindness in which you answered me long after I gave my own to be delivered to you. For I had written back when the holy brother and my fellow bishop Possidius was still with us and had not yet sailed. But the letter which you deigned to give him for my sake I received on the twenty-seventh of March, almost eight months after I had written. Why, then, my writings reached you so late, or yours reached me, I do not at all know. Unless perhaps it has only now pleased your prudence to write back what before you had disdained to do. If this is the case, I wonder what the reason is. Or have you heard something which is still hidden from us—that my brother Possidius has obtained against your fellow citizens (whom, with your leave I may say, he himself loves far more wholesomely than you do) that they be punished more severely? For your letter too indicates that you fear this, since you admonish me to set before my eyes what the appearance of that city is, from which men are dragged out to be led to punishment; what the lamentation is of mothers, of wives, of children, of parents; with what shame those who are set free, but tortured, can come back to their fatherland; what griefs or groans the consideration of wounds and scars renews. Far be it that we should press, either through ourselves or through anyone, that such things be inflicted on any of our enemies; but, as I said, if some such report has reached you, set it out more plainly, so that we may know either what to do that these things may not happen, or what we ought to answer to those who believe them.

**1. 2.** Look rather at my letter, which you were reluctant to answer; for there I expressed our mind sufficiently. But, as I think, having forgotten what I wrote back to you, you reported to me things altogether other, far different and dissimilar. For, as though recalling what I had set down in my letter, you inserted into yours what I did not at all set down. For you say that it was stated last in my letter that the Church does not demand life or blood in vengeance, but the goods of which they are most afraid of being despoiled. Then, showing how great an evil this is, you add and weave in that, unless your opinion deceives you, you judge it graver to be despoiled of one's goods than to be killed. And, to set out more plainly of what goods you spoke, you go on and add that I know, from what is often repeated in letters, that death brings an end of all the wicked's sense, but that a needy life begets eternal calamity. Then you concluded that it is graver to live amid evils than to end in a bad death.

**1. 3.** And for my part, I do not recall ever having read anywhere—neither in our writings, to which I confess I applied my mind later than I would have wished, nor in yours, which I learned from my earliest years—that a needy life begets eternal calamity. For laborious poverty has never been a sin, and it is somewhat a restraint and check upon sins. And by this, it is not to be feared that, after this brief life, the fact of having lived poor here should avail anyone toward eternal calamity of soul; and in this very life which we spend on earth, no calamity can in any way be eternal, since this life itself cannot be eternal, which is not even long-lasting, to whatever age and old age it may attain. For I rather read this in those writings, that the very life we enjoy is brief—the life in which you suppose, and now, as often repeated in letters, you admonish, that there can be eternal calamity. But that death is the end of all the wicked's evils, your writings indeed contain, but not even all of them; for that is the opinion of the Epicureans, and of any others who think the soul mortal. But those whom Tullius calls, as it were, consular philosophers—because he sets great store by their authority—hold that when we discharge our last day the soul is not extinguished but emigrates, and they assert that its merits too, whether good or evil, remain, whether for blessedness or for misery. This accords also with the sacred writings, of which I desire to be a man of letters. The end of evils, then, is death, but in those whose life is chaste, devout, faithful, innocent—not in those who, burning with desire for temporal trifles and vanities, even when they here seem happy to themselves, are by the very depravity of their will convicted of being wretched, and after death are compelled not only to have graver miseries but even to feel them.

**1. 4.** Since, then, these things are often repeated both in certain of your writings, which you hold in higher honor, and in all of ours, O good lover even of your earthly fatherland, fear for your fellow citizens a life of luxury, not a needy one; or if you fear a needy one, admonish them rather to avoid that neediness which, though it flows around with great prosperity of earthly things, is yet, by gaping insatiably after them—to use the words of your own authors—diminished by neither abundance nor want. Yet in those letters of mine to which you replied, I did not say that the enemies of the Church, your fellow citizens, were to be amended by that neediness in which the necessities of nature are lacking—neediness which mercy relieves, of which you thought it right even to prescribe to us that the principle of our works indicates it, by which we support the poor, relieve the sick with care, apply medicine to afflicted bodies; although even so it is more useful to be in want than to abound in all things for the satisfying of wickedness. But far be it that I should judge that those of whom we treat ought to be reduced by such coercion to this distress.

**2. 5.** Review my letter—if indeed you held it worthy, if not as one to be reread when it was to be answered, at least as one to be filed away so that, at your command, it might be produced when you wished—and attend to what I said; this surely you will find: that you have not answered it, as far as I judge, as you yourself will confess. For I now insert my own words from that letter: "We are not eager," I say, "to feed our anger by avenging the past, but we are busy taking thought mercifully for the future. Evil men have ways in which, even through Christians, they may be punished not only gently but even usefully and wholesomely. For they have the fact that they live with bodies unharmed; they have the means by which they live; they have the means by which they live badly. Let the first two be kept safe, so that there may be those who repent; this we desire, this, as far as is in us, we even press for with effort expended. But the third, if the Lord wills, like something rotten and harmful to be cut away, he will punish most mercifully." If you had reviewed these words of mine when you deigned to write back to me, you would think that we ought to be entreated, not concerning the death, but concerning the bodily torments to be avoided of those on whose behalf you act, in a way more spiteful than dutiful; for of these I said that we wished to be kept safe in them the fact that they live unharmed in body. Nor would you at all dread on our part a needy life for them, such that they should lack food shared by others; for of these I also said, as the second thing, that we wished to be kept safe the fact that they have the means by which they live. But the third—that they have the means by which they live badly, that is, to say nothing else, certainly the means by which silver images of false gods are truly fashioned—for the saving or adoring of which, or for worshiping them still with sacrilegious rite, men go so far as to set fire to the church of God, and offer up the supports of the most religious poor to be plundered by the unhappy mob, and blood is shed: why, you who take thought for your city, do you fear that this should be cut away, lest by altogether pernicious impunity audacity be nourished and strengthened? Set this forth to us, teach us by careful disputation what evil there is; attend diligently to what we say, lest, under the guise of petitioning, you seem in a manner to bring against us a charge by an oblique accusation.

**2. 6.** Let your fellow citizens be honorable, of upright character, not of superfluous means: we do not wish them to be led by us, through that coercion, to the plow of Quintius and the hearth of Fabricius. By that poverty those leaders of the Roman republic not only did not become cheap to their fellow citizens, but were on that account especially dearer, and more fit for governing the resources of the fatherland. Nor do we even desire or aim that there remain to the rich men of your fatherland only ten pounds of silver, like that of Rufinus, twice consul; which a praiseworthily severe censorship then judged should be cut away as a fault. Only the custom of a degenerate age persuades us to handle too softly souls too flaccid, so that what seemed just to those censors seems too much to Christian gentleness; and you see how great a difference there is whether it is now a fault to be punished merely to have so much, or whether, on account of other most grave faults, it is to be permitted that one have so much; for what was then a sin, we now wish to be at least the penalty of sin. But there is something that can and ought to be done: that severity not go so far as to those things, nor too secure impunity rejoice and run riot, and that an example for imitation, leading to the gravest and most hidden punishments, be set before the unhappy. At least grant that those who strive to burn and lay waste our necessities should fear for their own superfluities. Let it be permitted to bestow even this benefit on our enemies, that, while they fear for goods which it is not harmful to lose, they may not attempt to admit what is harmful to themselves. For this is not to be called the avenging of sin, but the safeguarding of counsel; this is not to inflict punishment, but to fortify against incurring punishment.

**2. 7.** Anyone who deprives an imprudent person, with some sense of pain, lest, accustomed to needless crimes, he pay the most atrocious penalties, plucks the hair of a boy lest he clap his hands at serpents; and so, where love is troublesome, no limb is injured, but where it deters, safety and life are endangered. We are not beneficent then, when we do what is asked of us, but when we do what does not harm those who ask. For in very many cases we profit by not giving, and we would harm if we gave. Whence that proverb: "Not even a sword to a boy." But you, says Tullius, "not even to an only son." For the more we love anyone, the less we ought to entrust to him in matters where there is great danger of sinning. And he was speaking, if I am not mistaken, of riches when he said this. Accordingly, things that are dangerously entrusted to those who misuse them are for the most part also wholesomely withdrawn. Physicians, when they see that the rotten part must be cut and cauterized, often grow mercifully deaf to many tears. If, as often as we as little children, or even as somewhat older boys, begged pardon when sinning, we had as often received it from our parents or teachers, which of us would have grown up to be tolerable? Which of us would have learned anything useful? These things are done providently, not cruelly. Do not, I beg, in this matter intend nothing else than how you may bring it about that we do what is asked of you by your people; but consider everything diligently. If you neglect the past, which cannot now be made undone, look somewhat ahead to the future; attend prudently, not to what those who ask you desire, but to what is expedient for them. We are surely not convicted of loving them faithfully if we look to this alone, lest, by not doing what they demand, we be loved less by them. And where is that which even your writings praise—that ruler of the fatherland who takes thought for the people's benefit rather than for their wish?

**3. 8.** "It makes no difference," you say, "of what sort the sin appears to be when indulgence is asked." You would rightly say this if it were a question of punishing men, not of correcting them. For far be it from a Christian heart that it be carried away to anyone's punishment by a lust for vengeance. Far be it that, in remitting anyone's sin, it either fail to forestall the prayers of the one who asks, or at any rate fail at once to follow them; but this surely is the point—that it should not hate the man, should not repay evil for evil, should not be inflamed with the ardor of harming, should not desire to feed even on vengeance owed by law; not, however, that it should not take thought, look ahead, restrain from evils. For it can happen that one who opposes too vehemently neglects the amendment of a man whom he hates too gravely, and that one renders better by some trouble of coercion the man whom he loves most.

**3. 9.** For "repentance too, as you write, obtains pardon and purges the offense": but that repentance which is carried on in true religion, which considers the future judgment of God; not that which is exhibited or feigned to men for the hour, not so that the soul may be purged of the offense for eternity, but so that meanwhile a life soon to perish may be freed from the present fear of trouble. Hence it is that, in the case of Christians confessing and entreating, who had been implicated in that offense—whether by not coming to the aid of a church about to be burned, or by carrying off something in most criminal plundering—we have believed the grief of repentance to be fruitful, and have judged it sufficient for their correction that there is in their hearts the faith by which they could consider what they ought to dread concerning the divine judgment. But what repentance can heal those who not only neglect to acknowledge the very fount of indulgence, but do not even cease to mock and blaspheme it? And against these, nevertheless, we hold no enmities in our heart, which is open and bare to him whose judgment we both fear and whose help we hope for, both in the present and in the future life. But we think that we provide something even for them, if men who do not fear God fear something by which their benefit is not harmed but their vanity is chastised; lest God himself, whom they despise, be more gravely offended by them through harmful security, in bolder deeds, and lest the same security be set before others to imitate, much more perniciously. Finally, on behalf of those for whom you ask us, we ask God on their behalf that he turn them to himself, so that, cleansing their hearts by faith, he may teach them to carry out a true and wholesome repentance.

**3. 10.** See how much more orderly than you—with your leave I may say it—and more usefully we love those with whom you suppose us angry, on whose behalf we entreat both for the avoiding of so much greater evils and for the obtaining of so much greater goods. And if you too loved them from God's heavenly gift, not after the earthly manner of men, and wrote back to me sincerely that, when I urged you toward the worship and religion of the most surpassing God, you gladly listened, you would not only desire these things for them, but would yourself go before them toward these very things. Thus the whole business of your petition would be ended among us with great and sound joy. Thus you would earn that heavenly fatherland, which you said you gladly received when I persuaded you that it was to be gazed upon, by a true and devout love even of this fatherland which bore you in the flesh; truly taking thought for your own, not toward the vanity of temporal gladness, nor toward the most pernicious impunity of crime, but toward the grace of everlasting happiness.

**3. 11.** You have, in this matter, the thoughts and wishes of my heart set forth. But what lies hidden in the counsel of God, I confess—I am a man—I do not know: whatever it is, it is more just, and wiser, and most firmly established, with incomparable excellence above all the minds of men. For it is true what is read in our books: *Many are the thoughts in the heart of a man, but the counsel of the Lord abides forever*[^1]. Accordingly, what the times may bring, what facility or difficulty may arise for us, what disposition, finally, may suddenly come to be from the correction or hope of present affairs; whether God is so indignant at these deeds that, by the very impunity they ask, they should be punished more severely, or whether he judges that they should be coerced mercifully in the way that pleases us, or whether, by some harder but more wholesome correction of theirs going before, and by a truthful conversion not to the mercy of men but to his own, he may turn aside whatever of terror was being prepared and turn it into joy—he himself now knows, but we are ignorant. Why, then, should I and Your Eminence labor here in vain, before the time, between ourselves? Let us set aside for a little the care whose hour has not come, and let us, if you please, do what always presses. For there is no time in which it is not fitting and necessary to do that whereby we may please God; and that this be fulfilled in this life to that perfection, so that no sin at all be in a man, is either impossible or perhaps most difficult: whence, all delays being cut off, we must flee to the grace of him to whom can most truly be said what someone said in a flattering poem to I know not what noble man—though he confessed that he had received it from the Cumaean song, as from a prophetic one:  
They will free the lands from perpetual dread.  
For under this guide, with all sins loosed and dismissed, by this way one comes to the heavenly fatherland, by whose dwelling, when I commended it to you to be loved as much as I could, you were greatly delighted.

**4. 12.** But because you said that all laws seek that city by diverse ways and paths, I fear lest perhaps, when you think that even the way in which you are now placed tends thither, you may be the more sluggish to hold to the one which alone leads there. But again, attending diligently to the word you used, I seem to myself to disclose your meaning not impudently: for you did not say which all laws "attain" or "show" or "find" or "enter" or "obtain" by diverse ways and paths, or anything of the sort, but, by saying "seek," with a word weighed and pondered, you signified not attainment but the desire of attaining. Thus you neither excluded the one which is true, nor admitted the others which are false; for that one indeed seeks which leads, but not every one which seeks this leads; and whoever is led thither is without any doubt blessed. But we all wish—that is, seek—to be blessed; yet not all of us who wish are able—that is, attain what we seek. He, then, attains who holds the way not only by which he seeks it, but by which one also arrives, leaving others in the journeys of seeking, without end of attaining. For there would be no error if nothing were sought, nor if the truth, once sought, were held. But if you spoke of diverse ways in such a way that we do not understand them as opposed—as we speak of diverse precepts which yet all build up a good life, some about chastity, some about patience, some about faith, some about mercy, and whatever else there is—then that fatherland is not only sought by ways and paths thus diverse, but is even found. For in the holy Scriptures both ways and a way are read of: ways, as is that, *I will teach the wicked your ways, and the impious will be turned to you*[^2]; a way, as is that, *Lead me in your way, and I will walk in your truth*[^3]. These ways are not one thing, that way another; but all are one, of which in another place the same holy Scripture says, *All the ways of the Lord are mercy and truth*[^4]: which, if they be diligently considered, beget a copious discourse and a most pleasant understanding; but this, if there be need, I will defer to another time.

**4. 13.** But now—what I think is enough for the office of writing back to Your Eminence which I have undertaken—since Christ said, *I am the way*[^5], in him are mercy and truth to be sought; lest, if we seek them elsewhere, we go astray, holding a way that seeks but does not also lead. As if we wished to hold this very thing of which you made some mention, that all sins are equal: would it not send us far off, exiles from that fatherland of truth and blessedness? For what more absurd, what more insane can be said than that he who laughed somewhat too immoderately and he who set fire to his fatherland more savagely should be judged to have sinned equally? Which way you thought should be applied—from the opinion of certain philosophers—not as a diverse way which yet leads to the heavenly dwelling, but as a plainly perverse one which leads to the most pernicious error, not according to your own sense but for the cause of your fellow citizens; so that we should pardon those by whose raging fires the Church was burned in the same way as we would pardon if we were attacked by them with some insolent insult.

**4. 14.** But see how you established this: "And if, as some philosophers hold," you say, "all sins are equal, indulgence ought to be common to all." Then, as you set yourself, as it were, to show that all sins are equal, you subjoin and say: "Someone spoke too insolently—he sinned; he heaped up insults or accusations—he likewise sinned." This is not to teach, but to propose what is perversely held, without any establishment of proofs. For to this that you say, "he likewise sinned," it is quickly answered: he did not likewise sin. You demand, perhaps, that I prove it: but what have you yet proved, that he sinned equally? Or is that which you join to be heard? "Someone seized what belonged to others—it is counted among offenses." Here even you yourself were abashed: for you were ashamed to say that he sinned equally; but, you say, "it is counted among offenses." But the question there is not whether this too "is counted among offenses," but whether it is joined to that offense in equality. Or if they are equal for the reason that both are offenses, then mice and elephants will be equal, because both are animals; flies and eagles, because both are flying things.

**4. 15.** Still further you advance and conjecture: "He violated profane and sacred places—he is not to be set apart from indulgence." Here indeed, from violated sacred places, you have arrived at the crime of your fellow citizens: but you yourself did not equate it even with insolent speech; you only asked indulgence for them, which is rightly asked of Christians on account of abounding compassion, not on account of the parity of sins. But I have set down above what is written in our writings: *All the ways of the Lord are mercy and truth*[^6]. They will accordingly obtain mercy, if they do not hate truth. Which mercy is owed by Christian right, not as to those who sin equally, as though they had spoken too insolently, but to those who rightly repent of a most monstrous and impious crime. But you, a man deservedly praiseworthy, do not, I beg, teach that those paradoxes of the Stoics are to be followed—you, our Paradox, whom we wish to grow up for you in true devotion and happiness. For what can a high-born young man think more unjust, and more dangerous to yourself, than if, having hurled an insult at some stranger, he should equate—I do not say parricide, but the very insult against his father?

**4. 16.** Fittingly, then, you plead with us on behalf of your fellow citizens by pressing upon us the mercy of Christians, not the hardness of the Stoics: which hardness in no way favors the cause you have undertaken, but rather greatly opposes it. For that very mercy, which if we did not have, we could be bent by no petition of yours, by no prayers of theirs, the Stoics reckon among vices, and they utterly expel it from the mind of the wise man, whom they want to be wholly iron and inflexible. It would therefore better occur to you to say something from your own Cicero, who, praising Caesar, says: "Of your virtues none is more admirable or more pleasing than mercy." How much more ought it to prevail in the churches, since they follow him who said, *I am the way*[^7], and read, *All the ways of the Lord are mercy and truth*[^8]. Do not, then, fear that we plot destruction for the innocent, we who do not wish even the guilty to come to deserved punishment, being forbidden by that mercy which we love in Christ together with truth. But he who spares and fosters vices to be nourished, lest he sadden the will of sinners, is as little merciful as one who is unwilling to snatch a knife from a boy lest he hear him cry, and does not fear lest he grieve over him wounded or dead. Keep, then, for the opportune time what you would do among us on behalf of these men, in whose love (forgive me) you not only do not at all go before us, but do not even yet follow us; and write back rather what moves you against this way which we hold, and on which we urge that you walk with us toward the heavenly fatherland, by which we know and rejoice that you are delighted.

**4. 17.** But the citizens of your carnal fatherland, though you said that not all, but some, were indeed innocent—yet, as you ought to notice when that letter of mine is reread, you did not defend them. When I said that we had felt not their flowers but their thorns, answering that which you had written, that you desired to leave your fatherland flourishing, you thought I was jesting. As if, indeed, one could find pleasure amid such great evils! That is exactly so. The ruins of the burned church still smoke, and in that cause we jest! And for my part, although no innocent men occurred to me there except those who were either absent, or suffered those evils, or had no strength or authority to prevent them, yet in writing back I distinguished the more guilty from the less guilty, and set down one case for those who feared to offend the powerful enemies of the Church, another for those who willed this to be committed; one for those who committed it, another for those who instigated it—wishing nothing to be done about the instigators, because this perhaps cannot be discovered without bodily torments which are abhorrent to our purpose. But your Stoics grant that all are equally guilty, those who hold that all sins are equal; who also, joining to this opinion their hardness by which they disparage mercy, in no way reckon that all are equally to be pardoned, but that all are equally to be punished. Remove them, then, as far as you can, from the patronage of this cause, and desire rather that we act as Christians, so that, as we desire, we may win in Christ those whom we spare, and not spare them by a pernicious dissolution. May God, merciful and truthful, deign to bestow this and true happiness.
