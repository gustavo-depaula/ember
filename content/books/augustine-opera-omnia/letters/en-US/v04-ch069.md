# Letter 5\*

TO VALENTINIANUS, A MOST BLESSED AND VENERABLE AND DESIRABLE LORD AND BROTHER AND FELLOW BISHOP, AUGUSTINE SENDS GREETINGS IN THE LORD.

**1.** You promise your presence, and in place of it you send us questions to be resolved, by which, as you write, your heart is shaken. But I, although I would prefer that you be present, yet, amid my occupations, to one to whom, present, I could have answered more readily by conversation, absent I answer as I can by writing.

**2.** Your first question is why the baptized straightway, in prayer, confess as debts the things they ask to be forgiven them, when in baptism itself all things were forgiven; and when you yourself had resolved this same question, on account of the noxious desires and illicit movements of the heart, which most easily creep upon human weakness, you say it occurred to you about little children, who can neither think nor speak; as though anyone bids or compels little children to be regenerated by the holy washing, except they be compelled to be washed on account of original sin, or those greater than they answer something for them about the Lord's Prayer, as about the faith of the Creed, without which they cannot be baptized at all, and therefore those who carry them, questioned at those very words, answer for them. There is, then, nothing about little children that ought to move you concerning the debts, which by the baptized, straightway after that washing—where all past things have been forgiven—are again sought to be forgiven on account of those things which creep in by the easiness of human thoughts.

**3.** Likewise you ask how to take what God said: *My Spirit will not remain in these men forever*—but our codices have it thus: *My Spirit will not remain in these men unto eternity*[^1]. Let the faultiness of your codex or codices be corrected, then, and there will be no question, in which you labor by inquiring why it set down "forever," as though it signified that for wicked sinners the punishment of present destruction suffices, when he rather threatened an eternal one, saying: *My Spirit will not remain in these men unto eternity*; but the ambiguity of the Greek word deceived the translator who so rendered it as to say "forever," although he ought at least to have said "for an age," not "forever." For what the Greeks say, εἰς τοὺς αἰῶνας, can in Latin be said both "for an age" and "unto eternity"; where the translator must consider the sense of the Scripture, lest he be deceived by the ambiguity. For as to what you ask, of which spirit it was said, whether the one that gives life to the body or the one that sanctifies the soul, it has no question at all, when you hear him saying: *My Spirit.* For he would say "their spirit," if he wished the one by which the body lives to be understood, since it pertains to the nature of man, and of that surely it could rightly be said: *My Spirit will not remain in these men*—which does not pertain to the nature of men, which could remain in them, unless, overcome by the pleasure of the flesh and deserting God, they deserved to lose the Holy Spirit, which is the gift of God. For their spirit, by which they were men, if you refer it to the bodies, how could it remain in them, who are someday to die; but if to that part by which the body is alive, how could it not remain in them, since they themselves are spirits who go out in death from the body, and must, wherever they be, exist, whether it be well or ill with them? I think I have sufficiently answered your inquiries. But your bodily presence, not promised, I would not exact, but, promised, I demand as a debt.

[^1]: See Gen 6:3
