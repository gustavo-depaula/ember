# Letter 149

TO THE MOST BLESSED AND VENERABLY DESIRABLE AND DESIRABLY VENERABLE, MY HOLY AND HOLILY DEAREST BROTHER AND FELLOW BISHOP PAULINUS, FROM AUGUSTINE, GREETINGS IN THE LORD.

**1. 1.** Since the Lord quickly gladdened us about the prosperous arrival of our brother and fellow presbyter Quintus, and of those who sailed with him, by the letter of your Holiness announcing it, I now, giving thanks to him who refreshes the afflicted and consoles the humble, pay back to the sincerity of your heart a reply, on the occasion of our son and fellow deacon Rufinus, which presents itself next; for he set out from the shore of Hippo. And I approve the counsel of mercy which the Lord inspired in you, and which you deigned to make known to me. May he himself aid this, may he himself prosper it, who has already in great part eased our care, since that most dear man has been brought across and commended, not only by your good works, but also by your holy prayers.

**1. 2.** The letter of your Veneration, indeed, in which you asked many things, and admonished what should be asked, and by asking taught, reached me. But those which I myself immediately wrote back, through the men of those same holy comforts of ours, were not delivered to your Veneration, as I have learned from these more recent letters of yours. In them I have not been able to recall how far I answered your questions; nor, when I had sought it, did I find a copy of them by which I could recognize this. Yet I am altogether certain that I answered some things, and therefore not all, because the haste of the carrier was pressing me to finish quickly. At the same time I had also sent, as you had bidden, a copy of that letter which I had written back to your Charity at Carthage about the resurrection of bodies, where a question had arisen about the use of the members. Now, therefore, I have both sent this, and the one along with the other, since I have surmised that neither did it reach your hands, because you again asked certain things which I read and recognize that I already answered in it. But by whom I sent it I do not know. For the writing of your Charity, to which I replied with it, as it itself also indicates, was sent to me from Hippo by our people, when I was with our holy brother and fellow bishop Boniface (for I did not see the carrier of them), and I did not delay an immediate reply.

**1. 3.** Because, then, at that time, as I wrote, I had not been able to inspect the Greek manuscripts concerning certain matters of the sixteenth psalm, I afterward inspected those I found. And one had what our Latins also have: *Lord, destroying them from the earth, divide them*; another had it as you yourself put it: "From a few, from the earth." And the former indeed has a plain sense: "destroying" from the earth "which you gave them, divide them" among the nations: which was also done to them, when by a grievous war they were assailed and overthrown. But how that other is to be taken does not occur to me, except that, in comparison with the destroyed multitude of them, a remnant was made safe, namely in the few from whom Scripture foretold that they were to be divided—that is, separated and set apart—saying: *Lord, from the few*, that is, from the remnant which you made safe out of that nation, *divide them from the earth*: so that we may understand the earth to be the Church and the inheritance of the faithful and the saints, which is also called the land of the living, and which can also in that place be rightly understood: *Blessed are the meek, for they shall possess the earth as their inheritance*[^1]. But when it had been said, *From the few, from the earth divide them*, there was added, *in their life*; so that it might be understood that this would be in the open, while they live here. For many are separated from the Church, but when they die—who yet, while they live, seem joined to the Church through the communion of the sacraments and of catholic unity. These, then, are divided from the few who believed out of them, from the earth which the Father, the husbandman, cultivates as his own field; but divided in their life, that is, here in the open, as we see. And there follows: *And their belly was filled from your hidden things*; that is, besides this, that they were divided in the open, also from your hidden things, which you secretly render to the conscience of the evil, their belly was filled: he put "belly" for the secrets of inner and hidden things.

**1. 4.** But as for what follows, *They were filled with swine's flesh*, I have already disclosed what seemed to me from this. But what other manuscripts have, and are reported to have more truly, since they are more careful copies, resolving the ambiguity of the same Greek word by an accent-mark in the Greek manner of writing, is indeed more obscure, but seems to suit better the more select sense. For because he had said, *And their belly was filled from your hidden things*, by which words the hidden judgments of God are signified—for those are hidden wretches who even rejoice in evils, whom *God handed over to the desires of their heart*[^2]—as though it were asked whence they can be known who in secret are filled with the wrath of God, and the answer were given which was said in the Gospel, *By their fruits you shall know them*[^3], there was immediately added: *They were filled with sons*, that is, with fruits, which is said more evidently, with their works. Whence elsewhere it is read: *Behold, he was in travail with injustice, he conceived sorrow, and brought forth iniquity*[^4]; and elsewhere: *Then desire, when it has conceived, brings forth sin*[^5]. Evil sons, then, are evil works, by which they are known, and who, from the hidden judgments of God, are filled as it were into the belly with the inner things of their thoughts. Good works are good sons. Whence to the bride, the Church, it is said: *Your teeth are like a flock of shorn sheep coming up from the washing, all of which bear twins, and none is barren among them*[^6]. In which twin offspring is recognized the twofold work of love, namely of the Lord God and of the neighbor: *on which two commandments hang all the Law and the Prophets*[^7].

**1. 5.** But this sense, by which what is written, *They were filled with sons*, is thus expounded, had not occurred to me when I wrote back before; but I have reviewed a certain very brief exposition of the same psalm, which I had dictated long ago, and I found this set down by me briefly enough. I also inspected the Greek manuscripts, whether what was said, *with sons*, was the dative case, or the genitive, which that language uses for the ablative, and I found the genitive: which, if it were translated word for word, would be written, *They were filled of sons*; but the translator rightly followed the sense, and in the Latin manner said: *They were filled with sons*. But as for what follows, *And they left their remnants to their little ones*, I think that "little ones" are to be taken as the manifest sons of the flesh. Whence also, according to this exposition—not of swine's flesh, but of sons—there remains that saying by which they said: *His blood be upon us and upon our sons*[^8]: for thus they left the remnants of their works to their little ones.

**1. 6.** But in the fifteenth psalm, where it is written, *He has made wonderful*, or, *May he make wonderful, all his wishes among them*, nothing forbids us to understand—indeed it even seems more fitting—not "among them," but "in them." For thus the Greek manuscripts have it: and often what that language has, "in them," our translators render "among them," where it seems to suit the sense. Let us take it, then: *To the saints who are in his land, he has made wonderful all his wishes in them*; which most manuscripts have: and let us understand "his wishes" to be the gifts of grace, which was given freely, that is, because he willed it, not because it was owed. Whence is: *With the shield of your good will you have crowned us*[^9]; and: *In your will you have led me*[^10]; and: *Of his own will he begot us by the word of truth*[^11]; and: *Setting apart a voluntary rain, O God, for your inheritance*[^12]; and: *Dividing to each his own as he wills*[^13]; and innumerable others. Accordingly, *He has made wonderful all his wishes in them*—in whom, except in the saints who are in his land? For if the earth, as we showed above, can be taken in a good sense even where "his" is not added, how much more where it says "his land"! He has made his wishes wonderful, then, in them: he has made them altogether wonderful, because he marvelously freed them from despair.

**1. 7.** Out of this wonder the Apostle exclaims: *O the depth of the riches of the wisdom and knowledge of God!*[^14] For he had said above: *For God has shut up all in unbelief, that he may have mercy on all*[^15]. For this too follows here: *Their weaknesses were multiplied; afterward they hastened*[^16]. He put "weaknesses" for sins, as the Apostle says to the Romans: *For if Christ, while we were yet weak, in due time died for the ungodly*[^17]: those he called weak whom he called ungodly. Then a little after, repeating the same sentiment: *God commends his love toward us*, he says, *in that, while we were yet sinners, Christ died for us*[^18]: those whom he called weak above, he here called sinners. Likewise in what follows, turning this same thought in other words: *For if, while we were enemies, we were reconciled to God through the death of his Son*[^19]. And through this, in what was said, *Their weaknesses were multiplied*, their sins multiplied are understood. *For the law entered in, that the offense might abound; but since where the offense abounded, grace did more abound*[^20], therefore *afterward they hastened*. For he came not *to call the righteous, but sinners*[^21]: since *the healthy have no need of a physician, but those who are ill*[^22]; whose weaknesses, namely, were multiplied, so that for healing them the medicine of so great a grace was necessary, and he to whom many sins are forgiven might love much[^23].

**1. 8.** This the ashes of the heifer and the sprinkling of blood and the multiplication of bloody victims signified, but did not effect. Therefore he says next: *I will not gather their assemblies from blood*[^24]—that is, from those sacrifices which were offered as a figure of the blood of Christ. *Nor will I remember their names through my lips*[^25]. For their names were in the multiplication of weaknesses: *fornicators, those serving idols, adulterers, the effeminate, men who lie with men, thieves, the greedy, robbers, drunkards, the abusive*[^26], and any others who *shall not possess the kingdom of God*[^27]. But where, sin abounding, grace did more abound, afterward they hastened. *These things indeed they were, but they were washed, but they were sanctified, but they were justified in the name of the Lord Jesus Christ and in the Spirit of our God*[^28]: therefore he will not remember their names through his lips. To be sure, the more corrected manuscripts, and those of more select authority, do not have "his wishes," but "my wishes"; which means the same, since it is spoken from the person of the Son. For it is he who speaks, whom those words evidently show, which the Apostles too used: *You will not leave my soul in hell, nor will you give your holy one to see corruption*[^29]. The gifts of grace of the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit are surely the same; and therefore most rightly can the Son too call them his wishes.

**1. 9.** But as for what is understood of the Jews in the fifty-eighth psalm, where it says, *Slay them not, lest at any time they forget your law*[^30], it seems to me fittingly understood to have been foretold thus: that this same nation, even conquered and overthrown, would not pass over into the superstitions of the victorious people, but would remain in the old Law, so that among them there might be the testimony of the Scriptures throughout the whole world, from which the Church was to be called forth. For by no more evident document is it shown to the nations—what is most healthfully observed—that no unforeseen and sudden thing was instituted by a spirit of human presumption, so that the name of Christ should prevail with so great authority in the hope of eternal salvation, but that it was long ago prophesied and written down. For what else would that very prophecy be thought, but devised by our people, if it were not proved from the books of our enemies? Therefore: *Slay them not*; do not extinguish the name of that very nation, *lest at any time they forget your law*: which would surely happen if, compelled to worship the rites and sacred things of the gentiles thoroughly, they should retain not at all any name of their own religion. This too was written in their figure concerning Cain, that the Lord set a sign upon him, lest anyone should kill him[^31]. Finally, when he had said, *Slay them not, lest at any time they forget your law*, as though it were asked what should be done with them, so that for some uses of the testimony of truth they should not be slain, that is, not consumed, nor forget the law of God, he immediately added: *Scatter them by your power*[^32]. For if they were in one place of the earth, they would not aid by their testimony the preaching of the Gospel, which bears fruit throughout the whole world. Therefore, *scatter them by your power*, so that of that very one of whom they were the deniers, persecutors, slayers, they may everywhere be witnesses through that very Law which they do not forget, in which he was prophesied whom they do not follow. For it profits them nothing that they do not forget it: for it is one thing to have the law of God in memory, and another in understanding and effect.

**1. 10.** But in the sixty-seventh psalm, as for what you ask is the meaning of: *But God will shatter the heads of his enemies, the crown of the hair of those who walk in their offenses*[^33], it seems to me nothing else is said than: God will shatter the heads of his enemies who are exceedingly proud, exceedingly exalting themselves in their offenses. For by hyperbole he signified pride exalting itself so greatly, and advancing with such great elation, as though by walking it trod upon the crown of the hair. Likewise in the same psalm, as for what is written: *The tongue of your dogs, from the enemies, from him*[^34], dogs are not always to be taken in a bad sense. Otherwise the prophet would not reproach *dumb dogs, not knowing how to bark, and loving to slumber*[^35]: for surely they would be praiseworthy dogs, if they both knew how to bark and loved to keep watch. And certainly those three hundred in number, by the most sacred letter of the cross, would not have been chosen for winning the victory, who like dogs lapped the water[^36], unless they signified something great. For good dogs keep watch and bark, both for the house and for the master, both for the flock and for the shepherd. Finally, here too, in the praises of the Church, when the prophecy was being set forth, the tongue of dogs is mentioned, not the teeth. But "your dogs," he said, "from the enemies"—that is, that those who were your enemies might become your dogs, and might bark for you who were raging against you. And he added, "from him," that they might understand this to have been done not by themselves, but by him, that is, by his mercy and grace.

**2. 11.** As for what the Apostle says of the prophets, *Some indeed God gave in the Church as apostles, but some as prophets*[^37], I understand this, which you yourself have written, that in this place are called prophets those of whom Agabus was one[^38], not those who prophesied that the Lord would come in the flesh. But we find evangelists who, we do not read, were apostles, as were Luke and Mark. As for pastors and teachers, whom you especially wished me to distinguish, I think they are the same, as it also seemed to you, so that we understand not some to be pastors, others teachers; but that, having premised "pastors," he subjoined "teachers," so that pastors might understand that teaching belongs to their office. For therefore he did not say: Some indeed as pastors, but some as teachers; though he distinguished the earlier ones by that very manner of speech, saying: Some indeed as apostles, but some as prophets, but some as evangelists; but this he embraced as some one thing by two names, "but some as pastors and teachers."

**2. 12.** Those things, plainly, are most difficult to distinguish, where, writing to Timothy, he says: *I beseech, therefore, first of all, that supplications, prayers, intercessions, thanksgivings be made*[^39]. For according to the Greek expression they must be distinguished; for scarcely are translators of ours found who have taken care to render them diligently and knowingly. For behold, as you yourself have set them down, *I beseech that supplications be made*—the Apostle did not say both by the same word, who surely wrote that letter in Greek; but for what in the Latin is "I beseech," he in Greek said παρακαλῶ. But for what your Latin has, "supplications," he set down δεήσεις. Accordingly, other manuscripts, among which are ours too, do not have "supplications," but "entreaties." Further, the three that follow, "prayers, intercessions, thanksgivings," most Latin manuscripts have thus.

**2. 13.** Whence, if we wish to distinguish these according to the properties of the Latin language by the manner of speaking, we will perhaps hold to our own view, such as it is; but it would be surprising if we should obtain the sense of the Greek language or usage. "Precation" and "deprecation" many of ours think the same, and this has now altogether prevailed in daily use. But those who spoke Latin more distinctly used "precations" in wishing for goods, but "deprecations" in averting evils. For they said that to "pray" (precari) is to wish for goods by praying; to "imprecate" (imprecari) evils, which is now commonly called to curse; but to "deprecate" (deprecari) is to drive off evils by praying. But let us rather follow the now customary manner of speaking; and whether we find "precations" or "deprecations," which the Greeks call δεήσεις, let us not think it should be emended. But "prayers," which the Greek has as προσευχάς, to distinguish from "prayers" or "precations" is altogether difficult. As for the fact that certain manuscripts do not have "prayers" but "adorations," because it was said in Greek not εὐχάς but προσευχάς: I do not think this was knowingly translated; for it is most well known that προσευχάς are called "prayers" by the Greeks. And surely it is one thing to pray, another to adore. Finally, it is read in Greek not by this word, but by another: *You shall adore the Lord your God*[^40]; and: *I will adore toward your holy temple*[^41]; and any such things.

**2. 14.** But for "intercessions," which ours have, according, I believe, to your manuscripts, you set down "petitions." These two, then—that is, that some translated "petitions," others "intercessions"—wished to render one word which the Greek has, ἐντεύξεις. And surely you observe, and know, that it is one thing to intercede, another to petition. For we are not wont to say, They petition about to intercede, but, They intercede about to petition: yet a word borrowed from nearness, for which the very nearness obtains understanding, is not to be blamed as if by a censor's mark. For of the Lord Jesus Christ himself it was said that *he intercedes for us*[^42]: does he merely intercede, and not also petition? Rather, because he petitions, "intercedes" was put in its place. For evidently elsewhere it is said of him: *And if anyone sins, we have an advocate with the Father, Jesus Christ the righteous, and he is the propitiation for our sins*[^43]. Although perhaps the manuscripts among you, in that place too about the Lord Jesus Christ, have not "intercedes for us," but "petitions for us"; for in the Greek, by whatever word "intercessions" are here set down, which you yourself put as "petitions," that same word is there too, where it is written, "intercedes for us."

**2. 15.** Since, then, both he who entreats prays, and he who prays entreats, and he who intercedes with God intercedes for this, that he may pray and entreat, what does it mean that the Apostle so set these down that their distinction is not to be neglected? Apart, therefore, from the general name, and saving the custom of speech, according to which, whether you say entreaty, or prayer, or intercession, or petition, one and the same thing is understood, some property also of each of these is to be sought; but to arrive at it clearly is difficult: for many things can be said here that are not to be disapproved.

**2. 16.** But I choose to understand in these words what all, or almost all, the Church practices: that we take "entreaties" to be said of what we make in the celebration of the sacraments, before that which is on the Lord's table begins to be blessed; "prayers," when it is blessed and sanctified, and broken up for distribution—which whole petition almost the entire Church concludes with the Lord's prayer. To this understanding the origin of the Greek word too aids us. For that which they call εὐχήν, Scripture rarely so sets down that "prayer" is understood; but generally, and much more customarily, it calls εὐχήν a "vow"; but προσευχήν, which word is so set down in the passage we are treating, it always calls "prayer." Whence certain people, regarding this origin of the word, as I said above, less learnedly, wished to call προσευχήν not "prayer" but "adoration," which is rather called προσκύνησις: but because prayer is sometimes called εὐχή, προσευχή was thought to be adoration. Further, if more customarily, as I said, in the Scriptures εὐχή is called a "vow," then, apart from the general name of prayer, that is properly to be understood as prayer which we make at a vow, that is, πρὸς εὐχήν. But all things which are offered to God are vowed, especially the offering of the holy altar; by which sacrament is proclaimed that vow of ours, the greatest, by which we vow that we will abide in Christ, namely in the framework of the body of Christ. Of which thing it is the sacrament, that *we who are many are one bread, one body*[^44]. Therefore in the preparation of this sanctification I think the Apostle commanded that there properly be made προσευχάς, that is, prayers, or, as some have translated less skillfully, adorations; for this is at the vow, which more customarily in the Scriptures is named εὐχή. But intercessions, or, as your manuscripts have, petitions, are made when the people are blessed: for then the bishops, as advocates, offer those received under their care, through the imposition of hands, to the most merciful Power. When these are done, and so great a sacrament has been partaken of, thanksgiving concludes all, which last too the Apostle commended in these words.

**2. 17.** But this was the chief reason for saying these things: that, when these had been briefly touched on and signified, it should not be thought that what follows is to be neglected: *for all men, for kings and those who are in high place, that we may lead a quiet and tranquil life in all piety and love*[^45]; lest anyone, as the weakness of human thought tends, should suppose that these things are not to be done for those by whom the Church was suffering persecution, since the members of Christ were to be gathered from every kind of men. Whence he adds and says: *For this is good and acceptable before God our Savior, who wills all men to be saved and to come to the knowledge of the truth*[^46]. And lest anyone should say that the way of salvation could be in a good manner of life and the worship of the one almighty God, without partaking of the body and blood of Christ: *For there is one God*, he says, *and one mediator of God and men, the man Christ Jesus*[^47]: so that what he had said, "wills all men to be saved," is understood to be granted in no other way than through the mediator, not God, which the Word always was, but the man Christ Jesus, when *the Word was made flesh and dwelt among us*[^48].

**2. 18.** Whence let not this move you either, that the same apostle says of the Jews: *According to the Gospel, indeed, they are enemies for your sake; but according to election, they are beloved for the fathers' sake*[^49]. For that *depth of the riches of the wisdom and knowledge of God*[^50], and those inscrutable judgments of his, and his untraceable ways, work great wonder in faithful hearts, which do not doubt about his wisdom, *reaching mightily from end to end and disposing all things sweetly*[^51]—what it is that it pleases him that those should be born, grow, and multiply, whom, though he himself did not make them evil, yet he foreknew would be evil. For exceedingly hidden is his counsel, by which he uses even the evil well for the advancement of the good, magnifying even in this the omnipotence of his goodness: because, as it belongs to their wickedness to use his good works ill, so it belongs to his wisdom to use their evil works well.

**2. 19.** This depth of the mystery, then, the Apostle thus commends: *I do not wish you to be ignorant, brothers, of this mystery, that you may not be wise in your own eyes, that blindness in part has happened in Israel, until the fullness of the Gentiles should enter in, and so all Israel should be saved*[^52]. "In part," he said, because not all were blinded; for there were among them those who knew Christ. But the fullness of the Gentiles enters in among those who *are called according to the purpose*[^53]; and so all Israel shall be saved, because both from Jews and from Gentiles, those who are called according to the purpose are themselves the true Israel, of whom the same Apostle says: *And upon the Israel of God*[^54]; but those he calls Israel according to the flesh: *See Israel according to the flesh*[^55], he says. Then he interposes the testimony of the prophet: *There shall come out of Zion one who shall deliver and turn away ungodliness from Jacob; and this is my covenant to them, when I shall take away their sins*[^56]—not, surely, of all Jews, but of the elect.

**2. 20.** From this he adds those things you set down to be inquired into: *According to the Gospel, indeed, they are enemies for your sake*. For the price of our redemption is the blood of Christ, which surely could be shed only by enemies. This is that use of the evil for the advancement of the good. But as for what he adds, *but according to election they are beloved for the fathers' sake*, hence he shows that those enemies are not, but the elect are, the beloved. But it is the custom of Scripture so to speak of a part as of the whole: just as in the first parts of his Letter he praises the Corinthians as though all were such, when certain of them were praiseworthy; and afterward in some places of that same Letter he reproaches them as though all were blameworthy, on account of certain who were such. Whoever diligently observes this custom of the divine Scriptures, scattered most frequently throughout the whole body of his Letters, resolves many things which seem to be contrary among themselves. He calls some, then, enemies, others beloved; but because they were in one people, he seems to speak as it were of the same ones. Although even of those very enemies who crucified the Lord, very many were converted, and appeared elect. Then elect, when converted, as far as pertains to the beginning of salvation; but as far as pertains to the foreknowledge of God, not then elect, but before the foundation of the world, as the same apostle says, that *he chose us before the foundation of the world*[^57]. In two ways, then, are the very enemies those who are beloved: either because both were in one people; or because, out of enemies raging even to the blood of Christ, certain of them had been made beloved according to the election which lay hidden in the foreknowledge of God. For to this he added, "for the fathers' sake," because what was promised to the fathers had to be fulfilled, as toward the end of the Letter to the Romans he says: *For I say that Christ was a minister of the circumcision for the truth of God, to confirm the promises of the fathers. But that the Gentiles should glorify God for his mercy*[^58]. According to this mercy it was said, "they are enemies for your sake": which he had also said above: *By their offense salvation has come to the Gentiles*[^59].

**2. 21.** But when he had said, *according to election they are beloved for the fathers' sake*, he added: *For the gifts and the calling of God are without repentance*[^60]. You see certainly that those are signified who belong to the number of the predestined. Of whom in another place he says: *We know that for those who love God all things work together unto good, for those who are called according to the purpose*[^61]. For *many are called, but few are chosen*[^62]: but those who are chosen are themselves called according to the purpose; in whom, without doubt, the foreknowledge of God cannot be deceived. *These he foreknew and predestined to be conformed to the image of his Son, that he himself might be the firstborn among many brothers. But those whom he predestined, them he also called*[^63]. This is the calling according to the purpose; this is the one without repentance. *But those whom he called, them he also justified; but those whom he justified, them he also glorified. If God is for us, who is against us?*[^64]

**2. 22.** Those are not in this calling who, in the faith which works through love, even if they walk in it for a while, do not persevere to the end. And surely they could have been caught up, *lest wickedness should change their understanding*[^65], if they belonged to that predestination and calling which is according to the purpose and without repentance. But lest any presumptuous person should so judge about the hidden things of others as to say, "They were therefore not caught up out of this life before they became deserters of the faith, because in that same life they did not walk faithfully, and the Lord knew this in their hearts, although to men it appeared otherwise"—what will he say about little infants, very many of whom, having received in that age the sacrament of Christian grace, while without doubt they would belong to eternal life and the kingdom of heaven if they departed at once from this life, are allowed to grow up, and some even become apostates? Whence is this, except that they do not belong to that predestination, and to the calling according to the purpose and without repentance? But why some belong to it and others do not, may have a hidden cause; it cannot be an unjust one. *For is there injustice with God? By no means*[^66]. For this too belongs to that depth of judgments, which, marveling, the Apostle as it were dreaded. For therefore he calls those judgments, lest anyone should think such things come about either by the injustice or rashness of the one acting, or by chance and the fortuitous slipping of any parts of the ages, so ordered under so great a wisdom of God.

**2. 23.** Further, in the Letter to the Colossians, as for what is written: *Let no one seduce you, willing in humility*[^67], and the rest that follows, as far as you said it was obscure to you, neither do I yet understand it without a fog. And would that you had asked me these things when present: for in the sense which I seem to myself to have in these words, a certain delivery in the countenance and manner of voice must be applied, which cannot be expressed in letters, so that it may be opened up in some part; which is therefore made more obscure, because it is not rightly, as I think, pronounced. For what is written: *Touch not, taste not, handle not*[^68], is thought to be as it were a precept of the Apostle, forbidding I know not what to touch, taste, handle. But it is the contrary, if, however, in so great obscurity I am not deceived. For by way of mockery he set down the words of those by whom he did not wish them to be deceived and seduced, who, distinguishing foods according to the worship of angels, and thus making decrees about this world, say: *Touch not, taste not, handle not*; though *all things are clean to the clean*[^69], and *every creature of God is good*[^70]: which in another place he himself evidently commends.

**2. 24.** Let us see, then, the whole circumstance of his thought: for thus, the Apostle's intention being perceived, we shall perhaps hold his sense, as far as we are able. For those to whom he writes these things, he feared lest they be seduced by shadows of things, under the sweet name of knowledge, and turned away from the light of the truth which is in Christ Jesus our Lord. And he discerned that this care for vain and superfluous observances, under the name of wisdom or knowledge—both from the superstition of gentilism, and especially from those who are called philosophers, and from Judaism—must be guarded against, where the shadows of things to come were to be removed, since Christ, their light, had now come. When, therefore, he had mentioned and commended how great a struggle he had for them, and for those who were at Laodicea, and whoever had not seen his face in the flesh, that *their hearts might be comforted, being knit together in love, and unto all the riches of the fullness of understanding, to the knowledge of the mystery of God, which is Christ, in whom are hidden all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge*[^71]: *This I say*, he says, *that no one may circumvent you with a verisimilar speech*[^72]: because they were led by love of truth, he feared for them the likeness of truth, lest they be deceived. And therefore he commended in Christ that sweetest treasure they had, namely of wisdom and knowledge, by the name and promise of which they could be led into error.

**2. 25.** *For though I am absent in body*, he says, *I am with you in spirit, rejoicing and seeing your order, and that which is lacking to your faith in Christ*[^73]. Therefore he feared for them, since he saw what was still lacking to them. *As, then, you have received Jesus Christ our Lord*, he says, *walk in him; rooted and built up in him, and confirmed in faith, as you also have learned, abounding in it with thanksgiving*[^74]. He wishes them to be nourished by faith, so that they may become capable of partaking of the treasures of wisdom and knowledge which are hidden in Christ; lest, before they are fit for these, they be captured by a verisimilar speech, and stray from the path of truth. Then, showing more openly what he fears for them: *See*, he says, *that no one deceive you through philosophy and empty seduction, according to the tradition of men, according to the elements of the world, and not according to Christ; for in him dwells all the fullness of divinity bodily*[^75]. He said "bodily" because they were seducing them in a shadowy way; using a transferred word, just as the name "shadow" too in these matters is surely not a proper word, but transferred by a certain reasoning of likeness. *And you are filled in him*, he says, *who is the head of all principality and power*[^76]. For through principalities and powers the superstition of the gentiles, or the philosophers, seduced, proclaiming that which they call theology through the elements of this world. But he wished Christ, the head of all things, to be understood as the beginning of all things; just as he himself, when it had been said to him, *Who are you?* answered: *The Beginning, who also speak to you*[^77]. For *all things were made through him, and without him was made nothing*[^78]. But marvelously he wishes them to be despisers of these as it were marvels, when he shows them made the body of that head, by saying: *And you are filled in him, who is the head of all principality and power*.

**2. 26.** Hence now, lest they be seduced by the shadows of Judaism, he adds: *In whom also you were circumcised with a circumcision not made by hand, in the despoiling of the body of the flesh*; or, as some have it, *in the despoiling of the body of the sins of the flesh, in the circumcision of Christ; buried with him in Baptism, in which also you rose again through faith in the working of God, who raised him from the dead*[^79]. See how here too he shows them to be the body of Christ, that they may despise these things, cleaving to so great a head of theirs, the mediator of God and men, Christ Jesus, and seeking no false or invalid middle through which to cleave to God. *And you*, he says, *when you were dead in your offenses and the foreskin of your flesh*—he called "foreskin" that which is signified by the foreskin, that is, the carnal offenses of which we are to be despoiled—*he made alive with him, forgiving you all offenses, blotting out the handwriting that was against us in its decrees, which was contrary to us*[^80]; because the Law, which had entered in *that the offense might abound*[^81], made men guilty. *Taking it*, he says, *out of the way and fixing it to the cross, stripping himself of the flesh, he made an example of the principalities and powers, confidently triumphing over them in himself*[^82]: not, surely, the good principalities, but the evil ones, and the evil powers, namely the diabolical and demonic, he made an example of; that is, he gave an example concerning them, so that, just as he stripped himself of the flesh, so he might show that his own are to be stripped of carnal vices, through which those powers had dominion over them.

**2. 27.** Now here attend diligently how he infers that for the sake of which we have mentioned all these things. *Let no one, then*, he says, *judge you in food*[^83]: as though he said all those things for the reason that they were being seduced through observances of this kind from the truth, by which they were made free; of which it was said in the Gospel: *And the truth has freed you*[^84], that is, will make you free. *Let no one, then*, he says, *judge you in food, or in drink, or in respect of a feast day, or of a new moon, or of sabbaths, which is a shadow of things to come*[^85]: this on account of Judaism. Then follows, on account of the superstitions of the gentiles: *But the body of Christ—let no one convict you*[^86]: it is shameful, he says, and exceedingly unfitting, and alien to the nobility of your freedom, that, since you are the body of Christ, you should be seduced by shadows, and seem to be convicted as it were of sinning, if you neglect to observe these things. *The body of Christ, then—let no one convict you, willing in humility of heart*. If this were said by the Greek word, it would sound more customary even in the common Latin usage. For thus it is even commonly said of one who affects to be rich, "thelo-rich," and of one who affects to be wise, "thelo-wise," and other such things: so here too "thelo-humble," which more fully is said "wishing humble" (thelon humilis), that is, willing to be humble, affecting humility; which is understood as wishing to seem humble, affecting humility. For by such observances this is done, that the heart of a man may be rendered humble as it were by religion. And he added also "the worship of angels," or, as your manuscripts have, "the religion of angels," which in Greek is called θρησκεία; wishing the angels to be understood as the Principalities, whom they suppose to be set over the elements of this world, and to be worshipped in these observances.

**2. 28.** *Let no one, then*, he says, *convict you*, since you are the body of Christ, willing to seem humble of heart in the worship of angels, *treading on things he has not seen*; or, as certain manuscripts have, *treading on things he has seen*. For either he wished to say thus, "treading on things he has not seen," because men do these things by suspicions and opinions, not because they have seen that these things are to be done. Or certainly, "treading on things he has seen," that is, esteeming them great, because he has seen them observed in certain places elsewhere by men to whose authority he gives credit even without a reason being rendered; and therefore he seems great to himself, because it has fallen to him to see the secrets of certain sacred rites. But the fuller sense is, "treading on things he has not seen, *vainly puffed up by the mind of his flesh*." Marvelously he there called him "puffed up by the mind of his flesh," where above he had called him "thelo-humble": for it comes about in marvelous ways in the soul of a man that he is more puffed up by false humility than if he were more openly proud. *And not holding the head*, he says—by which he wishes Christ to be understood—*from which the whole body, joined and connected, supplied and knit together, grows unto the increase of God*[^87]. *If, then, you have died with Christ to the elements of this world, why do you still, as though living, make decrees about this world?*[^88]

**2. 29.** Having said these things, he inserts the words of those who make decrees about this world through these observances as though reasonable, puffed up by an affected vanity of humility: *Touch not, taste not, handle not*[^89]. Let us recall what was said above, that we may understand this. For he does not wish them to be judged in those observances where it is said: *Touch not, taste not, handle not*; *which are all*, he says, *unto corruption by the using*[^90]. All these things, he says, rather avail unto corruption, when they are superstitiously abstained from, so that a man "abuses" them, that is, does not use them according to *the precepts and teachings of men*[^91]. This is plain; but what follows you greatly inquire about: *which things indeed have a reason of wisdom in the observance, and in humility of heart, and in the affliction of the body*; or, as others have translated, *not sparing the body, not in any honor unto the satisfying of the flesh*[^92]. Why, you say, does he say these things have a reason of wisdom, which he so reproaches?

**2. 30.** I will say what you too can observe in the Scriptures, that often wisdom is set even in the seats of this world, which he calls more expressly the wisdom of this world. Nor let it move you that here he did not add "of the world." For neither in another place, when he says, *Where is the wise man? Where is the scribe?*[^93], did he add "of this world," so as to say, Where is the wise man of this world: and yet this is understood. So too this "reason of wisdom." For nothing is said by them in superstitious observances of this kind where some reason of wisdom of the elements and natures of the things of this world does not seem to be rendered. For when he says, *Beware lest anyone deceive you through philosophy*[^94], he does not say "of this world"; and what is "philosophy" in Latin, but the study of wisdom? They are, then, he says, things "having indeed a reason of wisdom," that is, concerning which some reason is rendered according to the elements of this world, and the principalities and powers. "In observance and humility of heart": for these things effect this, that by the fault of superstition the heart is humbled. "Not sparing the body": while it is cheated of these foods which it is compelled to abstain from. "Not in any honor unto the satisfying of the flesh": not that the flesh is more honorably satisfied with this food than with that, since it does not pertain to its need except to be refreshed and sustained by any food suited to health.

**3. 31.** From the Gospel your inquiry is wont to move many: how the Lord after the resurrection, since he rose in the same body, both was not recognized and was recognized by certain people who knew him, of both sexes. Here it is first wont to be asked whether something was done in his body, or rather in their eyes, by which he could not be recognized. For when it is read, *Their eyes were held that they should not recognize him*[^95], some impediment to recognizing seems to have been made in the eyes of those seeing; but when it is openly said elsewhere, *He appeared to them in another form*[^96], surely in that body, of which there was another form, something was done by which they were held by an impediment, that is, the eyes of those people suffered a delay in recognizing. But I wonder: since there are two things in a body by which the appearance of each is recognized, lineaments and color, why does it move no one that before the resurrection, that on the mountain he was so transfigured that even his face became most splendid like the sun[^97], he could change the color of his body even unto so great an excellence of brightness and light; and after the resurrection it moves some that the lineaments were changed, so that he could not be recognized, and again, by the same faculty of power, just as then he recalled the former color, so after the resurrection he recalled the former lineaments. For those three disciples too, before whose eyes he was transfigured on the mountain, would not have recognized him, if such a one had come to them from elsewhere: but because they were with him, they most certainly held that it was he. But the body itself was that in which he rose. What is this to the matter? For that was surely the same body in which he was transfigured on the mountain; and it was the same as the young man's in which he was born: and yet, if anyone had suddenly seen him as a young man who had known him only as a little infant, he would surely not recognize him. Or can the power of God not quickly change lineaments, which a man's age can do through the delays of years?

**3. 32.** But as for what he said to Mary, *Touch me not, for I have not yet ascended to the Father*[^98], you may know that I have understood nothing else than you. For he wished thus to signify that he requires a spiritual touch, that is, the approach of faith, by which he is believed to be exalted with the Father. And that he was recognized by those two in the breaking of bread[^99], that it is the sacrament which gathers us unto recognition of him, no one ought to doubt.

**3. 33.** Concerning the words of Simeon, where he says to the Virgin mother of the Lord, *And a sword shall pierce through your own soul*[^100], in another letter, a copy of which I have also now sent, I said what seems to me; which also seemed so to you among other things. And as for what he added, *That the thoughts of many hearts may be laid bare*[^101], this I think is to be understood thus: that through the Lord's passion both the snares of the Jews and the weakness of the disciples lay open. It is credible, then, that by the name of "sword" tribulation was signified, by which the mother's soul was wounded with the feeling of grief. He himself was the sword in the mouth of the persecutors, of whom in the Psalm it is said: *And a sword is in their mouth*[^102]. For they were the sons of men, *whose teeth are weapons and arrows, and their tongue a sharp sword*[^103]. For the iron too which pierced the soul of Joseph seems to me to be set for hard tribulation: for it is openly said: *Iron pierced his soul, until his word came*[^104]; that is, he was for so long in hard tribulation, until what he foretold came to pass. For hence he was held great, and freed from that tribulation. But lest it be attributed to human wisdom that his word came, that is, that what he foretold came to pass, holy Scripture, after its manner, gives glory to God for it, and immediately subjoins: *The word of the Lord set him aflame*[^105].

**3. 34.** As I could, I have not failed your questions, aided by your prayers, and by your very disputations which you sent. For when by questioning you dispute, you both seek keenly and teach humbly. And it is useful that concerning the obscurities of the divine Scriptures, which God willed to be for the sake of our exercise, many views be found, when one thing seems so to one and another to another, which yet all agree with sound faith and teaching. You will surely pardon my pen, which is much in haste, that I might meet the carrier when already set on board ship. But our son Paulinus, most sweet in the love of Christ, I rather greet again in this letter, and exhort in a few words, as one in haste, that he give thanks, as much as he can, to the mercy of the Lord, who, knowing how to give help out of tribulation, sent him in a most turbulent storm into the harbor, through which you passed by a more tranquil sea, trusting that marine tranquillity not at all, and who gave you to receive and nourish his beginnings; and that *all his bones may say: Lord, who is like you?*[^106] For with no richer fruit does he read or hear me teaching and discoursing, or kindling with whatever exhortations, than he beholds you living. My fellow servants who are with me greet again your holy and most sincere Kindness. But our fellow deacon Peregrinus, since he set out from me with our holy brother Urbanus, when he went to take up the burden of the episcopate, has not yet returned to Hippo: yet we learn that they are safe in the name of Christ, both their letters and report announcing it. Our fellow presbyter Paulinus, and all who enjoy your presence in the Lord, we greet with brotherly affection.
