# Letter 6

Nebridius to Augustine

**1.** *It pleases me greatly to keep your letters as I keep my own eyes. For they are great, not in size, but in their matter, and they contain great proofs of great things. They will sound to me of Christ, of Plato, of Plotinus. They will therefore be both sweet for me to hear because of their eloquence, easy to read because of their brevity, and wholesome to understand because of their wisdom. So you will take care to teach me what has seemed holy and good to your mind. And you will reply to this letter when you have argued something more subtly about imagination and memory. For it seems to me that, although not every imagination is accompanied by memory, yet no memory can exist without imagination. But you say: What of when we remember that we have understood or thought something? Against this I answer and say that this has come about because, when we understood or thought something corporeal and temporal, we generated what pertains to imagination: for either we joined to our understanding and thoughts words, which words do not exist without time and pertain to sense or imagination; or our understanding underwent in thought something of such a kind as could make a memory in the imaginative mind. This I have said, as I usually do, without due consideration and confusedly: you will examine it and, rejecting what is false, will set down the truth in writing.*

**2.** *Hear another point: why, I ask you, do we not say that the imagination has all its images from itself rather than from sense? For just as the intellectual mind is prompted by sense to see its own intelligible objects rather than receiving anything from it, so too the imaginative mind may be prompted by sense to contemplate its own images rather than taking anything from it. For perhaps it happens from this that it can behold those things which sense does not see—which is a sign that it has all its images in itself and from itself. On this matter too you will reply with what you think.*
