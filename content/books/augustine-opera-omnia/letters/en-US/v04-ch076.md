# Letter 12\*

A MEMORANDUM TO THE HOLY LORD, AND TO ME FOREVER TO BE REVERED, AND VENERABLE FATHER AUGUSTINE, FROM CONSENTIUS

**1.** *The books of the* Confessions *and very many others I obtained about twelve years ago, rather from a damnable desire of possessing them than from a good and praiseworthy zeal for learning; these even now, oppressed by an incredible stupor, I have possessed as though sealed up; which, having very lately begun to read, I find certain things there said most fully resolved, over the inquiry of which, seething, I labored, and, recognizing many forms of my thoughts as though a picture displayed them, I begin to perceive, in learning also the other things which I desire to know, that not the teacher is lacking to me, but I to the teacher. Finally, to confess simply in the sight of the Lord, about four years ago, that is, before I thought of seeking the sight of Your Holiness, I had read two or three leaves, not more, from the first book of the* Confessions*; but, just as Your Paternity is wont to compare the minds of all vain men to bleary eyes, struck by the troublesome splendor of your sentences—because I found there nothing soft or tender that would soothe the wounds of my eyes—I at once ran back to the most pleasant darkness of my ignorance, and not only those, but also the other books I avoided more warily than viper's blood*[^1].

**2.** *For I was of so great and so deadly a fastidiousness, that—the canonical Books alone excepted, which fame had made venerable to me—my insane stomach nauseated at the letters of all treatise-writers. Lactantius alone pleased me, on account of his plain and well-composed manner of speaking; whom nevertheless, by a most ardent love of sloth, once read, I had cast aside with the rest, and, since by an inestimable fastidiousness of reading the health of my soul was pressed as by a lethargic disease, scarcely through so many years did I run through the canonical Scriptures once or twice most slothfully. And yet, although sloth possessed me utterly, both in mind and body, if, as is wont, any question of divine matters had arisen among the servants of Christ in my presence, I was one of those whom the apostolic word depicted, wishing to be a teacher of the law and understanding neither what I was saying nor about what I was affirming*[^2]. But whatever seemed more just to me I labored to defend with empty words.

**3.** *Hence it also came about that I was moved against the priest Leontius of holy memory, not by an affection of doctrine but of natural justice, and against that question which was discussed by the judgment of Your Holiness—in which he seemed to me to think not rightly—I contended most pugnaciously, and soon, kindled by rivalry of him, and admiring the fame of Your Holiness, and seething with the love of coming to know the truth, I wrote those things in which many were condemned, few praised; and yet, by a divine judgment, as I think, all things were done—both that I should write, and that I should write reprehensibly.*

**4.** *For such a thought then impelled me to writing, that—the solitude of the Balearic Islands, in which to find not, I will not say a learned man, but even a faithful Christian, is most rare, being set before my eyes—I utterly rejected, not the love of doctrine, which, knowing it to be most laborious, [I shunned], but I desired to arrive at a simple knowledge of the truth of the Catholic faith, which it is deadly to be ignorant of, utterly without the zeal of reading or the work of discerning. Which perhaps could have been done, if I had either myself heard something of those questions about which I especially doubted disputed by the wise, or had found those who had heard them; I would perhaps have accepted at least the small labor of reading, if anyone had handed me books which properly resolved the knots by which my thought was specially bound; for, on account of the uncertain hope of finding, I refused to take up the certain labor of inquiring.*

**5.** *Since, then, with the writings of all the wise utterly renounced, I was turning over in my mind so great and so arduous questions, and it is not possible even now to find in the islands anyone (I will not say who would teach great things and clear up cloudy ones, but even who would understand small things and consider plain ones), my mind was so perversely corrupted by the plague of diseases, that, through too great a fear of reading in me, too great an audacity of writing was begotten. So it came about that, counseling myself perversely, I preferred to write two ridiculous volumes than to scrutinize the many admirable things of many men. It seemed good, accordingly, that I should bring forth all my thoughts in writing, as in an assembly of two, so that the most insane quarrel of my heart, comprehended in a literary office, might be set before your gaze and pacified by the clemency of your teaching. But this could be done in no other way than by letters.*

**6.** *For I confess, if I had believed that I could enjoy the bodily sight of your Paternity in those bodily eyes of mine in that way, I would surely never have labored in composing such long ditties. But because I had come to these islands with such an intention or vow, that here all the time of my age might be worn away, inglorious, in a leisure and slothfulness which even now I not slothfully covet, the inmost languor of my fevers could not be shown to you, the physician, unless the offices of words were committed to letters. But if in those writings of mine nothing at all had been found that pleased, or nothing that displeased, by no means would shame have permitted me to hasten to your sight. For I am of so weak and so timid a nature that I blush more, if it is right to believe it, at praises than at reproaches.*

**7.** *But it was so done by the dispensation and judgment of Christ, that your Paternity directed letters so wonderfully tempered, and through such bearers. For unless my Lord's worshipers and your devotees, the deacons Maximianus and Caprarius, had pressed me with the violent goads of love, never would our slothfulness—allured though it was by the devout and most sweet counsels of your writings—have preferred even for a brief time a lukewarm desire of learning to the most flaming loves of being at leisure. For if that young man of Terence, who was moved against the harlot who had shut out her lover, could not, even for a brief time—even when he was compelled by insults—be removed from his beloved and offended, so that with the highest intention of virtue he seemed to himself to do something memorable, if for only three days he lacked the harlot whom he had offended*[^3]: how much more should I, in whose marrow a greater fire of leisure perhaps burns—especially never offended by it, always sweetly invited—not have wished to lack, even for a few days, the pleasure of a prepared solitude, except that that Lord and charioteer who rules the manifold chariot of ten thousand was turning, as has been said, by the hidden reins of our minds, the mouths of our minds[^4]?

**8.** *I came, then, to you—nay rather, in the halter of the Lord, when I struggled, I was dragged*[^5]*; with many conversations you laid bare my mind, with many disputations you handled the hidden wounds of my heart. You saw my soul full of illusions, and you wished to cut away the phantasms of my presumptions like rottenness, applying, as far as was in you, both the sharpest instruments of arguments and the fiery cauteries of admonitions and the most soothing poultices of exhortations. But when, compelling me to the zeal of reading, you frequently inquired whether I had read the books which you yourself had handed to me to read, like sick people who are compelled by physicians to take food in their loathing, I was forced by shame to taste a very little of your letters.*

**9.** *And now the uselessness of my fasting had begun to displease me, when suddenly it happened that I was bound by too great an affection to the mind of a certain holy and venerable man, by whose familiarity constrained, when I weighed all his movements in my mind, I noticed and more vehemently loved my own vice in him. For I congratulated myself that I had found someone of the highest authority and merit, a fellow-lover, who sighed with the loves of the same leisure; but my conscience was being flattered with vain joys, that, comparing myself, an evil tree, to that fruitful wood, I rejoiced that I had found in him something like my own sterility. For I gloried in the likeness of the leaves, not seeing with how great fruits of virtues that good tree was bent down. Leaning back, then, upon that man with all the powers of my weakness, I drove off all the medicines you applied, I rejected all the cups you offered; for that seemed to me most sweet, that, anointed with an alluring sweetness above all things, that I might always hear my desire praised by the mouth of a most holy friend, and that the words of that venerable man should re-echo what the execration of my heart was turning over.*

**10.** *For with such conversation we scratched each other's itch in turn, that we affirmed nothing to be more useless than the zeal of doctrine and nothing more perilous, saying: what is so useless as that the desire of so great a vain glory should solicit a Christian man, that, leavened with the vices of the Pharisees, he should most boastfully covet to be called Rabbi by men*[^6], and, although he himself is perhaps blinded in the light of understanding, yet should try to draw all, without doubt about to fall into the pit[^7]; especially since there are most lofty things which ought not to be inquired into at all, and Scripture announces that the searcher of hedges is to be bitten by a serpent[^8]?

**11.** *But what is more perilous than that a man, although he could arrive at life without so great a circuit of useless doctrine, yet, incited by the insane goads of unlawful curiosity, should take up the winding of a long and arduous journey, from which no one as yet has escaped without a wound? For what else did Origen, the greatest of all treatise-writers, obtain by the power of unwearied labor, except that he passed over, by the vice of long scrutiny, the salvation of the word which is near and dwells in our heart and mouth, and there befell him what befell the prudent of grace, who, the more zealously and laboriously they pursued wisdom, the further they withdrew from it? Finally, if the desire of doctrine had not driven him to scrutinize the unlawful things in which he fell, he would surely rather have merited the glory of martyrdom; but because he had fallen into the pit of insane doctrine while he wandered too much, of necessity it came about that the reward of suffering, which the unlearned and the little one coveted, the most learned old man refused. Who, then, will cling to the zeal of doctrine more bravely, more skillfully, more attentively, more prudently, more cautiously than Origen, or who—as though vain curiosity by laborious scrutiny [might] find [something]—will wisely escape the death which Origen unwisely incurred?*

**12.** *Let us review in order the other treatise-writers, however great, however Catholic! Yet it is difficult that we should not note some stains of errors in their otherwise most beautiful body. For even if we should say that Bishop Augustine writes irreprehensible things, yet we do not know what posterity is going to judge of his volumes; for no one reprehended all the authors of heretical perversity—and especially Origen—while living, who, it is not doubtful, was condemned after two hundred years or more. With such embraces of arguments did our sloth bind us to itself, with the poultices of such words did it suggest fuel to the fires of our love.*

**13.** *Amid these things I read indeed unwillingly what I had received, and, like that aforesaid comic young man, while I embraced my beloved in my heart, yet, the journey of reading being seized, I went out, mad, with alternating steps; and when I passed through the fields, full of fruits, of the holy father, and my mad mind was blinded to the varied fruit of your doctrine, it panted only for the ardor of sloth, until, struck back by the great powers of my love, I turned my steps, not sluggishly, to my negligence, and with so swift a course hastened to embrace leisure the more strongly, that I thought even of seeking it in the East, the labor of a long journey being taken up. For this my most loving fellow-lover had persuaded me; but when we felt the strong hand of the Lord resisting our vows and attempts, I was, I know not how, snatched from the purpose of the journey seized and recalled to my own affairs, holding this in mind and purpose, that I should only run through the canonical books without labor, but my own writings, if it could be done, I should renounce even in familiar letters; which indeed, having diligently carried out for no long time, I so ran through the canon that the affection of the sloth I cultivated was never injured. But just as it greatly helps to be set apart a little from the things we love, that, with the powers of desire renewed, they may be sought more ardently, so I interpolated the pleasure of leisure with the zeal of a most brief and most rare reading. At the same time it happened that certain marvels were wrought among us by the command of the Lord. When the blessed bishop, the brother of Your Paternity, Bishop Severus, had reported these to me with the rest who had been present, he burst into my purpose with the highest powers of love, and, that he himself might write a letter containing the order of the deed, he borrowed from me only the words. From then on a greater transgression of the law which I had written for myself grew, and it was decided that—lest the slowness of our memory be helped by a recent reading of the canon, however slow and most lukewarm—I should produce for our leader some arms against the Jews, by whose battles we were being pressed, yet on this condition, that the name of the office be utterly kept silent.*

**14.** *When I was dictating these things, and thinking to lay open to you both all the perversity of our will, and the variety of our thoughts, and the incursion of sins, and the lot of chances, and the order of life, and the sloth of reading, and the temerity of writing, suddenly the bearer, vehemently insisting, extorted everything, that it might be at once cut short. Accordingly, breaking off the very long end of my discourse by a necessary taciturnity, I bind everything together with the knots of brief words, indicating that I had wished to declare war upon my sloth, but, taking up rash arms against a most languid mistress, I accomplished only this, that I, that enervated Hercules, was more grievously and more basely subjected to my Omphale*[^9]. Finally, as though *my head softened by the sandal*[^10], I bowed to her alluring blows, so that, a contumacious servant, I might serve the more miserably and disgracefully, the more dread lays the conquered low before proud conquerors.

**15.** *But the custom of my insanity has compelled me to write very many things, I know not what; which, if it were possible, I would have wished all to come to the examination of your Paternity. But now, the rest being kept with me, I have directed twelve chapters against the Jews and one letter only, which I lately sent to your most blessed brother Bishop Patroclus; in which, indeed, when your venerable Paternity has come to know both his views and his words, may you stretch forth to your little one—following his father with unequal steps—the most devout right hand of your kindness and favor, and deign to carry on the strongest shoulders of your prayers a weak infancy, lest it utterly slip away. But especially, if there had been afforded me the occasion of such a bearer as could assume some confidence with you of conversing and conferring about everything, I would have wished to transmit, even unfinished, those things which I dare to write against the questions of Pelagius.*

**16.** *For last year there came to us that letter of Zosimus of holy memory, bishop of the city of Rome, in which, the questions of Pelagius and Caelestius being subjoined, their deadly poison is shown, that it may be avoided. These having been read—although hindered by many anxieties of the world—yet, kindled with the usual fury for writing, we labor to forge now almost a fourth book; but yet, steadfastly retaining the law of negligence, since we keep at home almost all that has been written against Pelagius, either sealed up [or unread]. We have added also a most powerful argument, on which our sloth chiefly relies, that we persuade ourselves of the most absurd thing, that we ought not to read all those things before we ourselves write anything; with so incredible a perversity of judgment is there a fear in me lest perhaps the tortuous impulse of my sense be set straight. But if writers can be believed, who, like fathers, embrace whatever they have begotten—however forced and deformed—yet with the highest sweetness, I seem to myself to bring forth an Achilles, about to produce a Thersites to the rest*[^11]. Mindful of me, may you flourish forever in Christ, holy and most blessed Father.
