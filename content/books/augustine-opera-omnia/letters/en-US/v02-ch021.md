# Letter 120

To his most beloved brother, and to be honored in the bowels of Christ, Consentius, Augustine sends greetings in the Lord.

**1. 1.** I asked you to come to us for this reason, that in your books I was much delighted by your talent. Accordingly I wished that you should read certain little works of ours, which I judged necessary for you, placed not far from us, but rather with us, so that those things which you perhaps understood too little you might, being present, not difficultly ask about, and from our conversation and mutual colloquy—as much as the Lord granted both us to bring forth and you to grasp—you yourself might know what was to be emended in your books, you yourself might emend it. For you are of such ability that you can unfold the things you have thought; and further of such probity and humility that you deserve to think true things. And now I am of the same opinion, which ought not to displease you either; whence I lately admonished you that, in those things elaborated by us which you read with you, you make signs at those places which move you, and come to me with them, and ask about each. I do not yet exhort you to do what you have done. For you would rightly be ashamed, and it would irk you to do this, if you had wished it even once and had found me difficult. I had also said—when I had heard from you that you were wearied by most faulty manuscripts—that you should read in ours, which you could find more emended than the rest.

**1. 2.** But as for what you ask, that I discuss the question of the Trinity—that is, of the unity of the Godhead and the distinction of persons—cautiously and prudently, so that the serenity of my teaching and talent, as you say, may so wipe away the mist of your mind that what you now cannot think of, declared by the light of my understanding, you may in some way be able to see with the eyes: see first whether this petition agrees with your earlier definition. For earlier in the same letter in which you ask this, you say that you have determined with yourself that the truth ought to be perceived from faith rather than from reason. "For if the faith of the holy Church," you say, "were apprehended from the reasoning of disputation, and not from the piety of believing, no one but philosophers and orators would possess blessedness. But because it pleased God," you say, "who chose the weak things of this world to confound the strong, to make believers safe through the foolishness of preaching, not so much is reason to be sought as the authority of the saints is to be followed." See, then, according to these words of yours, whether you ought not rather—especially in this matter, in which our faith chiefly consists—to follow the authority of the saints alone, and not to seek of me the reason of that understanding. For when I shall have begun to introduce you in some way into the understanding of so great a secret (which, unless God help within, I shall not at all be able to do), I shall do nothing else, by discoursing, than render a reason as I can: which if you not unreasonably demand of me, or of any teacher, that you may understand what you believe, correct your definition—not so as to reject faith, but so that the things you already hold by the firmness of faith you may also behold by the light of reason.

**1. 3.** For far be it that God should hate in us that in which he created us more excellent than the rest of living beings. Far be it, I say, that we should believe in such a way as not to receive or seek a reason; since we could not even believe, unless we had rational souls. That, therefore, in certain matters pertaining to saving doctrine, which we cannot yet perceive by reason but at some time shall be able, faith should precede reason, by which the heart is cleansed so as to receive and bear the light of great reason—this surely is of reason. And therefore it was reasonably said through the prophet, *Unless you believe, you will not understand*[^1]. Where without doubt he distinguished these two, and gave counsel by which we should first believe, that we may be able to understand what we believe. Accordingly, that faith should precede reason was reasonably commanded. For if this precept is not reasonable, then it is unreasonable: far be it. If, then, it is reasonable that, toward certain great things which cannot yet be grasped, faith should precede reason, without doubt the small reason which persuades this also precedes faith.

**1. 4.** Therefore the apostle Peter admonishes that we ought to be ready to answer everyone who asks of us a reason for our faith and hope: since, if an unbeliever asks of me a reason for my faith and hope, and I see that before he believes he cannot grasp it, I render him this very reason in which, if it can be, he may see how preposterously he asks, before faith, a reason for those things which he cannot grasp. But if one already faithful asks a reason, that he may understand what he believes, his capacity is to be regarded, so that, a reason being rendered according to it, he may take of his faith as much understanding as he can: greater, if he grasps more; lesser, if less: provided, however, that until he comes to the fullness and perfection of knowledge, he not withdraw from the way of faith. Hence it is that the Apostle says, *And yet, if you think anything otherwise, God will reveal this too to you; nevertheless, in that which we have attained, let us walk*[^2]. If, then, we are faithful, we have come to the way of faith, which if we do not abandon, we shall without doubt come not only to so great an understanding of incorporeal and unchangeable things as cannot in this life be grasped by all, but even to the summit of contemplation, which the Apostle calls *face to face*[^3]. For some even of the least, yet walking most perseveringly in the way of faith, come to that most blessed contemplation: but some, knowing in some way what the invisible, unchangeable, incorporeal nature is, yet refusing to hold the way which leads to the dwelling of so great a blessedness—because it seems foolishness to them, which is Christ crucified—cannot come to the inner sanctuary of that very rest, by whose light, as if radiating from afar, their mind is already struck.

**1. 5.** But there are certain things which, when we have heard them, we do not give them faith, and, a reason being rendered to us, we know them to be true which we were not able to believe. And all the miracles of God are for this reason not believed by unbelievers, because their reason is not seen. And in truth there are things of which a reason cannot be rendered—yet there is one: for what is there in the nature of things that God has made unreasonably? But of certain of his marvelous works it is even expedient that the reason be hidden for a while, lest, among minds languid with disdain, they grow cheap by the knowledge of that same reason. For there are, and many there are, who are held more by the admiration of things than by the knowledge of causes, where marvels cease to be marvelous, and there is need that they be roused to faith in invisible things by visible marvels, so that, purified by love, they may come where, by familiarity with truth, they cease to marvel. For in theaters too men marvel at a ropewalker, are delighted by musicians: in the one the difficulty astonishes; in the others the pleasantness holds and feeds them.

**1. 6.** I have said these things to exhort your faith to the love of understanding, to which true reason leads, and for which faith prepares the mind. For that reason which persuaded that, in that Trinity which is God, the Son is not coeternal with the Father, or is of another substance, and in some part dissimilar, and in that way the Holy Spirit inferior; and likewise that which persuaded that the Father and the Son are of one and the same substance, but the Holy Spirit of another—must be said to be guarded against and detested, not because it is reason, but because it is false reason. For if it were true reason, it surely would not have erred. Wherefore, just as you ought not to avoid all speech because there is also false speech, so you ought not to avoid all reason because there is also false reason. This I would say also of wisdom. For wisdom is not to be avoided because there is also a false wisdom, to which Christ crucified is foolishness, who is the Power of God and the Wisdom of God: and therefore through this foolishness of preaching it pleased God to make believers safe, since what is foolish of God is wiser than men. This could not be persuaded to certain of the philosophers and orators, who follow not the true way but one resembling the true, and in it deceive themselves and others; but to certain of them it could. And to those to whom it could, Christ crucified is neither a scandal nor foolishness; for they are among those to whom, called Jews and Greeks, he is the Power of God and the Wisdom of God. In which way—that is, in the faith of which Christ crucified—those who were able through the grace of God to comprehend its rightness, even if they were called philosophers or orators, surely confessed with humble piety that fishermen had gone before them far more excellently in it, not only by the most firm strength of believing, but even by the most certain truth of understanding. For when they had learned that the foolish and weak things of the world were chosen for this, that the strong and wise things might be confounded, and had recognized that they themselves were fallaciously wise and weakly prevailed; confounded wholesomely, they were made foolish and weak, so that through the foolish and weak thing of God, which is wiser and stronger than men, they might, among the chosen foolish and weak things, become truly wise and effectively strong.

**2. 7.** But before what, if not before most true reason, does faithful piety blush, so that we should not doubt to overturn a certain idolatry which the weakness of human thought tries to establish in our heart from the habit of visible things; nor dare to believe that the Trinity, which we worship as invisible and incorporeal and unchangeable, is, as it were, three living masses, however great and beautiful, yet bounded by the proper spaces of their own places, and cohering to one another in their places by contiguous nearness—whether one of them so set in the middle as to part the two joined to it on its single sides, or each in the manner of a triangle touching the other two, so that none is separated from any; and that of those three so great and such persons, bounded—though in a very large mass—from above and below and round about, there is one divinity, a kind of fourth thing, not such as is any one of them, but common to all, as it were the numen of all, and whole in all and in each, through which one divinity that same Trinity is called one God; and that its three persons are nowhere but in the heavens, but that that divinity is nowhere not, but everywhere present: and through this it is rightly said indeed that God is both in heaven and on earth, on account of that divinity which is everywhere and common to the three; but not rightly said that the Father is on earth, or the Son, or the Holy Spirit, since for this Trinity there is no seat but in heaven. This composition of carnal thought and empty figment, where true reason begins to shake it, with him helping and illuminating within who does not wish to dwell with such idols in our heart, we so hasten to feign such things and in a manner shake them out of our faith that we do not allow even any dust of such phantasms to remain there.

**2. 8.** Wherefore, unless the reason of disputation—by which, admonished from without, with truth itself shining within, we perceive these things to be false—had been preceded in our heart by faith, which would clothe us with piety, would we not hear in vain the things that are true? And through this, since faith did what pertained to it, therefore reason following found something of the things it was inquiring. To false reason, then, not only is true reason to be preferred—by which we understand what we believe—but even faith itself, of things not yet understood, is without doubt to be preferred. For it is better to believe what is true, though not yet seen, than to suppose you see the true which is false. For faith has its own eyes, by which in a manner it sees that to be true which it does not yet see, and by which it most certainly sees that it does not yet see what it believes. Moreover, he who by true reason now understands what he only believed is surely to be preferred to him who still desires to understand what he believes; but if he does not even desire it, and thinks that the things which are to be understood are only to be believed, he is ignorant of what use faith is to him: for devout faith does not wish to be without hope and without love. So, then, the faithful man ought to believe what he does not yet see in such a way that he both hopes for and loves the vision.

**2. 9.** And of past visible things, which have passed away temporally, there is faith alone, since they are not hoped to be still seen, but are believed to have happened and passed; as is that, that *Christ died once for our sins and rose again, and dies no more, and death will no longer have dominion over him*[^4]. But those things which are not yet, but are future, like the resurrection of our spiritual bodies, are so believed that they are also hoped to be seen; but they can in no way be shown now. But those things which are so that they neither pass away nor are future, but remain eternal, are partly invisible, like justice, like wisdom; partly visible, like Christ's body, now immortal: but invisible things are beheld when understood, and through this they too are in a manner congruous to themselves seen; and when they are seen, they are much more certain than those things which the sense of the body reaches, but they are called invisible because they can in no way be seen with these mortal eyes. But those things which are visible and permanent can, if they be shown, be beheld even with these mortal eyes; as the Lord showed himself to the Disciples after the resurrection, as also after the ascension to the apostle Paul and to the deacon Stephen.

**2. 10.** Accordingly, these visible and permanent things we so believe that, even if they are not demonstrated, we hope that we shall at some time see them; nor do we try to comprehend them by reason or understanding, except so that, because they are visible, we may think of them more distinctly from invisible things: and when in thought we imagine of what sort they are, we surely know well enough that they are not known to us. For I think of Antioch, unknown to me, but not as of Carthage, which is known. For that vision my thought feigns; this it recalls; yet I in no way doubt either what I believed about the one from many witnesses, or what I believed about the other from my own sight. But justice and wisdom and whatever is of this kind we do not imagine in one way and behold in another; but these invisible things, understood by the simple intention of mind and reason, we behold without any corporeal forms and masses, without any lineaments and figures of members, without any local places, whether finite or infinite spaces. And that very light by which we discern all these things—in which it sufficiently appears to us what we believe unknown, what we hold as known, what form of body we recall, what we feign in thought, what the sense of the body reaches, what the mind imagines as like a body, what the understanding contemplates as certain and most unlike all bodies—this light, then, where all these things are adjudicated, is not, like the brightness of this sun and of any corporeal light, diffused round about through local spaces, and does not illuminate our mind as with a visible brightness, but shines invisibly and ineffably, and yet intelligibly, and is as certain to us as it makes certain to us the things which according to it we all behold.

**2. 11.** Since, then, there are three kinds of things that are seen—one of corporeal things, like this sky and this earth, and whatever the corporeal sense in them perceives and touches; a second like corporeal things, like those which, thought by the spirit, we imagine, or, recalled or forgotten, we behold as bodies, whence are also the visions which, whether in sleep or in some excess of mind, are presented to us with these, as it were, local quantities; a third distinct from both, which is neither a body nor has any likeness of a body, like wisdom, which is beheld when understood by the mind, and in whose light all these things are truly judged—in which of these kinds is that Trinity, which we wish to know, to be believed to be? Surely either in some one, or in none. If in some one, surely in that which is more excellent than the other two, like wisdom; but if its gift in us, which is called our wisdom, is less than that highest and unchangeable, which is called the Wisdom of God, I think we ought not to think the giver lower than his gift: but if some splendor of it is in us, however much of it we can grasp through a glass and in a riddle, we ought to set it apart both from all bodies and from all likenesses of bodies.

**2. 12.** But if this Trinity is to be thought to be in none of these kinds, and is so invisible that it is not even seen by the mind, much less ought we to hold of it an opinion of this kind, that we believe it to be like corporeal things or the images of corporeal things. For it surpasses bodies not by the beauty of mass or by magnitude, but by the dissimilarity and disparity of nature: and if it is distinct from comparison with the goods of our mind, such as are wisdom, justice, love, chastity, and other such things, which surely we do not weigh by the mass of a body, nor figure in thought their corporeal forms, but when we rightly understand them, we see them without any corpulence or likeness of corpulence in the light of the mind—how much more distinct is it from comparison with all corporeal qualities and quantities? Yet that it is not altogether foreign to our understanding, the Apostle is witness, where he says, *For his invisible things, from the creation of the world, are beheld, being understood through the things that have been made; his everlasting power also and divinity*[^5]. And through this, since the same Trinity made both body and soul, it is without doubt more excellent than both. The soul therefore, considered—especially the human and rational and intellectual, which was made to its image—if it does not overcome our thoughts and understandings, but we are able to apprehend by mind and understanding that which it has as its chief part, that is, the very mind and understanding, it will perhaps not be absurd that we should meditate, with his help, to lift it up to understanding even its Creator. But if it fails in itself and succumbs to itself, let it be content with devout faith while it journeys away from the Lord, until what is promised be done in man by him who is able, as the Apostle says, to do above what we ask or understand.

**3. 13.** Since these things are so, meanwhile I wish that you read the many things we have already written pertaining to this question; also those which we have in hand, and, on account of the magnitude of so difficult a question, cannot yet unfold. But for now hold by unshaken faith that the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit are the Trinity, and yet one God; not that there is, as it were, a common fourth divinity of them, but that the Trinity itself is ineffably inseparable, and that the Father alone begot the Son, and the Son alone was begotten by the Father, but the Holy Spirit is the Spirit of both the Father and the Son. And whatever corporeal likeness occurs to you when you think of these things, drive off, refuse, deny, spit out, cast away, flee. For it is no small beginning of the knowledge of God if, before we can know what he is, we begin already to know what he is not. But love understanding greatly; because even the holy Scriptures themselves, which persuade faith before the understanding of great matters, cannot be useful to you unless you understand them rightly. For all heretics who receive them in authority seem to themselves to follow them, when they rather follow their own errors; and through this they are heretics, not because they despise them, but because they do not understand them.

**3. 14.** But you, dearest one, pray strongly and faithfully that the Lord give you understanding, and so the diligence of a preceptor or teacher which he applies from without may be fruitful; since *neither he who plants is anything, nor he who waters, but God who gives the increase*[^6]: to whom we say, *Our Father who are in heaven*[^7]; not because he is there and is not here, who by incorporeal presence is everywhere whole, but because he is said to dwell in those to whose piety he is present, and these are most of all in the heavens, where our conversation too is, if our mouth answers truly that we have our heart on high. For even if we have taken carnally what is written, *Heaven is my seat, and the earth is my footstool*[^8], we ought to believe him to be both there and here: although not the whole there, because the feet would be here; nor the whole here, because the upper parts of the body would be there. Which carnal thought that which is written of him can again shake out of us: *Who has measured the heaven with the span, and the earth with the fist*[^9]. For who would sit in the space of his span, or set his feet in so great a place as his fist grasps? Unless perhaps vain flesh advances so far that it is too little for it to attribute human members to the substance of God, if it does not even feign monstrous ones, where the span is broader than the loins, and the fist than both palms joined. But these things are said so that, when the things we hear carnally do not agree with one another, we may, admonished by them, think ineffably of spiritual things.

**3. 15.** Whence even if we think of the body of the Lord, which, raised from the tomb, he lifted into heaven, only in human appearance and members, yet it is not to be thought that he so sits at the right hand of the Father that the Father seems to him to sit at the left. For in that blessedness which surpasses all human understanding there is only a right hand, and the same right hand is the name of that same blessedness. Accordingly, neither is that which he said to Mary after his resurrection, *Do not touch me, for I have not yet ascended to my Father*[^10], to be taken so absurdly that we should suppose he wished, when he had ascended, to be touched by women, who offered himself to be touched by men before he ascended. But surely to Mary—in whom the Church is figured—when he said that, he wished it to be understood that he had then ascended to the Father when she knew him to be equal to the Father; and by such faith she wholesomely touched him, lest, if she believed him to be only that which had appeared in the flesh, she should not touch him well. So Photinus the heretic touched him, who believed Christ to be a man only.

**3. 16.** And if perhaps something else can be more fitly and better understood in these words of the Lord, yet the opinion by which it is supposed that the substance of the Father is in heaven—according to which the Father is one person in the Trinity—but the divinity not in heaven only, but everywhere: as though the Father were one thing, his divinity (which is common to him with the Son and the Holy Spirit) another; so that the Trinity itself were as if in corporeal places and as if corporeal, but the one divinity of the three persons were present everywhere, and it alone, as if incorporeal, were everywhere whole—this is without doubt to be spat out. For if it were their quality (which God forbid, that in the Father or the Son or the Holy Spirit quality be one thing, substance another)—yet if it could be their quality, surely it could not be more anywhere than in its own substance: but if it is a substance, and is other than they themselves are, it is another substance; which is nonetheless believed most falsely.

**3. 17.** But if perhaps you understand too little what difference there is between substance and quality, this at least you notice more easily, that the divinity of the Trinity too (which is supposed to be other than the Trinity itself, and on account of which it is said that there are not three gods, but one God, since it is the one common to the three) either is a substance, or is not a substance. If it is a substance, and is other than the Father, or the Son, or the Holy Spirit, or the Trinity itself together: it is without doubt another substance: but this truth refutes and spits out. But if this divinity is not a substance, and it is itself God, because it is everywhere whole, not that Trinity; then God is not a substance: who, a Catholic, would say this? Likewise, if this divinity is not a substance, and according to it the Trinity is one God, because this is one in the three; the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit ought not to have been said to be of one substance, but of one divinity which is not a substance. But you recognize, in the Catholic faith, that this is true, this is confirmed, that the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit, therefore, though they are a Trinity, are one God; because they are inseparably of one and the same substance, or (if this is better said) essence. For some of our people, and especially the Greeks, have said that the Trinity which is God is rather an essence than one substance; thinking, or understanding, that there is some difference between these two names: whereof there is no need to dispute now; for even if we should call that divinity, which is judged to be other than the Trinity itself, not a substance but an essence, the same falsehood will follow. For if it is other than the Trinity itself, there will be another essence: which God forbid that a Catholic should hold. It remains, then, that we so believe the Trinity to be of one substance that the essence itself is nothing other than the Trinity itself. To see which, however much we progress in this life, what we shall see will be through a glass and in a riddle. But when we have begun to have a spiritual body—which is promised in the resurrection—whether we see it with the mind, or in a marvelous way even with the body (since the grace of a spiritual body is ineffable), yet not through intervals of places, nor lesser in part, greater in part, since it is not a body, and is everywhere whole, we shall see it according to our capacity.

**4. 18.** But as for what you set down in your letter—that it seemed to you, or rather that it was seeming to you, that nothing living, according to substance, is in justice, and therefore that you cannot yet think of God, that is, a living nature, as like justice: because justice, as you say, lives not in itself, but in us; nay rather we live according to it, but justice itself in no way lives by itself—to answer yourself in this, look at whether it can rightly be said that life itself does not live, by which it comes about that whatever we not falsely say to live lives. For I think it seems absurd to you that there should be living by life, and life does not live. Moreover, if life itself especially lives, by which lives everything that lives, recall, I beg, which souls divine Scripture says are dead; surely you will find the unjust, the impious, the unbelieving. For although through them the bodies of the impious live—of whom it is said that *the dead bury their dead*[^11], and there even the iniquitous souls are understood not to be without some life; for otherwise their bodies could not live except by some life or other, of which souls cannot at all be lacking, whence they are rightly called immortal—yet they are called dead, justice being lost, for no other reason than that, of souls too (though living immortally by some life or other), a truer and greater life is justice, as it were the life of lives, which, since they are in bodies, even those bodies are alive, which cannot live by themselves. Wherefore, if souls cannot but live in some way even in themselves, because from them the bodies too live, which, deserted by them, die; how much more is true justice to be understood to live even in itself, from which souls so live that, this being lost, they are called dead, although by some little life they do not cease to live!

**4. 19.** Moreover, that justice which lives in itself is without doubt God, and lives unchangeably. But just as this, since it is in itself life, is made also our life when we are made in some way partakers of it, so, since it is in itself justice, it is made also our justice when, cleaving to it, we live justly; and we are just the more or less the more or less we cleave to it. Whence it is written of the only-begotten Son of God—since he is surely the Wisdom and Justice of the Father, and always is in himself—that *he was made for us by God wisdom and justice, and sanctification and redemption; that, as it is written, He who glories, let him glory in the Lord*[^12]. Which indeed you too saw, by adding and saying: "Unless perhaps it be asserted that that alone is justice which is not of this human equity, but which is God." That highest God is plainly true justice, or that true God highest justice; to hunger and thirst for which is our justice in this pilgrimage, and to be afterward satisfied with which is our full justice in eternity. Let us not, then, think God like our justice, but let us rather think ourselves the more like God the more, by participation in him, we shall be able to be more just.

**4. 20.** If, then, it is to be guarded against that we think God like our justice, since the light which illuminates is incomparably more excellent than that which is illuminated; how much more must it be guarded against that we believe him to be something lower and in a manner more discolored than is our justice! But what else is justice, when it is in us, or any virtue by which one lives rightly and wisely, than the beauty of the inner man? And surely according to this beauty rather than according to the body we were made to the image of God: whence it is said to us, *Do not be conformed to this world, but be reformed in the newness of your mind, to prove what is the will of God, what is good and well-pleasing and perfect*[^13]. If, then, not in mass nor in parts distant by their places, as bodies are either perceived or thought, but in an intelligible virtue, such as justice, we say, or know, or wish the mind to be beautiful, and according to this beauty we are reformed to the image of God: surely the beauty of God himself, who formed us and reforms us to his image, is not to be suspected to be in any corporeal mass; and he is to be believed incomparably more beautiful than the minds of the just by as much as he is incomparably more just. These things, as far as pertains to the usual measure of letters, are perhaps more lengthy to your Love than you expected; but as far as pertains to the question of so great a matter, let it suffice to have admonished briefly: not so that it be enough for your erudition, but so that, diligently instructed by reading or hearing others too, you may yourself more richly refute the things ill said by you; which is surely the better the more humbly and faithfully it is done.
