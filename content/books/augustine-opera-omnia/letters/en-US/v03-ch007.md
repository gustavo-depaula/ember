# Letter 162

To the most blessed lord, and venerable brother and fellow bishop, holy Evodius, and to the brothers with you, from Augustine, and the brothers with me, greetings in the Lord.

**1.** You ask many things of a man much occupied; and, what is more serious, you think that those things ought to be hurried out by dictating which are so difficult that, even dictated or written with great diligence, they can scarcely be brought to the understanding even of such men as you yourself are. To this is added that not only you and such as you are to be reckoned as going to read what we write, but surely also those who, endowed with a less keen and less exercised mind, are nevertheless borne by such zeal to come to know our writings—whether with a friendly or a hostile mind—that they cannot be withheld from them at all. Considering this, you see how great care there ought to be in writing, especially about things so great that even great men labor over them. But if, when I have something in hand, it must be set aside and deferred so that answer may rather be made to the things that come up demanding it, what will happen if, even while these are being answered, others come up? Is it your pleasure that, even these being omitted, those be taken up, and that the later things which arise always become the prior ones, and that those alone get to be completed in which, while they are being written, nothing came up to be inquired into? Which, that it should happen to me, is most difficult; but I do not think this pleases you. I ought not, then, to have interrupted other things when yours came up, just as I ought not yours, if others again should rush in; and yet I am not permitted to keep this justice. For behold, in order to write back to you these very things by which I might admonish you of it, I set aside what I was doing, and turned my mind to this letter from another great intention.

**2.** But it was easy, as I think, to send back to you this excuse, not an unjust one, put into a letter; but to answer your inquiries is not so easy: and I think that, in the very little works which now hold me most intent, there will not be lacking certain places where I may unfold these very things which you ask, if the Lord favors. Already, too, of those questions which you just now sent, many are solved in the books I have not yet published, whether on the Trinity or on Genesis. Although, even if you reread those things which have long been known to you—or, unless I am mistaken, were known, since you have perhaps forgotten the things I wrote while you conferred and conversed with me, whether on the Size of the Soul or on Free Will—you will find whence to dissolve your doubts, even without my help, by applying, of course, some labor of thought, so that to those things which have there been brought to clear and certain understanding the consequent things may be knit. You have also the book on True Religion, which, if you recall and examine it, it would never seem to you that God is compelled by reason to be, or that by reasoning it is brought about that God ought to be. Since, in the reasoning of numbers, which we certainly have in daily use, if we say, Seven and three ought to be ten, we speak less considerately; for they ought not to be ten, but they are ten. About what things, therefore, it is rightly said that they ought to be—whether they already are, or that they may be—we have discussed enough, as I think, in those books which I have mentioned. For a man ought to be wise, if he is, that he may remain so; if he is not yet, that he may become so: but God ought not to be wise, but is.

**3.** Those things too about the visions, which I most recently wrote to you, and which you recall as subtly said, but as having entangled you in greater questions—review them again and again, and think more diligently, and let it not pass over, but let your consideration dwell on them; and from there perhaps you will somehow conjecture how the soul is present or absent. For in those visions it lingers in dreams, when it is absent from the sense and presence of beholding which it offers to the eyes while waking; but this absence from the eyes—that is, as it were from the luminaries of the body—of the soul, which happens when we sleep, if a greater force should increase it, so that the whole of what is there be withdrawn, is death. As, therefore, from the sense of beholding to the visions of dreams it does not depart with some body—unless perhaps we are going to suppose that those things which are seen in dreams are corporeal, and we ourselves among them are carried this way and that with some body; which I now think does not seem so to you—so, if it be wholly withdrawn and absent, which happens in death, it must not be thought to carry off with it some body from the body. For if it carried it off, surely even when we sleep and it departs from the eyes, insofar as it leaves them, it would carry off the eyes with it, however more subtle, yet corporeal; which it does not do. Nevertheless it carries off with it certain very similar things, but not corporeal, by which it beholds in dreams very similar visions, but not themselves corporeal either.

**4.** Further, if anyone contends that even the visions of dreams which appear similar to bodies are nothing but corporeal, he seems to himself to say something, nor is this slowness of mind easily convinced; for it belongs to many who are even not slightly acute, since they pay too little attention to how much images of bodies avail, which come to be in the spirit, and are not at all bodies. But when they are compelled to behold them, if they rightly attend, and find that they are not corporeal but most like bodies, they are nevertheless not at once able to render a reason about them—by what causes and how they come to be, by what nature, finally, they subsist of themselves, or in what subject they are: whether they come to be in the soul as letters from ink on parchment, where both substances are present, namely the parchment and the ink; or as a seal in wax, or any figure of which the wax is the subject—the former in the subject; or whether these things come to be in our spirit in both ways, sometimes thus, sometimes thus.

**5.** For not only do those things move us to think of the things which are absent from the senses of the body and are found in our memory, or which we ourselves, as it pleases, make, dispose, increase, diminish, and vary by position, condition, motion, and innumerable qualities and forms. Such perhaps are also those things by which we are deluded while sleeping, when we are not divinely admonished; except that these we do willingly, those we suffer apart from our choice. Not only do these things move us, which one not absurdly thinks come to be in the soul from the soul itself (although this too by more hidden causes, by which it is brought about that this rather than that comes into the soul's view); but also what the prophet says: *And the angel who spoke in me said to me*[^1]. For we must not believe that voices came from without to the corporeal ears of the prophet, when he says, who spoke in me, not, to me. Were they voices made of spirit, similar to corporeal ones, such as we make when, silently within ourselves, we run through many things by memory, very often even by singing, but yet uttered by the angel suggesting them to himself? And what is written in the Gospel: *Behold, an angel of God appeared to him in sleep, saying*[^2]—for how did it appear, whether an angelic body to closed eyes (for to Abraham awake they so appeared that he even perceived them by touching, when he washed their feet), or a spirit to the spirit of one sleeping in some form like a body, just as we ourselves seem to ourselves in such a figure to be moved even through places while dreaming, far otherwise than the limbs lying on the bed are moved.

**6.** These things, therefore, are marvelous because they have a more hidden reason than can be seen or rendered by one man to another. For these are the causes of wonder, when either the reason of any thing lies hidden, or the same thing is not usual, because it is either singular or rare. From that cause, then, of the hidden reason, I said in the letter which you recall having read, when I was answering those who deny that it should be believed that a virgin bore Christ and remained a virgin: *If a reason is sought, it will not be marvelous*: for this was said, not because the thing lacks reason, but because it lies hidden from those for whom God willed this to be marvelous. But from another cause of wonder, which is for this reason, because something unusual occurs, it is written of the Lord that *he marveled* at the faith of the centurion; for neither could the reason of any thing lie hidden from him, but the wonder was set down as praise of one whose equal had not appeared among the Hebrew people: whence the wonder itself is sufficiently set forth, when the Lord says: *Amen I say to you, I have not found such faith in Israel*[^3].

**7.** But as to what I added in that same letter: *If an example is demanded, it will not be unique*; in vain did you seem to yourself to have found, as it were, examples about the little worm that is born in an apple, and the spider that brings forth the thread of its weaving as it were from an intact body. For some things are said cleverly for the sake of a certain likeness, others more remotely, others more fittingly; but Christ alone was born of a virgin: whence you now understand, as I judge, why I said that this is without an example. All things, then, have their causes and their right and blameless reasons, which God works, whether usual or unusual. But when these causes and reasons lie hidden, we marvel at the things that come to be; but when they are open, we say accordingly that they come to be fittingly, and that there is nothing to wonder at, because the things have been done which reason required to be done. Or, if we marvel, we marvel not by being stupefied at unexpected things, but by praising the excellent: by which kind of wonder that centurion was praised. Nor for this reason is the sentence to be blamed in which it was said: *If a reason is sought, it will not be marvelous*; since there is another kind of wonder, even when the reason is manifest to the one wondering: for not on that account is the sentence blamed in which it was said: *God tempts no one*[^4], since there is another kind of temptation, on account of which it was rightly said likewise: *The Lord your God tempts you*[^5].

**8.** Nor let anyone suppose that for this reason it can rightly be said that the Father is seen by the Son with corporeal eyes, and not rather as the Son by the Father, because those who think this, when they have failed in rendering a reason, can themselves too say: "If a reason is sought, it will not be marvelous": for this was said, not because there is no reason, but because it lies hidden. But of that—not of a miracle, but of an error—whoever undertakes to refute those who hold the opinion ought to demonstrate that there is no reason. For just as there is no reason by which the nature of God should die, or be corrupted, or sin; and when we say that God cannot do this, we do not derogate from his power, but praise his eternity and truth: so when we say that he cannot be seen by corporeal eyes, the reason does not lie hidden, but is open to those who understand well, by which it is clear that God is not a body, and that nothing can be discerned by corporeal eyes except what is discerned by the interposition of some interval; but that this can be nothing but a body, and that substance which is less in a part than in the whole; which to believe of God ought to be wicked even for those who are not yet able to understand this.

**9.** The reason of the various changes lies hidden; and from this is the whole forest of visible marvels: yet does it therefore lie hidden that there are bodies, that we have a body, that there is no little body, however small, which does not according to its own measure occupy space of place; nor is whole everywhere in what it occupies, but is less in a part than in the whole? Since these things do not lie hidden, to them are to be knit the consequent things—which now it is too long to do—by which it may be shown that the reason does not lie hidden, but that there is no reason at all by which it ought to be believed, or can be understood, that God, who is everywhere whole, and is not diffused through spaces of places by corporeal mass, in which he would necessarily consist of greater and lesser parts, can be discerned by corporeal eyes. Whence I would say more if I had undertaken this in this letter, into whose length I have advanced without feeling it, almost forgetting my occupations, so that perhaps, which I did not think, I have satisfied your intention, who can, when admonished by a few things, think of more that fit; but not also the intention of those into whose hands these things can come not in vain, if they be discussed more diligently and more copiously. But men labor in learning, and cannot understand brief things, do not love to read lengthy ones. They labor likewise in teaching, who in vain force both few things on the slow and many on the lazy. Send also the copy of that letter which, going astray among us, could not be found.
