# Letter 171/A

From Augustine to Maximus

**1.** Conform your life and morals to the precepts of God, which we have received that we may act well, beginning from religious fear. For *the beginning of wisdom is the fear of the Lord*[^1], where human pride is broken and weakened. Then, that, gentle and meek through piety, you may not, by spirited contention, struggle against even those things you do not yet understand, and which seem to the unskilled absurd and self-contradictory in the holy Scriptures, nor superimpose your own sense upon the senses of the divine Books; but rather yield, and meekly defer understanding, than, unmeek, accuse what is hidden. Third, when human weakness begins to be opened to you for the knowledge of yourself, and you know where you lie, what penal bonds of mortality, propagated from Adam, you drag with you, and how far you are a pilgrim from the Lord; and you have perceived *another law in your members fighting against the law of your mind, and leading you captive in the law of sin which is in your members*; you may cry out: *Wretched man that I am, who will free me from the body of this death?*[^2]—so that, mourning, you may be consoled by the grace of God through Jesus Christ our Lord, promising the very liberation. Fourth, now desire to fulfill righteousness much more vehemently and fervently than the pleasures of the flesh are wont to be desired by the most worthless men; except that, in the hope of divine help, in this appetite there is a tranquil ardor and a more secure flame. But in this fourth step of life one presses earnestly upon prayers, that to those hungering and thirsting the fullness of righteousness may be granted; so that it may not only not be burdensome, but even delight, to abstain from the pleasure of all corruption, whether one's own or another's, whether by struggling or by opposing. That this may be easily granted from above, a fifth is added: the counsel of showing mercy, that in what you can you may help the needy; because in what you cannot yet, you desire to be helped by the Almighty. But the gift of mercy is twofold: when punishment is remitted, and when kindness is shown. These two the Lord briefly comprehended thus: *Forgive, and it will be forgiven you; give, and it will be given you*[^3]. But this work avails also for the cleansing of the heart, so that, as far as is permitted in this life, we may be able to behold by pure understanding the unchangeable substance of God. For something is held against us which must be relaxed, that our gaze may break forth into the light: whence the Lord himself says: *But give alms, and behold, all things are clean to you*[^4]. Wherefore a sixth follows: the cleansing itself of the heart.

**2.** But that a right and pure gaze may be directed into the true light, neither those things which we do well and laudably, nor those things which we discern keenly and sagaciously, are to be referred to the end of pleasing men, or of relieving the necessities of the body. For God wishes to be worshiped freely; because there is nothing on account of which he himself should be sought. When we come to this purity of understanding by the steps of a good life, whether more slowly or more quickly, then let us dare to say that we are able somewhat to touch with the mind the unity of the supreme and ineffable Trinity: where the highest peace will be; because there is nothing beyond which to be awaited, when, reformed to the image of their own kind, made sons of God out of men, they fully enjoy the paternal unchangeableness. For first: *Blessed are the poor in spirit*[^5]; where there is the fear of God. Then: *Blessed are the meek*[^6]; where there is docile piety. Third: *Blessed are those who mourn*[^7]; where there is the knowledge of one's own weakness. Fourth: *Blessed are those who hunger and thirst for righteousness*[^8]; where there is the fortitude of striving to have the lusts subdued. Fifth: *Blessed are the merciful, for God will have mercy on them*[^9]; where there is the counsel of helping, that you may deserve to be helped. Then one comes to the sixth step, in which it is said: *Blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God*[^10]; where the pure understanding, fit for understanding, cannot behold the Trinity, in however small a part, unless we also do not seek human praise, though we do praiseworthy things. Accordingly, by the seventh step we come to the tranquility of that peace which the world cannot give. For even to those four virtues which the philosophers too were once able to trace out with memorable industry, that is, prudence, fortitude, temperance, and justice, if for the perfect worship of religion we add these three joined, that is, faith, hope, and love, we surely find the sevenfold number. For rightly these three are not omitted, without which we know that no one can either worship God or please him.
