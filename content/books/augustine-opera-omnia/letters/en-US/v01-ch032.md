# Letter 32

To the deservedly praiseworthy and honorable lord and brother Romanianus, Paulinus and Therasia

**1.** *The day before we sent this, our brothers having returned from Africa—in expectation of whom you saw us hanging—we had received from there, most longed-for of holy and most dear men, letters, that is, of Aurelius, Alypius, Augustine, Profuturus, Severus, now all alike bishops. Therefore, rejoicing at the most recent discourses of so many such holy men, we hastened to report to you our gladness, that to you too we might convey, with most festive tokens, a joy longed-for amid your anxious pilgrimage. If by chance you have learned the same things about these venerable and most loving men through the arrival of other ships, receive them, repeated, through us as well, and exult again with renewed cheerfulness. But if this first message should come to you from us, rejoice that so great a love has been won for us, by Christ's gift, in your homeland, so that whatever divine Providence does there, always wonderful, as it is written, in his saints*[^1], we may know either among the first, or with the first.

**2.** *But we write not only that this is to be rejoiced at, that Augustine has received the episcopate, but that the African Churches have deserved this care of God, that they should receive heavenly words from the mouth of Augustine, who, advanced in a new manner to the greater grace of the Lord's gift, was so consecrated that he did not succeed the bishop in his chair, but was added to him. For while Valerius, bishop of the Church of Hippo, is unharmed, Augustine is his co-bishop. And that blessed old man, whose most pure mind no stain of envious ill-will has ever overspread, now takes from the Most High fruits worthy of the peace of his heart, so that he whom he simply wished to be the successor of his priesthood, he deserves to hold as a colleague. Could this be believed before it happened? But in this work too of the Almighty that gospel saying can be spoken:* These things are arduous for men; but with God all things are possible[^2]. *Let us therefore exult and be glad in him who* alone does wonders[^3], *and* who makes those of one mind to dwell in a house[^4], *since* he himself has looked upon our lowliness[^5], *and* visited *in good* his people[^6]: *who* raised up a horn in the house of David his servant[^7], *and now has exalted the horn of his Church among his elect,* that the horns of sinners*, as he promises through the prophet—that is, of the Donatists and Manichees—*he may break[^8].

**3.** *Would that the hearing of our son Licentius might now be struck by this trumpet of the Lord, which sounds through Augustine, but that he might hear it with that ear by which Christ enters, from which the enemy does not snatch the seed of God*[^9]! Then Augustine will truly seem to himself the highest pontiff of Christ, because then he will feel himself heard from on high, if him whom he begot worthy of you in letters, this one he may worthily beget for himself as a son in Christ. For even now, I would have you believe, he wrote to us with the most burning solicitude about him. We trust in almighty Christ that the spiritual prayers of Augustine will prevail over the carnal wishes of our young man. He will be conquered, even unwilling (believe me); he will be conquered by the faith of his most pious parent, lest an evil victory conquer, if he should prefer to conquer to his own ruin rather than to be overcome for his salvation. Lest the duty of brotherly kindness should seem empty, from the biscuit of the Christian campaign, in whose battle-line we daily serve as soldiers for the ration of frugality, we have sent five loaves to you and to our son Licentius alike. For we could not separate from the blessing him whom we take in the same grace to be inwardly bound to us. Yet let us speak in a few words to him also, lest he deny that what is written to you about him is written to him. For it is to Aeschinus that what Micio hears is said. But why should I speak of another's affairs, when from my own I can speak all things, and to speak of another's is not usually the mark of a sound head? By which sound head, by God's grace, we are sound and safe, we whose head is Christ. May we have you unharmed for very many years, and ever blessed with your whole household, as we desire, in Christ, lord brother, deservedly most honorable and most longed-for. **4.** Hear*, then,* my son, the law of your father*, that is, the faith of Augustine,* and do not reject the counsels of your mother[^10]*, which name the piety of Augustine equally and rightly claims for itself over you, who carried you, so small, in his bosom, and, when from your childhood you had been steeped in the first milk of worldly wisdom, now also yearns to suckle and nourish you for the Lord at spiritual breasts. Since he sees you, grown up in bodily age, still wailing in your spiritual cradle, still an infant in the word of God, scarcely creeping with your first steps in Christ and with a faltering footing, if only the teaching of Augustine, as a mother's hand and a nurse's arm, may guide the unsteady little one. Whom if you hear and follow, that I may again entice you with the discourse of Solomon,* my son, you will receive a crown of graces upon your head[^11]. *And then truly you will be that consul and pontiff, not dreamed in a phantasm, but formed by Truth itself, Christ filling the empty images of a false work with the solid effects of his own working. For truly a pontiff and truly a consul, Licentius, you will be, if you cleave to the footsteps of Augustine, to prophetic and apostolic disciplines, as blessed Elisha to the consecrated Elijah*[^12], as the young Timothy to the illustrious Apostle[^13], with an undivided company along the divine journeys, so that you may both learn to deserve the priesthood with a perfect heart, and take counsel for the peoples' salvation with the mouth of a teacher. **5.** *Enough of this for warning and exhortation: for I judge, my Licentius, that with little discourse and labor you can be stirred toward Christ, since from childhood you were inflamed for the pursuits of truth and wisdom—which both truly are Christ, and the highest good of all good—by the spirit and mouth of the venerable Augustine. Who, if he availed little with you on your behalf, what shall I, so far his inferior by distance, and poor in all his resources, accomplish? But because, trusting both in the power of his ability and in the kindness of your talent, I hope for fuller and greater things wrought in you than to be wrought, I have dared to open my mouth with a double grace: both that I might be compared, with due love, to that man in solicitude for you, and that, among those who love your salvation, I might be numbered at least by attested affection. For I know that the palm in your perfecting is destined chiefly for Augustine. I fear, my son, that I have offended your ears by the roughness of a rash discourse, and through your ears have inflicted on your mind too the wound of my weariness. But your letter came to mind, by which I understood you to be familiar with musical measures: from which pursuit I myself, once of your age, did not shrink. And so, to soothe your mind, if in anything I had chafed it, I found a remedy, recalling your letter, that I might call you, by the modulation of a song, to the Lord, the artificer of all-formed harmony. I beg you to hear with your ear, and not to spurn the cause of your salvation in my words; but to receive gladly the pious care and fatherly mind even in despised discourses, in which the implanted name of Christ, which is above every name*[^14], makes this veneration due, that it cannot be despised by a believer. An elegiac poem of Paulinus to Licentius *Come then, break off delays*[^15], and the tenacious chains of the age:  
nor fear the mild yoke of the gentle Lord.  
Fair indeed, but a marvel to wandering minds, is the present face of things; yet a wise spirit is not amazed at these.  
Now Rome, ill-counseling, solicits you with various figures—alas, mighty even to overturn the strong!  
But for you, my son, I pray, may father Augustine always meet you against all the City's enticements.  
Looking upon him amid such perils of fragile life, and holding him in your heart, you will be safe.  
This too, again and again, I will warn[^16]: that you flee the slippery ways of hard warfare.  
Honor is a flattering name, an evil servitude, a sickly end—what now it pleases to will, soon it grieves to have willed.  
It pleases to climb the heights, but it is a terror to descend from the heights: if you stumble, you fall worse from the topmost citadel.  
Now false goods please you, now ambition snatches you with every breeze, and hollow rumor bears you in its empty bosom.  
But when the ruinous belt has girded you with great heat, and barren labor has then broken you,  
too late and in vain will you accuse empty hopes, and the chains you now weave you will wish to break:  
then you will remember, grieving, that you despised in vain the truth-telling warnings of father Augustine.  
Wherefore, if you are wise and if you are pious, boy, hear, and take the words of the fathers and the counsel of the elders.  
Why do you draw back your wild neck from the yoke? My burden is gentle, the yoke of Christ is sweet[^17]—a pious voice; believe God:  
and add your head to the yoke, give your face to the soft halter, and put your lowered shoulders under the light burden[^18].  
Now you can do this, while you act free, while no chains hold you back, no care of the marriage-bed, nor lofty honor.  
This is good freedom: to serve Christ, and in him to be above all things; not to the lords of men,  
not to vices does he serve, not to proud kings, who only has given himself to Christ the Lord.  
Nor let that nobility seem free to you, which you now see borne aloft in the astonished City.  
Which you see to seem to itself of such great freedom that it scorns to bend its neck to God.  
That wretch serves many mortals[^19], and even slaves, and buys handmaids that they may lord it over him.  
They know it who have endured eunuchs and the great palaces, and whoever, of his own will wretched, endures Rome:  
at what price of sweat, and at what loss of honor, the honor of the cloak costs there, the honor of office here.  
Nor yet does the powerful man himself, who has bought to be loftier than all, that he might serve no one, attain it.  
When he has well boasted himself lord through the whole City, he serves demons, if he worships idols.  
Alas the grief! For the sake of these you linger in the City, Licentius, and spurn the kingdom of Christ, that you may please these:  
these you call lords, and with bent neck you greet, whom you behold to be the slaves of wood and stone!  
Under a divine name they venerate silver and gold; what the disease of avarice loves is their religion.  
I pray that he may love these who does not love Augustine, nor worship Christ, to whom it is pleasing to worship these.  
Hence God himself says that two lords cannot be served[^20], since one mind pleases God:  
one faith, one God[^21], and an only Christ from the Father; no double servitude belongs to one lord.  
For as great as is the distance between heaven and earth, so great is that between the things and the rules of Caesar and of Christ.  
Be lifted from the ground; but now, while the spirit rules these limbs[^22], penetrate the sky with your mind: no delay of the flesh stands in the way.  
Die now to bodily acts, and with a clear soul premeditate the goods of the heavenly life.  
You are a spirit, though you are held by the body, if now with a pious mind, victor, you destroy the work of the flesh.  
These things, dear boy, compelled by faithful love, I have written for you; if you receive them, you will be received by God.  
Believe that Augustine is now doubled for you in me; take two fathers with one piety.  
Are we spurned? You will be torn away with greater grief from the two: are we heard? You will be a sweet pledge to the two.  
In you the glad care of two fathers has sweated, and to you it is a great honor to have gladdened two.  
But when I couple myself with Augustine, I do not boast myself equal in merits; I compare myself only by love of you.  
For what should I pour upon you, dripping in a poor stream? Besides me, you would be washed by a twin river:  
Alypius is your kinsman, and Augustine your teacher—the one a sharer of your blood, the other the begetter of your talent.  
With so great a brother you are strong, and with such a teacher, Licentius, and do you hesitate to follow the stars on such wings?  
Whatever you do (for the world should not hope you a friend), you will not be given to the earth, a soul owed to Christ.  
Though you meditate marriage-chambers and lofty honors, now or one day you will be restored to your Lord.  
I believe two just men will conquer one sinner, and the prayers of your brother will drive away your wishes.  
Return, then, by the voice with which your parent, by the blood with which your brother—both priests—bid you come back.  
They draw you back to your own, for now you seek what is alien; these things rather, which hold the kingdoms of you, are your own.  
Seek these again, gape for these, do not waste your time on alien things; if you should be unwilling for your own, who will give you another's?  
You will not be your own, but, sent far through foreign places, alas, you will live an exile from the senses of your own breast.  
It is enough that an anxious parent has sung these things to his son, while for you I either wish or fear what I do for myself.  
These things, if you receive them, this page will one day bring you life: if you refuse, this same page will be a witness.  
May Christ grant you to me unharmed, dearest son, and give you to himself as a servant forever.  
Live, I pray, but live to God: for to live to the world is the work of death; to live to God is to live the living life.
