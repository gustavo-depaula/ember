# Letter 169

Bishop Augustine, to bishop Evodius.

**1. 1.** If your holiness counts it of such value to know the things which occupy me more, from which I do not wish to be turned aside to something else, send someone who may copy them for you. For already several have been completed which had been begun by us this year before Easter, as Lent drew near. For to those three books on the City of God, against the demon-worshipers, its enemies, we have added two others: by which five books I think enough has been disputed against those who think the gods must be worshiped for the felicity of the present life, and, supposing that this felicity is hindered by us, are hostile to the Christian name. Next must be said, as we promised in the first book, against those who think the worship of their gods necessary for the life to come after death—on account of which life we are Christians. I have also dictated expositions of three psalms, in no small volumes: the sixty-seventh, the seventy-first, the seventy-seventh. The rest, not yet dictated or treated, are vehemently awaited and demanded of us. From these I do not wish to be called back and delayed by any questions whatever rushing in from the side: so much so that I do not now wish to attend even to the books on the Trinity, which I have long turned over in hand and have not yet completed, since they are too laborious, and I think can be understood by few; whence the things which we hope will be useful to many press more urgently.

**1. 2.** For the Apostle did not say, as you write, *He who is ignorant will be ignored*[^1], about this matter—as though he is to be punished with this penalty who is not able by his understanding so to distinguish the ineffable unity of the Trinity as memory, understanding, and will are distinguished in our mind: the Apostle said this for another reason. Read, and you will see that he was speaking of those things which would build up the faith or morals of the many, not of those which scarcely reach the understanding of a few, and that a slight one—however slight it can be in this life about so great a matter; that is, that prophecy be preferred to tongues, that these be not conducted in confusion, as though the spirit of prophecy compelled even the unwilling to speak, that women keep silence in church, that all things be done honestly and according to order. While he was dealing with these things, he says: *If anyone seems to be a prophet, or spiritual, let him acknowledge that the things I write to you are the Lord's commandment. But if anyone is ignorant, he will be ignored*[^2]: by these words restraining, and recalling to a peaceful order, the restless—so much the readier to sedition the more they seemed to themselves to excel in spirit, while by being proud they threw all into confusion. If anyone, then, seems to be a prophet or spiritual, let him acknowledge, he says, that the things I write to you are the Lord's commandment. If anyone seems to be—and surely is not: for he who is, without doubt acknowledges it, and has no need of admonition and exhortation, because *he judges all things, and is judged by no one*[^3]. Those, then, were making seditions and disturbances in the Church who seemed to be in the Church what they were not. These he teaches to acknowledge that it is the Lord's commandment, since *God is not a God of sedition, but of peace*[^4]. But if anyone is ignorant, he will be ignored, that is, he will be disapproved. For God, if you refer it to knowledge, does not ignore those to whom he is going to say, *I do not know you*[^5]; but their disapproval was intimated by this word.

**1. 3.** But since the Lord says, *Blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God*[^6], and that vision is promised to us at the end as the highest reward; it is not to be feared, if we are now not able to behold to clarity the things we believe about the nature of God, that on this account it was said, *He who is ignorant will be ignored*. For because *in the wisdom of God the world by wisdom did not know God, it pleased God by the foolishness of preaching to save those who believe*[^7]. This foolishness of preaching, and the foolish thing of God which is wiser than men, draws many to salvation, so that not only those who are not yet able by certain understanding to behold the nature of God which they hold by faith, but even those who do not yet in their own soul distinguish incorporeal substance from the general class of body as certainly as they are certain that they live, know, will—are not on that account estranged from the salvation which that foolishness of preaching confers on the faithful.

**1. 4.** For if Christ died only for those who can by certain understanding distinguish these things, we labor almost in vain in the Church. But if, as the truth holds, the weak peoples of believers run to the physician, to be healed through Christ, and him crucified, so that *where sin abounded, grace may superabound*[^8]; it comes about in marvelous ways, through the depth of the riches of the wisdom and knowledge of God, and through his inscrutable judgments, that both some who distinguish the incorporeal from bodies, when they seem to themselves great because of this, and mock the foolishness of preaching by which believers are saved, wander far from the one way which alone leads to eternal life; and many, glorying in the cross of Christ and not departing from that same way, even if they are ignorant of those things which are most subtly discussed—since not one of the little ones for whom Christ died perishes—come to that same eternity, truth, love, that is, to a stable, certain, and full felicity, where, to those abiding, seeing, loving, all things are perspicuous.

**2. 5.** Accordingly, let us believe with firm piety in one God, the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit; in such a way that neither is the Son believed to be him who is the Father, nor the Father him who is the Son, nor the Father nor the Son him who is the Spirit of both. Let nothing in this Trinity be thought to differ in times or places; but let these three be equal and coeternal, and altogether of one nature: not that one creature was created by the Father, and another by the Son, and another by the Holy Spirit; but that all things and each, which have been or are created, subsist with the Trinity creating; nor that anyone is freed by the Father without the Son and the Holy Spirit, or by the Son without the Father and the Holy Spirit, or by the Holy Spirit without the Father and the Son; but by the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit, the one, true, and truly immortal—that is, in every way unchangeable—God alone. But many things are said even separately in the Scriptures of the individual persons, so that it may be intimated that, although the Trinity is inseparable, it is nevertheless a Trinity: so that, just as they cannot be said at once when they are mentioned with corporeal sounds, although they are at once inseparably, so too in certain places of the Scriptures, and through certain creatures, they are shown individually and by turns—as the Father in the voice with which it sounded, *You are my Son*[^9]; and the Son in the man whom he assumed from the Virgin; and the Holy Spirit in the corporeal form of a dove: these show those three separately indeed, but in no way separated.

**2. 6.** Toward understanding this in some way we take up memory, understanding, will. For although we enunciate these individually, one by one, in their own separate times, yet we do nothing and say nothing of these without the other two. Nor on that account are these three to be thought so compared to the Trinity that they fit in every part: for to what likeness, in disputing, is so great a fitness granted that it is fitted in every part to the thing to which it is to be applied? Or when is anything similar taken up from the creature to the Creator? First, then, this likeness is found dissimilar in this: that these three, memory, understanding, will, are in the soul; the soul is not these same three: but that Trinity is not in God, but is itself God. Therefore a marvelous simplicity is there commended; because there it is not one thing to be, another to understand, or whatever else is said of God's nature: but the soul, because it is, even while it does not understand, is one thing that which it is, another that which it understands. Then who would dare to say that the Father does not understand through himself, but through the Son, as memory does not understand through itself, but through understanding, or rather the soul itself, in which these are, understands only through understanding, as it remembers only through memory, and wills only through will? To this, then, that likeness is applied, so that in some way it may be understood how, of these three in the soul, although the individual names are enunciated by which those same individual things are shown, yet each name is enunciated with these three cooperating, since it is said both by remembering, and by understanding, and by willing: so there is no creature by which either the Father alone, or the Son alone, or the Holy Spirit alone is shown, which the Trinity does not work at the same time—the Trinity which works inseparably: and through this, neither the voice of the Father, nor the soul and flesh of the Son, nor the dove of the Holy Spirit was made except with that same Trinity cooperating.

**2. 7.** Nor indeed was that sound of the voice, which immediately ceased to be, fitted into the unity of the person of the Father, nor was that corporeal form of the dove fitted into the unity of the person of the Holy Spirit. For it too, like that bright cloud which covered the Savior on the mountain with the three disciples, or rather like that fire which showed that same Holy Spirit, the office of signification being fulfilled, soon ceased to be. But the man alone—because for the sake of that very nature to be freed all those things were done—was fitted by a marvelous and singular assumption into the unity of the person of the Word of God, that is, of the only Son of God, the Word nevertheless remaining unchangeably in its own nature, in which nothing composite, with which it subsists, is to be suspected by any phantasm of the human mind. It is read indeed: *And the Spirit of wisdom is manifold*[^10]; but it is rightly called also simple. For manifold, since there are many things which it has; but simple, because it is not other than what it has: as the Son was said to *have life in himself*[^11], and is himself that same life. But the man came to the Word, the Word did not come into the man convertibly; and so the Son of God is said together with the man assumed: whence the same Son of God is unchangeable and coeternal with the Father, but in the Word alone; and the Son of God was buried, but in the flesh alone.

**2. 8.** Accordingly, the words which are said of the Son of God must be seen in what respect they are said. For by the man assumed the number of persons was not increased, but the same Trinity remained. For just as in any man, except that one who was singularly assumed, the soul and body are one person, so in Christ the Word and the man are one person. And just as a man, for example, a philosopher, is so called only according to the soul, and yet we do not on that account absurdly, but by a most congruous and most usual manner of speech, say a philosopher beaten, a philosopher dead, a philosopher buried, although the whole happens according to the flesh, not according to that which is philosopher: so Christ is God, Son of God, Lord of glory, and whatever of this kind is said according to the Word; and yet it is rightly said that God was crucified, since it is not held uncertain that he suffered this according to the flesh, not according to that by which he is Lord of glory.

**2. 9.** But that sound of the voice, and the corporeal form of the dove, and the divided tongues as it were of fire which settled upon each of them, just as those things on Mount Sinai which were made in a terrible form, and just as that pillar of cloud by day and of flame by night, were done and finished by a significative operation. In these, this is especially to be guarded against, lest the nature of God—whether of the Father, or of the Son, or of the Holy Spirit—be believed to be changeable and convertible. Nor let it move us that sometimes the thing which signifies receives the name of the thing which it signifies. The Holy Spirit was said in corporeal form, as it were a dove, to have descended and remained upon him. For so too *the rock was Christ*[^12], because it signifies Christ.

**3. 10.** But I wonder that it seems to you that the sound of that voice by which it was said, *You are my Son*, could be made by sole corporeal nature, not with the soul mediating, but by the divine nod; and that it does not seem to you that in the same way there could be made the corporeal form of any animal whatever, and a motion like that of a living thing, by the divine nod, with no animal spirit interposed. For if a corporeal creature obeys God without the ministry of an animating soul, so that sounds are produced such as are wont to be produced from an animate body, so that the form of articulate speech is brought to the ears; why should it not obey, so that without the ministry of an animating soul the figure and motion of a bird, by the same power of the Creator, is brought before the sight? Or can the sense of hearing merit this, the sense of seeing not, since from the adjacent matter of body both are formed—both what sounds to the ears and what appears to the sight, both the articulations of voice and the lineaments of members, both audible and visible motion—so that both there is a true body which is perceived by the bodily sense, and there is nothing more than what is perceived by the bodily sense? For the soul is perceived by no bodily sense even in any animate thing. There is no need, therefore, to ask how the corporeal form of the dove appeared, just as we do not ask how the voices of an articulate body sounded. For if the soul could not be the mediator where the voice—not as a voice—is said to be made; how much more could it not be, where it is said as a dove, signified by this word with sole corporeal form rendered to the eyes, not the nature of a living animal expressed? In this way too is that said: *There came suddenly from heaven a sound, as of a rushing mighty wind, and there appeared to them divided tongues as of fire*[^13]: where a certain form, as it were of wind, and as it were of perceptible fire, like to the usual and known natures, not the very usual and known natures made for a time, seem to be signified.

**3. 11.** But if a subtler reason, or a more excellent investigation of the matter, shows that the nature which is moved neither temporally nor locally is not moved except through that which can be moved only temporally, not locally, it will follow that all those things were effected through the ministry of a living creature, as they are done through angels: whence to discuss it more diligently is both long and not necessary. To this is added that there are visions which appear to the spirit as if to the senses of the body, not only to those sleeping or raving, but sometimes to those of sound mind and awake; not by the deceit of demons mocking, but by some spiritual revelation, which is made through incorporeal forms like to bodies; which can in no way be distinguished, unless they be more fully revealed by divine help, and judged by the understanding of the mind, scarcely sometimes when they happen, but for the most part afterward when they have passed. Since these things are so—whether in corporeal natures, or with sole corporeal form, but spiritual nature, they seem to appear to our spirit as if to the senses of the body—when holy Scripture mentions these, we ought not rashly to judge of which of these two kinds they are, and whether they happen with a living creature mediating, if they happen by a body: provided, however, that we either without any doubt believe, or by whatever understanding grasp, that the invisible and unchangeable nature of the Creator, that is, of the supreme and ineffable Trinity, is removed and separated both from the senses of mortal carnal beings and from all convertibility, whether for the better, or for the worse, or for anything else and another.

**4. 12.** These things, about the two questions—that is, about the Trinity, and about the dove, in which the Holy Spirit was shown not by his own nature, but by a significative form (just as the Son of God too was crucified by the Jews not by his own nativity, of which the Father said, *Before the morning star I begot you*[^14], but in the man assumed from the womb of the Virgin)—you see how I, most occupied, was nevertheless able to write to you who are at leisure. Nor did I think all the things must be treated which you set down in your letter; but I judge that I have answered those two which you wished to hear from me, even if not sufficiently for your avidity, yet obediently for your charity.

**4. 13.** But besides those two books which I mentioned above that I added to the three, and the exposition of three psalms, I have also written a book to the holy presbyter Jerome on the Origin of the Soul, consulting him how that opinion can be defended which he wrote to Marcellinus of religious memory to be his own, "that new individual souls are made for those being born," so that the most well-founded faith of the Church may not be undermined, by which we believe unshaken that in Adam all die, and, unless they are freed by the grace of Christ—which he works through his sacrament even in little ones—they are drawn into condemnation. I have also written another to the same, how it seems to him that what is written in the letter of James is to be taken: *But whoever keeps the whole law, but offends in one point, has become guilty of all*. But in this I have said also what seems to me: while in that, about the origin of the soul, I only asked what seems to him, by a certain consultative disputation. For I did not wish to lose the occasion of a certain most holy and most zealous young presbyter, Orosius, who came to us from farthest Spain, that is, from the shore of the ocean, inflamed by the ardor of the holy Scriptures alone; whom I persuaded to go to him too. To this very Orosius also I answered, in one not large book, with as much brevity and clarity as I could, certain questions which disturbed him about the heresy of the Priscillianists, and certain opinions of Origen which the Church has not received. I have also written a certain large book against the heresy of Pelagius, certain brothers compelling me, on whom he had urged a most pernicious opinion against the grace of Christ. If you wish to have all these, send someone who may copy them all for you. But permit me to be free for seeking out and dictating these things, which, since they are necessary to many, I judge must be preferred to your inquiries, which pertain to very few.
