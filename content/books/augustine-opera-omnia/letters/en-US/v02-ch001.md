# Letter 100

To the most excellent lord, deservedly honorable and notably praiseworthy son Donatus, Augustine sends greetings in the Lord

**1.** I would indeed have wished that the African Church were not so situated in these afflictions that it had need of the help of any earthly power. But since, as the Apostle says, *there is no authority except from God*[^1], then beyond doubt, when she is assisted through you, the most sincere sons of our Catholic mother, our help is in the name of the Lord, who made heaven and earth. For who would not perceive that no small consolation has been sent to us from on high amid such great evils, when a man such as you, most devoted to the name of Christ, has been raised to the insignia of the proconsulship, so that a power allied to your good will might restrain the enemies of the Church from their criminal and sacrilegious attempts, most excellent lord, deservedly honorable and notably praiseworthy son? In the end there is but one thing we dread in your administration of justice: that perhaps, since whatever evil is committed against the Christian community by impious and ungrateful men is surely graver and more atrocious than if such things were committed against others, you too may judge that it should be punished according to the enormity of the crimes, rather than out of regard for Christian gentleness; and we beg you through Jesus Christ not to do this. For we do not seek vengeance upon our enemies on this earth, nor ought the distresses we suffer to constrain our minds so narrowly that we forget what he has commanded us, for whose truth and name we suffer: we love our enemies and pray for them. And so, on the occasion of these dreadful judges and laws, we desire that they be corrected, not killed, lest they fall into the punishments of the eternal judgment; we wish neither that discipline toward them be neglected, nor that they be subjected to the punishments they deserve. Restrain their sins, therefore, in such a way that there may be those who repent of having sinned.

**2.** We ask, therefore, that when you hear the cases of the Church, however much you find her assailed or afflicted by wicked wrongs, you may forget that you have the power to kill, and not forget our petition. Do not regard it as cheap or contemptible, son honorably and most dearly beloved, that we ask you not to kill those for whom we ask the Lord that they be corrected. Apart from the fact that we must not depart from our settled purpose of overcoming evil with good, let your prudence also consider this: that no one but churchmen takes the trouble to bring ecclesiastical cases before you. And so, if you think that men should be killed for these crimes, you will deter us from letting any such matter come before your tribunal through our agency; and once this is found out, those men will rage against us with bolder audacity to our destruction, a necessity being thrust upon us and laid down for us, so that we should even choose to be killed by them rather than bring them before your courts to be killed. I beg you not to receive this admonition, petition, and entreaty of mine with disdain. For I do not think you fail to recall that I could have had great confidence with you, far higher than you now are, even if I were not a bishop. Meanwhile let the heretical Donatists soon learn, through an edict of Your Excellency, that the laws passed against their error remain in force, laws which they now suppose and boast to be of no effect, so that they may not in this way find any room to spare us. You will greatly help our labors and dangers to bear fruit if you take care not to have their most empty sect, full of impious pride, repressed by the imperial laws in such a way that they seem to themselves or to their own to be enduring some kind of hardship for the sake of truth and justice; but rather, when this is asked of you, allow them to be convicted and instructed by the clearest documents of certain facts in the records, whether of Your Eminence or of the lesser judges, so that those who are detained at your command may, if it can be done, bend their hard will toward something better, and others may read these things to their benefit. For coercion that only compels men without teaching them, though it be aimed at abandoning a great evil and holding fast to a great good, is more burdensome than useful.

[^1]: Rom 13:1
