# Letter 71

To the venerable and desirable holy lord, brother and fellow presbyter Jerome, Augustine sends greeting in the Lord

**1. 1.** From the time I began to write to you and to desire your writings, no better occasion has ever occurred to me than that my letter should be brought to you through a servant and most faithful minister of God, and most dear to me, such as is our son Cyprian the deacon. Through him, surely, I so hope for your letters, that I could hope for nothing more certain in this kind of matters. For neither will zeal in asking for replies be lacking to our said son, nor grace in deserving them, nor diligence in guarding them, nor alacrity in conveying them, nor faith in delivering them: only if in any way I deserve it, let the Lord help, and be present to your heart and to my desire, that no greater will hinder your brotherly will.

**1. 2.** Since, then, I have already sent two letters, but have received none of yours afterward, I wished to send those same ones again, believing that they had not reached you. Which, even if they did reach you, and perhaps rather yours could by no means reach me, send again those very writings which you have already sent, if perhaps they were kept; but if not, dictate again what I may read, provided yet that you be not reluctant to answer these, which I have now long awaited. The first letter too, which I had prepared while still a presbyter to be sent to you through a certain brother of ours, Profuturus, who afterward, made our colleague, has now migrated from this life, and could not then himself convey it, because immediately, while he arranged to set out, he was detained by the burden of the episcopate, and then shortly after died, even now I wished to send; that you may know how long I have burned for your conversations, and what violence I suffer, that the senses of your body are so far away from me, through which my mind could approach your mind, my most sweet brother, and to be honored in the members of the Lord.

**2. 3.** But in this letter I add this, which we learned afterward, that Job was translated by you from the Hebrew, when we already had a certain translation, yours, of the same prophet turned from the Greek tongue into Latin: where, however, you marked with asterisks the things which are in the Hebrew and are lacking in the Greek; but with obelisks the things which are found in the Greek and are not in the Hebrew, with so marvelous a diligence, that in certain places we see, for single words, single stars, signifying that those same words are in the Hebrew, but are not in the Greek. But in this latter translation, which is turned from the Hebrew, the same fidelity of words does not occur, and it not a little disturbs one thinking, either why in that first one the asterisks are fixed with so great diligence, that they indicate even the smallest particles of speech to be lacking in the Greek codices, which are in the Hebrew; or why in this other, which is from the Hebrew, this was more negligently cared for, so that these same particles were found in their places. I wished to set down something thence, for example; but at the hour the codex which is from the Hebrew was lacking to me. Nevertheless, because you fly ahead by your talent, you sufficiently understand, as I think, not only what I have said, but even what I wished to say, so that, the cause being given, you may unfold what moves me.

**2. 4.** I, indeed, would rather that you translate for us the Greek canonical Scriptures, which are said to be of the Seventy translators. For it will be very hard, if your translation begins to be read more frequently through many churches, that the Latin churches should be dissonant from the Greek churches, especially because a contradictor is easily convicted by the Greek book being produced, that is, of the most well-known tongue. But whoever shall be moved by something unusual in that which is translated from the Hebrew, and shall bring a charge of falsehood, scarcely or never will one come to the Hebrew testimonies, by which the objection may be defended. But if one even comes to them, who would bear that so many Latin and Greek authorities be condemned? To this is added that even Hebrews, when consulted, can answer otherwise: so that you alone seem necessary, who can convince even them; but yet, by what judge it is a wonder if you can find.

**3. 5.** For a certain brother of ours, a bishop, when he had appointed your translation to be read in the church over which he presides, came upon something placed by you far otherwise in the prophet Jonah than was settled in the senses and memory of all, and chanted through so many successions of ages. So great a tumult was made in the people, especially with the Greeks arguing and inflaming the accusation of falsehood, that the bishop (for it was the city of Oea) was compelled to demand the testimony of the Jews. But they, whether through inexperience or malice, answered that this was in the Hebrew codices which both the Greeks and the Latins had and said. Why say more? The man was compelled, as it were to correct a fault, willing, after great peril, not to remain without a people. Whence it also seems to us that you too could sometimes be deceived in some things. And see what sort of thing this is, in those writings which cannot be amended by the testimonies of the customary tongues collated.

**4. 6.** Accordingly we give no small thanks to God for your work, by which you translated the Gospel from the Greek, because almost everywhere there is no offense, when we collate the Greek Scripture. Whence, if anyone, contentious, should favor the old falsehood, the codices being produced and collated, he is either most easily taught or refuted. And if certain most rare things deservedly move one, who is so hard that he would not easily pardon a labor so useful, to which it does not suffice to render the recompense of praise? But what seems to you, why in many things the authority of the Hebrew codices is one way, and that of the Greek which is called the Seventy's another, I would wish you to deign to open. For neither has that one a small weight, which deserved so to be spread abroad, and which the Apostles used, not only the thing itself indicates, but also I remember you to have attested. And thereby you will profit very much, if you restore to Latin truth that Greek Scripture which the Seventy wrought: which is so various in different codices that it can scarcely be borne; and so suspect, lest something else be found in the Greek, that one hesitates to produce or prove anything thence. I thought this letter would be brief; but somehow it has become so sweet to me to go on in it, as if I were speaking with you. But I beseech you, by the Lord, that it not weary you to answer all things, and to afford me, as far as you can, your presence.
