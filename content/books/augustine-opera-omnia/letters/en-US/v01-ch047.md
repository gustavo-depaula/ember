# Letter 47

To the honorable and most beloved son Publicola, Augustine sends greeting in the Lord

**1.** After I learned the surgings of your mind from your letter, mine too at once became such—not that all such things moved me, by which you indicated that you were moved: but how these surgings might be taken from you, I confess, I surged; especially because you ask that I write back to you definitively, lest you fall into greater doubts than before you asked. For I see that this is not in my power. For however I shall write the things which seem to me most certain, if I do not persuade you, you will without doubt be more uncertain. But it is not the case that, as it lies with me to persuade, in the same way it lies with me to persuade anyone. Nevertheless, lest I deny your love my little labor, after some deliberation I have thought I ought to write back.

**2.** You are surely moved whether the faith of one who has sworn by demons that he will keep it is to be used. Here I want you first to consider whether, if anyone swears by false gods that he will keep faith, and does not keep it, it does not seem to you that he has sinned twice. For if, by such an oath, he kept the promised faith, he would be judged to have sinned only on this account, that he swore by such gods; but that, no one would rightly reproach, that he kept faith. But now, because he both swore by those by whom he ought not, and acted against the promised faith, which he ought not, he has surely sinned twice; and thereby he who uses the faith of one who is established to have sworn by false gods, and uses it not for evil, but for what is lawful and good, associates himself not with that sin of his by which he swore by demons, but with that good pact of his by which he kept faith. Nor do I here speak of that faith by which those are called faithful who are baptized in Christ. For that is far other and far distinct from the faith of human agreements and pacts. Nevertheless, without any doubt it is a lesser evil to swear truthfully by a false god than to swear deceitfully by the true God. For the more holy that by which one swears is, the more penal is the perjury. Therefore it is another question whether he sins not who has had an oath sworn to him by false gods, because he who swears to him worships false gods. To which question those testimonies can lend support which you yourself recalled, of Laban and Abimelech, if indeed Abimelech swore by his own gods, as Laban by the God of Nahor. This, as I said, is another question, which would perhaps rightly move me, had not those examples of Isaac and Jacob occurred, and any others that can be found. If indeed that does not yet move you which is said in the New Testament, that we should not swear at all[^1]. Which indeed seems to me to be said for this reason, not because to swear truly is a sin, but because to swear falsely is a monstrous sin; from which he wished us to be far, who admonished us in every way not to swear. But I know that it seems otherwise to you; whence we must not now dispute, that we may rather deal with that on which you thought I should be consulted. Accordingly, just as you do not swear, so neither do you compel another, if this please you, to swear: although it is said that we should not swear; but nowhere in the holy Scriptures do I remember to have read that we should not accept an oath from another. But it is another question, whether we ought to use that peace which has been made between others swearing to one another. Which, if we are unwilling, where we may live on earth I do not know whether we can find. For not only to the frontier, but to all the provinces, peace is procured by barbarian oath-swearing. Whence this too will follow, that not only the crops which are guarded by those who have sworn by false gods, but everywhere all things are defiled which are protected by that very peace which that oath-swearing confirms. But if it is most absurd to say this, neither let those things move you which were moving you.

**3.** Again, if from the threshing floor or the winepress something is taken for the sacrifices of demons, with a Christian knowing, he sins if he permits it to be done, where there is the power of forbidding. But if he learns that it was done, or did not have the power of forbidding, he uses the rest of the clean fruits from which those were taken; just as we use springs from which we most certainly know that water is drawn for the use of sacrifices. The same reasoning holds also for the baths. For we do not hesitate to draw breath from the air, into which we know smoke goes from all the altars and incense of demons. Whence it appears that this was forbidden: that we not use anything in honor of strange gods, or be thought to use it, by so accepting it that, although we despise them in our mind, we nevertheless build up to honoring these things those who are ignorant of our mind. And when temples, idols, groves, and anything of this kind, the power being given, are overthrown, although it is manifest, when we do this, that we do not honor them, but rather detest them, yet we ought not, for our own private and proper uses only, to usurp anything thence, so that it may appear that we destroy these things from piety, not from avarice. But when they are converted to common uses, not proper and private, or to the honor of the true God, that is done concerning them which is done concerning men themselves, when from sacrilegious and impious they are changed into the true religion. This God is understood to have taught by those testimonies which you yourself set down, when, concerning the grove of strange gods, he ordered wood to be applied for a holocaust[^2]; and concerning Jericho, that all the gold, silver, and bronze should be brought into the treasuries of the Lord[^3]. Wherefore that too which is written in Deuteronomy: *You shall not covet their silver or gold, nor take any of it for yourself, lest you transgress on account of it: for it is an abomination to the Lord your God: and you shall not bring an execration into your house, and you will be accursed, as it too is, and by offense you will offend, and by defilement you will be defiled with that abomination, because it is accursed*[^4]; sufficiently appears that either the private uses themselves in such things are forbidden, or that nothing be so brought in thence as to be honored: for then it is an abomination and an execration; not when the sacrilegious honor of such things is overthrown by a most open destruction.

**4.** But about the foods of idols, be certain that we ought to observe nothing more than what the Apostle commanded. And therefore recall his words about this matter, which, if they were obscure, we would expound according to our small measure. But he does not sin who afterward, not knowing, eats food which before he rejected as offered to an idol. A vegetable or any fruit born in any field is his who created it; because *the earth is the Lord's, and its fullness*[^5]; and *every creature of God is good*[^6]. But if that which is born in the fields is consecrated to an idol, or sacrificed, then it is to be reckoned among things offered to idols. For care must be taken, lest, if we think that we must not eat a vegetable which is born in the garden of an idol's temple, it follow that we should think the Apostle ought not to have taken food at Athens, because the city was Minerva's and consecrated to her divinity. This I would answer also about a well or a spring that is in a temple. But it moves one more, in truth, if something of the sacrifices is thrown into a spring or well. But the same reasoning holds for the air, which receives all that smoke, about which we spoke above: or if this is thought to differ on this account, that the sacrifice from which the smoke is mingled with the air is made not to the air itself, but to some idol or demon, whereas sometimes things are sent into the waters in sacrifice in such a way that sacrifice is made to the waters themselves; we surely do not on that account fail to use the light of this sun, because the sacrilegious cease not to sacrifice to it where they can. Sacrifice is made also to the winds, which nevertheless we use for our so great conveniences, when they themselves seem in some way to draw in and devour the smoke of those same sacrifices. If anyone doubts about some meat, whether it is offered in sacrifice, and it is not offered, if he holds that knowledge that it is not offered, and eats of it, he surely does not sin; because it neither is, nor is now thought, offered in sacrifice, even if before it was thought so. For it is not unlawful to correct thoughts from falsehood to truth. But if anyone thinks that to be good which is evil, and does it, in thinking this he surely sins. And these are all sins of ignorance, when anyone thinks that to be done well which is done ill.

**5.** About killing men, lest anyone be killed by them, the counsel does not please me; unless perhaps he be a soldier, or held by a public function, so that he does this not for himself, but for others, or for the city in which he himself also is, having received lawful power, if it befits his person. But those who are repelled by some terror, lest they do evil, something is perhaps even afforded to those very men. But it was said, *let us not resist evil*[^7], lest vengeance delight us, which feeds the mind on another's evil; not that we neglect the correction of men. Whence neither is he guilty of another's death who has drawn a wall around his own property, if someone, struck by its ruins, perishes. For neither is a Christian guilty, if his ox by goring, or his horse by kicking, kills someone; nor on that account ought Christians not to have oxen with horns, or a horse with hoofs, or a dog with teeth; nor indeed, because the apostle Paul took sufficient care that it be brought to the tribune's notice that ambushes were being prepared for him by certain abandoned men, and on this account received armed escorts[^8], would Paul, if wicked men had fallen upon those arms, recognize his crime in the shedding of their blood. Far be it that the things which we do, or have, for the sake of what is good and lawful, if through these any evil should happen to anyone beyond our will, should be imputed to us. Otherwise neither domestic nor rustic iron tools are to be had, lest anyone kill himself or another with them; nor a tree or a rope, lest anyone hang himself from it; nor is a window to be made, lest anyone cast himself down through it. Why should I recall more, when in recalling them I could not finish? For what is there in the good and lawful use of men, from which ruin too could not be inflicted?

**6.** It remains, unless I am mistaken, that we say something about that Christian traveler whom you recalled, overcome by the necessity of hunger, if he finds nothing anywhere except food set in an idol-shrine, where there is no other man, whether it is better for him to die of hunger than to take that for nourishment: in which question, since it is not consequent that that food be offered to an idol—for it could have been left, by forgetfulness or by will, by those who, turning aside from their journey, had refreshed their body there, or set there for any other cause whatever—I answer briefly. Either it is certain that it is offered to an idol, or it is certain that it is not, or it is unknown: if, then, it is certain that it is, it is better rejected by Christian virtue; but if either it is known not to be, or it is unknown, it is taken for the use of necessity without any scruple of conscience.
