# Letter 231

AUGUSTINE, A SERVANT OF CHRIST AND OF THE MEMBERS OF CHRIST, TO DARIUS, A SON, A MEMBER OF CHRIST, GREETINGS IN HIM.

**1.** That I received your letter gladly, you wished my reply to be the indication. Behold, I write back, and yet by my reply I cannot indicate this, whether by this one or by any others, whether I write briefly or most at length: for what cannot be indicated by words can be indicated neither by few words nor by many. And I indeed say too little, even if I speak much: but I would not grant to any eloquent man at all that he could, by any letter of his, however worded and however long, explain the affection which yours has wrought in me—which I cannot do, even if he could see it in my mind as I do. It remains, then, so to indicate to you what you wished to know, that in my words you may perceive even what they do not indicate. What, then, shall I say, except that I was delighted by your letter, delighted very, very much? The repetition of this word is not a repetition, but, as it were, a perpetual saying: because it could not come about that it should always be said, therefore it has come about that it should at least be repeated; for so, perhaps, can be said what cannot be said.

**2.** Here, if someone should ask what in your letter so greatly delighted me—whether the eloquence? I will answer, "No": and he perhaps will answer: "Then your praises"; but about these too I will answer, "No": and not because these things are not in that letter; for both the eloquence there is so great that it shines forth most clearly that you were born with an excellent genius and are sufficiently educated in such disciplines, and it is altogether full of my praises. "Then," someone says, "do these things not delight you?" On the contrary indeed. *For I have not,* as a certain one says, *a heart of horn*[^1], that I should not perceive these things, or perceive them without delight. These things too delight; but compared to that by which I said I was greatly delighted, what are these? For your eloquence delights me, because it is gravely sweet, or sweetly grave: but by my praises—since indeed I am neither delighted by all praises nor delighted from all, but by such as you judged me worthy of, and from such a one as you are, that is, those who love the servants of God for the sake of Christ—even by my praises in your letter I cannot deny that I was delighted.

**3.** Let grave and learned men see what they think of that Themistocles—if indeed I rightly recall the man's name—who, when at a banquet (which the renowned and learned of Greece were wont to do) he had refused to sing to the lyre, and on this account was held more unlearned, and had spurned that whole kind of pleasantry, it was said to him: *What then do you delight to hear?* To which he is reported to have answered: *My own praises.* Let them see, then, with what end and with what intention they have believed he said this, or he himself with what intention he said it. For he was, according to this world, a magnificent man. For even when it had been said to him: *What, then, do you know?* he said, *To make a commonwealth great out of a small one*[^2]. But as for what Ennius says—*All mortals wish themselves to be praised*[^3]—I think it is partly to be approved, partly to be guarded against. For just as truth is to be sought, which without doubt, even if it is not praised, is alone praiseworthy; so too that vanity which easily creeps in is to be fled in the praise of men: and this vanity is either when the good things themselves, which are worthy of praise, are not thought to be possessed, unless a man is praised by men; or when each one wishes also those things to be much praised in himself which are worthy either of slight praise, or even of blame. Hence Horace, more wakeful than Ennius, says: *Do you swell with the love of praise? There are sure expiations, which, the little book read three times with a pure heart, will be able to restore you*[^4].

**4.** Thus he thought the swelling from the love of human praise was to be charmed away, as it were, by medicinal words, like the bite of a serpent. So the good Master taught us through his Apostle, both that we do not for this reason act rightly, that we may be praised by men—that is, that we should not place the end of our rightness in the praises of men—and yet that, for the sake of those very men, we should seek the praises of men. For when the good are praised, it profits not those praised but those praising. For to the former, as far as concerns themselves, it suffices that they are good: but those for whom it is expedient to imitate the good are to be congratulated when the good are praised by them; since they thus indicate that those please them whom they truly praise. The Apostle therefore says in a certain place: *If I were pleasing men, I would not be a servant of Christ*[^5]: and the same one says in another place: *Please all men in all things, as I too please all in all things;* but adding the cause, *not seeking what is useful to me, but what is useful to many, that they may be saved*[^6]. Behold what he sought in the praise of men, where he also said: *For the rest, brothers, whatever things are true, whatever modest, whatever chaste, whatever holy, whatever most dear, whatever of good report, if there is any virtue, if there is any praise, think on these things: the things you have learned and received and heard and seen in me, do these; and the God of peace will be with you*[^7]. The other things, then, which I mentioned above, he embraced under the name of virtue, saying, *if there is any virtue:* but that which he subjoined, *whatever of good report,* he followed up with another single fitting word, by saying, *if there is any praise.* What he says, then—*If I were pleasing men, I would not be a servant of Christ*—is surely to be taken as if he had said: "If I did the good things I do with the end of human praise, I would swell with the love of praise." The Apostle, then, wished to please all, and rejoiced to please those—not by whose praises he swelled in himself, but whom, being praised, he edified in Christ. Why, then, should I not be delighted to be praised by you, since you are both a good man, so as not to deceive me, and you praise the things that you love, and that it is useful and wholesome to love, even if they are not in me? Nor does this profit you alone, but me also. For if they are not in me, I wholesomely blush, and burn that they may be. And through that which I recognize as mine in your praise, I rejoice that I have it, and that it, and I myself on account of it, are loved by you: but the things I do not recognize, I desire not only to obtain myself, but also that those who sincerely love me may not always be deceived in my praise.

**5.** Behold how many things I have said, and what that is which delighted me in your letter far more than your eloquence, far more than my praises, I have not yet said. But what do you think it is, O good man, except that I have made you, such a man, and unseen, my friend—if indeed I ought to say "unseen," whose not body but soul I saw in those very letters of yours, where about you I believed, not as before my brothers, but myself? For who you were I had indeed already learned, but what you were toward me I did not yet hold. From this friendship of yours I do not doubt that even my praises—the end with which they delight me I have said enough—will profit the Church of Christ far more richly; since you so regard, so read, so love, so proclaim even my labors in the defense of the Gospel against the remnants of the impious demon-worshipers, that in them I become the better known the nobler you yourself are: for, illustrious, you secretly make them illustrious, and, renowned, you declare them, and where you perceive they can profit, you will not at all allow them to be unknown. If you ask whence I know this: such you appeared to me in your letters. Hence now see how much those letters could delight me: if you think well of me, you consider how much the gains of Christ delight me. And now, as for the fact that you yourself, who, as you write, "could receive the laws of Christ from your parents, from your grandparents, and even to the last offspring of the line," yet signify that you have been helped, against the gentile rites, as never otherwise, by these same labors of mine; do I think too little how much good, to others—and to how many, how renowned, and how easily, and how wholesomely through them to the rest to whom such things are fitting—our writings can confer, with you commending and disseminating them? Or, thinking this, can I be flooded with the delightfulness of small or middling joys?

**6.** Since, then, I could not explain in words how great a delight I took from your letter, I have said whence they delighted me: now what I could not say enough—that is, how much they delighted me—I leave to you to conjecture. Take, therefore, my son, take, good man, and not on the surface but in Christian love, as a Christian; take, I say, the books you desired, of my *Confessions*: there look upon me, lest you praise me beyond what I am; there believe not others about me, but me; there attend to me, and see what I have been in myself, through myself; and if anything in me pleases you, praise there with me him whom I wished to be praised concerning me—for I did not wish myself to be praised. *For he made us, and not we ourselves*[^8]; but we had lost ourselves, yet he who made us remade us. But when you have found me there, pray for me that I may not fail, but be perfected; pray, son, pray. I feel what I say, I know what I ask; let it not seem to you unworthy, and as it were beyond your merits: you will defraud me of a great help, if you do not do it. Not only you, but all who from your mouth have loved me, pray for me: indicate to them that I have asked this of them; and if you attribute much to us, suppose that we have commanded what we ask, and yet give to those asking, or obey those commanding. Pray for us. Read the divine Scriptures, and you will find that our very rams, the Apostles, asked this of their sons, or commanded it to their hearers. I certainly, since you asked this of me for you, how much I do it, he sees who hears, who saw that I did it even before: but render also in this matter the return of love. We are your superiors; you are the flock of God[^9]: consider and see that our perils are greater than yours, and pray for us. For this is fitting both for us and for you, that we may render a good account of you to the chief of shepherds and the head of us all[^10], and that we may alike escape this world's more perilous flatteries than its troubles, except when its peace profits to this, that, as the Apostle admonished us to pray, *we may lead a quiet and tranquil life in all devotion and love*[^11]. For if devotion and love are lacking, what is tranquillity and quiet from those and from the other evils of the world, but the matter of luxury and perdition, whether the inducement or the support? That, then, we may have *a quiet and tranquil life in all devotion and love*—which I pray for you—pray for us, wherever you are, wherever we are: for nowhere is he not, whose we are.

**7.** I have sent also other books which you did not ask for, that I might not do only what you asked: *Faith in the Unseen, Patience, Continence, Providence,* and one large one *On Faith, Hope, and Charity*. All these, if you read them while you are within Africa, send your judgment about them; either send it to us, or leave it where it may be sent to us by the aged lord Aurelius: although wherever you are, we hope for your letters from there, and you for ours from here, while we are able. I received most gratefully what you sent; in which you deigned to help both my health, though bodily—since you wish me at any rate to be free for God without the impediment of bad health—and our library, that there may be means whence books may be either procured or restored. May the Lord repay you, both here and in the future world, with the good things he has prepared for such as he willed you to be[^12]. The pledge of peace deposited with you, and most sweet to us both, as it was greeted by me before, so now I ask to be greeted in return.
