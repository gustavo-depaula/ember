# Letter 139

To the deservedly distinguished lord, and much most dear and most longed-for son Marcellinus, Augustine sends greetings in the Lord.

**1.** The records which your Eminence promised I vehemently await, and I desire that they be recited in the church of Hippo at once, and, if it can be done, even through all the churches established in the diocese; so that men may hear, and fully recognize the confessors of iniquity—not the fear of God extorting repentance, but judicial diligence opening the hardness of most cruel hearts; whether of those who confessed about the homicide and about the blinded and maimed body of the priest; or of those who did not dare to deny that they could have known these things, although they said these displeased them, fleeing Catholic peace, as though lest they be polluted by the crimes of others, and persevering in that sacrilege of schism among so great a multitude of such monstrous criminals; or even of those who said that they would not withdraw from there, even when Catholic truth and the perversity of the Donatists had been shown them. It is no light thing that God willed to be done through your work. Would that you may so hear their causes frequently, and that their crimes and insane pertinacity may be so often disclosed, and that the same published records be brought to the knowledge of all! But as for what your Eminence wrote, that you doubt whether you ought to order the same records to be posted in the Theoprepia: let it be done, if a frequent multitude can flow together there: otherwise, another more frequented place is to be provided; yet it is in no way to be passed over.

**2.** The penalty indeed of those—although confessors of such great crimes—I ask you that it be apart from the punishment of death, both for the sake of our conscience, and for commending Catholic meekness. For the very fruit of their confession comes to us, because the Catholic Church has found where it may keep and exhibit its gentleness toward most atrocious enemies. For in so great a cruelty, whatever vengeance shall proceed apart from blood will appear great gentleness. Which, even if now to certain of our people, moved by that atrocity, seems unworthy, and as it were like to dissolution and negligence; yet, the stirrings of minds being over—which are wont to be more turbulently roused by more recent deeds—a notably brilliant goodness will appear, and on this account it will the more delight to read and show the same records, deservedly distinguished lord, and much most dear and most longed-for son. There is my holy brother and fellow bishop Boniface, and through the deacon Peregrinus, who set out with him, I have sent a memorandum; which hold as my presence. And what shall please you in common for the benefit of the Church, let this be done with the Lord helping, who is able mercifully to aid in so great evils. Just now Macrobius, their bishop, surrounded by bands of lost men of both sexes, goes about here and there, and has opened to himself the basilicas which the timidity—however slight—of the possessors had closed. But with the steward of the most illustrious Celer, Spondeus—whom I have commended to your love and much commend—being present, their audacity was in some way broken: but now, after he set out for Carthage, he has opened the basilicas even on his estates, gathers the peoples. With him too is that rebaptized deacon Donatus, when he was a tenant of the church, who was especially involved in that slaughter. What sort are not with him, when he too is with him? If the Proconsul, or both of you together, are about to bring forth a sentence against them, and perhaps he persists in wishing to avenge with the sword—although he is a Christian, and, as far as we could notice, is not prone to these torments; yet, if it be necessary, order even my letters to be alleged in the records, which I have thought should be sent to you, one each, about this matter. For I am wont to hear that it is in the power of the judge to soften a sentence, and to avenge more mildly than the laws command. But if he will not assent to this even by my letters, let him at least grant this, that they be received into custody, and we will take care to obtain this from the clemency of the Emperors, lest the sufferings of the servants of God—which ought to be glorious in the Church—be dishonored by the blood of enemies. For I know that, in the cause of the clerics of Anaunia, who, slain by the Gentiles, are now honored as martyrs, the Emperor, being asked, easily granted that those who had slain them, and were already held captive, should not be punished by a like penalty.

**3.** The books On the Baptism of Little Ones—when I had already sent the very codex to your Eminence—why I received them again from you, I have forgotten: unless perhaps, when I inspected them, I found them faulty, and wished to emend them, which, marvelously hindered, I have not yet done. The letter too, to be written to you and joined to these, which, when I was there, I had already begun to dictate, know to be thus, with a little added, unfinished. But if I could render you an account of all the days and lucubrations spent on other necessities, you would, gravely saddened, marvel how greatly the things which can in no way be deferred, and do not permit me to do those things into which you, by asking and admonishing, urge me, willing, and ineffably (because I cannot) grieving. For when I have a little leisure from the necessities of those men who so compel me that it is in no way lawful to avoid them, nor ought they to be despised, there are not lacking things which I set before what is to be dictated, so set in the joints of times that they bear no delay. As for me there was the abbreviation of the records of our conference, laborious enough, when I saw that no one wished to commit himself to so great a mound of writing by reading: as for me there was also the letter to the Donatist laymen themselves, about this same conference of ours, which I have just finished in some lucubrations: as the two letters, not brief; one to your Love, the other to the illustrious man Volusianus, which I believe you have already received: as now I have in hand the book to our Honoratus about certain five questions, which he set forth to me, and intimated by letter; to whom you see how little it behooves not to answer at once. For love, which like a nurse fosters her sons, not by the order of loving but by the order of succoring, sets the weaker before the stronger, whom she wishes to be such as those already are, whom, not by despising but by being confident about them, she meanwhile passes by. Such necessities, then, of dictating something which hinders me from those dictations for which I more burn, cannot be lacking to me, when a little space is scarcely given between the heaps of occupations, by which the desires or necessities of others drag us, compelled: and what I should do I altogether know not.

**4.** You have heard whence you should entreat the Lord with me: but also, that you so insistently and so frequently admonish me, I do not wish you to cease, doing not nothing. I too commend to your Excellency the Church established in Numidia, for whose necessities my holy brother and fellow bishop Delphinus was sent by my brothers and fellow bishops, laboring and sharing the danger there. Nor do I write more about this matter, since you will hear him in person. The rest you will find in the memoranda which I sent to the priest, whether now or through the deacon Peregrinus, lest it be necessary for me to repeat them so often. May your heart always be vigorous in Christ, deservedly distinguished lord, and much most dear and most longed-for son. I commend to your Eminence our son Ruffinus, a principal man of Cirta.
