# Letter 7

Augustine to Nebridius

**1. 1.** I shall pass over a preface and quickly begin what you now want me to say, especially since I shall not quickly leave off. It seems to you that there can be no memory without images or imaginary sights, which you chose to call by the name of imaginations: I think otherwise. First, then, we must see that we do not always remember things that pass away, but for the most part things that abide. Therefore, although memory claims for itself a tenacious grasp of past time, it nevertheless consists partly of things that desert us and partly of things deserted by us. For when I remember my father, I remember what has deserted me and now no longer exists; but when I remember Carthage, I remember what exists and what I myself deserted. In both of these kinds, however, memory holds the past. For I remember both that man and that city from the fact that I saw them, not from the fact that I see them.

**1. 2.** Here perhaps you ask: To what end is all this? especially since you observe that neither of these can come into memory except through that imaginary sight. But for me it is enough to have shown thus far, for the time being, that there can be said to be a memory even of those things that have not yet perished. But pay close attention, so that you may grasp how this helps me. Some bring a charge against that most noble discovery of Socrates, by which it is asserted that the things we learn are not, as it were, implanted in us as new, but are recalled to memory by recollection. They say that memory is of past things, but that these things which we learn by understanding remain forever—Plato himself being the authority—and cannot perish, and so are not past. But they do not notice that the vision is past by which we once saw these things with the mind; and because we have flowed down from that vision and have begun to see one thing in one way and another in another, we revisit those things by recollecting, that is, through memory. Therefore, if—to pass over other examples—eternity itself always abides and seeks no imaginary fictions as vehicles, so to speak, by which to come into the mind, and yet could not come unless we remembered it, then there can be a memory of certain things without any imagination.

**2. 3.** Now as for what seems to you—that the soul, even without having used the senses of the body, can imagine corporeal things—this is shown to be false in the following way. If the soul, before it uses the body for perceiving bodies, can imagine those same bodies, and was better disposed before it became entangled with these deceitful senses (which no sane person doubts), then the souls of sleepers are better disposed than those of the waking, and those of the frenzied better than those free from such a plague; for these are affected by the images by which they were affected before these most deceitful messengers, the senses. And either the sun that those people see will be truer than the one the sane and waking see, or false things will be better than true. And since these conclusions are absurd, as indeed they are, that imagination, my Nebridius, is nothing other than a blow inflicted through the senses—by which, not (as you write) a certain reminding takes place so that such things are formed in the soul, but rather the very introduction, or, to put it more expressly, the impression of this falsity. What truly moves you—how it comes about that we think of those faces and forms which we have never seen—moves you with good reason. And so I shall do what may stretch this letter beyond the usual length; but not with you, for whom no page is more welcome than one that brings me to you more talkative.

**2. 4.** All these images, which you, along with many, call imaginations, I see may be most conveniently and most truly distributed into three kinds: one of them is impressed from things sensed, another from things supposed, the third from things reckoned. Examples of the first kind are when the mind forms within itself your face, or Carthage, or our former friend Verecundus, or anything else of abiding or dead things which nevertheless I have seen and sensed. To the second kind belong those things which we suppose to have been so or to be so—as when for the sake of argument we ourselves invent certain things that in no way hinder the truth, or such things as we picture when we read histories, and when we either hear or compose or suspect fabulous tales. For I shape for myself, as I please and as it comes to mind, the face of Aeneas, of Medea with her winged serpents yoked to her chariot, of Chremes or of some Parmeno. To this kind also belong those things which, whether wise men wrapping some truth in such figures, or fools, the founders of various superstitions, have put forward as true: such as the Tartarean Phlegethon, and the five caverns of the race of darkness, and the northern pillar holding up the heaven, and a thousand other portents of poets and heretics. We also say, in arguing, 'suppose there are three worlds one above another, like this one' and 'suppose the earth is contained within a square figure' and the like. For all these we invent and suppose as the storm of our thinking happens to have it. Now as to the third kind of images, it has to do chiefly with numbers and dimensions: which is partly in the nature of things—as when the figure of the whole world is discovered, and an image follows this discovery in the mind of the one thinking—and partly in the disciplines, as in geometrical figures, in musical rhythms, and in the infinite variety of numbers; and although these are grasped as true, as I maintain, they nevertheless beget false imaginings which reason itself scarcely resists—though it is not even easy for the very discipline of argument to be free of this evil, since in our divisions and conclusions we imagine certain counters, as it were.

**2. 5.** In this whole forest of images, I believe that the first kind does not seem to you to pertain to the soul before it clings to the senses; and on this we need not argue any longer. Regarding the two remaining kinds, a question could still rightly be raised, were it not manifest that the soul is less liable to falsehoods when it has not yet undergone the vanity of sensible things and of the senses; but who could doubt that these images are far falser than those sensible things? For the things we suppose and believe, or invent, are in every respect wholly false, and surely, as you see, the things we see and sense are far truer. Now in that third kind, whatever corporeal extension I shall have shaped in my mind—although thought seems to have produced it by the principles of the disciplines, which by no means deceive—I prove by those same principles, when they argue, that it is false. Hence it comes about that I can in no way believe that the soul, not yet sensing through a body, not yet beaten by the most deceitful senses with their mortal and fleeting substance, lay in so great a disgrace of falsehood.

**3. 6.** Whence, then, does it come about that we think of things we have not seen? What do you suppose, except that there is a certain power of diminishing and enlarging implanted in the soul, which it must bring with it wherever it comes? This power can be observed especially in numbers. By it, for example, the image of a crow set before the eyes, as it were—which of course is known from what we have looked upon—is led, by taking away and adding certain things, to any image at all that has never been seen. By it, it comes about that, as such souls turn these things over in habitual fashion, figures of this sort rush into our thoughts as if of their own accord. Thus the imagining soul may, from those things which sense has brought into it, by taking away (as has been said) and adding, beget things which as wholes it has reached by no sense, though it has reached the parts of them in various things. So we, born and reared among inland peoples, could already imagine the seas, having seen water even in a small cup; whereas the taste of strawberries and cornel-cherries, before we tasted them in Italy, could in no way come into our minds. Hence it is that those blind from earliest age, when questioned about light and colors, do not find what to answer. For they undergo no colored images, who have sensed none.

**3. 7.** And do not wonder how it is that the things which are figured and can be invented in the nature of things are not first turned over by the soul, which is mingled and present in all things, though it has never sensed them from without. For we too, when by indignation or joy and the other motions of the mind of this sort we form in our body many expressions and colors, our thought does not first conceive that we can make such images. These follow in those wonderful ways—to be entrusted to your own reflection—when in the soul, without any figure of corporeal things, hidden numbers of falsehoods are set in motion. From which I would have you understand that, since you perceive there to be so many motions of the soul devoid of all those images about which you are now inquiring, the soul obtains a body by some other motion than by the thinking of sensible forms, which I judge it can in no way undergo before it uses a body and the senses. Therefore, by our friendship and by faith in the divine law itself, I would earnestly warn you, my dearest and most delightful friend, to couple no friendship with those infernal shades, and not to delay tearing apart that friendship which has been coupled. For in no way are the senses of the body resisted—which is for us a most sacred discipline—if we flatter the blows and wounds inflicted through them.
