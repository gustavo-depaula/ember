# Letter 89

TO THE MOST BELOVED LORD, AND HONORABLE AND ESTEEMED SON FESTUS, AUGUSTINE SENDS GREETING IN THE LORD.

**1.** If, for the sake of error and a damnable dissension and a falsity convicted in every way, men presume so much that they do not cease so boldly to lay snares and threaten the Catholic Church which seeks their salvation, how much more fair is it, and behooving, that those who assert the truth of Christian peace and unity—manifest even to all who dissemble and resist—should busy themselves insistently and tirelessly, not only for the protection of those who are already Catholics, but also for the correction of those who are not yet? For if obstinacy tries to have insuperable powers, how great a constancy ought to have, which, in that good which it does perseveringly and indefatigably, both knows that it pleases God, and doubtless cannot displease prudent men?

**2.** But what is more unhappy and more perverse than, as the Donatists do—who boast that they endure persecution—not only not to know to be confounded about the coercion of their iniquity, but even to wish to be praised; ignorant by a marvelous blindness, or by a damnable animosity dissembling that they know, that it is not the penalty that makes true martyrs, but the cause? And this indeed I would say against those whom the mere gloom of heretical error enwrapped, for which sacrilege they would pay most worthy penalties, and yet would not dare by any violent madness to injure anyone. But against these men, what shall I say, whose so pernicious perversity is either coerced by the terror of fines, or taught by exile how widely the Church is spread, as was foretold to come[^1]—which they prefer to attack rather than to acknowledge? And if the things they suffer through a most merciful discipline are compared with those deeds which they commit with frenzied rashness, who does not see which are rather to be called persecutors? Although evil sons, only by living so destructively, even if they lay no violent hands, persecute the piety of their parents more grievously than when the father or mother, the more they love them, the more they compel them, without any dissembling, to a good life.

**3.** There exist most firm documents of the public monuments, which you can read if you will—indeed, I ask and exhort that you read them—by which it is proved that the forebears of those who first divided themselves from the peace of the Church dared, of their own accord, through Anulinus, then proconsul, to accuse Caecilianus before the emperor Constantine. In which judgment, surely, if they had won, what was Caecilianus to suffer from the Emperor, except what he pronounced against these men after they were defeated? But evidently, if, with them accusing and prevailing, Caecilianus and his colleagues had been expelled from the seats they held, or even, persisting in their conspiracy, had been more grievously punished (for the royal censure could not despise the defeated and resisting), then these men would fan their providence and their solicitous care for the Church to be proclaimed with praises. But now, because they themselves were overcome, because they could by no means prove what they intended, if they suffer anything for their iniquity, they call it persecution; and not only do they not repress their abandoned fury, but they even seek the honor of martyrs: as though indeed the Christian Catholic emperors followed against their most obstinate iniquity anything other than the judgment of Constantine, before whom they were, of their own accord, the accusers of Caecilianus, whose authority they preferred to all the overseas bishops, so as to bring the case of the Church not to them, but to him; so as to accuse again before him the episcopal judgment given in the city of Rome, in which they were first defeated; so as to appeal to him from another episcopal judgment given at Arles: before whom, nevertheless, finally overcome, they remained in their perversity. I think that the devil himself, if by the authority of a judge whom he had of his own accord chosen, he were so often defeated, would not be so shameless as to persist in that case.

**4.** But let these be reckoned human judgments, and let it be said that they could have been circumvented and deceived, or even corrupted. Why, then, is the Christian world still accused, and defamed by I know not what crimes of traditors—the world which surely neither could nor ought to believe any but the chosen judges rather than the defeated litigants? Those judges have their case before God, whether good or evil: what did the Church, spread through the whole world, do, which is judged to be rebaptized by these men for no other reason than that, in that case in which it could not judge what was true, it thought it should believe rather those who could judge than those who, not even defeated, yielded? O great crime of all the nations, which God promised would be blessed in the offspring of Abraham[^2], and, as he promised, displayed! Which, when they say with one voice, Why do you wish to rebaptize us? are answered: Because you do not know who were the traditors of the holy books in Africa, and in that which you did not know, you chose to believe the judges rather than the accusers. If another's crime burdens no one, what does it concern the world what anyone committed in Africa? If an unknown crime burdens no one, whence could the world know either the crime of the judges or of the accused? Judge, you who have a heart. This is heretical justice: that, because the world does not condemn an unknown crime, the party of Donatus condemns the world unheard. But surely it suffices the world to hold the promises of God, and to see fulfilled in itself what the Prophets sang so long before; to recognize the Church in the same Scriptures, where its king Christ also is recognized. For where such things were foretold of Christ as we read fulfilled in the Gospel, there were foretold of the Church such things as we now discern fulfilled throughout the whole world.

**5.** Unless perhaps some prudent man will be moved by what they are wont to say about Baptism: that the baptism of Christ is true only when it is given by a just man—since the world holds even from this the most evident and evangelical truth, where John says: *He who sent me to baptize in water, he himself said to me: On whomever you see the Spirit descending like a dove, and remaining upon him, he it is who baptizes in the Holy Spirit*[^3]. Hence the secure Church does not place its hope in man, lest it fall into that sentence in which it is written: *Cursed is everyone who places his hope in man*[^4]; but places its hope in Christ, who so took the form of a servant that he did not lose the form of God[^5], of whom it was said: *He it is who baptizes.* Accordingly, any man whatever is the minister of his baptism—whatever burden he bears—not he, but the one upon whom the dove descended, he it is who baptizes. But so great an absurdity follows those who think vain things that they do not find how to flee from it. For when they confess that Baptism is ratified and true when some criminal among them baptizes, whose crimes are hidden, we say to them: Who then baptizes? And they have nothing to answer except, God; for they cannot say that an adulterous man sanctifies anyone. To whom we answer: If, then, when a manifestly just man baptizes, he himself sanctifies, but when a hidden iniquitous man baptizes, then not he, but God sanctifies, those who are baptized ought to wish to be baptized rather by hidden evil men than by manifest good men; for God sanctifies them much better than any just man whatever. But if it is absurd that anyone to be baptized should wish to be baptized rather by a hidden adulterer than by a manifest chaste man, it surely remains that whichever of the human ministers approaches, Baptism is ratified for this reason, that the one upon whom the dove descended, he himself baptizes.

**6.** And yet, though so perspicuous a truth strikes the ears and hearts of men, so great a gulf of evil habit has submerged some that they prefer to resist all authorities and reasons than to consent. But they resist in two ways: either by raging, or by growing slothful. What, then, shall the medicine of the Church do here, seeking the salvation of all with maternal love, as if heated amid the frenzied and the lethargic? Ought it, or can it, despise or desist? It must be troublesome to both, because it is an enemy to neither. For both the frenzied do not wish to be bound, and the lethargic do not wish to be roused; but the diligence of love perseveres—to chasten the frenzied, to goad the lethargic, to love both. Both are offended, but both are loved; both, while troubled, as long as they are sick, are indignant, but both, when healed, give thanks.

**7.** Finally, we do not, as they think and as they boast, receive them such as they were, but altogether changed; for they do not begin to be Catholics unless they have ceased to be heretics. For their sacraments, which are common to them with us, are not hostile to us; because they are not human, but divine. Their own error is to be taken away, which they evilly imbibed, not the sacraments which they likewise received, which they carry and have to their own punishment—the more unworthily they have them, yet they have them. And so, the error having been abandoned, the depravity of separation corrected, they pass over from heresy to the peace of the Church, which they did not have, and without which what they had was pernicious to them. But if, when they pass over, they are feigned, this is now not our judgment, but God's. And yet some, when they were thought feigned—because they passed over through the terror of the command to us—were afterward found, in certain temptations, to be such that they were preferred to certain old Catholics. Nothing, then, is done in vain, when it is done insistently. For the wall of hard habit is not stormed by human terrors alone; but the faith and understanding of the mind is instructed also by divine authorities and reasons.

**8.** Since these things are so, let your Benignity know that your men who are in the region of Hippo are still Donatists, and that your letters availed nothing among them. But why they did not avail, there is no need to write; rather, send some one of your own—whether of your household or your friends—to whom you can entrust this faith, who may come not to those places, but first to us, with them altogether unknowing, and, the matter having first been treated in counsel with us, may do what shall have seemed, with the Lord helping, ought to be done. For we do not act so much for them when we do this, as also for our own who are already made Catholics, to whom the nearness of those men is so hostile that it can in no way be despised by us. And this indeed I could write briefly; but I wished you to have something of our letters, by which you might not only yourself know the reason of my care, but might also have what to answer to anyone dissuading you from insistently spending labor on the correction of your own people, and disparaging us because we wish such things. But if I have done this superfluously, because you had already either learned these things, or yourself thought of them, or have been burdensome because, to one so occupied with public cares, I have thrust a lengthy letter, I ask you to grant pardon—provided, however, that you do not spurn what I have suggested and asked. So may the mercy of God protect you.
