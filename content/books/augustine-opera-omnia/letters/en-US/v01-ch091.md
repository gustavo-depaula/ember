# Letter 91

TO THE DISTINGUISHED LORD, AND DESERVEDLY HONORABLE BROTHER NECTARIUS, AUGUSTINE.

**1.** That, with your limbs now growing cold with age, your mind is fervent with love of your native land, I neither wonder at nor blame; and that you not only hold it in memory, but also demonstrate by your life and morals that there is no measure or limit to consulting one's native land for good men, I accept not unwillingly—indeed, even gladly. Hence we would wish you to have yourself such a citizen also of a certain higher native land—in whose holy love, according to our small measure, among those whom we counsel to lay hold of it, we are in peril and labor—that you would judge there to be no measure and limit of consulting for its little portion sojourning on this earth; becoming so much the better, the more you paid the duties owed to a better city, destined to find no end of rejoicing in its eternal peace, for whose temporal labors you would set yourself no end of consulting.

**2.** But until this comes to pass—for it is not to be despaired of, that you can acquire that native land, or already most prudently think of acquiring it, to which your father too, who begot you in this one, has gone before you—until this comes to pass, then, grant us pardon, if, on account of our native land which we desire never to leave, we sadden your native land which you desire to leave flourishing. About whose flowers, indeed, if we dispute with your prudence, it is not to be feared either that you will be persuaded with difficulty, or that it will not even readily occur to you in what way a city ought to flourish. That most renowned poet of your literature recalled certain flowers of Italy[^1]; but in your native land we have not so much experienced by what men that land flourished, as by what arms it burned—indeed, not by arms, but by flames; nor that it burned, but that it set fire. If so great a crime goes unpunished, with no worthy correction of the depraved, do you think you will leave your native land flourishing? O flowers plainly not of fruits, but of thorns! Compare now whether you would rather your native land flourish by piety or by impunity, by corrected morals or by secure outrages. Compare these, and see whether you overcome us in the love of your native land; whether you desire it to flourish more and more truly than we do.

**3.** Look a little at those very books On the Republic, from which you drank in that affection of a most loving citizen, that there is no measure or limit to consulting one's native land for good men. Look, I beg you, and discern with how great praises frugality and continence are there proclaimed, and fidelity toward the marriage bond, and chaste, honest, and upright morals, in which when a city excels, it is truly to be said to flourish[^2]. But these morals are taught and learned in the Churches growing throughout the whole world, as in holy auditoriums of the peoples; and especially the piety by which the true and truthful God is worshiped, who both commands that all these things—by which the human mind is instructed and fitted for the divine fellowship, to inhabit the eternal and heavenly city—be undertaken, and also grants that they be fulfilled. Hence it is that he both foretold that the images of the many false gods would be overthrown, and commanded that they be overthrown[^3]. For nothing renders men so unsociable by the perversity of life as the imitation of those gods, such as they are described and commended in their literature.

**4.** Finally, those most learned men, who sought out, or even described, the republic and the earthly city, such as it seemed to them it ought to be, in domestic disputations rather than established and formed it by public actions, proposed for the instructing of the disposition of youth the excellent and praiseworthy men, whom they thought such, rather than their gods, to be imitated. And truly that young man of Terence, who, looking at a picture painted on the wall, where there was a painting of the adultery of the king of the gods, kindled the lust by which he was carried away even with the goads of so great an authority, would in no way slip into that disgrace either by coveting, or be immersed in it by perpetrating, if he had preferred to imitate Cato rather than Jupiter; but how could he do that, when in the temples he was compelled to adore Jupiter rather than Cato? But these things from comedy, by which the luxury and sacrilegious superstition of the impious might be convicted, perhaps we ought not to bring forward. Read, or recall, in those same books how prudently it is discussed that the writings and performances of comedies could in no way have been received, unless the morals of those receiving them were in accord: thus, by the authority of most renowned men excelling in the republic and disputing about the republic, it is confirmed that the most wicked men are made worse by the imitation of gods—not, indeed, true ones, but false and fictitious.

**5.** But, you say, all those things which were written of old about the life and morals of the gods are to be understood and interpreted by the wise far otherwise. And yet that such wholesome interpretations are recited in the temples to the peoples gathered together—we heard yesterday and the day before. I ask you, is the human race so blind against the truth that it does not perceive things so open and manifest? In so many places Jupiter is painted, cast, hammered, sculpted, written, read, acted, sung, danced, committing so great adulteries; how much would it have been, that at least in his own Capitol he be read forbidding these things? These evils, most full of dishonor and impiety, if, with no one forbidding, they grow hot among the peoples, are adored in the temples, laughed at in the theaters; if, when they immolate victims to these gods, even the cattle of the poor are laid waste; if, when actors perform and dance these things, the patrimonies of the rich are poured out—are cities said to flourish? Of these flowers, plainly, no fertile earth, no opulent virtue, but that goddess Flora has been found a worthy mother, whose scenic games are celebrated with such more lavish and licentious turpitude that anyone may understand what sort of demon it is, which cannot be appeased otherwise than that there—not birds, not quadrupeds, not, finally, human blood, but, much more wickedly, human modesty—should perish, as if immolated.

**6.** I have said these things because you wrote that, the nearer your age is to its end, you desire to leave your native land safe and flourishing. Let all those vain and insane things be taken away; let men be turned to the true worship of God and to chaste and pious morals; then you will see your native land flourishing, not in the opinion of fools, but in the truth of the wise—when this native land of your carnal generation shall be a portion of that native land into which we are born, not by the body, but by faith, where all the saints and faithful of God, after the as-it-were wintry labors of this life, will flourish in unending eternity. And so it is in our heart neither to lose Christian gentleness, nor to leave in that city a pernicious example of imitation to others. In what way we may do this, God will be present, if he is not too grievously angry with them. Otherwise, both the gentleness which we desire to preserve, and the discipline which we strive to use moderately, can be hindered, if something else pleases God in secret—whether judging this so great evil to be struck with a sharper scourge, or even more vehemently angry, if he should will it, for a time, to be unpunished, with them neither corrected nor turned to himself.

**7.** Your prudence in a manner prescribes to us concerning the episcopal person, and you say that your native land has fallen through no light error of its people: which indeed, if we measure it by the rigor of public law, ought to be struck with a severer censure; *But it is not lawful for a bishop,* you say, *except to impart salvation to men, and to be present for cases for a better state, and before the omnipotent God to deserve pardon for the faults of others.* This we altogether try to preserve, that no one be struck with a severer censure, neither by us, nor by any other with us interceding; and we desire to impart salvation to men, which is placed in the felicity of living rightly, not in the security of doing evil. We also insist on deserving pardon not only for our own faults, but also for those of others—which we can obtain only for the corrected. You add too, and say: *With all the supplication I can, I beseech that, if the matter is to be defended, the harmless be defended, that trouble be separated from the innocent.*

**8.** Receive briefly what was committed, and yourself distinguish the guilty from the innocent. Against the most recent laws, on the calends of June, the sacrilegious solemnity of a pagan festival was conducted, with no one forbidding, with so insolent an audacity that—what was not done even in the times of Julian—a most petulant crowd of dancers passed through that very street right before the doors of the church. When the clergy tried to forbid this most unlawful and most unworthy thing, the church was stoned. Then, after about eight days, when the bishop had recounted the most well-known laws to the town council, and while they were, as it were, disposing to fulfill what was commanded, again the church was stoned. The next day, when our people wished to say before the records, in order to impose fear on the abandoned, the public laws were denied them. And on that same day, that they might at least be divinely terrified, hail was rendered for the stonings; which being over, immediately they brought a third stoning, and finally fires upon the ecclesiastical buildings and men; one of the servants of God, who, wandering, could meet them, they killed, while the rest partly hid where they could, partly fled by whatever way they could, when meanwhile the bishop, thrust together and confined in a certain place, concealed himself, where he heard the voices of those seeking him to death, and reproaching themselves that, with him not found, they had perpetrated so great a crime for nothing. These things were done from about the tenth hour until no small part of the night. No one tried to restrain, no one to come to the aid, of those whose authority could be weighty, except one stranger, through whom both very many servants of God were freed from the hands of those trying to kill them, and many things were extorted from the plunderers; through whom it was made clear how easily those things would either not be done at all, or, once begun, would desist, if the citizens, and especially the leading men, had forbidden them to be done and accomplished.

**9.** Accordingly, in that whole city you will perhaps be able to distinguish not the innocent from the guilty, but the less guilty from the more guilty. For those are in a small fault who, deterred by fear—and especially lest they offend those whom they knew to be very powerful in that town and enemies of the Church—did not dare to bring aid; but all are wicked by whom, even if not doing or instigating, yet wishing, these things were committed; more wicked, those who committed them; most wicked, those who instigated them. But let us suppose the instigation to be a suspicion, not the truth, and let us not discuss those things which can in no way be found out except by the torments of those through whom they are investigated. Let us also grant pardon to the fear of those who thought God should rather be beseeched for the bishop and his servants than the powerful enemies of the Church should be offended. Do you think that those who remain are to be coerced by no discipline, and do you judge that so monstrous an example of fury is to be set forth unpunished? We do not study to feed our anger by avenging the past; but we busy ourselves by mercifully consulting for the future. Evil men have wherein they may, even through Christians, be struck not only gently, but also usefully and wholesomely. For they have that they live with body unharmed; they have wherewith they live; they have wherewith they live evilly. Let the first two be safe, that there be those who may repent; this we wish, this, as far as in us lies, we insist on even with labor expended. But the third, if God will, to be cut away like something rotten and harmful, he will punish very mercifully. But if he should will either more, or should not even permit this, the reason of a higher and surely juster counsel is in his hands: by us the care and duty must be expended, as far as it is granted to see, beseeching him that he approve our mind, by which we wish all to be consulted for, and that he allow nothing to be done through us which he knows far better than we do not to be expedient for us and for his Church.

**10.** Lately, when we were at Calama, that our people in so grave a grief might either be consoled when afflicted, or calmed when inflamed, as much as we could, what we judged behooved at the time, we dealt with the Christians. Then we also admitted the pagans themselves, the head and cause of so great an evil, who asked that they be seen by us, so that on this occasion we might admonish them what they ought to do, if they were wise—not only for removing the present anxiety, but also for inquiring into perpetual salvation. They heard many things from us; they also themselves asked much; but far be it that we be such servants as it delights to be asked by those by whom our Lord is not asked. Hence you perceive, by the vivacity of your mind, that we must strive for this, with Christian gentleness and moderation preserved: that either we deter others from imitating their perversity, or we wish others to imitate their correction. The damages that were inflicted are either tolerated by Christians, or repaired through Christians. The gains of souls, which we gape after to acquire, even with peril of blood, we desire both to come forth more profitably in that place, and not to be hindered in other places by that example. May the mercy of God grant us to rejoice over your salvation.
