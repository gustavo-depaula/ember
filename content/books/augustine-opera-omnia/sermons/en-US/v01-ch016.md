# Sermon 14/A: &lt;A Sermon on the Verse of the Psalm:

*your Discipline Has Directed Me Unto the End,*

*And Your Discipline Itself Will Teach Me*&gt;

**1.** Brothers, let us attend to what we have sung. From the same source the psalm was spoken, from there the psalm is proposed. With the Lord's help let us say something from what is written, and what is fresh in our voices and ears: *Your discipline has directed me unto the end, and your discipline itself will teach me*[^1]. For we ask toward what end we are directed, and what the discipline is that directs us, and what the teaching of this matter is. We wish to be directed, and perhaps we ask whither we are directed. We have heard, *unto the end*. But "end," when it is said, is either of consuming or of consummating. Consuming makes a thing not be, consummating makes it be perfect. Food is ended by eating, a garment is ended by weaving. Food is ended by eating, so that it is not; a garment is ended by weaving, so that it is perfect. Toward such an end, then, without doubt we seek to be directed, by which we may be perfected, not by which we may be consumed.

**2.** What, then, is this end, and what discipline? The end is Christ, the discipline is the law. Hear the Apostle: *The end of the law is Christ, unto righteousness for everyone who believes*[^2]. This, then—that we may now say it more plainly and expound what we have sung—this is, *Your discipline has directed me unto the end*[^3], that is, your law has directed me unto the end, your law has directed me unto Christ. The Jews have the law, but it did not direct them unto the end. They came indeed to the end, because they saw Christ, but by stumbling at the final stone they transgressed, by stumbling at Christ they fell and fell outside the end. For they rejected him to whom they ought to have come. Finally, that very end became for those not believing a stone of stumbling, for those believing the cornerstone. *To you,* says the apostle Peter, *who have believed, the stone which the builders rejected has become the head of the corner, but to those not believing a stone of stumbling and a rock of offense*[^4].

**3.** Who the end is, we know; what it is to be directed unto the end, we have already spoken last Sunday. For to be directed unto the end is to come to Christ, that is, to believe in Christ. Are the same things to be repeated rather often? For you are to be reminded, not burdened. Although to say the same things to you is indeed not irksome to us, and safe for you. For we have treated this on account of those who think it suffices them to believe and love to live badly, thinking that by believing they will be saved and by living badly they will not perish. This, then, we treated, that there ought to be a difference between the faith of Christians and the faith of demons, because they too believe—they said to Christ, *We know who you are*[^5]. They believed Christ, but they did not believe in Christ. Whence, then, is the one who believes Christ distinguished from the one who believes in Christ? Because everyone who believes in Christ at once without doubt believes Christ, but not everyone who believes Christ at once believes in Christ. And the Son of God defined the whole work of God in this, in saying, *This is the work of God, that you believe in him whom he sent*[^6]. By what end, except himself? Do not seek an end besides himself, lest by seeking an end besides himself you be consumed, not consummated. For what is an end, except that to which we wish to come, to stand and seek nothing beyond? For if you come, but still seek, you have not yet arrived at the end. Therefore to arrive at the end is to arrive there where you say, "It is enough."

**4.** When Philip thought the Father alone was this, and said, *Lord, show us the Father, and it is enough for us*[^7], the Lord showed him that the end is God, but God the Trinity. Accordingly, when you say, "The end is Christ," you do not thereby separate God the Father, nor, when you say, "The end is God the Father," do you thereby separate Christ. He who wished, as it were, to separate, thinking that Christ was only this which he knew with his eyes, said joyfully, *Show us the Father, and it is enough for us*. What is, *It is enough for us*? There is the end of desire: we will seek nothing further; there will be our satiety, there we will say, "It is enough, I want nothing more." Why this? Because we now know you, show us the Father. For when we see you and do not see him, it is not enough for us. We rejoice that we see you, but show him, and it is enough for us: we will seek nothing more. And the Lord to him, because he himself is the end, because he himself is the one who satisfies, that he might take from him what he thought—for he thought that he did not see the Son of God, when he saw the form of a servant—said to Philip, *So long a time I am with you, and you have not known me?*[^8] When you seek the end and do not see what you see—therefore certainly you seek the end, because you do not see that the end stands before you—*Do you not believe,* he says, *that I am in the Father and the Father in me?*[^9]

**5.** To this we too are exhorted by the reading that follows, which today was recited from the Gospel: *This is the will of the Father, that whoever sees the Son and believes in him may have eternal life. And I will raise him up on the last day*[^10]. You were seeking the end: do you seek more than eternal life? *This is the will of the Father, that whoever sees the Son and believes in him may have eternal life. And I will raise him up on the last day*. What do we say, my brothers? For this vision what eyes do we seek? For see another definition to be shown today, like indeed to the past one, of which we have already spoken: *This is the work of God, that you believe in him whom he sent*[^11]. And here what does he say? *This is the will of the Father*, as though he said, *This is the work of God*. For whoever does the work of God, does the will of God. *This is the will of the Father, that whoever sees the Son and believes in him may have eternal life*. He said two things. *This is the work of God, that you believe in him whom he sent*. But here he added, *whoever sees and believes*. The Jews saw, but did not believe: they had one, the other was lacking; how could they come to eternal life without the other? If, then, those who saw therefore did not arrive because they did not believe, we who have believed but have not seen—if those two merit eternal life, to see and to believe, and those to whom one is lacking do not arrive at the reward of eternal life—what shall we do? One was lacking to the Jews, the other to us. To them was present seeing, but believing was lacking. To us believing is present, but seeing is lacking. For according to this, that believing is present and seeing is lacking, in prophecy we have been blessed by the Lord himself, since, when Thomas, one of the twelve, felt the scars by touching them...
