# Sermon 162/C: (Dolbeau 10)

**1.** We know, brethren, and with sure love we are confident that you rejoice with us when you see justice in us. For it follows that, when God's holy ones see just priests, they rejoice in their shepherds and in turn gladden them by their own conduct. This too we have just now sung to the Lord our God, with one voice and one heart: *Let your priests be clothed with justice, and your holy ones shall exult with exultation*[^1]; seeing them clothed with justice, they will rejoice with true joy and with wholly entire love, with no flattery. Therefore, that you may exult with exultation, holy ones of God, we must be clothed with justice and offer you an example for every good work. But you who wish to rejoice over us, pray for us. For justice must be put on, as we have sung. And who gives this garment, but he who, after the error and ruin of the younger son, brought forth the first robe[^2]? For by the very fact that justice is commended as a garment, this is signified, that we do not have it from ourselves . . .

**2.** (*the leaf being torn, the text is missing, of which a small fragment was preserved by Bede*) *A bishop, therefore, must be irreproachable*[^3]. Who denies this? But since a bishop must be irreproachable, is it fitting that a Christian be reproachable? "Bishop" is a Greek word, but in Latin it can be said "overseer" or "visitor." We are bishops, but with you we are Christians. We are properly called from the visitation, all in common from the anointing. If the anointing is common, the struggle too is common. But why do we visit, if there is no good that we may see in you?

**3.** . . . be found. If we wished this, if we dared this by a headlong fall and rashness, surely we would be terrified by today's reading. For we all heard, when the letter of the apostle Paul to the Galatians was read: *When Peter had come to Antioch, I withstood him to his face, because he was reproachable*[^4]. Peter *was reproachable,* and shall I dare to profess myself altogether without any reproach? Shall I, a weak sheep, not dread this whirlpool, when I see the ram that dried the fleeces? He himself rose bravely from this whirlpool; if I fall in, who will find me? I will avoid, then, the swelling of this wave, I will not throw myself in, I will not hurl myself down, even if I were altogether conscious that nothing could be found in me by men for which I might be reproached. The eyes of God are one thing, the eyes of him are another, to whom it was truly said: *No living man will be justified in your sight*[^5]. And yet Peter, as we heard just now when it was read, had been reproached even by men. Whence the Christian mind ought to be wholly a stranger to this rashness, lest it dare to profess a life of such a kind in which not even men find what they could reproach. Let even a man reproach, if there is something to reproach; let him by all means reproach; if I take it badly, let him reproach twice. For Peter brought it about that he was reproached once, when the one who reproached him was borne with an even mind; he did not offer an example, as Christ did, of every kind of perfection, but he offered an example of all humility. He received the reprover with an even mind, the one who in the apostolate did not precede but followed. Let the apostle Paul grant pardon: what he did is easy; what Peter did is hard. We live in human affairs, we are surrounded by daily experiences: I have often seen a man reproaching; I do not know whether I have ever seen one bearing his reprover with an even mind. Paul, then, acted more sincerely, but Peter more wonderfully. But I do not know whether it is even more sincere to recognize another's fault than to acknowledge one's own willingly.

**4.** But it troubles some that Peter is said to have been reproached, it troubles them and they dispute; but they are not to be despised, if it is not their own presumption that troubles them, but love for Peter. They are unwilling to believe that he was truly reproached; they think this was pretended, shadowed forth to the eyes of men. "Within," they say, "one thing was being done, another was being shown to the people." O man, you fear the fault of one: do you not wish me to fear the deceit of both? Out of love for Peter you do not believe he was reproached. You, out of love for Peter, do not believe he was reproached; I, out of love for Paul and Peter, believe he was reproached. I am unwilling to believe of the apostles that they did one thing within and held out another to the people. We are bishops, we follow their footsteps according to our strength: I do not wish it to be permitted us to deceive you. If one thing is done within and another is held out to the people, what holiness is not feared? We neither wish to deceive you nor to be deceived by you. For if you think that we deceive you and we think that you deceive us, where is the love that *believes all things?* *Love,* it says, *believes all things*[^6]. That it may possess them, it believes, not that it may be deceived.

**5.** "But answer: What did Paul reproach in Peter?" What but what he himself said, he himself wrote? He himself put the letter on record for memory, he himself propagated it to posterity to be read in the Church. What in the divine books do I believe more securely, if I do not believe what is written in that letter? It is an apostolic letter, a canonical letter, a letter of Paul, who labored more than all of them, yet not he, but the grace of God with him. Therefore it is a letter of the grace of God. And if we recall who was speaking in him, it is a letter of Christ. *Do you wish,* he says, *to receive proof of him who speaks in me, Christ?*[^7] Hear and fear. He says *proof,* not fiction. But if you think this too little, hear the very preacher, indeed the one who calls God to witness. What he was setting forth he began to narrate thus, as if foreseeing that there would be certain disputers: *But the things I write to you, behold, before God, that I do not lie*[^8]. Does he, then, calling God to witness thus, lie, when even without God's witness *the mouth that lies,* he says, *kills* not the body but *the soul*[^9]? I beg you: do not so love Peter as to kill Paul; I beg you: let not Paul be killed in his soul for Peter's sake—they were slain together in the flesh for Christ. And again you ask and say to me: "What did Paul reproach in Peter?" Again I answer you: This Paul reproached in Peter, which he said and wrote. Under God's witness Paul narrated that he had reproached Peter: what do you ask of me? The letter is read by all: recall it with me. *When Peter,* he says, *had come to Antioch, I withstood him to his face, because he was reproachable. For before certain men came from James, he ate with the gentiles. But when they had come, he withdrew, fearing those who were of the circumcision, and the rest of the Jews also pretended along with him, so that even Barnabas was led into their pretense*[^10]. See, you have heard what Paul reproached in Peter. Let us expound it, and this I will do with the Lord's help, that you may understand. For I will do it not for your sake, but for the sake of those who hear both me and you.

**6.** The sacraments of the Jews—namely circumcision, the keeping of the Sabbath, the avoidance of certain foods, and the rest of this kind—were given by God, written in the law, commanded by God as a figure of things to come. These were not like the abominable sacrileges of the gentiles, these were not like the sacrifices of demons, this was not like the worship of idols. God had commanded this to his people through Moses, the one God, the true God, that God who said: *I am who I am*[^11]. But *after the fullness of time came, God sent his Son, made of a woman, made under the law*[^12], for he was circumcised. But *of a woman* a virgin, indeed according to the custom of Hebrew speech: for unless a virgin too were called a woman, Eve would not first be formed in a woman. *After,* then, *in the fullness of time God sent his Son, made of a woman* through whom he made the woman, *made under the law* through whom he gave the law, *that he might redeem those who were under the law, that we might receive the adoption of sons*[^13], the shadows began to be unnecessary, the light having come; yet not because they were not necessary were they now, like the sacrileges of the gentiles, sacred things to be condemned. It was not to be said to a Jew, "Do not circumcise, because you have believed in Christ," as it was to be said to a pagan made a Christian, "Do not sacrifice to idols, because you have believed in Christ." For if you set up two things—the sacrifice of the pagans and the sacrament of the Jews—one of these was never necessary, always ruinous; but the other was once necessary, afterward not necessary, but for those who then received the Gospel from among the Jews, not yet ruinous. Since, then, even those sacraments of the Jews were not necessary after the coming of Christ, but through them was foretold the one through whom they would not be necessary, they were to be ended with honor, not rejected with horror. Since these things were so, the apostle Paul, who had been sent especially to preach the Gospel to the gentiles, ought to have borne especial care in his desire that the salvation of the gentiles not be hindered by the sacraments of the Jews. Therefore, with Christ speaking this in him, with God breathing and revealing this in him, he decreed at that time, in the newness of the Gospel, to forbid none of the Jews from these sacraments, to compel none of the gentiles to these sacraments. Thus those, like a father's funeral rites to be quickly abolished, like bodies now without the soul of prophecy, were to be led with respect as it were to the tomb; yet, because they were unaccustomed and dead, he judged that they must by no means be laid on gentile shoulders.

**7.** What sin, then, there was at that time in those sacraments, for which Peter was reproached, attend, and aid our powers, perhaps less than fit to break and resolve so great a mass of a question, which then agitated the apostles themselves; but aid us: I beseech you. You have indeed already heard much, but as new men hear what you more wish to hear, and I stand speaking to you and grow weak. Surely, since the question is as it were set before you anew, our narrative seems to bear on this. For now already the one who was questioning me a little before, before I set forth by my narrative what I wished—what Paul reproached in Peter—made more cautious by my narrative, says this: "If at that time abstinence from such sacraments was not laid on those who had come from among the Jews, and the observance of such sacraments was not laid on the gentiles, Peter, who did such things, came from among the Jews; how did Paul rightly reproach him?" I say, brethren, what the very words indicate. Paul did not reproach this in Peter, that he observed the Jewish sacraments, but that he laid them on the gentiles. For a Jew who then believed in Christ, if he wished to observe those things, was not forbidden; if he did not wish, was not compelled. But of the gentiles, whoever had believed, when would he wish to be circumcised, when he heard that this was not necessary to salvation? Perhaps the Jews themselves would scarcely be circumcised, unless infants underwent this. But what was it to compel the gentiles to observe those sacraments, in which we said Peter was reproached by Paul—if perhaps someone asks, what was it to compel them? Were they held unwilling, bound, and circumcised? Far from it! What, then, was it to compel, what do we think, but to say to them: "You will not be able to be saved, unless you observe those legal precepts, as the Jews do"? For by this proposed condition, because they were seeking salvation and were told that they could not otherwise attain salvation, they were compelled, unwilling, to observe those sacraments, not because they loved them, but because they desired salvation.

**8.** That moderation, then—that neither the Jews be forbidden nor the gentiles compelled—was preserved by the apostles and decreed in council. For when this question agitated many, troubled many, they came together at Jerusalem, and with all the apostles and elders of the Church gathered, that is, the presbyters and all the preachers of the Gospel and overseers of the Churches, by common council, with the Lord surely inspiring, it was established that neither should the Jews be forbidden these things, nor the gentiles compelled to them. Many recall that this is written in the Acts of the Apostles; let those who do not recall it read it. This way, then, was most temperate, most religious, most cautious. If they had commanded those things to be at once so rejected, like the sacrifices of idols, the one who had commanded them would not be believed to be the true God. This, then, Paul reproached in Peter, not because he himself observed these things, but because he was driving the gentiles to them. How driving? By consenting to those, though in pretense, who said the gentiles could not otherwise be saved unless they observed the Jewish sacraments. This too is written in the Acts of the Apostles, narrated in the same book: There came, it says, to Antioch certain men from James, that is, from Jerusalem, namely Jews, who had now believed in Christ, and they said to the brethren from among the gentiles who were coming: "You cannot otherwise be saved, unless you observe the legal precepts." This is to compel: to say that they cannot otherwise be saved.

**9.** Against this the apostle Paul fought with most ardent zeal. He was removing this stone of dreadful pressure, that the wretched gentiles might at last rise again as from the tomb of Lazarus. For if he reproached that observance, not the compulsion, he would reproach even himself. For he himself too undertook them, and celebrated certain Jewish sacraments at Jerusalem with the Jews in the temple; but, as one coming from among the Jews, he observed these, he did not compel the gentiles to them. Coming from among the Jews, Paul, the evangelist of the gentiles, both the decreer and the fulfiller of the holy counsel of the apostles, showed rather by his own example what pleased the Holy Spirit in such a question, what he decreed and established. For James said to him, when he had now arrived at Jerusalem: "Many," he says, "think that you oppose the ancestral legal observances and are an enemy and foe of the law. *What, then? Hear me.* There are here certain men who have come to undertake a purification: purify yourself with them, that all may know"—it is not said "that all may think": for devotion was commanded, not pretense suggested—"that all," he says, "may know that you are a zealot of the law and of the ancestral observances." This Paul did as a Jew from among the Jews, yet not that he might be justified by those very sacraments, but lest he be thought to condemn them.

**10.** Therefore he also did this. For he even circumcised Timothy, born of a Jewish mother, on account of the scandal of the Jews—Timothy being willing, not compelled—that through him too he might show that he did not condemn those things, but did not lay them on the gentiles as necessary to salvation. But after he had circumcised Timothy by this indifference which I have often commended and which I think is now aptly understood by you, certain men from among the Jews, who did not lay aside that old skin and therefore wished the gentiles to be circumcised, because they said that without those sacraments there could be no salvation for them. Whence a heresy too arose: for even today there are some who both believe in Christ and circumcise their sons, that is, who believe that they cannot have salvation without those sacraments. When, then, by this indifference of devotion, not by necessity, he had circumcised Timothy, certain of these, as I said, placing salvation there, which displeased Paul, for which he reproached Peter, began to boast to deceive others, that Paul too held this. They said: "What we too say, this he also believes, that without these sacraments there can be no salvation. For if he does not believe this, why did he circumcise Timothy?" When Paul heard this—Paul, who had done it by liberty, not by necessity, on account of the scandal of the Jews, not on account of Timothy's salvation—he saw that they had taken occasion for another sort of preaching and for stirring up an evil suspicion against Paul, and he was unwilling to circumcise Titus. But it appears why he was willing for the one, why he was unwilling for the other. He was willing for the one on account of the scandal of the Jews, he was unwilling for the other on account of the occasions of those who believed badly.

**11.** And see how evidently and clearly he commends this in his letter. *But not even Titus,* he says, *since he was a Greek,* like Timothy, *was compelled to be circumcised*[^14]. And as if it were said to him: "Why was Titus not compelled to be circumcised, when Timothy, though likewise a Greek, was compelled to be circumcised?" Why? Do you wish to hear? *On account of the false brethren stealthily brought in, who slipped in to spy out our liberty*[^15]. In circumcising Timothy, then, there was not necessity, but liberty; but in Peter the necessity was reproached, not the liberty. *On account of the false brethren stealthily brought in,* he says, *who slipped in to spy out our liberty, that they might reduce us to servitude.* *That they might reduce us,* he says, *to servitude,* by making out of liberty a necessity, *to whom we yielded in subjection not even for an hour*[^16]. To what end? *That the truth of the Gospel might remain among you*[^17]. What is *the truth of the Gospel?* That a Jew then was not to be forbidden, a gentile not to be compelled; the sacraments of the Jews not to be condemned for the Jews, not to be laid on the gentiles. What is *the truth of the Gospel?* That one can attain to salvation through Christ without those sacraments. This those men denied, to whom Peter consented in pretense. And therefore it is added: *Those other Jews consented in pretense, so that even Barnabas was led into their pretense. But when I saw,* he says, *that they did not walk rightly toward the truth of the Gospel*[^18], as there he says: *That the truth of the Gospel might remain among you.* You hear the truth commended everywhere, and you suspect deceit everywhere, so everywhere: even in the apostles. He saw, then, *that they did not walk rightly toward the truth.* He saw, beyond this, that they thought those sacraments were necessary to the salvation of the gentiles. This is not *the truth of the Gospel.* Those things were no longer necessary to salvation: perhaps once, when they foretold that Christ would come. The Petrine knife was once necessary, before the rock itself came.

**12.** *When I saw,* he says, *that they did not walk rightly toward the truth of the Gospel, I said to Peter before all*[^19]. *I said to Peter:* Paul to Peter, the lesser to the greater, the follower to the one who preceded; and *I said* and *to Peter* and *before all:* great confidence, but great patience on his part. Since you have made yourselves attentive, let us hear what you said to Peter, Paul, and where. Let us understand what you did, what you arranged, because nowhere did you wish to be a liar. *I said,* he says, *to Peter before all: "If you, being a Jew"*—what is "*since you are a Jew*"? To whom it was permitted to observe those things indifferently—"*live like a gentile and not like a Jew*"—because *before certain men came from James, he ate with the gentiles*—"*if you, being a Jew, live like a gentile and not like a Jew,*" it should have followed and said: "Why now do you keep the Jewish sacraments?" He did not say this, but where Peter was reproachable, whence these things were being stirred, whence he was zealous for God, what hindrance he was striving to remove from the Gospel, this he said with confidence, this he said *before all:* "*If you, being a Jew, live like a gentile, why do you compel the gentiles to judaize?*" "*You compel them,*" he says, "*to judaize.*" I have already said how: by consenting to those who said that the gentiles could not otherwise have salvation, "*you compel the gentiles to judaize.*" This Paul did not do, when he undertook the purifications with certain men; he did not compel *the gentiles to judaize,* when he circumcised Timothy according to that indifference, because both *circumcision is nothing and uncircumcision is nothing*[^20]; he did not compel *the gentiles to judaize.* And lest he be thought to compel, he did not circumcise Titus; Paul did not compel *the gentiles to judaize.* This he himself did not do, and therefore he rightly reproached this in Peter, because he himself did not do it.

**13.** But let me suppose that he too did this—although it is not found, not proven; I do not say this on my own view, but on that of certain men, that for now I may say it—let us suppose that Paul did what Peter did, and that Paul reproached in Peter what he himself, Paul, had also done: both would more easily have to be corrected, than that a detestable moth should creep upon us, that is, lying or the suspicion of lying, like a malignant little worm, to gnaw at every letter of Scripture, to be feared lest it persuade us that Paul lied about something in his canonical letter, which he commends to the Church propagated by posterity. I beg you: fear this evil; let us all fear it, lest we all afterward lament in vain. This is no light evil: I admonish you, fearing I terrify. Pardon my solicitude: we speak to you more rarely than you wish. Those whisper who find your ears ready, when they wish—we are not permitted to speak to you except rarely and from this place—I do not say malevolent in mind, I do them no injury, but beware of those who err, and so deal with them that they themselves rather be corrected, not that you be depraved by them. For when they have said these things to you: "Peter acted in pretense, in pretense Paul rebuked him; Paul did not write the truth in his letter that Peter *was reproachable;* Paul did not write the truth in his letter that he saw them *not walking rightly toward the truth of the Gospel;* Paul did not write the truth in his letter that Peter was compelling *the gentiles to judaize,* but all was pretended"—what truth shall we hold, what page shall we not hold suspect of lying?

**14.** See, we men are pressed by most evident authority, and scarcely do they yield to the thundering and flashing truth! Where shall we go? What shall we do, when perhaps I shall say to someone: "It is good to marry, but better not to marry, as the apostle Paul wrote," if the condemner of marriage says to me: "Paul utterly condemned marriage, but wrote this in pretense, since the truth itself could not be borne by the weak; because continence is laid on laboriously, therefore he said: It is good to marry, for he knew it was evil to marry"? Whence do you prove that Paul lied, where he says: *He who gives in marriage does well*[^21]? "Whence do I prove it?" he says: "in the same way as he lied, where he says: *When I saw that they did not walk rightly toward the truth of the Gospel*—for Peter was not failing to walk rightly toward the truth of the Gospel—; in the same way as *I said to Peter before all: If you, being a Jew, live like a gentile and not like a Jew, why do you compel the gentiles to judaize?*—for Peter was not really compelling the gentiles to judaize. As, then, Paul said these things in pretense, so too, when he saw that the excellence of virginity could not be borne by fleshly men, he said: *He who gives in marriage does well.* He was doing one thing, holding out another." If these things are so, where shall we go? What oracles shall we consult? For the divine words committed to us in the canonical Scriptures ought to be to us just as they truly are, oracles. Is deceit to be feared there, where truth is found, where one presumes upon truth? I beseech you: foresee this little worm, that you may beware of it; do not admit it to the cupboard of your heart. If you admit it, unless you quickly shake out the cloths, you will find nothing whole there.

**15.** I have said what I thought, with great solicitude, had to be said; long have I held your love, long has your solicitude held me. All the things written in the holy canonical books, we who dispute and write books write far otherwise: we write by advancing, we learn daily, we dictate by searching, we speak by knocking. Surely I do not rest, as much as I can, where I can be useful to the brethren, both by speaking and by writing. I admonish your love, from me, up to me, that you not wish to hold the book or disputation of anyone's argument as canonical Scripture. In the holy Scriptures we learn to judge; in our own writings we do not disdain to be judged. That indeed is to be chosen, and this rather of the two to be wished, that in writing or speaking we say true things, that we err nowhere. But since this is difficult to fulfill, therefore the firmament of the canon is something else, like the heaven where the luminaries of the Scriptures are set, as it were between the waters and the waters, between the peoples of the angels and the peoples of men: those above, these below. Let us hold Scripture as Scripture, as God speaking; let us not seek there an erring man. For not in vain was the canon established in the Church: this is the office of the Holy Spirit. If anyone, then, reads my book, let him judge me: if I have said something reasonable, let him follow not me, but reason itself; if I have proved this by a most evident and divine testimony, let him follow not me, but the divine Scripture. But if he wishes to reproach something that I have rightly said, he does not act rightly, but I am more angry with such a praiser of mine, who takes my book as if canonical, than with one who reproaches in my book even things not to be reproached. I beg you: although I see you attentive and as it were quite fresh, as if you had just begun to listen, yet I do not wish to say anything more, that you may firmly hold this which I said last. Turned to the Lord, etc.
