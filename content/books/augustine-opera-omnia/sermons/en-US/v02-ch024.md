# Sermon 36

**1** The holy Scripture which has just been read in your ears admonished us—or rather, through it the Lord, who commands us to speak to you—to inquire with you and to treat what it is and what it means that was read: *There are some who make themselves out rich, having nothing, and there are some who humble themselves, though they are rich*[^1]. For it is not to be thought, nor at all to be believed, that the holy Scripture cared to admonish us about those riches by which the proud are puffed up—I mean these visible and earthly ones—either that we should hold them as something great, or fear not to have them. "For what does it profit a man," someone says, "who affects to seem rich, when he has nothing?" This man Scripture noted and reproached. But neither is the one to be greatly wished for or imitated and held great whom it seems to have placed in praise, if he understood it of temporal and earthly riches. *And there are*, it says, *some who humble themselves, though they are rich*. That man rightly displeases us, who, having nothing, makes himself out rich. What, does this one please us, who, though rich, humbles himself? Perhaps he pleases because he humbles himself; yet he does not please because he is rich.

**2** Let us take this too, then. It is not unseemly, nor dishonorable, nor unprofitable, that the holy Scriptures wished to commend to us humble rich men. For nothing is so to be feared in riches as pride. Finally, the apostle Paul admonishes Timothy of this: *Charge*, he says, *the rich of this world not to be proud in mind*[^2]. For he did not dread riches but the disease of riches. And the disease of riches is great pride. For it is a great soul that, amid riches, is not tempted by this disease—a soul greater than its own riches, conquering not by coveting but by despising. He is a great rich man, then, who does not think himself great because he is rich. But he who therefore thinks himself great is proud and needy. In the flesh he is bursting, in the heart he begs; he is puffed up, not full. But if you should see two wineskins, one full, the other puffed up, in both there is the same size, but not in both the same fullness. If you attend to it, you are deceived; if you weigh it, you will find out. He who is full is moved with difficulty; he who is puffed up is quickly carried off.

**3** *Charge*, then, he says, *the rich of this world*[^3]. He would not add *of this world*, unless because there are rich men who are not of this world. Who are the rich who are not of this world? Those whose prince and head is the one of whom it was said: *He was made poor for us, though he was rich*[^4]. But if he alone, what did it profit? See what follows: *That by his poverty you might be enriched*[^5]. I think the poverty of Christ brought us not money but justice. But whence his poverty? Because he was made mortal. Therefore the true riches are immortality. For there is true abundance where there is no want. Because, then, we could not be made immortal unless Christ were made mortal for us, therefore *he was made poor, though he was rich*. And he did not say, He was made poor, though he had been rich, but: *He was made poor, though he was rich*. He took up poverty and did not lose his riches. Within rich, without poor. God lying hidden in riches, man appearing in poverty. See his riches: *In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God. He was in the beginning with God. All things were made through him*[^6]. What is richer than the one through whom all things were made? A rich man can have gold, he cannot create it. And so, when these riches of his had been commended, see his poverty: *And the Word became flesh, and dwelt among us*[^7]. By this poverty of his we have been enriched, because in his blood, which flowed from his flesh—in that the Word became flesh that it might dwell among us—the sackcloth of our sins was torn open. Through that blood we cast off the rags of iniquity, that we might be clothed with the robe of immortality.

**4** All good faithful, then, are rich. Let no one despise himself, poor in his cell, rich in his conscience. For the rich man in conscience sleeps more securely on the ground than the rich man in gold on purple. There evil anxiety does not rouse him, his heart pricked by crime. Keep in your heart the riches which the poverty of your Lord conferred on you. Rather, set him himself as your guardian. Lest what he gave perish from the heart, let the one who gave it keep it. All good faithful, then, are rich, but not rich of this world. Finally, they themselves do not even feel their riches; they will feel them afterward. The root lives, but in the winter season even a green tree is like a dry one. For in the winter season both the tree that withers and the tree that flourishes are both bare of the adornment of leaves, both empty of the adornment of fruits. Summer will come and distinguish the trees. The living root produces leaves, is filled with fruits; the dry one, empty, will remain in summer as in winter. And so for the one a barn is prepared, to the other the axe is applied, that, cut down, it may be cast into the fire. So our summer is the coming of Christ. Our winter is the hiding of Christ; our summer is the revelation of Christ. Finally, the Apostle offers this address to the good and faithful trees: *For you have died, and your life is hidden with Christ in God*[^8]. Surely dead, but dead in appearance, alive in the root. But attend to the coming season of summer, how it follows and says: *But when Christ, your life, appears, then you too will appear with him in glory*[^9]. These are the rich, but not of this world.

**5** Yet neither are the rich of the world despised. He gained them too by his poverty, who, *though he was rich, became poor for us*[^10]. For if he had despised them and not wished to have them in the number of his own, the Apostle, as I was saying, would not have charged Timothy that he too should charge: *Charge*, he says, *the rich of this world*[^11]. Among those who are rich in faith, there are some rich of this world. Charge them, because they too have been made members of that poor one. Charge them what you fear for them from riches: *Not to be proud in mind, nor to hope in the uncertainty of riches*[^12]. For the rich man is proud because he hopes in the uncertainty of riches. For if he prudently attended to the uncertainty of riches, he would never be proud, but always fear. The richer he were, the more anxious he would become, even according to this life, not only according to that one. For many poor men have been more secure in these disturbances of the world. But many, on account of their riches, have been sought out and seized. Many have lamented that they had what they could by no means always have. Many have regretted not having received the counsel of their Lord, who said: *Do not lay up for yourselves treasures on earth, where moth and rust destroy, and where thieves break in and steal. But lay up for yourselves treasure in heaven*[^13]. I do not tell you to lose, but to transfer. For many were unwilling to do this, and grieved that they had not obeyed, when they not only lost their goods, but perished themselves on account of them. *Charge*, then, *the rich of this world not to be proud in mind*, and there will come about in them what we heard in the proverb of Solomon: *There are some who humble themselves, though they are rich*[^14]. And it can come about even according to these temporal riches. Let him be humble. Let him rejoice more that he is a Christian than that he is rich. Let him not be puffed up, not lifted up. Let him attend to his poor brother, let him not disdain to be called the brother of a poor man. For however rich he is, Christ is richer, who willed to be the brother of those for whom he shed his blood.

**6** Yet lest the rich should say they had nothing to do with their riches, he admonished Timothy that he should rule them also by counsel, not only restrain them by command. When he had said, *Nor to hope in the uncertainty of riches*, lest they should think they had lost hope, he added: *But in the living God, who furnishes us all things abundantly to enjoy*[^15]—temporal things to use, eternal things to enjoy. But what should they do with their riches? *Let them be*, he says, *rich in good works, let them give easily*[^16]. Let your riches profit this, that there be for you no difficulty in giving. For the poor man wishes, and cannot. The rich man wishes, and can. *Let them give easily, let them share, let them treasure up for themselves a good foundation for the future, that they may lay hold of the true life*[^17]. For this life is false. Deceived by the falsity of this life, that man in purple and fine linen despised the ulcerous poor man lying before his gate. But the poor man, licked by the dogs, was procuring for himself an eternal treasure in Abraham's bosom—if not by abundant means, yet by a devout and best will. But that rich man, who seemed great to himself in purple and fine linen, died and was buried. And what did he find? Eternal thirst, unfailing flames. Fire succeeded the purple and fine linen. He burned in that tunic of which he could not strip himself. In place of banquets, dryness, and a longing for a drop from the poor man's finger, as the poor man had longed for the crumbs from the rich man's table. But the poor man's want was passing, the rich man's punishment abiding. Let the rich of this world attend to this, and not be proud in mind. Let them give easily, share. Let them treasure up for themselves a good foundation for the future, where there are the true rich, but not of this world, that they may lay hold of the true life.

**7** Perhaps, then, the divine Scripture admonished this, where it says, *There are some who make themselves out rich, having nothing*[^18], on account of the ragged proud. For if a proud rich man is scarcely tolerated, who could bear a proud poor man? Better, then, are those who humble themselves, though they are rich. Yet Scripture attests that it speaks of other riches. For it adjoined next: *The redemption of a man's soul is his riches; but the poor man does not endure threats*[^19]. We ought to understand the poor man from some other poverty, and the rich man from some other riches. For more deeply rich, rich in heart, full of strength, abounding in devotion, abounding in love, are rich within themselves, are rich inwardly. *But there are some who make themselves out rich, though they are poor*. They seem just to themselves, though they are unjust. For we ought to understand those riches, since Scripture has made plain what it meant: *The redemption of a man's soul is his riches*. "Understand," it says, "what riches I commend to you. Since I said, *There are some who make themselves out rich, having nothing; and there are some who humble themselves, though they are rich*, you were thinking of those temporal and earthly and visible riches. But I do not mean those; I admonish, as follows, of those I shall name: *The redemption of a man's soul* is *his riches*." Therefore those who do not have the redemption of the soul, because they are unjust and affect to seem just, because they are hypocrites, are the very ones of whom it says: *There are some who make themselves out rich, having nothing*. They want to seem just, when in the cell of conscience they do not have the gold of justice. And there are some full, the more humble the richer, of whom it was said: *Blessed are the poor in spirit, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven*[^20].

**8** Why, then, do you seek riches that flatter human and fleshly eyes? Gold shines, but faith shines more. Choose what you ought to have in your heart. Be full within, where God sees your riches, man does not see them. Yet not because man does not see them ought you therefore to despise what you have within. Do you want to see that faith shines more than gold even in the eyes of the unjust? How even a greedy master praises a faithful servant? He says nothing is more precious than he—rather he testifies that he has no price at all. "I have a servant," he says, "he has no price." Do you wait to hear why? Perhaps he dances well, or is an excellent cook. No. Attend to the inner praise: "Nothing," he says, "is more faithful." A man, your faithful servant, pleases you, and do you not want to be a faithful servant to God? You attend to the fact that you have a servant; attend to the fact that you also have a Lord. You could buy your servant, not create him. Your Lord both created you by his word, and redeemed you by his blood. If you have become cheap in your own eyes, recall the price. If you have forgotten even this, read the Gospel, your deed. You love faith in your servant, and does your Lord not seek it in his own? Render what you demand. What you rejoice to have rendered to you by an inferior, render to your superior. You love a servant who faithfully guards your gold. Do not despise the Lord who mercifully guards your heart. All, then, have eyes to praise faith, but when they demand it to be rendered to them. For when it is demanded of them, they shut their eyes, they refuse to see how beautiful it is. Or perhaps by foolish madness they refuse to render it for this reason, lest they lose it, just as anyone fears to render money: when he has rendered it he will not have it. Faith is not so rendered. It is both rendered and had. A wondrous thing to say! Rather, if it is not rendered, it is not had.

**9** *The redemption of a man's soul is his riches*[^21]. Rightly did God insult that most vain rich man, to admonish us not to imitate such things—him to whom a fruitful region succeeded, and abundance disturbed the man more than want would have. *For he thought within himself, saying: What shall I do, that I may gather my fruits?*[^22] And when he had burned, hemmed in, at last he seemed to himself to have found counsel. But it was vain counsel. For this counsel prudence did not find, but avarice. *I will destroy*, he said, *my old, smaller barns, and make new and larger ones, and fill them; and I will say to my soul: Soul, you have many goods, be satisfied, be merry. He said to him: Fool*[^23]—in what you seem wise to yourself, fool, what did you say? "I say to my soul: You have many goods, be satisfied." *This night your soul will be taken from you. The things you have prepared, whose will they be?*[^24] *For what does it profit a man, if he gain the whole world, but suffer the loss of his own soul?*[^25] Therefore the redemption of a man's soul is his riches. These riches that vain and foolish man did not have. For he did not redeem his soul by alms; he stored up perishing fruits. He stored up, I say, perishing fruits, himself about to perish, bestowing nothing on the Lord to whom he was about to go forth. What face will he have at that judgment, when he begins to hear: *I was hungry, and you did not give me to eat?*[^26] For he desired to satisfy his soul with superfluous and excessive feasts, while, most proud, he despised the so many empty bellies of the poor. He did not know that the bellies of the poor were safer than his barns. For what he stored in those barns was perhaps even carried off by thieves. But if he had stored it in the bellies of the poor, on earth indeed it was digested, but in heaven it was kept more safely. Therefore, *the redemption of a man's soul is his riches*.

**10** And what follows? *But the poor man does not endure threats*[^27]. The poor man, that is, empty of justice, not having within the fullness of the spirit, the spiritual ornaments, the spiritual furnishings, and all that which is not seen by the eyes but weighed more by the mind. Not having these within, *he does not endure threats*. When it is said to him by some powerful man: "Say this word against my enemy, say false testimony, that I may oppress and subdue the one I wish." Perhaps he tries: "I will not do it, I will not bring sin on myself." He refuses only until the rich man begins to threaten. But because he is *poor*, *he does not endure threats*. What is "he is poor"? He does not have the inner riches which the martyrs had, who for the truth and faith of Christ despised all the threats of the world. They lost nothing from the heart; how much did they find in heaven? *The poor man, then, does not endure threats*. He cannot say to the rich man forcing him to someone's injury or to false testimony: "I will not do it." He does not have within whence to answer; he is not made solid and full by an inner treasure; there is no one to say—because he does not have whence to say—there is no one to say: "What will you do to me, you who threaten? At most, you will take away what I have. You take away what I shall leave behind; you take away what, even if you do not take it, while I live I shall perhaps lose. From my inner chest I lose nothing. When you threaten to take away what I have within, do you really want to take away what I have within? But that you can both take away and have. If by threatening you take away faith, both I lose it and you will not have it. I will not, then, do what you urge, I do not care what you threaten. But by raging you can even drive me from my homeland. You have harmed me, if you drive me out there where I cannot find my God. Perhaps you will be able even to kill me. When the fleshly house collapses, the inhabitant departs unharmed, and to the one to whom I keep faith I go forth secure, and I will fear you no more. For see what you threaten, that I should speak false testimony. You threaten death, but of the body. I fear more the one who said: *The mouth that lies kills the soul*[^28]." Full within and abounding with these riches, he answers such things, or better things, to the one who so threatens. *But the poor man does not endure threats*.

**11** Let us, then, be rich, and fear to be poor. But let us seek to have our heart filled with riches by the one who is truly rich. And if perhaps each of you enters into his heart and does not find those riches there, let him knock at the rich one's door, let him become a devout beggar before the door of that rich one, that, by his giving, he may be filled rich. And truly, my brothers, we ought to confess our poverty, our want, to the Lord our God. This the publican confessed, who did not even dare to lift his eyes to heaven. For the sinful man had no substance with which to lift his eyes. He attended to his own emptiness, but he recognized the fullness of the Lord. He knew that he came thirsting to a fountain. He showed his dry throat, he devoutly knocked to have his breast filled: *Lord*, he said, beating his breast, casting his eyes to the ground, *be merciful to me a sinner*[^29]. I say that he was already in some part rich, when he was thinking and asking these things. For if he was still in every way poor, whence did he bring forth these gems of confession? Yet he went down from the temple more abounding and fuller, justified. But that Pharisee went up to pray and asked nothing. *They went up*, it says, *to the temple to pray*[^30]. The one asks, the other does not ask. But the latter, whence was he? *There are some who think themselves rich, having nothing*. *Lord*, he said, *I give you thanks that I am not like other men, the unjust, robbers, adulterers, like even this publican. I fast twice on the sabbath, I give tithes of all that I possess*[^31]. He boasted of himself, but that is a puffing-up, not a fullness. He thought himself rich, having nothing. The other recognized himself as poor, now having something. Not to say anything else, he had that very devotion of confession. And both went down. But, it says, that publican was justified rather than that Pharisee. *Because everyone who exalts himself will be humbled, and he who humbles himself will be exalted*[^32].
