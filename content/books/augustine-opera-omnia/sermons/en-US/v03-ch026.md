# Sermon 68

On the Chapter of the Gospel According to Matthew Where the Lord Says: "I Confess to You, Father, Lord of Heaven and Earth, Because You Have Hidden These Things from the Wise and Prudent," and What Follows

**1.** This reading of the holy Gospel we heard yesterday too, on the Lord's Day, as you remember: but today we wished it to be read, because yesterday the crowd, packed together and somewhat restless in the cramped space, did not give ease to our voice; since it is not such as to suffice except in great silence. Accordingly, today, with the Lord's help, I think what we passed over yesterday must be examined, and treated according to the weakness of our little measure: not because yesterday we begrudged this to the crowd, but because by the weakness of our voice we did not suffice for the crowd. Now, then, by your attention help me before the Lord our God, that he may grant us what we may say, and you to hear wholesomely.

**2.** The Son of God, only-begotten to the Father, always God, man for our sake, made what he made—for he was made man, who made man—says to the Father: *I confess to you, Father, Lord of heaven and earth*[^1]. To me Father: Lord of heaven and earth, the Father of him through whom all things were made. For every creature is briefly expressed by these two words, when heaven and earth are named; therefore the first book of God's Scripture says: *In the beginning God made heaven and earth*[^2]; and, *my help is from the Lord, who made heaven and earth*[^3]. But by the name of heaven is understood whatever is in heaven, by the name of earth is understood whatever is on earth; so that, these two parts of creation being named, no creature is passed over: since it is either here, or there. But the Son says to the Father: *I confess*; and admonishes us that confession is owed to God not in sins alone. For very often, when in the Scriptures one hears, *Confess to the Lord*, many who hear beat their breasts: it seems to them that confession signifies nothing other than that which penitents are wont to use, confessing their sins, awaiting from God their deserts—not what they deserve to suffer, but what he mercifully deigns to do. But if confession were not in praise, he would not say: *I confess to you, Father*, who had no sin to confess. It is also said in a certain book of Scripture: *Confess to the Lord, and say this in confession: all the works of the Lord are exceedingly good*[^4]. Here too confession is of praise, not of fault. When, then, you praise God, you confess to God; when you accuse your sins before God, you confess to God: but all this pertains to the praise of the Creator, both that you proclaim him, and that you accuse yourself.

**3.** But since it pertains to his praise that you proclaim him, no one doubts; but how it pertains to his praise that you accuse yourself, perhaps you ask. This is what can be briefly said and understood: when you accuse yourself in sin, you praise him who made you without sin; for if he had made you with sin, you would not accuse yourself sinning, but him creating. So in the proclamation of God there is praise; and your accusation is the praise of God: both pertain to confession. We have heard the Son of God saying: *I confess to you, Father, Lord of heaven and earth.* What does he confess to him? in what does he praise him? *Because you have hidden these things from the wise*, he says, *and prudent, and revealed them to little ones*[^5]. Who are the wise and prudent? who the little ones? What did he conceal from the wise and prudent, and reveal to the little ones? By "the wise and prudent" he signifies those of whom Paul says: *Where is the wise? where the scribe? where the inquirer of this age? has not God made foolish the wisdom of this world?*[^6] Even now perhaps you still ask who these are. Perhaps they are those who, disputing much about God, said false things; puffed up with their doctrines, they could in no way find and know God. These perhaps someone may say were signified by the apostle Paul, who says: *Where is the wise? where the scribe? where the inquirer of this age?* Let them too be such: let those also be understood who could in no way know God, and instead of God, whose substance is incomprehensible and invisible, thought God to be air, ether, thought God to be the sun, thought God to be something that sublimely stands out in creation; for attending to the greatnesses and beauties and strengths of creatures, they remained there, they did not find the Creator, marveling at the things that were made. It is not beside the point to take these too to be signified.

**4.** Yet, dearly beloved, it is more wonderful that we find in a certain place of holy Scripture even those reproved who knew, and their foolishness accused, and their false wisdom derided. For those who did not know the Creator from the creature, that book of Wisdom convicts, where it says, that *the circle of the stars, the sun, the moon, they thought to be the gods, rulers of the world*[^7]. And of these it was said, that, although they are preferred to those who worshiped idols, and thought the works of men, not the works of God, to be gods; yet they too were accused, so that it was said: *Again, neither are these to be pardoned*[^8]. For now, in comparison with those who have as gods the works of men, surely better are those who have as gods the works of God. For a craftsman made the idol, God made the sun: in comparison with one who thinks god what a craftsman made, better is he who thinks god what God made. But yet attend how even these are convicted, and how rightly they are accused: *Again*, it says, *neither are these to be pardoned; for if they were able to be of such power that they could estimate the world, how did they not more easily find its Lord?*[^9] They are accused as consuming their time and the occupations of their disputations in scrutinizing and in a way measuring the creature: they sought the courses of the stars, the intervals of the planets, the journeys of the heavenly bodies; so that by certain computations they came to that knowledge, that they predicted an eclipse of the sun, an eclipse of the moon, and when they predicted it, it happened, on that day and that hour, by so much, and by so great a part, as they had predicted. Great industry! great skill! But where they scrutinized the Creator, set not far from themselves, they did not find him; whom if they had found, they would have him with them. Just as if anyone, entering this building, were to count the columns, measure how many cubits they were, gather the height of the roof, the width of the floor, and the stature of the walls, and report back to you about all the number which you did not know; but you nevertheless knew by whom the building was constructed, while he did not, and, too uncertain about things, did not think this building was established by a man, but estimated that these columns, this roof, these walls, came into existence by their own force and nature, with no maker; or attributed to some part of this building such power that he believed the rest had been constructed by it: and when you had said: "A man made this building"; he said: "What man? when could a man build this building? This roof, which you see high, this roof itself built all these things that you see, low." I do not say he would appear to you foolish, but raving. And what would it profit him, that he computed for you the measure of all the columns and the numbers of the whole building, and said what you did not know? You, endowed with a better knowledge, would know the maker of this building: for it is more to know that a man made it, that it was made by reason, that it was constructed by a rational mind, that counsel preceded this mass; than to know how many cubits a column is, or how many columns there are, or how wide the floor or the roof is.

**5.** I think your love distinguishes these kinds of knowledge. For neither in this do you know anything great, that you know a man made it, if you attribute this building to the body itself of a man. You know something great, if you know it was made by counsel, by a rational mind, where the building itself was before it was seen by the eyes. For the counsel of building preceded, and then the effect followed: that preceded which you would not see, that there might be what you would see. Now, then, you see the building, you praise the counsel: you attend to what you see, you praise what you do not see; and that which you do not see is more than that which you see. So most excellently and most rightly were those accused who could investigate the numbers of the stars, the intervals of times, and know and predict the eclipse of lights: rightly were they accused; since they did not find by whom these things were made and ordered, because they neglected to seek. But do not greatly care, if you are ignorant of the courses of the stars and of the heavenly or earthly bodies: see the beauty of the world, and praise the counsel of the Creator: see what he made, love him who made it. Hold this most of all, love him who made it; because he made you too his lover, to his own image. So what wonder is it, that to such wise men, occupied about creatures, who, neglecting the Creator, were unwilling to seek and could not find him, those things were hidden of which Christ said: *You have hidden these things from the wise and prudent?* More wonderful is what you are about to hear, that wise and prudent men are reproved even who could know. *The wrath of God*, it says, *is revealed from heaven upon all impiety and injustice of men, who hold the truth in iniquity*[^10]. You ask perhaps what truth they hold in iniquity? *Because what is known of God is manifest in them*[^11]. Whence manifest? It follows and says: *For God manifested it to them.* Still you ask, how did he manifest it to those to whom he did not give the law? How, then? *For his invisible things, from the creation of the world, are clearly seen, being understood through the things that are made*[^12]. So he manifested it, because his invisible things, from the creation of the world, are clearly seen, being understood through the things that are made.

**6.** Another, to find God, reads a book. There is a certain great book, the very form of creation: behold the upper and the lower, attend, read. God did not make of ink the letters whereby you might know him: he set before your eyes these very things which he made. Why do you seek a greater voice? Heaven and earth cry out to you: God made me. You read what Moses wrote. What did Moses, a temporal man, read, in order to write? Attend piously to heaven and earth. There were, then, certain ones, not like Moses the servant of God, not like the many Prophets, beholding and understanding these things, helped by the Spirit of God; which Spirit they drew in by faith, drank with the throat of godliness, belched forth with the mouth of the inner man. Not such, then. But there were others, dissimilar, who through this creature could come to understanding the Creator, and to say of these things that God made: "Behold, what he made, he governs and holds together; he himself, who made it, fills with his presence these things which he made." They could say this: for the apostle Paul mentioned them too in the *Acts of the Apostles*, where, when he had said of God: *For in him we live and move and are*, since he was speaking among the Athenians, where those most learned ones had appeared, he at once added: *As certain ones also among you have said*[^13]. It is no light thing that they said, that *in God we live and move and are.* Whence, then, dissimilar? whence blamed? whence rightly accused? Hear the Apostle's words, which I had begun to say: *The wrath of God*, he says, *is revealed from heaven upon all impiety*, namely of those who did not receive the law; *upon all impiety and injustice of men, who hold the truth in iniquity.* What truth? *Because what is known of God is manifest in them.* Manifest how? *For God manifested it to them.* How did he manifest it? *For his invisible things, from the creation of the world, are clearly seen, being understood through the things that are made, his everlasting power too and divinity.* Why did he manifest it? *That they may be inexcusable.* If he manifested it that they may be inexcusable, whence then are they blameworthy? *Because, knowing God, they did not glorify him as God.* What is it you say: "They did not glorify him as God"? *Nor gave thanks*[^14]. This, then, is to glorify God, to give thanks to God. This surely: for what is worse, if, created to the image, you are ungrateful when God is known? This is, utterly this is, to glorify God, to give thanks to God. The faithful know where and when it is said: *Let us give thanks to the Lord our God.* But who gives thanks to the Lord, except one who has his heart on high toward the Lord?

**7.** There are among these words, among you, those who hear, and do not hear: but let them not be angry with us, when they themselves defer themselves. So those are blameworthy: who are inexcusable, *because, knowing God, they did not glorify him as God, nor gave thanks.* But what? *But they became vain in their thoughts*[^15]. Whence did they become vain, except because they were proud? Smoke too vanishes by rising on high; and rather the fire shines and is strengthened by taking hold more humbly. *They became vain in their thoughts, and their foolish heart was darkened.* Smoke too, though it is higher than the fire, is dark. Finally, attend to what follows, and see on what the whole matter hangs: *For saying they were wise, they became fools*[^16]. By arrogating to themselves what God had furnished, they took away what God had given. So to the proud he himself hid himself, who, to those diligently scrutinizing the Creator through the creature, had conveyed nothing but himself. Rightly, then, the Lord: *You have hidden these things from the wise and prudent:* whether from those who, by manifold disputations and most skillful inquiry, came to the investigation of the creature, but in no way knew the Creator; or from those who, knowing God, did not glorify him as God or give thanks, and could not see perfectly and wholesomely, because they were proud. *You have hidden these things*, then, *from the wise and prudent, and revealed them to little ones.* To which little ones? the humble. He says: *Upon whom will my Spirit rest? Upon the humble and the quiet and the one who trembles at my words*[^17]. These words Peter trembled at, Plato did not tremble: let the fisherman hold what the most noble disputant lost. *You have hidden these things from the wise and prudent, and revealed them to little ones:* you have hidden them from the proud, and revealed them to the humble. What, then, are we, however much we are? If we are humble, we shall merit to be made blessed by the full sight of God, if we have merited to be numbered among the little ones. *Yes, Father*, he says, exulting in the Holy Spirit—he approved, it pleased him, he praised that it was so, he praised that it was so done—*yes, Father, for so it was pleasing before you*[^18].

**8.** We heard what he says: *You have hidden these things from the wise and prudent, and revealed them to little ones*[^19]; who are the wise and prudent from whom you hid them, and who are the little ones to whom you revealed them. What are these very things? For when he said this: *You have hidden these things from the wise and prudent, and revealed them to little ones*, he was not attending to heaven and earth, and pointing them out, as it were, with his hand, when he said these things. For who does not see these things? The good see them, the evil too see them, because *he makes his sun rise upon the good and the evil*[^20]. What, then, are these things, of which he says, that *you have hidden these things from the wise and prudent, and revealed them to little ones?* What, except what he adds next, and says: *Yes, Father, for so it was pleasing before you?*[^21] Here he praised, it pleased him. What, then, are these things? *All things have been handed over to me by my Father*[^22]. That Christian grace, that all things have been handed over to him by his Father, lay hidden from all the wise of this world. And not only those who, occupied with too much and most intent curiosity about creatures whether earthly or heavenly, neglected to seek and were unable to find the Creator, but even those who, from the creature and from the things that were seen, that is, from visible things, could come in thought to him by whom they were made, did not recognize what was said: *All things have been handed over to me by my Father.* These things Moses saw, the Prophets saw, the Patriarchs saw; but those great wise men, the keen disputants, the inquirers, the pourers-forth of thundering, as it were, eloquence, were altogether ignorant of them. This is *the mystery which was hidden from the ages in God, but now is manifest to his saints*[^23], his little ones, that is, his humble ones, upon whom his Spirit rests, the quiet and those who tremble at his words: *All things*, he says, *have been handed over to me by my Father.*

**9.** But since among all these things we vehemently wish and desire to see God himself, and we desire this the more, the better we are, the more godly, the more faithful, the more instructed and firm in the progress of the mind—this desire conquers all desires; he, as it were, gently addresses his very little ones, to whom he gave to know his grace, that all things have been handed over to Christ by his Father, that they not bear it ill that now they do not see; that, being prepared for that vision, they may bear with the medicinal delay. *All things*, he says, *have been handed over to me by my Father.* And the little ones were going to say: "We wish to see the Father himself, as Philip says: *Show us the Father, and it suffices us*[^24]." As if he said: "I know what you desire, and how little ones you are toward so great a good: *No one knows the Son, except the Father.* You thought me already known: *No one knows the Son, except the Father.* As though, me being known, you were seeking to see and know the Father: *Nor does anyone know the Father, except the Son.*" Yet you will not remain separated from this vision, because it follows: *and he to whom the Son wishes to reveal him*[^25]. To whom will the Son wish to reveal him, except those of whom it was said: *you have revealed them to little ones?* Let us be little ones, then: let us seek and learn this from the great Master. Since you are nothing, will you not be a little one, for whose sake so great a one became a little one? The Father, then, reveals the Son to whom he wishes, and the Son reveals the Father to whom he wishes. For it is not that the Son reveals the Father, and the Father does not reveal the Son. Here we have heard, here we have read: *Nor does anyone know the Father except the Son, and he to whom the Son wishes to reveal him.* We have learned that the Son is the revealer of the Father: whence do we know that the Father is the revealer of the Son? Hear the same Son. When Peter had said: *You are the Christ, the Son of the living God*, he received the answer: *Blessed are you, Simon Bar-Jonah, because flesh and blood did not reveal it to you, but my Father who is in heaven*[^26]. So both the Father reveals the Son, and the Son reveals the Father. For how do you recognize the Son, unless you recognize that he has a Father? and how do you recognize the Father, unless you recognize that he has a Son? For neither can he be called Father, if he does not have a Son; nor can he be called Son, if he does not have a Father. So if he is not Father except because he has a Son, the Father reveals the Son. By the very fact that fatherhood is recognized in him, offspring is sought; if he is Father, you ask whom he begot: that is Christ God. If Christ is the Son, you ask by whom he was begotten: that is God the Father. So when you direct the eye of the mind, the eye of faith, to the Son, insofar as he is the Son, you recognize him to have been begotten, that he might be the Son; and so the Son reveals the Father. But to whom, except to little ones?

**10.** Whence do we now not see God? because our sins separate between us and God. If, then, for this reason we do not see, because our sins separate between us and God, and on account of our offenses he turns away his face from us[^27]; sweating under the burden of our breasts, let us hear him crying consequently: *Come to me, all you who labor*[^28]. Why do you sweat in vain under sins? *Come to me, all you who labor.* For where do you labor, except when you desire what is not in the power of the one desiring? You desired gold, you loved gold: do you, by loving, have the gold? But what is this? what is it that you love? By loving you thirst, by thirsting you seek, by finding you are tormented. For behold, before you find what you desire to have, before you grasp it, before you have it, before you possess it, you burn with craving: this torturer disembowels the heart, the very craving tears you. But how long? Until you arrive. Behold, you have arrived. You burned with craving, when you wished to have what you might possess: now you have what you may fear to lose. So not security succeeded craving, but fear, as it were a torturer, succeeded the torturer. Before you had anything, craving alone tormented you; when you have begun to have it, fear too torments you. I spoke badly, succeeded; rather, another torturer was added: for first there was the craving of having, the craving of increasing remains. For not because what you sought came to you, was a limit set to craving. Do you not see that by having more you crave more? When you had nothing, you sought small things; but because you have become rich, inheritances do not satisfy your cravings. You crave to have what you do not have, you fear to lose what you have: these two torturers throttle you. At least amid the torments confess to your God; hear him calling, feel him offering, that the torturers may depart; hear him who says: *Come to me, all you who labor and are burdened.* By the diverse and tearing affectations of the world you are wearied, and pressed by hard burdens: *I will refresh you*[^29]. Free, wandering through precipices, you rushed to ruin: *Take my yoke upon you.* By craving itself and the difficulty of having you were harsh, and by the empty success of things, exalted, you walked: against the bitterness and against the exaltation *learn from me, for I am meek and humble of heart*[^30]. Do not, he says, attend to one another, and have among you your exaltation.

**11.** Someone says: "What if I wish to have? Oh, that I might have! God, give me to have. Behold, my neighbor has what I do not have: he is greeted, and does not greet; I greet back, and he does not greet. God, give me." If he displeases you, why do you wish to be such? He himself says: "I greet back, and am not greeted"; and he craves to be what he condemns. "But I, he says, when I have it, not only greet back, but even greet first." By the craving of having you debase yourself: but he who made you knows you better; he counsels you better, who even to one asking does not give what is not expedient for you. Surely you think you would have it well: because you rightly think you would have it, would use it well, would govern it piously: that you may have riches, you dread labor, want. Do you desire to be blessed? Come to him who cries: *I will refresh you.* It is only that you learn what he says: *Learn from me, for I am meek and humble of heart.* For you attend to your rich neighbor, having, growing proud: by attending to him and emulating him, you will be proud; you will not be humble, unless you attend to him who for your sake became humble. Learn from Christ what you do not learn from man: in him is the rule of humility; he who approaches this one is first formed in humility itself, that he may be adorned in exaltation. For what was his form? *Who, though he was in the form of God, did not count it robbery to be equal with God; but emptied himself, taking the form of a servant, made in the likeness of men, and found in habit as a man: he humbled himself, made obedient even to death, the death of the cross*[^31]. He said such great things, and had not yet bounded the mode of his humility, unless he had added: *the death of the cross:* for this kind of death had a great reproach among the Jews. This he took up, what had a great reproach, that he might give a reward to those not ashamed of that very humility. How far did he go, cutting away your swelling? Even to the reproaches of the cross.

**12.** But perhaps as one small? *Who, though he was in the form of God.* Attend: when were you in the form of God? And do you blush to be humbled, for whose sake the form of God was humbled? *Learn*, he says, *from me.* You have not found whence you knew this so great foundation perhaps of your loftiness: *From me*, he says, *learn that I am meek and humble of heart, and you will find rest for your souls*[^32]. For surely in all your desires you seek rest: for to this end you are restless, while you seek, that, finding what you seek, you may at some time rest. In vain you think this: by finding what you badly seek, you will be more restless. *From me*, he says, *learn that I am meek and humble of heart, and you will find rest for your souls; for my yoke is gentle*[^33]. Did you fear to be bound? My yoke is gentle, is sweet; if you feared to be bound, desire to be made glad. Do you not see, even in the very bodies of those seeking human things and craving temporal things, that there are certain bindings, of which men please themselves? How hard it is that they let themselves be loosed! He who has a necklace pleases himself with the necklace, which craving twists about him. And do you think that the yoke of Christ will throttle you? Do not fear, take it up: it is gentle; it restrains wretched liberty, it brings no harshness. *And my burden is light*[^34]. Do not think I will not lay something upon you, if you will be my beast of burden—this your Lord says to you—I too will lay my burden: but do not fear, it is light; it does not press you, but lifts you up; it does not load you, but honors you. It is not so light, although it is not very heavy: as small burdens are, which, when they are carried, the one who carries says: "It is light"; yet even that light thing, although not much, has some weight. The burden of Christ is so light that it lifts up: it is not that with it or by it you are pressed, but that without it you do not rise. Think such a burden to be for you as the burden of wings is for birds: if birds have the burdens of wings, they are lifted up; if they are removed, they will remain on the earth. For what is heavy to one who loves? Do we not see—to pass over the innumerable other things by which the human race burns and is worn down—how much one zealous for hunting labors, what he endures, what heats in summer, what colds in winter, what thickets of forests, what difficulties of journeys, what steepnesses of mountains? Yet love makes all these not only bearable but even sweet: so sweet that, if you forbid him to hunt, then he labors, then he suffers a horrid weariness of mind; he does not endure rest. So much is endured, that one may reach a boar; and is it hard to endure, that one may reach God!

**13.** So Christ said this. When you heard: *My burden is light*, do not think of what the martyrs suffered here, and say within yourselves: "How is the burden of Christ light?" Men confessed him, and suffered such great things: boys confessed him, girls confessed him; the stronger sex and the weaker, the greater age and the lesser, all merited to confess and to be crowned. I think they did not labor. Why did they not labor? because by love they endured all things. This is the burden of Christ, which he deigns to lay on: it is called love, it is called charity, it is named affection. By this whatever was before most laborious will be easy for you; by this whatever you reckoned heavy will be light. Take up this burden: it will not press you, it will lift you up; it will be wings for you, which, before you have them, cry to the one who calls: *Who will give me wings like a dove?*[^35]—not like a raven, but like a dove—*and I will fly*, he says. And as if you asked: To what end? *And I will rest*[^36]. So through that burden *you will find rest for your souls.* Receive this burden, these wings; and if you have begun to have them, nourish them. Let these wings reach such a measure that you can fly. One wing is, *you shall love the Lord your God with your whole heart, and with your whole soul, and with your whole mind*[^37]. But do not remain at one wing; for if you think you have one, you do not even have that. *You shall love your neighbor as yourself*[^38]. For if you do not love your brother whom you see, how can you love God, whom you do not see[^39]? Let the other come too: so you will fly, so you will take away craving from earthly things, you will fix love on heavenly things. By these two wings, insofar as you strive, you will meanwhile have the heart on high; that the heart on high may draw on high, in its own time, its own flesh too. Nor think that for having all the feathers there is much for you: many precepts of this love are indeed to be sought in the holy Scriptures, by which the reader and hearer may be exercised; but *on these two precepts the whole Law and the Prophets hang*[^40].
