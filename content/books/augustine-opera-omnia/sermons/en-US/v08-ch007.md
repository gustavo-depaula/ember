# Sermon 278: (*on the Calling of the Apostle Paul, and a Commendation of the Lord's Prayer*)

[For the Solemnity of the Conversion of Saint Paul]

**1. 1.** Today the reading from the *Acts of the Apostles* was proclaimed, in which the apostle Paul, from a persecutor of Christians, was made a herald of Christ. Today, in those regions, even the very places bear witness to what was then done: and now it is read, and believed. The usefulness of this deed is what the Apostle himself recalls in his Letters. For he says that pardon for all his sins was given him for this reason—and for that fury and madness with which he was dragging Christians to death, who was a minister of the fury of the Jews, whether in the stoning of the holy martyr Stephen, or in handing over and bringing the rest to punishment—so that no one might despair of himself, who has been entangled in great sins and ensnared in great crimes, as though he were not going to receive pardon, if he turns to him who, hanging on the cross, prayed for his persecutors, saying: *Father, forgive them, for they do not know what they are doing*[^1]. From a persecutor he was made a preacher and teacher of the Gentiles. *I was first*, he says, *a blasphemer and a persecutor and an insolent man: but for this reason I obtained mercy, that in me first Christ Jesus might show all his long-suffering, for the instruction of those who are to believe in him unto eternal life*[^2]. For by the grace of God we are made whole from our sins, in which we lie sick. His, his is the medicine that heals the soul. For it could wound itself, but could not heal itself.

**1. 2.** For even in the body itself a man has it in his power to fall sick, but does not likewise have it in his power to recover. For if he exceeds due measure, and lives intemperately, and does the things that are harmful to health and that assail soundness, in a single day, if he wishes, he falls into diseases; but, having fallen, he does not recover. For to fall sick, he applies himself to intemperance: but to recover, he applies a physician for his health. For he cannot, as we have said, have it in his power to receive health back, in the way he has it in his power to lose it.

**2. 2.** So too with regard to the soul: that man should fall into death through sin, that he should become mortal instead of immortal, that he should be subjected to the devil who deceived him—all this lay within his free choice. By that choice, turning down toward lower things, he abandoned the higher; lending his ear to the serpent, he closed his ear to God. Placed between one who commanded and one who deceived, he chose to obey the deceiver rather than the commander. For he heard the devil with the same ear with which he heard God. Why, then, did he not believe the better of the two? And so he found that what God had foretold was true, and what the devil had promised was false. This is the first origin of our evils, the root of all our miseries, the seed of death, springing from the first man’s own free will. He was made in such a way that, if he obeyed God, he would always be blessed and immortal; but if he neglected and despised the command of the one who wished to preserve his lasting health, he would plunge into the sickness of mortality. Then the physician was despised by a healthy man; now he treats a sick one. For the instructions medicine gives for maintaining health are one thing: they are given to the healthy so that they will not become sick. The instructions the sick receive, to recover what they have lost, are another.

**3. 2.** It would have been good for man to obey the physician while he was healthy, so that he would not need a physician. For *it is not* *the healthy who need a physician, but the sick.* Strictly speaking, a physician is someone through whom health is recovered. Yet even the healthy always need God as their physician, so that their health itself may be maintained. It would therefore have been good for man to maintain the lasting health in which he was created. He despised it and abused it; through his lack of restraint he fell into the ill health of this mortality. Let him at least listen now to the physician’s instructions, so that he may rise from the place where he himself has lain down through sin.

**3. 3.** But plainly, brothers and sisters, in medicine itself a healthy person keeps what he has by doing what the wisdom of health prescribes. If, however, he begins to fall sick, he begins to listen to the instructions and to carry them out, if he truly cares about recovering sound and complete health. When he begins to do this, he is not immediately healthy; rather, by following the instructions for a long time he reaches that health which he lost through too little self-restraint. Beginning to follow them already benefits him by keeping his sickness from increasing, so that he not only grows no worse but also begins to feel better as he gradually becomes healthy. For there is hope of perfect health when a person begins to be less and less sick. In the same way, what else is living righteously in this life but hearing the commandments of the law and doing them? Are all those who keep the commandments of the law, then, already healthy? Not yet; they keep them in order to become healthy. Let them not grow weary of keeping them, because what was lost all at once is recovered little by little. For if a person quickly returned to his former blessedness, falling into death through sin would be a game to him.

**4. 4.** Suppose someone has fallen into bodily illness through lack of restraint, and something has developed in his body that even needs to be cut out. He will certainly suffer pain, but that pain will not be fruitless. If he refuses to endure the pain of the incision, he will endure the worms of decay. So the physician begins to say, “Observe this precaution and that; do not touch this; do not take that food or drink; do not get agitated about that matter.” He begins to do so; he is already following the instructions, but he is not yet healthy. What good does following them do, then? It prevents the disease that has struck him from increasing, and even makes it diminish. What follows? To his observance of the instructions must also be added the hand of the physician, making an incision and inflicting healing pain. If, then, the man suffering from a festering ulcer says, “What good does it do me to follow the instructions if I suffer the pain of the incision?” the answer is, “But you will be cured by both: by following the instructions and by enduring the pain. For that is how much harm you did yourself by failing to follow them while you were healthy. So submit to the physician until you are healed; whatever distress you suffer is what your ulcer deserves.”

**5. 5.** In this way Christ the physician came to man in his affliction and distress. He says, *It is not the healthy who need a physician, but those who are ill. I have not come to call the righteous, but sinners*. He calls sinners to peace; he calls the sick to health. He commands faith; he commands continence, temperance, and sobriety; he restrains the desire of greed. He tells us what to do and what to observe. Those who observe these things can already be said to live righteously, according to the physician’s instructions; but they have not yet recovered that health, that perfect soundness, which God promises through the Apostle when he says, *This corruptible body must put on incorruptibility, and this mortal body must put on immortality. Then the saying that is written will come to pass: Death has been swallowed up in victory. Where, death, is your struggle? Where, death, is your sting*? Then there will be complete health and equality with the holy angels. But now, my brothers and sisters, before this comes about, when we have begun to follow the instructions the physician gives, and also suffer various temptations and tribulations, let us not think that we are following them to no purpose because greater pain seems to follow those instructions we observe. For the tribulations you suffer are the hand of the physician making an incision, not the sentence of a judge inflicting punishment. This happens so that your health may be complete. Let us endure it; let us bear the pain. Sin is sweet: so let the bitterness of tribulation dispel that deadly sweetness. You enjoyed doing evil, but by doing it you fell into sickness. The medicine works in the opposite way: it causes you pain for a time so that you may recover lasting health. Take it, and do not reject it.

**6. 6.** Above all, never let go of that antidote which is so powerful against every kind of decay and the poisons of every sin: say to the Lord your God, and say it truthfully, *Forgive us our debts, as we too forgive our debtors*. For this is the agreement the physician has drawn up and confirmed with the sick. There are two kinds of sins: one in which we sin against God, the other in which we sin against another human being. Hence there are also those two commandments on which the whole Law and the Prophets depend: *You shall love the Lord your God with all your heart, with all your soul, and with all your mind; and you shall love your neighbor as yourself*. These also contain the ten commandments of the Law, of which three concern love of God and seven love of neighbor. We have discussed these sufficiently on another occasion.

**7. 7.** Just as there are two commandments, then, so there are two kinds of sins. We sin either against God or against another human being. But you also sin against God by corrupting his temple within you, for God has redeemed you with the blood of his Son. Even before you were redeemed, whose servant were you but his who created all things? Yet he wished to possess you in a special way, as one redeemed by the blood of his Son. *And you are not your own,* the Apostle says, *for you were bought at a great price. Glorify and carry God in your body*. Thus the one who redeemed you made you his house. Do you want your house destroyed? Neither does God want his destroyed—that is, you yourself. If you will not spare yourself for your own sake, spare yourself for God’s sake, for he has made you his temple. *For God’s temple is holy,* he says, *and that is what you are;* and, *Whoever corrupts God’s temple, God will destroy*. When people commit these sins, they think they are not sinning because they are harming no other person.

**8. 8.** This, then, is what I want to impress upon Your Holiness, as far as our short time permits: what evil those people do who corrupt themselves through gluttony, drunkenness, and fornication, and answer those who rebuke them, “I did it out of my own funds, out of my own property. Whom have I robbed of anything? What have I taken from anyone? Whom have I wronged? I want to enjoy myself with what God has given me.” Such a person seems innocent because he supposedly harms no one. But how is someone innocent who does not spare himself? The innocent person is one who harms no one, since the standard of love for one’s neighbor begins with oneself. This is what God said: *You shall love your neighbor as yourself.* How, then, is love of your neighbor sound in you when love of yourself is wounded by lack of restraint? Besides, God says to you, “When you choose to corrupt yourself through drunkenness, you are not destroying just anyone’s house, but mine. Where shall I live? In these ruins? In this filth? If you were receiving some servant of mine as a guest, you would repair and clean the house for my servant to enter. Will you not clean your heart, where I wish to live?”

**9. 9.** I have mentioned this one thing, brothers and sisters, so that you may see how people who corrupt themselves sin even while they think themselves innocent. But because, in the very frailty and mortality of this life, it is difficult for a person not to go somewhat beyond the proper limit in the things he uses out of necessity, that remedy must be applied: *Forgive us our debts, as we too forgive our debtors*. It must be said, and said truthfully. You are forbidden to commit adultery so that you do not harm your neighbor. For just as you do not want someone to approach your wife, so you must not approach another man’s wife. But if you have relations with your own wife without sufficient restraint, do you appear to be harming anyone, since she is your own? Yet by making immoderate use of this very thing that is permitted, you corrupt God’s temple within you. No outsider accuses you; but what answer will your conscience give to God, who says through the Apostle, *Let each of you know how to possess his own vessel in holiness and honor, not in the sickness of desires, like the Gentiles who do not know God*? Yet what married man has relations with his wife in such a way that he never goes beyond the law of procreating children? For that is why she was given to him. The documents drawn up at your marriage convict you. You agreed on the terms under which you would marry; the written agreement proclaims them to you: *For the purpose of procreating children.* So, if you can, do not have relations except for the purpose of procreating children. If you exceed that limit, you will be acting against those documents and against the agreement. Is this not plain? You will be a liar and a violator of the agreement; and God looks for the integrity of his temple within you and does not find it, not because you had relations with your own wife, but because you did so immoderately. After all, you also drink wine from your own storeroom; yet if you drink so much that you become drunk, using your own property does not mean you have not sinned. You have turned God’s gift to your own corruption.

**10. 10.** What, then, brothers and sisters? It is certainly clear, and everyone’s conscience bears witness, that it is difficult to use things that are permitted without going a little beyond the proper limit. But when you exceed the limit, you offend God, whose temple you are. *For God’s temple is holy, and that is what you are.* Let no one deceive himself: *Whoever corrupts God’s temple, God will destroy.* The sentence has been pronounced; you stand guilty. What will you say in your prayers when you call upon God, whom you offend in his temple, whom you drive out of his temple? How will you cleanse God’s house within you again? How will you bring him back to you? How, except by saying from a truthful heart, in both words and deeds, *Forgive us our debts, as we too forgive our debtors*? For who will accuse you of immoderation in using your own food, your own drink, your own wife? No human being will accuse you. Nevertheless, because God reproves you, requiring you to preserve the integrity of his temple and the incorruption of his dwelling, he has given you a remedy, as though saying, “If you offend me by exceeding the limit, I too will hold you guilty where no human being accuses you. Forgive another person the sin he has committed against you, so that I may forgive you the sin you commit against me.”

**11. 11.** Hold firmly to this, brothers and sisters. For anyone who rejects even this antidote will have absolutely no hope of salvation left. If someone tells me, “I do not forgive the sins people may commit against me,” I have no basis for promising him salvation. For I cannot promise what God does not promise. I would then be a minister of the serpent, not a minister of God’s word. The serpent promised good to one who sinned, whereas God threatened death. What happened to him but what God had threatened? What the serpent promised was far from happening to him. Do you therefore want me to tell you, brothers and sisters, “Even if you sin, even if you do not forgive people their sins, you will certainly be saved; when Christ Jesus comes, he will grant pardon to everyone”? I do not say it, because I do not hear it; I do not say what is not said to me. God does indeed promise pardon to the sinner, but he forgives all past sins to those who turn to him, believe, and are baptized. This I read; this I dare to promise; this I promise, and what I promise is promised to me. When it is read, we all listen: for we are fellow students, and there is one teacher in this school.

**12. 12.** All past sins, then, are forgiven to those who turn to God. But in this life there are certain grave and deadly sins that are not remitted without the most intense distress of a humbled heart, a contrite spirit, and the affliction of repentance. These are forgiven through the keys of the Church. For if you begin to judge yourself, if you begin to be displeased with yourself, God will come to show mercy. If you are willing to punish yourself, he will spare you. One who repents well is his own punisher. He must be severe with himself, so that God may be merciful to him, as David says, *Turn your face away from my sins, and blot out all my iniquities.* But on what grounds? In the same psalm he says, *For I acknowledge my iniquity, and my sin is always before me*. If, then, you acknowledge it, he pardons it. There are also light and little sins that cannot be entirely avoided. They may seem smaller, but their multitude weighs us down. A heap of grain, too, is made up of very tiny grains, and yet ships are loaded with it; and if they are loaded too heavily, they sink. A single thunderbolt strikes someone down and kills him; but excessive rain, too, kills many people, although its drops are very small. The one destroys with a single blow; the other extinguishes life by its abundance. Large beasts kill a human being with one bite; but little creatures, when many of them gather together, often kill people and cause such destruction that Pharaoh’s proud nation deserved to be judged with punishments of this kind. Although these sins are little, then, there are so many of them that together they form a heap that can crush you. God is good: he forgives even these sins, without which this life cannot be lived. But how does he forgive them if you do not forgive what is committed against you?

**13. 13.** This saying is in the human heart like a bucket used to bail out a ship at sea. The ship cannot help letting water in through the cracks in its joints. Yet, by taking in small amounts of water little by little, it collects so much that, unless it is bailed out, the water overwhelms the ship. So too in this life we have certain little cracks in our mortality and frailty, through which sin enters from the waves of this world. Let us seize this saying like a little bucket to bail ourselves out, so that we do not sink. Let us forgive our debtors their debts, so that God may forgive us ours. Through this saying—if we act upon it, so that we can say it truthfully—you bail out whatever has flowed in. But be on your guard: you are still at sea. Doing this once is not enough until you have crossed this sea and reached the firm and solid ground of your homeland. There no waves will batter you; you will not have to forgive what is not committed against you, nor want forgiveness for what you do not commit.

**14. 14.** I think I have commended this sufficiently to Your Charity, and I commend it again: because of these waves in which we are in danger, let us hold fast to the remedy that saves us. Consider also how greatly someone sins who tries to harm an innocent person, when even someone who does not forgive the harm another has done him is already intolerable. So let our brothers and sisters take heed and consider against whom they have harbored any bitterness of hatred. If they have not let it go, let them at least consider during these days what such feelings are doing to their hearts. Or if they really think themselves safe, let them pour vinegar into the vessels in which they have been accustomed to keep good wine. They do not do it; they take care that the earthenware is not spoiled. Yet they pour hatred into their hearts without fearing that it may cause some corruption there! Take care, then, brothers and sisters, to harm no one, as far as you can. And if, through the weakness of human life, some immoderation creeps into your use of these permitted things, since this contributes to the corruption of God’s temple, hold this firmly in mind and put it into practice: quickly forgive people the sins they commit against you, so that your Father in heaven may forgive you your sins.

[^2]: 1 Tim 1:13,16
