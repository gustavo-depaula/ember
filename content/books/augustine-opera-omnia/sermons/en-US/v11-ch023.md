# Sermon 374: Augm. (Dolbeau 23)

**1.** The annual celebration of this day demands from us an annual sermon, owed to your ears and hearts and, if you hear it piously, owed also to your conduct. For the fruit of our whole life is justice, the reward is eternal life, the beginning of justice is faith. For we have not been called to that which we already see, nor do we already hold what was promised. But because the promiser is truthful, we must first live in hope, that we may deserve to live in the reality: what God promised he will without doubt give. But because what he is going to give is great, by deferring it he extends desire to this, that he may make us capable of so great a gift. For he is not going to give the perishing goods that are in this world, changing, fragile, which, when they abound, fill with fears, but when they depart, fill with sorrows. But he is going to give—what do you think?—something earthly? Far from it! Something heavenly, such as we see with the eyes of the body? This too is cheap in comparison with the promised gift, for receiving which hearts are cleansed. For if God now gave you something that pertains to the present time, he would seek your eyes for seeing what he gives. But because he will give that *which eye has not seen nor ear heard nor has it ascended into the heart of man,* as the Apostle says, what does he prepare in you to whom he may give it? *Blessed,* he says, *are the pure in heart, for they shall see God*[^1]. Therefore our whole reward will be to see God. Or is it a cheap reward to see him by whom you were made? Stretch out the bosom of your desire as much as you please, be greedy to desire what so great a one promised: he cannot give you better than himself. For whatever God made is cheaper and inferior to the maker.

**2.** And whence does the creation of God begin? From the angels. And where does the creation of God end? Down to earthly mortal things. Upward there is an end of creation, beyond which is God. Downward there is an end of creation, beyond which is nothing. Begin, then, to count for now the gifts you have, not yet having received those to which you have been called. Begin. See the earthly goods, light, a certain nourishment of air without which we can at no time exist, then the fruits of the earth, springs, the very health of the body, and whatever such things can be recalled, which had to be briefly touched on: they are gifts of God, they are divine bounties. But do not covet these things as a great thing from him to whose image you were made. For you see these things to be still common to you with the beast, nor will you find from whom you take them, except from that true God, the creator and bestower of all; yet not because he gives these things too ought you now to be content with these: he keeps something for his image which he does not give to the beast. Let us seek, then, what it is, and recalled let us desire it, desired let us await it and, that we may be able to attain it, let us do what he who promised commands.

**3.** There is the reward, here the work. The promiser of the reward is also the giver of the precept: he who foretells what you may receive commands what you may do. If you love the reward of faith, do not flee the labor of the work. He will give: for God is truthful. Whoever promises and does not give, in two ways does not give, either because he is a liar, or because he cannot. What such thing can we say of God? Is truth a liar? Is there anything the Omnipotent cannot do? If, then, he is both truthful and has whence to give, why should he deceive you? Lift up your mind, hope securely: he who promised is powerful, is eternal. Or do you fear lest, when you have done it, before you receive it, your promiser be succeeded? Let us rather see, then, what was promised. Let us transcend these goods common with the beasts; let us see what man has proper, more than the beast, which you already hold: to speak, to understand, to discern the true and the false, the just and the unjust, finally him himself by whom he was made.

**4.** It is a great work to seek him whom it is a great reward to find. Man, then, differs from the beasts by eloquence—indeed, he differs from the beasts by speaking, from certain men by eloquence. This gift of eloquence is great, a great good, but the bad too have it. It is not common with the beasts, but it is common with the wicked. Many have eloquence and are bad, and by that very eloquence they circumvent many and seek temporal power for themselves. All these are to be condemned, but they are nonetheless men. Seek something from God which you have in common neither with the beast nor even with a bad man. Now if I have spoken of eloquence, take this of riches too. For he gave riches not to the beasts, but to men. For to men it pertains to rule, to command, to govern. But yet many even of the bad have riches: still this earthly good both the good have and the bad have, and the good do not have and the bad do not have. Seek something higher. Why do you fear lest, when you have found something choice that can be given neither to the beasts nor to bad men, the Omnipotent cannot give it to you, who called you to do a work and to receive a gift? "I have found something." What will you find? Talent the bad too have, memory the bad too have, riches—as I said—the bad too have, temporal honors and power the bad too have. What will you find?

**5.** O if you would hear me, seek nothing from God but God. To whomever he gives this, he even makes him good. That cannot be common with the bad which, received, distinguishes from the bad. For one thing is a good of which you may do something good, and another a good by which you may become good. All goods except God are such that of them you may do well, but God is such a good by which you may become good. You have gold: it is a good, but whence you may do well. You have eloquence: it is a good, but whence you may work well. You have health of body: use it well. For many have been corrected by sickness and cast down by health. Many fell sick to their good, and recovered to their harm. Therefore even health of body itself, which is the patrimony of the poor, unless it is turned to good use, is even harmful. Sharp talent is a great good, but still such that both the good can use it well and the bad badly: it is not yet a good by which you become good. All the perversities of all errors, all the sects of transgression and impiety have had authors of great talent. Men of any sort whatever did not bear them; they were instituted by sharp men. The theatrical trifles, all to the ruin of the human race, were not invented except by clever men. "They are all hostile, certainly base." They are base, you say. How, then, is what is done basely watched honorably? You confess this to be base, and you favor that it can exist. I do not know which I should call worse: the seller of baseness or the buyer. And yet these things were invented and instituted by the sharpest talents. Therefore talent too is a good, but if you use it well. So far I have recalled those goods whence you may do well, not whence you may become good. If that good comes by which you become good, you use the other goods well. But if that good does not come by which you are made good, whence, being bad, will you be able to use the other goods well? What is the good by which you become good? He himself is God.

**6.** There is a certain thing which is beheld by the mind. For the eyes do not have what to see, and the mind not have. Which is better: the body or the mind? I think that even lifeless bodies, if they could, would answer that the mind is better. But it is unwilling to tell me that the mind is better than the body. So I ask: Who is better, he who rules or he who is ruled? Now here I think not even the beasts would doubt to answer that the ruler is better. Who, then, rules? Surely the mind, which is better than the body. If the mind is absent, the windows of the body, though they lie open, have no one to see through them. The eyes lie open, the ears lie open: if the inhabitant is absent, what do the unbarred doors profit? Therefore the mind through the eyes perceives certain things: light, colors, forms; the mind through the ears certain things: voices and sounds; the mind through smell certain things: all odors; the mind through the sense of taste all flavors; the mind through the whole body hard and soft, rough and smooth, cold and hot, light and heavy. The eye does not hear, nor the ear see, yet the mind both sees through the eye and hears through the ear. It cannot do both through either, but yet it alone can what it can through either. Does the mind do nothing through itself? Can it do things through the body, and through itself cannot? It sees through the body white and black things: can it not through itself see just and unjust things? And yet there are many who think that these alone exist which are seen through the body, doing injury to their own mind, ungrateful to God by whom they were made to his image. Within is the inhabitant, it has other eyes of its own.

**7.** If I wished to show gems, if I wished to show gold and silver, vessels skillfully wrought, precious garments, and whatever precious and most beautiful things are reckoned among the riches of men, if finally I wished to show the sky, and not a paneled but a starred ceiling of the poor, what would I seek? Eyes. When I wish to show the just and the unjust, what do I seek? to whom do I show? But perhaps I do not have what to show. For some have thought this too, that justice is not by nature, but is established by opinion, that is, that this is just—rather, is called just—which men willed to be just by a certain pact of human society, not by any nature of justice. Is there, then, after all, no nature of justice? We labor to show it—and yet we ought to know it most of all, which we know by the mind alone—we labor to show what the nature of justice is, and we do not labor to show that gold exists, that the earth exists, that the sky exists. Do I labor to show these things? Every man cries out that what can be seen through the body exists, and many deny that what cannot be seen except through the mind exists.

**8.** It is a wonder if this mind is not sick, a wonder if it does not have its own eyes wounded or even extinguished! Whence may they be repaired? whence healed? The door of medicine is faith. For that it may see what it does not see, it must believe that there is something it may see, but that it is not yet such as to see it. "Perhaps I can: show it." But to whom shall I show it? Indeed, neither can I, but he can who shows it even to me, if I see anything from this. The physician comes to a sick man having wounded eyes, or perhaps already lost—he has forgotten that he once saw, or perhaps from his birth he never saw—but yet he is so great a physician that he can by treatment drive off the ancient blindness: he promises that there is something he can show, if the man suffers himself to be cured. But further, before that man sees, if he does not believe, how will he be cured? "There is," he says, "what you may see, but then you will see, when your eyes have been made healthy." And the man: "Unless I see, I will not be cured." How absurd and perverse an answer: to wish first to see in order to be cured, who surely, if he could see, there would be no reason why he should be cured. Believe, then, the physician about to demonstrate, that you may not resist the one about to cure. "Show me," he says. What shall I show you? "God." To whom? "To me," he says, "if you wish me to believe. For I do not believe except what I see." Whom do you seek to have shown, to whom do you seek it shown, by whom do you seek it shown? God is to be shown, to a man he is to be shown, by a man he cannot be shown: he himself shows himself. I can admonish you what to do, that you may merit to see.

**9.** "There is no God at all," he says. All shudder. Who would say this: "There is no God"? And yet the psalm was not silent about it: *The fool said in his heart: There is no God*[^2]. Because it is such a word that all shudder at it, *he said it in his heart,* he did not commit it to tongue and voice. But yet I too seek something. "Show me," he says, "God." Brother, let us seek together. Yet even if I try to show it and am not able—for there is need perhaps both of time, there is need of some study, there is need of some precepts—yet I too ask something. You say: "Show me God"; I say: Show me your soul. You ask of me the highest thing, I a humble one; you ask the one from whom you are absent, I the one that is present; you ask the one to be sought, I the one seeking. See, if I say that you have no soul, what will you do? For if we must believe the eyes only, you have no soul; unless I see your soul, I will not believe it exists. You will say: "The soul indeed cannot be seen with the eyes, but it can be shown through its works. You see me walking, you hear me speaking; you speak, I answer, and do you doubt that I have a soul?" Meanwhile I have not seen your soul, but I have seen the works of your soul. If through the works of your soul I know your soul, through the works of God know God. Is there a soul, because it moves the flesh, and is there no God who moves the world? Does the order of things not strike you: earthly things subject to heavenly, the alternations of night and day, the disposition of the seasons, the brightness of the sun by which the clearness of day is filled, of the moon and stars by which the darkness of night is tempered, the fruits coming forth from the earth, the springs flowing? The very animals which are born on the earth are born with life: what is made lives, and does not he who makes it live? There is, then, a God: far be it that we doubt! But perhaps let him who doubts still seek, but so seek that he first believe, lest by not believing he fail to merit to find what he seeks.

**10.** There is, then, a God, and the question rather becomes how he is to be worshiped than whether he is. What then? Let us speak something on this too. To Christians indeed I think I speak easily: that God is to be worshiped just as he commanded himself to be worshiped. For if a Christian asks me how God commanded himself to be worshiped, I do not bring forth my own word, but I read out a book, to which without doubt he is subject by the merit of his faith. For about the divine writings it is not lawful for him to doubt: how God willed himself to be worshiped, he also willed to be written; what he willed to be written, he willed to be read; and he gave such a height and summit of authority to that same Scripture that he set the authors of all other books beneath its feet. For there were those who wrote what they wished, as they wished. Whose book is set on so great a summit of authority that the world answers it "amen"? When we deal with someone perhaps not subject to so great an authority of the books, and contending with me and saying: "Men wrote these things for themselves," what shall I do? whence may we prove that these things are written divinely?

**11.** Apart from that administration of nature by which the world is governed, men have not shuddered at certain divine works, except at wonderful deeds and sayings. Nature is indeed full of marvels, but all marvels have grown cheap by their constancy. Finally, let one man who was among men rise from death: it is proclaimed by all a divine work; so many daily who did not exist are born, and no one wonders. Christ turned water into wine, a great miracle: who else does this in the vine every year? Do you think it little to be wondered at that moisture is drawn from the earth, turned into the quality of that wood, goes through the branches, unfolds the foliage, even brings forth swelling clusters, increases the unripe, colors the ripe? Whence all these things come, ask the root. What numbers there, what measures, what art of working in so slight and contemptible a form? Still I marvel at the workmanship of the root; the grain of seed is more to be marveled at. How small, how almost nothing, where yet are all the numbers of the future root, trunk, branches, fruits, leaves, and the working numbers drawing the sap, turning it into a work so beautiful and pleasant. These are the astonishing works of the Creator. You have not yet ascended into heaven to see him present, and on earth you already find him fashioning. These things need a considerer. For what is more wonderful than such works? Yet because they are daily, as I said, they have grown cheap. Therefore God, wishing to rouse the minds of men, kept for himself certain works not greater, but certainly rarer. For it is more to make a man than to raise one again. But because no one now wondered at him making daily, he showed himself sometimes raising again. He bestowed light on the blind, hearing on the deaf, speech on the mute. He who does these things without a miracle in seeds, did them with a great miracle in men.

**12.** These things are written, these are read. "But now," he says, "they are not done, and I fear lest these things too were written by men and were not done." We sometimes endure such questioners; you indeed ought not to be such, but you ought to be able to answer such. I know that to believers I speak almost superfluously, but let me be like one persuading the unbelieving, that you may be equipped against the unbelieving. Certain men say, now indeed few, yet not none: "These things are written, but they were not done." Whence, then, shall I prove that they were done? Every miracle surely is either in a deed or in a saying. The wonderful deeds are those done beyond the accustomed course and order of nature; the sayings are those by which future things are announced. Accordingly, if you do not wish to believe the wonderful deeds which have been written to be read, believe at least the wonderful sayings which are shown true by the very effect. For he shows his trustworthiness who narrated to you wonderful past things, when you behold the present things which he foretold as future. Or is divination perhaps not divine, which from this even received its name, and to which the curiosity of men is so devoted that many today are for this reason unwilling to be Christians, while they wish it to be permitted them to consult the possessed, astrologers, augurs, and—what else shall I say?—magicians? But the power of Christ extinguishes also the works of this art, which led the Magi from so distant a nation to adore him.

**13.** They came to adore the infant, the Word of God. Why did they come? Because they saw an unusual star. And whence did they recognize it to be Christ's? For they could see the star, but could it speak to them and say: "I am the star of Christ"? Without doubt it was indicated in another way, through some revelation. Yet unusually a king had been born who was to be adored even by foreigners. Were kings not born before in Judea, or throughout the whole earth in diverse nations? Why was this one to be adored—and to be adored by foreigners—with no army terrifying, but with the majesty of his power hidden in the poverty of the flesh? When he was born, he was adored by the Israelite shepherds, to whom the angels announced him. But the Magi were not of Israel: they worshiped idols or the gods of the nations, that is, demons, by whose deceitful power they were deceived. They saw, then, a certain unusual star, they wondered: they sought without doubt of what it was the sign which they saw so new and unusual, and surely they heard. But you will say: "From whom did they hear?" Surely from angels, by some admonition of revelation. You ask perhaps: "From good angels, or bad?" Even the bad angels, that is, the demons, confessed Christ to be the Son of God. But why should they not have heard from the good ones too, since in adoring Christ their salvation was now being sought, not their iniquity being condemned? The angels, then, could say to them: "The star you saw is Christ's, go and adore him where he was born," and at the same time indicate of what kind and how great a one was born. But they, having heard this, came, adored. Gold, frankincense, and myrrh they offered as gifts, according to their custom: for such things they were wont to offer to their gods.

**14.** But indeed, before they did this, before they found him in the city where he had been born, they came seeking where the king of the Jews was born. Could they not also have known this by revelation, just as they knew that star to be of the king of the Jews, to be adored even by foreigners? Could not the same star have led them to that city, just as it afterward led them to the place where Christ was an infant with his mother? It could indeed, yet it was not done, that they might inquire this from the Jews. Why did God will this to be inquired from the Jews? That, while they show the one in whom they do not believe, they may be condemned by their own demonstration. Attend, because even now it happens. The first-fruits of the nations, the Magi, the more freed from a greater impiety, give the greater glory to the liberator. They ask: *Where is he who is born king of the Jews?*[^3] Herod, hearing the name of king, trembles as a rival. He calls the experts in the law, asks of them according to the Scriptures that they may indicate where Christ would be born. They answer: *In Bethlehem of Judah*[^4]. The Magi proceeded and adored: the Jews remained, who had demonstrated it.

**15.** O great sacrament! Today from the books of the Jews we convict the unbelieving, that they may become believing; through their books we show to the pagans what they are unwilling to believe. For sometimes the pagans make us a question of this kind, when they see the things that are written so fulfilled that they cannot at all deny that those things are presented through the name of Christ in all the nations which are read out as foretold in the holy books, in the faith of kings, in the overthrow of idols, in the change of human affairs; and sometimes they are so moved as to say: "You saw these things happen and wrote them as if they were foretold." A certain poet of theirs did this: those who have read recognize these things. He narrated that a certain man descended to the underworld and thence came into the region of the blessed, and that there were shown to him the princes of the Romans who were to be born, whom the very one who wrote these things already knew to be born. For he narrated past things, but wrote them as if they were foretold as future. "So too you," the pagans say to us, "saw all these things happen and wrote yourselves books, in which they are read as if foretold to come." O glory of our king! Rightly were the Jews conquered by the Romans and not destroyed. All the nations subdued by the Romans passed into the laws of the Romans: this nation was both conquered and remained in its own law; as far as concerns the worship of God, it keeps its ancestral custom and rite. With its temple too overthrown, its former priesthood extinguished, as was foretold by the prophets, it yet keeps circumcision and a certain custom, by which it is distinguished from the other nations. For what other reason, but for the testimony of truth? The Jews are scattered everywhere, carrying the books in which Christ, foretold—and, as he was foretold, presented—can be shown even to the pagans. I bring forth the book, I read the prophet, I show the prophecy to be fulfilled. The pagan doubts, lest perhaps I have feigned this myself: my enemy has this book, anciently commended to him by his forebears. I convict both from it: the Jew, because I have known to be fulfilled what was prophesied there; the pagan, because I did not feign this.

**16.** Let not the demons, then, seduce the incautious and those badly curious about temporal things by an appearance of divination, nor, deceiving with proud arrogance, demand for themselves the honor of impious sacrifices. The true divine predictions are of the one true God. The true sacrifice is owed to the one true God, whose figures were formerly shadowed forth in incense and victims, that what was going to be in one way the divine providence, foretelling in many ways, might show how great it was. For this too, among the other things foretold to come in the Christian times, was prophesied, that those things which were formerly offered to God in sacrifices were to be wholly changed. "Why, then," you say, "were they commanded, if they were to be changed?" O sick man, do not give counsel to the physician how you are to be cured. One man, Adam, filled the world by propagation. The human race, as one man, a great sick man lying from east to west, must be cured: a great sick man, but a greater physician. Let us, then, take a likeness from the very medicine of human art. The physician comes to the sick man and says: "Take this in the morning, but that in the afternoon." And the sick man to the physician: "Why," he says, "not this in the afternoon which I take in the morning?" Would not the physician rightly answer him: "You can be sick, you cannot cure yourself; leave to the art the counsel of your health"? "Therefore," says the sick man, "the art is inconsistent, ordering one thing first, another afterward." "Be quiet rather and be healed. The art is consistent, it knows what to apply in the morning, what in the afternoon. It is not changed, but your sickness is changeable. It knows what is expedient when, which comes to the aid of changeable diseases according to the diversity of times." So, then, for the benefit of the human race, in earlier times some things profited, in later times other things profit. You ask why? Be a friend of the physician, and perhaps you will know, lest perhaps even today you think we ought not to go to sacrifice to God, except with a bull, with a ram, with incense.

**17.** "Show," someone says, "that God commanded these things and foretold that they were to be changed, and I will believe, lest perhaps you say these things to me from yourself, not from divine authority." Hear, then, the words of God, a few of many: *Behold, the days will come, says the Lord*. The prophet speaks. If you think this was feigned by Christians, let the Jew bring forth the book. How, then, can I feign what my enemy anciently carries? Let the book, then, be brought forth from the library of the Jews, and let us read from it. What are we to read? *Behold, the days will come, says the Lord, and I will complete for the house of Israel and the house of Judah a new covenant*[^5]. "But perhaps he speaks of that covenant which the Jews received, and calls it new, since our fathers received that one which was given through Moses on Mount Sinai, because it was not before in the human race." He has admonished you well, but wait: I demand patience, until I read out the rest. Hear about the New Covenant: *Behold, the days will come, says the Lord, and I will complete for the house of Israel and the house of Judah a new covenant.* Still I do not say the rest. Who is it that speaks? Jeremiah. When did Jeremiah prophesy? Long after Moses, through whom the first covenant was given. If, then, Jeremiah, who says: *Behold, the days will come,* speaks of the future, *and I will complete for the house of Israel and the house of Judah a new covenant,* there is no reason it should be said that that covenant which was given through Moses is signified. Therefore he speaks of another covenant as new. But yet hear the rest too: *Behold, the days will come, says the Lord, and I will complete for the house of Israel and the house of Judah a new covenant, not according to the covenant which I made with their fathers, in the day when I took their hand, to lead them out of the land of Egypt*[^6]. What is more evident? For then was given through Moses the first covenant, which is called old.

**18.** Come now, brethren, attend to the divine words. We read it foretold, we behold it fulfilled, and do we still doubt about the divine authority of the books? Let no one, then, now say: "See, the Magi gave incense to Christ, why do we not also offer incense to Christ?" First attend that the Magi gave incense, they had not burned it. "But why," you say, "did he accept this gift?" As if indeed these men were going to forbid that any creature be offered to the Creator! God made incense—not the demons—God made myrrh, God made gold. The Magi, by giving these to the demons, sin and by honoring them with these, using a creature to the injury of the Creator. But yet today learn that they gave such things as they were wont to give to their gods. Yet not in vain did Christ permit them to give such things: they were signs rather than gifts. He accepted incense as God, gold as king, myrrh as one about to die, for burial. But then not only incense, but also the victims of cattle were offered both by pagans and by Jews, that is, both by those who worshiped many false gods and by those who worshiped the one true God. These things, I say, were offered to God according to the Old Covenant, but he changed this by the New Covenant. *Behold,* he says, *the days will come, says the Lord, and I will complete for the house of Israel and the house of Judah a new covenant, not according to the covenant which I made with their fathers, in the day when I took their hand, to lead them out of the land of Egypt.*

**19.** "But," someone says, "he did not speak of sacrifices." Do you wish, then, to hear something about sacrifices? That God foretold that it was going to be that all those sacrifices should be taken away and one sacrifice given that truly cleanses sins, the body of Christ, the faithful know. And what I say, I know is not understood by all, but let those who understand rejoice and live worthily of so great a sacrament. But those who do not yet understand, it is in their power to change their life, to receive the sacrament of change, and to know what is offered by the faithful, what is received. For there is that which was foretold: *You are a priest forever according to the order of Melchizedek*[^7]. For there was first a priesthood according to the order of Aaron, but afterward it was said to our priest, and this through the prophet long before he came in the flesh: *You are a priest forever according to the order of Melchizedek.* Therefore the order of Aaron was changed and the order of Melchizedek came. According to the order of Aaron, the victims of cattle; according to Melchizedek, the body of Christ. "You have not yet said," someone says, "whether God foretold that those sacrifices were to cease." See, I read to you, lest perhaps something escape my memory. For I, brethren, did not learn these letters from adolescence, and other things—which is worse—superfluous I can recite from memory. But these things, which I did not study from boyhood, unless I look at the books, I cannot pronounce. Or perhaps it is more useful that you hear, not from my mouth, but from the divine book, what you may healthfully know. Hear now and do not wonder that men now do not come to Christ with incense, from the time he instituted another sacrifice, of which all the past things were shadows.

**20.** Among many things the prophet Isaiah says, whose book I hold in my hands. What does Isaiah say? *There is no reason for you to be mindful of the former things, nor to bring the old things into your mind*[^8]. See now, brethren, the abolition of the old things is manifest. "But still," you say, "he has said nothing about sacrifices. Perhaps he changed other things, these he did not change." Although it is general, *nor bring the old things into your mind,* and there it is permitted me to understand what the apostolic teaching afterward taught me, what I am to offer, what I am now not to offer—I have an expounder of this obscurity, I have a master telling me according to what I am to take this—yet not even the prophet himself permits a man to conjecture what he pleases: for he openly says what you are about to hear. Therefore hear: *There is no reason for you to be mindful of the former things, nor to bring the old things into your mind. For I will make new things which shall now arise, and you will understand*[^9]. What is this: *For I will make new things which shall now arise, and you will understand?* That is: I will make new things which were not, that you may be able to understand those which were. A beast was slain, blood was poured out, and by blood God was appeased. Is God so appeased by blood, and does God desire blood, and is God delighted by the smoke of sacrifices, or does he seek the odor of incense or of the other spices who creates all things, who gives you all things? Far be it that you believe it! He is fed by your devotion, and that indeed useful to you, not to him whom you serve. Every man who is the servant of a human master serves his master for the benefit of his master, and again that master provides for his servant for the benefit of his servant. Not so is God. Whoever serves him, serves him for his own benefit, not for his. And do I perhaps say this from myself? Hear the prophet: *I said to the Lord.* What? *You are my Lord.* Why? *For,* he says, *you have no need of my goods*[^10]. You have an absolute sentence, there is no reason to doubt: God has no need of your goods. Do not, then, believe that your God needs such sacrifices, but seek what they teach, what they signify. Formerly blood was poured out in victims, because in one true victim blood was foretold to be poured out, the blood of your Lord, the blood your price, the blood by which the record of your debt would be blotted out, that is, the oldness of your sin abolished. It has come, it has been poured out, he himself is offered. *Let the day now breathe, and let the shadows be removed*[^11]. *There is no reason for you to be mindful of the former things, nor to bring the old things into your mind. For I will make new things which shall now arise, and you will understand.* Now then we understand why those things went before which foretold the things to come. All things are interpreted unto Christ, all things have their end unto Christ. *I will make,* he says, *new things which shall now arise, and you will understand.* Before all these new things were made, the old things were carried on and not understood.

**21.** What then follows? *I will make a way in the wilderness.* In what *wilderness?* Surely of the nations, where there was no worship of the true God. *I will make a way in the wilderness and rivers in the dry land*[^12]. Nowhere among the nations were the prophets read, now throughout all nations their Scripture is spread. See *rivers in the dry land. The beasts of the field shall bless me.* What is *the beasts of the field,* but the nations are understood? *The beasts of the field shall bless me, and the sirens, daughters of sparrows*[^13]. Certain impious souls, daughters of demons, *shall bless me.* Whence, but by forsaking the devil, converted to Christ? "But these things are still obscure and according to our talent something more open is interpreted. Say whence it may be demonstrated that the sacrifices of victims displeased God." If I could not read out something more open, I would not dare to take this book in my hands. Let your love, then, hear with patience. *The beasts of the field shall bless me, and the sirens, daughters of sparrows.* Why *shall they bless?* Hear what follows: *Because I have given water in the desert, and rivers in that place where there was no water, that I might give drink to my chosen race*[^14]. To what race of yours? *To my people whom I have acquired for myself. I did not now call you Jacob, nor did I make you Israel that you should labor. To what end have I sheep for whole-burnt offerings?*[^15] Come now, brethren. I do not know if there is anyone who would say to me: "I do not understand. You say what you will, you interpret what you will." I am now made not a disputer, but a reader. Refer this to that from which we began, where he says: *There is no reason for you to be mindful of the former things, nor to bring the old things into your mind.* For this appears, that God first commanded those things to be done for the sake of a certain sacrifice, foretelling that just blood was to be poured out and shadowing it forth by the likeness of victims. For now what does he say? *To what end have I sheep for whole-burnt offerings? For neither in your sacrifices did you honor me, but neither did you serve me in your sacrifices. For I did not make you for this, that you should labor in incense*[^16]. Lest perhaps someone say that he forbade the victims, but permitted the incense: *Neither did I make you for this,* he says, *that you should labor in incense, nor that you should buy me incense with silver, nor did I desire the fat of your sacrifices*[^17].

**22.** Let us dare to say to the Lord our God: "And why did you institute these things first?" Now here we seek understanding. Because in these there was not abolition, but the testifying of sins. What I have said, I know to be obscure and somewhat to be made manifest. But I say it briefly, because I have already said much; if perhaps I am less able to explain this on account of the shortness of time, the Lord will be present that at another time I may be able. Yet this I say: that people was such that it had certain wise men, holy, just, and had also a carnal crowd, not knowing why those things were commanded, doing rather than understanding; but now through this prophet he briefly shows why he commanded those things. When he had said: *Nor did I desire the fat of your sacrifices* and the rest of the kind, at once, as if it were asked why he then commanded them, he subjoined and said: *But in your sins and iniquities you stood before me*[^18]. For all those things availed for a testimony to the sinning. Why this? Unless to lay down the neck of pride. Why this? Unless because Christ was going to come with grace, blotting out the record of sins, and the Jews were going to say: "We are just." But what does the Apostle say to them? *For all have sinned and need the glory of God*[^19]. "Whence," he says, "do you prove that we have sinned?" "The sacrifices which you offered for sins speak as testimonies against you." This God says: *Neither did I desire the fat of your sacrifices, but in your sins and iniquities you stood before me.* Whence the sacrifices which you offered convicted you, did not cleanse you. Now then let the guilty people, with pride broken, confessing their sickness, seeking health, say to themselves: "What, then, shall I do? If by those sacrifices my sins were not cleansed, whence shall I be cleansed?"

**23.** Hear what follows: *I am, I am he who blots out your iniquities, that you may be justified*[^20]. *I am, I am,* not the bull, not the ram, not the he-goat, not any spices, not incense, but *I am, I am he who blots out your iniquities, that you may be justified.* How greatly he commended his grace! Lest anyone glory in the merits of works or the abundance of victims, it did not suffice to say once: *I am,* but by doubling it he more vehemently commended it: *I am, I am.* He himself the physician, he himself the medicine. The physician, because the Word; the medicine, because *the Word made flesh.* He himself the priest, he himself the sacrifice. *I am, I am he who blots out your iniquities, that you may be justified.* Lest perhaps, because he says: *I blot out your iniquities,* it always delight you to sin, to this end he blots them out *that you may be justified,* that is, that, the former iniquities blotted out, you may afterward live rightly, that you may receive what he promised. Rightly too those Magi, the first-fruits of the nations, in whom, as much as sin had abounded, so much grace superabounded, divinely admonished *not to return to Herod, returned by another way*[^21]. He who then changed the way of the Magi, he himself even now changes the life of the bad. His manifestation in the flesh, which in Greek is called Epiphany, the nations, justified in the Spirit, celebrate together on this day's solemnity, that the solemnity may renew the memory, piety flourish by devotion, love grow hot by assembly, truth shine to the envious.
