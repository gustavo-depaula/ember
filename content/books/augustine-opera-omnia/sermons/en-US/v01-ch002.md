# Sermon 2: On Abraham, Where He Is Tested by God

**1.** The well-known piety of our father Abraham has been brought back to our remembrance by the recent reading. And it is something so wonderful that we must suppose no heart so forgetful that it could ever slip from it. Yet somehow, however often it is read, it affects the minds of the hearers as though it were happening then. Great faith, great piety, not only toward God but even toward his only son, for whom the father believed there could be no evil in whatever he commanded who had created him. For Abraham could be his son's father according to the operation of the flesh, but not his creator and founder according to the operation of majesty. Indeed, as the Apostle says, Isaac was born to Abraham not *according to the flesh* but *by promise*[^1]. Not because he had not begotten him in the flesh, but because he had received him out of the deepest despair. And had not God been present to promise, the old man would no longer have dared to hope for any posterity from the womb of his aged wife. But he believed that the child would be born, and he did not mourn that he would die. His right hand is chosen for the sacrifice, that the son might die, whose heart was chosen for faith, that the son might be born. Abraham did not tremble to believe when it was promised. He did not tremble to offer when it was demanded. Nor was the believer's religion opposed to the devotion of one obeying. This I say: Abraham did not say to himself, "God has spoken to me. When he promised a son, I believed that God would give me posterity. And what posterity? Such that he said to me, *In Isaac shall your seed be named*[^2]. And lest perhaps my seed should be named in Isaac in such a way that my son should die before me, *In your seed,* he said, *shall all nations be blessed*[^3]. He himself, then, in speaking to me, promised a son, and he himself demands that I kill him!" He did not make a problem for himself, as though out of contrary and opposing words of God promising that a son would be born and afterward saying, "Kill your son for me." But in his heart there was always an unshaken faith, in no way failing. For Abraham reflected that God, who had granted that one should be born of aged parents who did not exist, was able even to restore him from death. For greater was what God had already done, when after such great despair he saw given to him a son who had not existed, which, if you consult human weakness, was impossible. And so he applied his mind to faith. He did not believe that anything was impossible to the Creator. As he had believed when he received the son, so he believed afterward when God commanded. In receiving the son he had already proved God. He believed when he was to receive the son, he believed when he was to kill him. Everywhere faithful, nowhere cruel. He led his son all the way to the place of the victim. He even armed his right hand with the knife. You attend to who strikes, and whom he strikes. Attend to who commands. Abraham, then, is dutiful in obeying. What of God in commanding? Lest perhaps to weak souls—I will not say sacrilegious—he himself who commanded be displeasing. But if the one who obeyed is pleasing, how is the one who commanded displeasing? For if Abraham did well in obeying, much better, and incomparably far better, did God in commanding.

**2.** Perhaps a hidden meaning is being sought. For God would not command this in vain, nor is it to be taken in a fleshly way—which, when read, has perhaps disturbed the hearts of some who understand too little. *God,* it says, *tested Abraham*[^4]. Is God, then, so ignorant of things, so unaware of the human heart, that he should discover by testing a man? Far from it. Rather, it is so that the man himself may discover himself. First of all, then, brothers, on account of those who oppose the old Law, the holy Scripture—because some who do not understand are quicker to attack what they do not understand than to seek so as to understand, and are not humble inquirers but proud slanderers—on account of these, then, who wish to accept the Gospel and to reject the old Law, thinking that they can be on God's way and rightly walk on one foot, since they are not scribes learned in the kingdom of God who bring forth from their treasure things new and old, on account, then, of such people, lest perhaps some of them be hiding here, or, even if they are not here, that those present may have something to answer such people, this question must be briefly resolved. We say to such people: You accept the Gospel, you do not accept the Law; but we say that the most merciful giver of the Gospel is the same who was also the giver of the terrible Law. For by the Law he terrified, and by the Gospel he healed those converted whom he had terrified by the Law that they might be converted. An emperor gave a law, and many things were committed against the law. The law the emperor gave knew only to punish sinners. It remained, then, that he who had sent the law ahead should come himself with pardon to undo their offenses. But what does the perverse heart say, since it says that it accepts the Gospel but rejects the Law? Why does it reject it? "Because it is written," it says, "*God tested Abraham*. Shall I worship a God who tests?" Worship Christ, whom you have in the Gospel. He himself recalls you to understanding the Law. But because they have not crossed over to Christ, they have remained in their own phantasm. For they do not worship Christ such as he is preached from the Gospel, but such as they have fashioned for themselves. Therefore over the veil of their natural folly they add another veil of perverse opinion. And how, through a double veil, can what shines in the Gospel be seen? A God who tests displeases you; let Christ who tests displease you. But when Christ who tests has pleased you, let God who tests please you. For Christ too, the Son of God, is God, and Christ with the Father is one God. Where, then, do we read of Christ testing? The Gospel speaks. It says: He says to Philip, *You have loaves. Give them to eat*. And the Evangelist adds, *But this he said to test him; for he himself knew what he was about to do*[^5]. Now turn your mind back to God testing Abraham. For this God too said in testing Abraham: he himself knew what he was about to do. Christ the tester is acknowledged, God the tester is acknowledged; let the heretic the tester be corrected. For the heretic does not test as God tests. For God tests in order to open a person up, the heretic tests in order to close God off from himself.

**3.** Therefore let your Charity know that God's testing does not aim at his coming to know something he did not know before, but that, by his testing, that is, his questioning, what is hidden in a person may be brought out. For a person is not so known to himself as to the Creator, nor is a sick man so known to himself as to the physician. A man is ill. He himself suffers, the physician does not suffer, and from him who does not suffer he waits to hear what it is he suffers. Therefore in the psalm a person cries out, *From my hidden faults cleanse me, O Lord*[^6]. For there are in a person things hidden from the very person in whom they are. And they do not come forth, nor are they opened up, nor are they found, except by tests. If God ceases to test, the master ceases to teach. But God tests in order to teach; the devil tests in order to deceive. Yet unless the one who is tested gives him room, he is driven off empty and laughable. Therefore the Apostle says, *Do not give place to the devil*[^7]. But people give place to the devil out of their own desires. For people do not see the devil with whom they fight, but they have an easy remedy. Let them conquer themselves within, and they triumph over him outside. Why do we say this? Because a person does not know himself unless in testing he learns himself. But when he has learned himself, let him not neglect himself. For if he neglected himself while hidden, let him not neglect himself once known.

**4.** What then shall we say, brothers? Even if Abraham knew himself, we did not know Abraham. He had to be made known either to himself or at least to us: to himself, that he might know whence to give thanks; to us, that we might know either what to ask of the Lord or what to imitate in the man. What, then, does Abraham teach us? To put it briefly: that we should not prefer to God what God gives. Meanwhile, as to the letter of the deeds done, before the hidden depth of the mystery is treated—that is, what lies hidden in this mystery in which Abraham was commanded to kill his only son: Therefore do not prefer to him who bestowed it even that which God bestows on you as something great. And when he chooses to take it from you, let it not become cheap to you, for God is to be loved freely. For what reward sweeter from God than God himself?

**5.** So Abraham fulfills the obedience of his devotion. He hears from God, *Now I know that you fear God*[^8]. This is understood to mean that God brought it about that Abraham should become known to himself. Just as when a prophet speaks (I am speaking to Christians, or to those advancing in the school of God; what I say is neither crude nor new, but most familiar and most evident to your Holiness along with us)—when a prophet speaks, what do we say? "God said." We say well. We also say, "The prophet said." And we say both rightly, and we find both in authority. And the Apostles understood the Prophets in such a way as to say, *God said*[^9]. And in another place, *Isaiah said*[^10]. Both are true, because we find both in the Scriptures. Let the Christian resolve for me the question I have just proposed, and he will resolve for himself the one I proposed a little earlier. How? Because what a man says concerning God's gift, God says, according to that text, *For it is not you who speak*[^11], and so on; and again, *Behold, I Paul speak to you*[^12]; and again, *Christ who speaks in me*[^13]. Therefore, brothers, transfer this rule to what once seemed twisted, and it will be straight.

**6.** Let us all, then, look to him, that he himself may feed our hungering souls who hungered for our sake, who became *poor, though he was rich, that by his poverty*[^14] we might be enriched. Opportunely a little while ago we sang to him, *All things look to you, that you may give them food in due season*[^15]. If all things, then all people. If all people, then we also. Therefore, if we are to give anything good in this discourse, it is not we who will give it, but he from whom we all receive, because we all wait upon him. It is the time; let him give—but let us do what he said in order that he may give, that is, let us wait upon him. Let us look to him with the heart. For just as the eyes and ears of the body are turned toward us, so the eyes and ears of the heart are turned toward him. With the ear of the heart open, hear a great mystery. All the mysteries of the divine Scriptures are indeed great and divine. Yet there are some more notable and more excellent, which especially demand that we be attentive, and which beyond the rest build up the fallen, satisfy the hungry—who are not so satisfied as to grow weary, but it is a satiety without weariness, driving out want while not taking on contempt. Whom would it not move that an only son is commanded to be sacrificed to the one by whom he was promised? And especially when the matter is recounted as it happened, it makes our minds attentive to seeking out the hidden meaning set before us.

**7.** Before all things, however, brothers, in the name of the Lord we both admonish you as much as we can and instruct you that, when you hear the hidden meaning of a passage of Scripture narrating what was done expounded, you first believe that what was read happened just as it was read, lest, the foundation of the deed being removed, you should seek to build, as it were, in the air. Abraham our father was in those times a faithful man, believing God, justified by faith, as Scripture says, both the old and the new. He received a son from his wife Sarah, both being now established in old age, out of great despair—but according to man. But what is not to be hoped for from God, for whom nothing is difficult? Who does great things as he does small ones; who raises the dead as he creates the living. If a painter makes a mouse by the same art by which he makes an elephant (different works, but one art), how much more God, who spoke and they were made, who commanded and they were created? What does he do with difficulty, who makes by a word? With the ease with which he created the angels above the heavens, with that ease the lights in the heavens, with that ease the fish in the sea, with that ease the trees and animals on the earth, with that ease the great as the small. To him, then, for whom it was easiest of all to make everything from nothing, is it to be wondered at that he gave a son to the aged? Such men, then, or such people, God had, and in that time he had made such heralds of the Son to come, that not only in what they said, but even in what they did, or in what happened to them, Christ might be sought, Christ might be found. Whatever was written and done concerning Abraham is also a prophecy, as the Apostle says in a certain place: *For it is written that Abraham had two sons, one by the slave woman and the other by the free woman, which things are an allegory*[^16]. These, then, are the two Testaments.

**8.** Now, then, we do not say imprudently that Isaac both was born to Abraham and signified something. So too, commanded to sacrifice his son, he obeys God, leads him to the place, arrives after three days, leaves his two servants with the beast of burden, goes on himself where God had commanded; he raises the wood onto the altar, raises the son upon the wood. Before the son comes to the place of sacrifice, he carries the wood by which he is to be raised. Then, when he was now almost being struck, a voice sounds that he be spared. And yet there is no withdrawing without a sacrifice and without blood poured out. A ram appears, its horns caught in a thicket. It is sacrificed; the sacrifice is carried out. With the sacrifice carried out, it is said to Abraham, *I will make your seed as the stars of heaven and the sand of the sea. And your seed shall possess the cities of its adversaries. And in your seed shall all the nations of the earth be blessed, because you have obeyed my voice*[^17]. See, then, when it was done, and when the commemoration of the very deed takes place. When that ram says, *They have pierced my hands and my feet*[^18], and so on. When that sacrifice in the psalm was carried out, then in that very psalm it was said, *All the ends of the earth shall be reminded and shall turn to the Lord. And all the families of the nations shall worship in his sight. For the kingdom is his, and he shall have dominion over the nations*[^19]. If *shall be reminded* was said, then something was prefigured beforehand which we now see being done.

**9.** Let us see, then, how it was fulfilled, and whence it was fulfilled, and by what preceding sacrifice it was fulfilled, which was said to Abraham, *In your seed shall all nations be blessed*[^20]. Happy the nations, which did not hear that, and now, reading it, have believed what he believed who heard it. *Abraham believed God, and it was reckoned to him as righteousness, and he was called the friend of God*[^21]. That he believed God, within, in the heart, is in faith alone. But that he resolved to sacrifice his son, that undaunted he armed his right hand, that he would even now have struck had he not been held back by the voice—that is surely great faith, and a great work. And the work itself God praised, when he said, *Because you have obeyed my voice*[^22]. Why, then, does the apostle Paul say, *We hold that a man is justified by faith apart from the works of the Law*[^23], and in another place says, *And faith which works through love*[^24]? How does faith work through love, and how is a man justified by faith apart from the works of the Law? How? Attend, brothers. Someone believes, receives the sacraments of faith on his bed, and dies. The time for working was lacking to him. What do we say? That he was not justified? We plainly say that he was justified, believing *in him who justifies the ungodly*[^25]. So this man was justified, and did not work. And the saying of the Apostle is fulfilled, *We hold that a man is justified by faith apart from the works of the Law*[^26]. The thief who was crucified with the Lord *believed with the heart unto righteousness, and confessed with the mouth unto salvation*[^27]. For faith which works through love, even if there is no occasion to work outwardly, is nevertheless kept burning in the heart. For there were some under the Law who boasted of the works of the Law, which perhaps they did not out of love but out of fear, and they wished to seem righteous and to be preferred to the gentiles who had not done the work of the Law. But the Apostle, preaching faith to the gentiles, since he saw that those who came to the Lord were justified by faith, so that those who had now believed should work well—not that by working well they should merit to believe—cried out securely and said that a man can be justified by faith apart from the works of the Law, so that those rather were not righteous who did what they did out of fear, since faith works through love in the heart, even if it does not go forth into outward work.
