# Sermon 8: A Sermon on the Ten Plagues of the Egyptians

And the Ten Commandments of the Law

Delivered at Carthage at the Table of Saint Cyprian

**1.** To the Lord our God, whose worshipers we are, it was said in praise in a certain place of the Scriptures, *You have arranged all things in measure and number and weight*[^1]. Then by the apostolic teaching we are taught to behold the invisible things of God understood through the things that are made, and to search out the things that are hidden through the things that are manifest. Whence creation, questioned in a certain way everywhere, answers, by a kind of voice of its own form, that it has the Lord God as its maker. Then the Apostle recalls that the things written in the books which are called the Old Testament happened in figure: *And they were written for our correction, upon whom the ends of the ages have come*[^2]. Accordingly, dearly beloved, if the things that seem to come about, as it were by chance, in the nature of things, when carefully examined and discussed and prudently traced out and discovered, intimate the praise of the Creator, and the divine providence diffused through all things and arranging, as it was said, sweetly all things since it reaches mightily from end to end—how much more the things that are recited as not only done but also commended in the divine writings? Whence we have undertaken, in the name of our Lord, with his help and gift, the question, or rather the inquiry and disputation, proposed to us by the brothers—what it means that the Egyptians are struck with ten plagues and the people of God is instructed with ten commandments—to unfold as far as we can, with the pious intent of your heart striving along with us. Those who proposed it know what they proposed, that is, they know that they proposed it, and they recall that I did not rashly bring this forth to you. But whoever of you did not propose it, hear alike what we can say, that the proposal of the brothers may be the care of all, and what is expounded of our ministry may be the nourishment of all. For we trust that he himself will help, if not for our sake, certainly for yours, that we may say the things that both ought to be said and are useful to hear, so that, walking together in the way of his truth and hastening together to our homeland, we may deserve, by recognizing the will of the law of God, to avoid the enemies and ambushers of our journey.

**2.** There are ten plagues with which the people of Pharaoh was struck. There are ten commandments with which the people of God was instructed. Let us see, brothers, since things done bodily are to be understood spiritually. For we do not think they were only said and written, not done, but we believe they were done as we read they were done, and yet we know by apostolic teaching that those very deeds were shadows of things to come. We think, then, that the things that were done are to be investigated spiritually, yet we cannot deny that they were done. Let no one, then, say, "It is written indeed that water was turned into blood in the plague of the Egyptians, but it signifies something, for it could not really have happened." Whoever says this seeks the will of God in such a way as to do injury to the power of God. For what? If by saying it he could signify, could he not also by doing? Or was not Isaac, or Ishmael, born? They were born, they were men, and they were born of Abraham, one of the slave woman, the other of the free. Although, then, they were men, although they were born, they nevertheless figured the two Testaments, the Old and the New. Thus, the firmness of the deeds being first set in the foundation, we ought to inquire into the things signified, lest, the foundation removed, we seem to wish to build in the air. I think, then, that all who despise the ten commandments of the law and do not observe them suffer spiritually the things that the Egyptians suffered bodily. This proposal, until I unfold it with the Lord's help, I want you to be attentive and praying for us, that we may say things useful to you. For what pertains to us, we perhaps consider; but what we speak, we serve to you.

**3.** Receive this first, then, lest you be deceived in the number: that to those ten plagues does not pertain what was done first by way of a sign, that the rod was turned into a serpent. For that was the approach itself to Pharaoh, by which Moses was commended as one about to lead the people of God out of Egypt. The obstinate were not yet being struck, but they were already being terrified by a divine sign. Nor is it now needful, nor is it proposed, to say anything about that rod turned into a serpent. Nevertheless, because we have made mention of it out of necessity, lest anyone err in the number, nor ought there to remain in the mind of any hearer a scruple, as of a matter not understood, we say briefly that the rod signifies the kingdom of God, and that the same kingdom is certainly the people of God; but the serpent signifies the time of this mortality: for death was administered by the serpent. As though, then, falling from the hand of the Lord to the earth, they were made mortal. Whence the rod, cast down from the hand of Moses, was made a serpent. The magicians of Pharaoh did likewise: their rods being cast down, they were made serpents. But first the serpent of Moses, that is, the rod of Moses, devoured all the serpents of the magicians. Then at last, the tail being grasped, it again became a rod, and the kingdom returned to the hand. For the rods of the magicians are the peoples of the ungodly. Yet these peoples of the ungodly, conquered in the name of Christ, when they are transferred into his body, are as it were devoured by the serpent of Moses, until we return the kingdom of God to the hand of God, but at the end of the mortal world, which the tail of the serpent signifies. A great sign: so be it, so be it. You have heard what you ought to desire; hear what you ought to avoid.

**4.** The first commandment in the law is about worshiping one God: *You shall not have,* he says, *other gods besides me*[^3]. The first plague of the Egyptians, water turned into blood. Compare the first commandment to the first plague. The one God, from whom are all things, understand by the likeness of water, from which all things are generated. But to what does blood pertain, except to mortal flesh? What, then, is the turning of water into blood, except that *their foolish heart was darkened? For saying that they were wise, they became fools; and they changed the glory of the incorruptible God into the likeness of an image of corruptible man*[^4]—the glory of the incorruptible God into the likeness of an image of corruptible man, and of birds and four-footed beasts and serpents. The glory of the incorruptible God, from whom are all things, is like water; the likeness of an image of corruptible man and of birds and four-footed beasts and serpents is like blood. And this indeed happens in the heart of the ungodly, for God remains unchangeable: for not because the Apostle too said, *They changed*, was God therefore changed.

**5.** The second commandment: *You shall not take the name of the Lord your God in vain; for whoever takes the name of the Lord his God in vain shall not be cleansed*[^5]. The name of the Lord our God, Jesus Christ, is truth: for he himself said, *I am the truth*[^6]. Truth, then, cleanses, vanity defiles. And since whoever speaks truth speaks of God's—for whoever speaks a lie speaks of his own—to speak truth is to speak rationally; but to speak vanity is to make noise rather than to speak. Rightly, because the second commandment is the love of truth, to which the love of vanity is contrary. Truth speaks, vanity makes a clamor. See, contrary to this second commandment, the second plague. What is that second plague? An abundance of frogs. You have vanity fittingly signified, if you attend to the loquacity of frogs. See the lovers of truth, not taking the name of the Lord their God in vain, speaking wisdom among the perfect, even among the imperfect: not indeed speaking what they cannot grasp, yet not withdrawing from truth and proceeding into vanity. For although the imperfect cannot grasp it, if anything somewhat loftier is discussed about the Word of God, God with God, through whom all things were made, and they can grasp what Paul speaks among them as among little ones in Christ Jesus, *Christ Jesus, and him crucified*[^7]—yet that is not truth, and this vanity. But it would be vanity if we said that Christ did not undergo death but feigned it, that those wounds were in phantasm, that not true but simulated blood flowed from the wounds, that he showed false scars as though after false wounds. But when we say all these things are true, say they were done, believe and proclaim them certain, expressed, fulfilled, although we do not speak of his sublime and unchangeable truth, yet we do not go into vanity. But those who say that all those things in Christ were false and simulated are frogs croaking in the muddy marsh. They can have the noise of a voice, they cannot instill the teaching of wisdom. Finally, in the Church those clinging to truth speak the truth through which all things were made: the truth, the Word made flesh and dwelling in us; the truth, Christ born of God, one from one, only-begotten and coeternal; the truth, having taken the form of a servant, born of the Virgin Mary, having suffered, been crucified, risen, ascended; everywhere truth, both what the little one can grasp and what the little one cannot grasp; truth both in bread and in milk, in the bread of the great, in the milk of the little. For the same bread, that it may become milk, is passed through the flesh. But those who contradict this truth and, deceived in their own vanity, deceive others, are frogs bringing weariness to the ears, not food to the minds. Hear, finally, people speaking rationally: *There are no speeches nor words whose voices are not heard*—but voices not empty, because *their sound went out into all the earth, and their words to the ends of the earth*[^8]. But if you wish to understand the frogs too by contrast, recall that verse of the psalm: *Each one has spoken vain things to his neighbor*[^9].

**6.** The third commandment: *Remember to sanctify the sabbath day*[^10]. In this third commandment is intimated a certain proclamation of rest, a quiet of heart, a tranquility of mind, which a good conscience makes. There is sanctification, because there is the Spirit of God. Finally, see the rest, that is, the quiet: *Upon whom,* he says, *does my Spirit rest? Upon the humble and quiet and one who trembles at my words*[^11]. The restless, then, recoil from the Holy Spirit—lovers of quarrels, sowers of slanders, more desirous of contention than of truth—by their restlessness they do not admit to themselves the rest of the spiritual sabbath. Against the restlessness of these it is said, as if that they may have a sabbath in their heart, the sanctification of the Spirit of God: *Be gentle to hear the word, that you may understand*[^12]. What am I to understand? God saying: "Cease from your restlessness; let there be no tumult in your heart through the corruption of fluttering phantasms that prick you; let it not be so." For you are to understand God saying to you, *Be still and see that I am God*[^13]. But you through restlessness are unwilling to be still, and, blinded by the corruption of your contentions, you demand to see what you cannot. For attend to the third plague contrary to this third commandment: gnats were born in the land of Egypt from the mud. They are certain tiniest, most restless flies, flying about in disorder, rushing into the eyes, not allowing a person to rest, who, when they go away, rush in, and when driven off, return again, just like the wholly vain phantasms of the heart of the contentious. Hold the commandment, beware the plague.

**7.** The fourth commandment is: *Honor your father and your mother*[^14]. Contrary to this is the fourth plague of the Egyptians. The dog-fly is the canine fly: it is a Greek word. It is canine not to recognize one's parents; nothing is so canine as when those who begat one are not recognized. Rightly, then, even the whelps of dogs are born blind.

**8.** The fifth commandment is: You shall not commit adultery; the fifth plague is death among the cattle of the Egyptians. Let us compare. Take a man committing adultery, not content with his own marriage. He is unwilling to tame in himself a certain appetite of the flesh, which is common to us and to the cattle. For to lie together and to beget belongs also to the cattle; to reason, to understand, belongs to men. Therefore reason, which presides in the mind, ought, as reigning and ruling, to bridle the motions of the lower flesh, not to loosen them immoderately and unlawfully, here and there and at random. And therefore to the cattle themselves it was given by nature, the Creator so ordaining, that they should not be moved toward the females and toward mating except at certain times. For the beast does not restrain itself at another time by reason, but altogether grows torpid, that very motion growing cold. But man can always be moved, because he can also bridle the motion. To you the Creator gave the dominion of reason. To you he granted the commandments of continence, as it were reins over the lower beasts. You hold what the beast cannot, and therefore you hope for what the beast cannot. You labor somewhat in containing yourself, the beast does not labor. But you will rejoice always in eternity, which the beast does not reach. If the work wearies, let the reward rouse you. For this too is patience, to bridle the inward motion, and not, like a beast, to loosen into all things what you have in common with the beast. But if you despise yourself in yourself, and neglect the image of God in which he made you, conquered by the desire of the beasts, you will be, as it were, a beast, the man being lost; not as though turned into the nature of a beast, but having in the form of a man the likeness of a beast, you who do not hear him saying, *Do not be like the horse and the mule, which have no understanding*[^15]. And do you perhaps choose to be a beast for yourself and to roam in free lust, restraining the appetite of the flesh to continence by no law? Attend to the plague. If you do not fear to be a beast, at least fear to die like a beast.

**9.** The sixth commandment: You shall not kill. The sixth plague, boils on the body, and blisters boiling and swarming, and burnings of sores from the ash of the furnace. Such are murderous souls. They burn with anger, because through anger the fellowship of brotherhood has perished. People burn with wrath, they burn also with grace. But one is the heat of health, another the heat of an ulcer. Pustules burning throughout the whole body are murders conceived. It swarms, and it is not healthy. It burns, but not with the Spirit of God. For both he who wishes to help burns, and he who wishes to kill burns; the one by a commandment, the other by a disease; the one with good works, the other with festering ulcers. If we could see the souls of murderers, we would lament more than the rotting bodies of the ulcerous.

**10.** There follows the seventh commandment: You shall not steal, and the seventh plague: hail upon the crops. What you steal away contrary to the commandment, you lose from heaven. For no one has unjust gain without just loss. For example, he who steals acquires a garment, but by the heavenly judgment loses faith. Where there is gain, there is loss; visibly gain, invisibly loss; gain from his own blindness, loss from the cloud of the Lord. For nothing is without providence, dearly beloved. Or do you truly think that the things people suffer they suffer while God sleeps? These things seem to happen at random, clouds to gather, rains to pour, hail to be cast, the earth to be shaken by thunder, to be terrified by lightning. They are thought to happen at random and, as it were, not to pertain to divine providence. Against such thoughts that psalm keeps watch: *Praise the Lord from the earth*—when praises had been spoken from heaven—*dragons and all deeps, fire, hail, snow, ice, the stormy wind, which do his word*[^16]. Therefore those who by their own evil desire steal outwardly are hailed upon inwardly by the judgment of God. Oh, if they could look into the field of their heart! Surely they would mourn, since they would not find there what to put into the mouth of the mind, even if in their theft they found what to swallow with the greed of the gullet. Greater is the hunger of the inner man, greater is the hunger and more dangerous the plague and heavier the death. Many walk about dead, and many, hungry, exult over vain riches. Finally, Scripture calls the servant of God rich within: *The hidden man of your heart,* it says, *who is rich before God*[^17]. Not rich before men, but rich before God; where God sees, there he is rich. What, then, does it profit you, if where man does not see you steal, and where God sees you are hailed upon?

**11.** The eighth commandment: *You shall not bear false witness*[^18]. The eighth plague: the locust, an animal harmful with its tooth. But what does a false witness want, except to harm by biting and to consume by lying? Finally, the Apostle, admonishing the people of God not to assail one another with false accusations, *If you bite,* he says, *and devour one another, see that you are not consumed by one another*[^19].

**12.** The ninth commandment: *You shall not covet* your neighbor's *wife*[^20]. The ninth plague: dense darkness. For there is a certain adultery, against which a commandment was given above also, in not desiring the chastity of another's wife. For he too is an adulterer who does not go to another's wife only because he is not content with his own. But now, not only to sin after his own wife but even to attempt another's, is truly dense darkness. For nothing so pains the heart of the one who suffers it. And he who does this to another would by no means wish to suffer it himself. To other things every man is more ready. But this—I do not know whether anyone has been found who would bear it tolerably. O the dense darkness of those who do these things, who covet such things! Truly they are blinded by a horrible frenzy: for it is an untamed frenzy to defile a man's wife.

**13.** The tenth commandment: *You shall not covet anything of your neighbor's, not his cattle, not his possession, not his beast of burden, nor anything at all of your neighbor's shall you covet*[^21]. Contrary to this commandment is the tenth plague: the death of the firstborn. In which plague, when I seek a certain comparison, nothing for the moment occurs to me—perhaps something better will occur to those inquiring more diligently—except that all the things that people have they keep for their heirs, and among heirs nothing is dearer than the firstborn. But here it is reproved to covet your neighbor's property. He covets too who takes by theft. For who steals his neighbor's property except by coveting? But about theft a commandment was already given above. There you may understand robbery too. For Scripture would not give a commandment about theft and be silent about robbery, unless it had wished you to understand that, if it is punishable to take away secretly, it is of much greater punishment to snatch away violently. To take away from one unwilling, then, whether secretly or openly, has its own commandment. But to covet your neighbor's property—which God marks in the heart—even if you seek there a just succession, is not lawful. Finally, those who wish to possess, as it were justly, the property of others, seek to be made heirs by the dying. For what seems so just as to possess what is left to one, to have it by common right? "What is it to you, man? It was left to me, I obtained the inheritance, I read the will." Nothing seems more just than this voice of avarice. You praise him as though possessing by right; God condemns him as unjustly coveting. See what you are, you who wish to be made heir by someone. You certainly do not wish that he should have his own heirs, among whom nothing is dearer than the firstborn. Accordingly, you are punished in your own firstborn, you who, by coveting another's property, seek out, as it were by a shadow of right, that which was not owed to you by right. And it is indeed easy, brothers, to lose firstborn bodily. For people are mortal: whether before their parents or after their parents, those about to die die. This is the troublesome thing: lest through this hidden and unjust coveting you lose the firstborn of your heart. For the firstborn in us bears the image of the grace of God: newly born, first born. Among all, as it were, the offspring of our heart, faith is the firstborn. For no one works well unless faith goes before. All your good works are your spiritual children, but among these faith was first born to you. Whoever you are who secretly covet another's property, you lose inner faith. For you will first without doubt be a pretender, complying not out of love but out of fraud. As though loving the one by whom you wish to be made heir, by loving him you seek his death, and that you may see yourself the possessor of his property, you envy him a successor.

**14.** These, brothers, the ten commandments and the ten plagues having been run through, may, by comparing the despisers of the commandments with the condemned obstinacy of the Egyptians, make you cautious, that you may have your goods secure in the commandments of God. Your goods, I say, your goods of your inner chest, of your inner treasure; your goods, which neither thief nor robber nor powerful neighbor can take from you, where neither moth nor rust is to be feared, with which the rich man goes out even when shipwrecked. For so you will be the people of God among the unjust Egyptians, they suffering these things in their heart, but you unharmed in your very inner men, until the people is led out of Egypt by a kind of exodus of yours. And this indeed happens: for that was done once, this does not cease to be done. For if we attend, we even despoil the Egyptians. For that too was not done apart from a mystery, that thing which people of less understanding dare here to accuse God of, because he ordered gold and silver and clothing to be asked of the Egyptians. These were given and taken away: those people would be thieves, had they not done it at God's command. Let your Charity attend: those people, I say, would be thieves, had they not done it at God's command. But because they did it at God's command, they were not thieves. These you no longer accuse: you are prepared to accuse God himself. To them belonged the obeying; to God belonged the counsel of commanding, who knows who ought to suffer what, who suffers what by what desert. It would be a most open and deadly parricide for Abraham, had he struck his son of his own accord. This so great a crime he did laudably, because he was obeying God's command. And what would be cruelty in a spontaneous will, became piety under God's command.

**15.** I want to say something about the Acts of the Apostles, shown in two Apostles, the great rams of the flock, whose birthday we celebrate on one day, Peter and Paul. When Peter was shut up in prison, an angel came to him, and, the chains loosed from his hands, ordered him to go out. He went out and followed the angel. He was freed from prison by the Lord's command, by God's authority. The next day the judge sought him for a hearing. He learned that he had departed, and ordered the keepers of the prison to be led off. The soldiers, it says, he ordered to be questioned and led off; he passed on them the sentence of the laws which he thought he was about to pass justly on Peter. What do we say? Was Peter the author of their death? Would he not be perversely pious if he contradicted the will of God and answered the angel commanding him to go out, "I will not go out, lest on my account the wretched men, the keepers of the prison, die"? It would be answered him: Leave these things to the Creator; because you are not the craftsman that a man should be born, you cannot be the judge of how he should die. But Paul, when, shut up and bound, he was singing to God, the earth being shaken and his chains relaxed, did not depart, lest the keeper of the prison should suffer something evil. For room was given to consider human justice, lest one be punished for another, when nothing was divinely commanded to the contrary. No one dies except whom God wills. The dying is left as a matter for the judge, to God, yet the desire of the murderer is condemned. For here it is not to be regarded what God has judged, but what the evil mind has desired. Through Judas the betrayer we were freed, but Judas did not bestow this on us: he wished to kill, not to free. Praise to God, condemnation to Judas; and yet Judas would not have done this unless God had permitted, who *did not spare his own Son, but handed him over for us all*[^22].

**16.** Therefore let no one, brothers, let no one scrutinize God. It is proud, it is ungodly, it is foolish. You, bridle your desires; do nothing with an evil mind; be ready to obey, not to harm. And so those men did, and God did. If they had committed theft, even so God had willed that those who suffered should suffer, when he permitted those who did it to suffer. Yet he would reserve punishment for the thieves and exact some temporal penalty from those who had suffered the thieves. But now those men did not do it of their own accord: God by a just judgment willed it to happen. Since, if you weigh the cause, they perhaps did not carry off another's gold, but exacted a due wage. Unjustly oppressed long in Egypt, they made bricks; for the hard works of servitude they did not go out without wages. And yet God did this for some certain cause. If we are like the people of Israel in Egypt in this world, certainly I dare say to you—for I think that I speak to you by the Spirit of God—take the gold, the silver, the clothing of the Egyptians. Their gold, their wise men; their silver, their eloquent men; their clothing, the varieties of their tongues. Do we not see all these in the Church? Does not the Church do this daily? How many wise men in the world believe in Christ! The gold is taken from the Egyptians. Saint Cyprian, whose table this is, was once either the gold or the silver of the Egyptians. But the clothing, in which in a certain way the meanings are clothed, are the various tongues. You see them migrate from Egypt to the people of God: For *there are no speeches nor words whose voices are not heard*[^23]. Here the gold, here the silver, here the clothing of the Egyptians. And we go out rich and carry our wage. For not without cause have we labored in the mud of Egypt.

**17.** So all things, brothers, whether those which can be expounded by us or those which cannot yet, whether those which you can understand or those which you cannot yet, whether in this way in which they have been said by us or in another better way, believe altogether that *all these things happened to them in figure, and they were written for our correction, upon whom the ends of the ages have come*[^24]. Should I, then, not be made attentive to these things? And you, any spiritual Christian—should we not be attentive, and should we say that it was done without cause that in the third plague the magicians of Pharaoh failed? Should I seek nothing here? Should I think it was done or written in vain? The magicians of Pharaoh make serpents from rods against Moses, blood from water, they make frogs, they do these things. They come to the third plague, that is, to those little flies that are called gnats, and there those fail who made serpents, who made frogs, they fail at the flies. It is not at all, it is not without cause. Knock with me. To what is this third plague contrary? To the third commandment of God, where the sabbath is commanded to the people, where rest is proclaimed, where sanctification is commended. For there it was said, *Remember to sanctify the sabbath day*[^25]. Finally, even in the first works of the world God makes the day, makes heaven and earth and the sea, the lights, the stars, animals from the waters, from the earth man to his own image. He makes all these: nowhere does anything sound as sanctified. These are accomplished in six days, and the seventh day of God's rest is sanctified. He does not sanctify the works, he sanctifies the rest. What shall we say? Just as to us laboring rest is generally sweeter than work, do we think it is so for God too? We ought not to think this, even if he had done it by laboring and not by commanding. *God said: Let it be, and it was made*[^26]. Doing it in this way, not even man himself would labor. But on that day a certain rest after all our works was commended to us, so that after our good works we might understand that we shall rest without end. For all the days there have an evening, the seventh does not. For we work with an end, we rest without end. There sanctification sounds, pertaining by a certain great mystery to the Holy Spirit. However I speak, brothers, receive me with indulgence, I beseech you, looking rather at what I attempt than at what I unfold. I know what I speak, and who I am who speak—a man, divine things to men. Behold, strive with me, labor with me, that you may rest with me. As much as the Lord grants, as much as he opens, as much as he signifies, as much as Wisdom itself intimates, showing itself cheerfully to its lovers in the ways and meeting them in all providence, the sabbath day, the rest of God, is sanctified. There sanctification first sounds. As much as it seems to me, as much as you too recognize, as much as we believe, no sanctification is divine and true except from the Holy Spirit. For not in vain is the Holy Spirit so properly called. For although both the Father is holy and the Son is holy, yet the Spirit received this as a proper name, so that the third Person in the Trinity is called the Holy Spirit. He rests upon the humble and quiet, as though in his sabbath. For this reason the number seven is also assigned to the Holy Spirit: our Scriptures indicate this well enough. Let better men see better things, and greater men greater, and let them say and unfold something subtler and more divine about this number seven. I, however, what is enough for the present, see this, I remind you to see this: that this seven-part reckoning of number is properly assigned to the Holy Spirit because on the seventh day sanctification sounds. And whence do we prove that the seven-part reckoning of number is assigned to the Holy Spirit? Isaiah says that the Spirit of God comes upon the faithful one, upon the Christian, upon the member of Christ: of wisdom and understanding, of counsel and fortitude, of knowledge and piety, the spirit of the fear of God. If you have followed, I have run through seven things, as the Spirit of God descending to us from wisdom down to fear, that we may ascend from fear to wisdom: for *the beginning of wisdom is the fear of the Lord*[^27]. So, then, the Spirit is both sevenfold and one Spirit, one in a sevenfold operation. Or do you want something more evident? Holy Scripture recalls that the feast day of Pentecost was made from weeks. You have it in the Scripture of Tobit: it evidently says that this feast day was made from weeks. For seven times seven come back to forty-nine in sum. But, as though to return to the head—for by the Holy Spirit we are gathered to unity, not scattered from unity—therefore to forty-nine is added one, the honor of unity, and they become fifty. Not without cause, then, did the Holy Spirit come on the fiftieth day, after the Lord's ascension. The Lord rises, ascends from the dead, not yet into heaven. From that resurrection, from that taking up from the dead, fifty days are counted; and the Holy Spirit came in the number fifty, as though making a birthday for itself among us. For forty days he conversed here with his disciples; on the fortieth day he ascended into heaven, and after ten days had passed there, the Holy Spirit came, as it were by the sign of the ten commandments, because no one fulfills the law except through the grace of the Holy Spirit. And so, brothers, you see manifestly that this number seven pertains to the Holy Spirit. But whoever does not cleave to the unity of Christ and barks against the unity of Christ, is to be understood as not having the Holy Spirit. For only the natural make contentions and dissensions and divisions, of whom the Apostle says, *But the natural man does not perceive the things that are of the Spirit of God*[^28]. Then it is written also in the epistle of the apostle Jude, *These are they who separate themselves*—he was speaking in reproof—*these are they who separate themselves, natural, not having the Spirit*[^29]. What more manifest? What more evident? Rightly do they come, and even if believing the same things as we, yet about to receive the Holy Spirit, whom they cannot have so long as they are enemies of unity? But the Apostle compares these to the magicians of Pharaoh: *Having,* he says, *a form of piety, but denying its power*[^30]. By having the form of piety they did similar things, but, denying its power, they failed at the third sign.

**18.** But seek still further with me why they failed at the third. For they would have failed at the second, at the first, at the fourth; what does it matter where they failed who were going to fail? Why, then, at the third? But first see what I had promised, whether the apostle Paul rather compared the heretics to them: *Having,* he says, *a form of piety, but denying its power; and these avoid. Of these are those who penetrate houses and lead captive silly women laden with sins, who are led by various desires, always learning and never coming to the knowledge of the truth*[^31]. For they always hear testimony about the Catholic Church, and they are unwilling to come to the Catholic Church. *Always learning*. For do they not always hear, *In your seed shall all nations be blessed*[^32]? Do they not always hear, *Ask of me, and I will give you the nations for your inheritance and the ends of the earth for your possession*[^33]? Do they not always hear, *All the ends of the earth shall be reminded and shall turn to the Lord, and all the families of the nations shall worship in his sight*[^34]? Do they not always hear, *He shall have dominion from sea to sea, and from the river to the ends of the earth*[^35]? They always hear these things, but always learning and never coming to the knowledge of the truth. See now what I promised, what the Apostle adds consequently: *But as Jannes and Jambres resisted Moses, so these too resist the truth: men corrupt in mind, reprobate concerning the faith*[^36]. What then? *But they shall not advance further*[^37]. Rightly do they fail at the third sign, but they advance no further. Why do they not advance? *For their folly shall be manifest to all, as theirs also was*[^38]. Now see why they failed at the third sign. Remember that those who oppose unity do not have the Holy Spirit. But those three first commandments in the decalogue are understood to pertain to the love of God, so that the seven others are understood to pertain to the love of neighbor, so that by the two tables of the law and the ten commandments those two, as it were, summary commandments are held: *You shall love the Lord your God with all your heart, and with all your soul, and with all your strength; and you shall love your neighbor as yourself. On these two commandments hang the whole Law and the Prophets*[^39]. Let us refer, then, the three first commandments to the love of God. Which three first? The first: *You shall not have other gods besides me*[^40]. To which the contrary plague is water turned into blood, because the highest principle of the Creator was brought down to the simulation of flesh. The second commandment, *You shall not take the name of the Lord your God in vain*[^41], pertains, as I think, to the Word of God, who is the Son of God: *For there is one God, and one Lord of ours, Jesus Christ, through whom are all things*[^42]. Against the Word, frogs. See, against the Word, frogs, against reason, clamor, against truth, vanity. The third commandment about the sabbath, pertaining to the Holy Spirit on account of the sanctification, which first sounded in the sabbath: which we commended to you vehemently a little while ago, as much as we could. Contrary to this commandment is the restlessness in the flies born from corruption, rushing into the eyes; whence those too were called men corrupt in mind. Therefore at this third sign they failed, who, enemies of unity, did not have the Holy Spirit. For the Holy Spirit does this as a penalty. For grace does one thing, a penalty another; for he does one thing by filling, another by abandoning. Finally, that we may now evidently be able to recognize, with the magicians of Pharaoh themselves confessing it, how the Spirit of God was named in the Gospel, let us see what name he received. The Jews, casting a reproach at the Lord, when they had said, *This man does not cast out demons except by Beelzebub the prince of demons*[^43], he answered, *If I by the Spirit of God cast out demons, surely the kingdom of God has come upon you*[^44]. Which another Evangelist narrates thus: *If I by the finger of God cast out demons*[^45]. What the one called the Spirit of God, the other called the finger of God. Therefore the Spirit of God, the Finger of God. Therefore the Law was given written by the finger of God, which law was given on Mount Sinai on the fiftieth day after the slaying of the lamb. The Passover being celebrated by the people of the Jews, fifty days are fulfilled after the slaying of the lamb, and the law written by the finger of God is given. Fifty days are fulfilled after the slaying of Christ, and the Holy Spirit comes, that is, the finger of God. Thanks to the Lord, who hides providently, who opens sweetly. Now see that even the magicians of Pharaoh confess this most evidently. Failing at the third sign, they said, *This is the finger of God*[^46]. Let us praise the Lord, the giver of understanding, the giver of the Word. If these things were not covered by mysteries, they would never be sought out zealously. But if they were not sought out zealously, they would not be found so sweetly.
