# Sermon 162/C

**1.** We know, brothers, and with sure love we are confident that you congratulate us when you see righteousness in us. For it is consequent that, when the holy ones of God see just priests, they rejoice in their shepherds and in turn gladden them by their own morals. This too we have just now sung to the Lord our God, with one voice and one heart: *Let your priests be clothed with righteousness, and let your holy ones exult with exultation*[^1]; seeing them clothed with righteousness, with true joy and wholly entire love, with no flattery, they will rejoice. That you may exult with exultation, then, you holy ones of God, we must be clothed with righteousness and offer you an example unto every good work. But you who wish to rejoice in us, pray for us. For righteousness must be put on, as we sang. And who gives this garment, but he who, after error and ruin, brought forth the first robe for the younger son[^2]? For by the very fact that righteousness is commended as a kind of garment, he signified this: that we do not have it from ourselves . . .

**2.** (the leaf being torn, the text is missing, of which a small fragment was preserved by Bede) *A bishop, then, must be irreproachable*[^3]. Who denies this? But since a bishop must be irreproachable, does it become a Christian to be reproachable? "Bishop" is a Greek name, but in Latin it can be called "overseer" or "visitor." We are bishops, but with you we are Christians. We are properly called from the visitation, in common all from the anointing. If the anointing is common, the struggle too is common. But why do we visit, if there is nothing good that we may see in you?

**3.** . . . be found. If we wished this, if by a headlong fall and rashness we dared this, surely we would be terrified by today's reading. For we all heard, when the epistle of the apostle Paul to the Galatians was read, *When Peter had come to Antioch, I withstood him to his face, because he was reproachable*[^4]. Peter *was reproachable*, and shall I dare to profess myself without any reproach at all? Shall I, a weak sheep, not be afraid of this whirlpool, when I see the ram that dries the fleeces in peril? He himself raised himself strongly from this whirlpool; if I fall in, who will find me? I will avoid, then, the swelling of this wave, I will not throw myself in, I will not hurl myself down, even if I were wholly conscious that nothing could be found in me by men for which I might be reproached. The eyes of God are one thing, the eyes of that one another, to whom it was truly said, *No living thing will be justified in your sight*[^5]. And yet Peter, as we just now heard when it was read, was reproached even by men. Hence the Christian mind ought to be wholly foreign to this rashness, lest it dare to profess a life of such a kind in which not even men may find what they can reproach. Let even a man reproach, if there is something to reproach; let him plainly reproach; if I take it badly, let him reproach twice. For Peter brought it about that he was reproached once, when the reproacher was borne by him with an even mind; he did not give an example, as Christ did, of every kind of perfection, but he gave an example of complete humility. He took with an even mind the one reproaching him—not preceding, but following him in the apostolate. Let the apostle Paul grant pardon: what he did is easy; what Peter did is difficult. We live among human affairs, we are surrounded by daily experiences: I have often seen a man reproaching, I do not know whether I have ever seen one bearing a reproacher with an even mind. Paul, then, more sincerely, but Peter more wonderfully. But I do not know whether it is even more sincere to know another's fault than gladly to acknowledge one's own.

**4.** But it troubles some that Peter is said to have been reproached; it troubles them and they dispute; but they are not to be despised, if it is not their own presumption that troubles them, but love of Peter. They are unwilling to believe that he was truly reproached; they think this was simulated, shadowed forth before the eyes of men. "Within," they say, "one thing was being done, another was being shown to the people." O man, you fear the fault of one: do you not wish me to fear the deceit of both? Out of love of Peter you do not believe him reproached. You do not believe him reproached out of love of Peter; I believe him reproached out of love of Paul and Peter. I do not wish to believe of the apostles that they did one thing within and held forth another to the people. We are bishops, we follow their footsteps as far as our strength allows: I do not wish it to be permitted us to deceive you. If one thing is done within, another is held forth to the people, what holiness is not to be feared? We neither wish to deceive you nor to be deceived by you. For if you think that we deceive you, and we think that you deceive us, where is the love that believes all things? *Love*, he says, *believes all things*[^6]. It believes that it may be kept, not that it may be deceived.

**5.** "But answer: What did Paul reproach in Peter?" What but what he himself said, he himself wrote? He himself committed the epistle to memory, he himself propagated it to posterity to be read in the Church. What do I believe more securely in the divine books, if I do not believe what is written in that epistle? It is an apostolic epistle, it is a canonical epistle, it is Paul's epistle, who labored more than all of them—yet not he, but the grace of God with him[^7]. Therefore it is an epistle of the grace of God. And if we recall who spoke in him, it is an epistle of Christ. *Or do you wish*, he says, *to receive proof of him who speaks in me, Christ*[^8]? Hear and fear. *Proof*, he says, not a figment. But if you think this too little, hear the very preacher, even the witness-caller to God. What he was intimating he began thus to relate, as though foreseeing that there would be certain disputers: *But what I write*, he says, *to you, behold, before God I do not lie*[^9]. Does he, then, so calling God to witness, lie, when [you] without God's witnessing—*the mouth*, he says, *that lies kills*, not the body, but *the soul*[^10]? I beg you: do not so love Peter that you kill Paul; I beg you: let not Paul be killed in soul for Peter's sake—they were together killed in the flesh for Christ's sake. And again you ask and say to me: "What did Paul reproach in Peter?" Again I answer you: This Paul reproached in Peter, what he said and wrote. Under the witness-calling of God Paul related that he had reproached Peter: what do you ask of me? The epistle is read to all: recall it with me. *When Peter had come*, he says, *to Antioch, I withstood him to his face, because he was reproachable. For before certain came from James, he ate with the gentiles. But when they had come, he withdrew himself, fearing those who were of the circumcision, and the rest of the Jews too feignedly consented to him, so that even Barnabas was led into their pretense*[^11]. Behold, you have heard what Paul reproached in Peter. Let us expound it, and I will do this with the Lord's help, that you may understand. For I will do it not on your account, but on account of those who hear both me and you.

**6.** The sacraments of the Jews—namely, circumcision, the resting on the sabbath, the avoidance of certain foods, and other such things—were given by God, written in the law, commanded by God as a figure of times to come. These were not like the abominable sacrileges of the gentiles, these were not like the sacrifices of demons, this was not as the worship of idols. God had commanded this through Moses to his people—the one God, the true God, that God who said, *I am who am*[^12]. But *after the fullness of time came, God sent his Son, made of a woman, made under the law*[^13]; for he too was circumcised[^14]. But *of a woman*, a virgin—for by the custom of Hebrew speech, unless even a virgin were called a woman, Eve would not first be formed into a woman[^15]. *After*, then, *in the fullness of time God sent his Son, made of a woman*—through whom he made the woman—*made under the law*—through whom he gave the law—*that he might redeem those who were under the law, that we might receive the adoption of sons*[^16]—the shadows began to be unnecessary, the light having come; yet not because they were unnecessary were they now sacred things to be condemned, like the sacrileges of the gentiles. It was not to be said to a Jew, "Do not circumcise, because you have believed in Christ," as it was to be said to a pagan made a Christian, "Do not sacrifice to idols, because you have believed in Christ." For if you set up two things—the sacrifice of the pagans and the sacrament of the Jews—of these one was never necessary, always pernicious; but the other was at one time necessary, afterward not necessary, but for those who then received the Gospel from the Jews not yet pernicious. Since, then, even those sacraments of the Jews were after the coming of Christ no longer necessary, but through them had been foretold the one through whom they would be unnecessary, they were to be ended with honor, not rejected with horror. Since these things were so, the apostle Paul, who had been especially sent to preach the gospel to the gentiles[^17], ought to take especial care, by his vow, that the salvation of the gentiles not be hindered by the sacraments of the Jews. Therefore, Christ speaking in him[^18], God breathing and revealing in him, he decreed at that time, in the newness of the Gospel, to forbid no Jew then from these sacraments, to compel no gentile to these sacraments. Thus he judged those things—as it were paternal funerals soon to be abolished, now as it were bodies without the soul of prophecy, to be led with respect as it were to the tomb—and yet, because they were unaccustomed and dead, by no means to be imposed on gentile shoulders.

**7.** What sin, then, was there at that time in those sacraments, for which Peter was reproached, attend, and help our strength to break and resolve so great a mass of a question, which then agitated the apostles themselves—for which we are perhaps less fit—but help us: I beseech you. You have indeed heard many things, but as though new hear what you more wish to hear, and I, standing, speak to you and grow weak. Surely, since the question is as it were set before you anew, to this our narrative seems to pertain. For now the one who a little before was questioning me, before I expounded by narrative what I wished—what Paul reproached in Peter—made more cautious by my narrative, says this: "If at that time abstinence from such sacraments was not imposed on those who had come from among the Jews, and the observance of such sacraments was not imposed on the gentiles, Peter, who did such things, came from among the Jews; how did Paul rightly reproach him?" I say, brothers, what the very words indicate. Paul was not reproaching this in Peter, that he observed the Jewish sacraments, but that he imposed them on the gentiles. For a Jew who then believed in Christ, if he wished to observe them, was not forbidden; if he did not wish, he was not compelled. But of the gentiles, whoever had believed, when would he wish to be circumcised, when he heard that this was not necessary to salvation? Perhaps the Jews themselves would scarcely be circumcised, unless infants suffered this. But what was it to compel the gentiles to observe these sacraments, in which we said Peter was reproached by Paul—perhaps if anyone asks, what was the very compelling? Were they held unwilling, bound and circumcised? Far from it! What, then, was it to compel, what do we suppose, but to say to them: "You will not be able to be saved, unless you observe those legal precepts, as the Jews do"? For with this condition set forth, because they were seeking salvation and it was said to them that they could not otherwise reach salvation, they were compelled unwilling to observe those sacraments—not because they loved these, but because they desired salvation.

**8.** That moderation, then—that neither the Jews be forbidden nor the gentiles compelled—was kept by the apostles and decreed by a council. For when this question agitated many, troubled many, they assembled at Jerusalem, and with all the apostles and elders of the Church gathered—that is, the presbyters and all the preachers of the Gospel and the heads of the churches—by common council, the Lord surely inspiring, it was established that neither the Jews be forbidden from these things, nor the gentiles compelled to these things[^19]. That this is written in the Acts of the apostles, many recall; let those who do not recall it read it. That measure, then, was most temperate, most religious, most cautious. If they commanded those things to be at once rejected, just like the sacrifices of idols, the true God who had commanded them would not be believed to be God. This, then, Paul reproached in Peter, not because he himself observed these things, but because he was driving the gentiles to them. How was he driving them? By consenting, though feignedly, to those who said that the gentiles were not otherwise saved unless they observed the Jewish sacraments. This too is written in the Acts of the apostles, it is related in the same book: There came, it says, to Antioch certain from James—that is, from Jerusalem—Jews, namely, who had now believed in Christ, and they said to the brothers from the gentiles who came: "You cannot otherwise be saved, unless you observe the legal precepts"[^20]. This is to compel: to say that they cannot otherwise be saved.

**9.** Against this the apostle Paul warred with most ardent zeal. That stone of dreadful pressure he was removing, that the wretched gentiles might at some time rise again as if from the tomb of Lazarus[^21]. For if he reproached this observance, not the compulsion, he would even reproach himself. For he himself too took these things up and celebrated certain Jewish sacraments with the Jews in the temple at Jerusalem; but, as one coming from among the Jews, he observed these things; he did not compel the gentiles to them. Paul, coming from among the Jews, the evangelist of the gentiles, both the decreer and the fulfiller of the holy counsel of the apostles, showed rather by his own example what had pleased the Holy Spirit in such a question, what it had decreed and established. For James said to him, now established at Jerusalem: "Many," he says, "think that you are opposed to the legal precepts of the fathers and are a foe and enemy of the law. *What, then, is it?* Hear me. There are here certain who have come to take up purification: be purified with them, that all may know"—it was not said, "that all may suppose": for devotion was commanded, not pretense suggested—"that all," he says, "may know that you are a zealot of the law and of the observances of the fathers"[^22]. This Paul did as a Jew from among the Jews, yet not that he might be justified by those sacraments, but that he might not be thought to condemn them.

**10.** Therefore he did this too. For he also circumcised Timothy, born of a Jewish mother, on account of the scandal of the Jews[^23]—Timothy willing, not compelled—that through him too he might show that he did not condemn those things, but did not impose them on the gentiles as necessary to salvation. But after he circumcised Timothy with this indifference which I have often commended, and which I think is now aptly understood by you, certain ones from among the Jews, who did not put off that old skin and therefore wished the gentiles to be circumcised, because they said that without those sacraments there could be no salvation for them. Whence even a heresy was made: for even today there are certain who both believe in Christ and circumcise their sons—that is, believing that without those sacraments they cannot have salvation. When, then, by this indifference of devotion, not by necessity, he had circumcised Timothy, certain of these, as I said, placing salvation there—which displeased Paul, for which he reproached Peter—began to boast, to deceive others, that Paul too held this. They said: "What we too say, this he too believes, that without these sacraments there can be no salvation. For if he does not believe this, why did he circumcise Timothy?" When Paul, who had done this by liberty, not by necessity, on account of the scandal of the Jews, not on account of the salvation of Timothy, had heard this, he saw that they had taken occasion for another preaching and for stirring up an evil suspicion against Paul, and he was unwilling to circumcise Titus[^24]. But it appears why he willed the one, why he was unwilling for the other. He willed the one on account of the scandal of the Jews, he was unwilling for the other on account of the occasions of those who believed badly.

**11.** And see in his epistle how evidently and clearly he commends this. *But neither Titus*, he says, *though he was a Greek*—as Timothy was—*was compelled to be circumcised*[^25]. And as though it were said to him: "Why was Titus not compelled to be circumcised, when Timothy, though similarly a Greek, was compelled to be circumcised?" Why? Do you wish to hear? *On account of the false brothers smuggled in, who had crept in to spy out our liberty*[^26]. In circumcising Timothy, then, there was not necessity, but liberty; but in Peter, necessity was reproached, not liberty. *On account of*, he says, *the false brothers smuggled in, who had crept in to spy out our liberty, that they might bring us into slavery. That they might bring us*, he says, *into slavery*, by making out of liberty a necessity, *to whom we did not yield in subjection even for an hour*, he says. For what reason? *That the truth of the Gospel might remain among you*[^27]. What is *the truth of the Gospel*? That the Jew was then not to be forbidden, the gentile not to be compelled; that the sacraments of the Jews were not to be condemned for the Jews, not to be imposed on the gentiles. What is *the truth of the Gospel*? That salvation can be reached through Christ without those sacraments. This those denied, to whom Peter feignedly consented. And therefore it is added, *Those other Jews feignedly consented, so that even Barnabas was led into their pretense. But when I saw*, he says, *that they were not walking rightly toward the truth of the Gospel*[^28], as he says there, *That the truth of the Gospel might remain among you*. You hear the truth commended everywhere, and you suspect deceit everywhere—so everywhere, even among the apostles. He saw, then, *that they were not walking rightly toward the truth*. He saw, over this, that they thought those sacraments were necessary to the gentiles for their salvation. This is not *the truth of the Gospel*. These things were no longer necessary to salvation: perhaps at one time, when they foretold that Christ would come. The knife of stone was once necessary, before the Rock itself came[^29].

**12.** *I, when I saw*, he says, *that they were not walking rightly toward the truth of the Gospel, said to Peter before all*. *I said to Peter*: Paul to Peter, the lesser to the greater, the follower to the one preceding; and *I said* both *to Peter* and *before all*: great boldness, but great patience in that one. Since you have made us attentive, let us hear what you said to Peter, Paul, and where. Let us understand what you did, what you arranged, since you nowhere wished to be a liar. *I said*, he says, *to Peter before all: "If you, being a Jew"*—what is, *"being a Jew"*? to whom it was lawful to observe those things indifferently—*"live in the manner of the gentiles and not in the manner of the Jews"*—because *before certain came from James, he ate with the gentiles*—*"if you, being a Jew, live in the manner of the gentiles and not in the manner of the Jews,"* it ought to follow and to say: "How do you now keep the Jewish sacraments?" He did not say this, but where he was reproachable, whence these things were stirred up, for which he had zeal toward God, because he was striving to remove an impediment from the Gospel, this he said with boldness, this he said *before all*: *"If you, being a Jew, live in the manner of the gentiles, how do you compel the gentiles to Judaize?"*[^30] *"You compel"*, he says, *"to Judaize"*. I have already said how: by consenting to those who said that the gentiles could not otherwise have salvation, *"you compel the gentiles to Judaize."* This Paul did not do, when he undertook purifications with certain ones; he did not compel *the gentiles to Judaize* when he circumcised Timothy according to that indifference, because both *circumcision is nothing and uncircumcision is nothing*[^31]; he did not compel *the gentiles to Judaize*. And lest he be thought to compel, he did not circumcise Titus; Paul did not compel *the gentiles to Judaize*. This he himself did not do, and therefore this he rightly reproached in Peter, because he himself did not do it.

**13.** But let me suppose that he himself did this, although it is not found, not proven; I say this not according to my own view, but according to certain people, that I may speak for the time being: let us suppose that Paul did this which Peter did, and that Paul reproached this in Peter which Paul himself had also done: both would more easily be corrected, than that the detestable moth—that is, a lie or the suspicion of a lie—should creep into us, like a malignant little worm to gnaw at every letter of Scripture, to be feared lest it persuade us that Paul lied in any way in his canonical epistle, which he commends to the Church spread by posterity. I beg you: fear this evil; let us all fear, lest afterward we all lament in vain. This is no light evil: I warn you, fearing I terrify. Pardon my anxiety: we speak to you more rarely than you wish. They whisper, those who find your ears ready when they wish—we are not permitted to speak to you except rarely and from this place—I do not say malignant in mind, I do not do them injury, but beware of those who err, and so deal with them that they themselves rather be corrected, not that you be depraved by them. For when they say these things to you: "Peter did this feignedly, Paul reproached him feignedly; Paul did not write truly in his epistle that Peter *was reproachable*; Paul did not write truly in his epistle that he saw them *not walking rightly toward the truth of the Gospel*; Paul did not write truly in his epistle that Peter compelled *the gentiles to Judaize*, but all was feigned"—what truth will we retain, what page will we not hold suspect of falsehood?

**14.** Behold, we men are pressed by the most evident authority, and scarcely yield to truth thundering and flashing! Where shall we go? What shall we do, when perhaps I say to someone, "It is good to marry, but better not to marry, as the apostle Paul wrote," if the condemner of marriages says to me: "Plainly Paul condemned marriages, but he wrote this feignedly, since the truth itself could not be borne by the weak; because continence is laboriously imposed, therefore he said: It is good to marry, for he knew it was evil to marry"? Whence do you prove that Paul lied, where he says, *He who gives in marriage does well*[^32]? "Whence do I prove it?" he says: "just as he lied where he says, *When I saw that he was not walking rightly toward the truth of the Gospel*—for Peter was not walking rightly toward the truth of the Gospel—; just as *I said to Peter before all: If you, being a Jew, live in the manner of the gentiles and not in the manner of the Jews, how do you compel the gentiles to Judaize?*—for Peter did not really compel the gentiles to Judaize. As, then, Paul said these things feignedly, so too, when he saw that the excellence of virginity could not be borne by carnal men, he said, *He who gives in marriage does well*. He was doing one thing, holding forth another." If these things are so, where shall we go? What oracles shall we consult? For the divine utterances committed to the canonical Scriptures ought to be to us truly as oracles. Is deceit to be feared there, where truth is found, where truth is presumed? I beseech you: foresee this little worm, that you may guard against it; do not admit it to the cabinet of your heart. If you admit it, unless you quickly shake out the cloths, you will find nothing whole there.

**15.** I have said what I reckoned must be said with great anxiety; long I have held your Charity, long has anxiety for you held me. All the things written in the holy canonical books, we who dispute and write books write far otherwise—we write by making progress, we learn daily, we dictate by searching, we speak by knocking. Surely I do not rest, as much as I can, where I can be useful to the brothers, both by speaking and by writing. I admonish your Charity, from me, even concerning me: do not wish to hold the book or disputation of anyone's discussion as canonical Scripture. In the holy Scriptures we learn to judge; in our own writings we do not disdain to be judged. That indeed is to be chosen, and this rather to be wished of the two: that in writing or speaking we say true things, that we nowhere err. But because this is difficult to fulfill, therefore there is another, the firmament of the canon—as it were the heaven where the luminaries of the Scriptures are set, as it were between the waters and the waters, between the peoples of the angels and the peoples of men: those above, these below. Let us hold Scripture as Scripture, as God speaking; let us not seek there an erring man. For not in vain was the canon established in the Church: this is the office of the Holy Spirit. If anyone, then, reads my book, let him judge me: if I have said something reasonable, let him follow not me, but reason itself; if I have proved this by a most evident and divine testimony, let him follow not me, but the divine Scripture. But if he wishes to reproach something that I have said rightly, he does not act rightly; but I am more angry with such a praiser of mine, who takes my book as canonical, than with the one who reproaches in my book even what is not to be reproached. I beg you: although I see you attentive and almost most fresh, as though you had only just begun to listen, yet I do not wish to say anything more, that you may firmly hold this last thing I have said. Let us turn to the Lord, etc.
