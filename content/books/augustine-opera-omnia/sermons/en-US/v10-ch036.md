# Sermon 359/A

**1.** As long as we are in this world, if we take care to have our heart on high, it will not harm us that we walk below. For we walk below in this flesh. But fixing our hope on high, we have as it were placed an anchor on solid ground[^1]: so as to resist somehow the waves of this world, not through ourselves but through him in whom that very anchor is fixed, our hope, since he who made us hope will not deceive, so as to render the reality in place of hope. *For hope*, as the Apostle says, *which is seen, is not hope. For what one sees, why does he hope for it? But if*, he says, *we hope for what we do not see, we await it through patience*[^2].

**2.** About this patience, what the Lord grants, I wish to speak to your love. For the Lord Jesus Christ too says in the Gospel in a certain place: *In your patience you will possess your souls*[^3]. It is said also in another place: *Woe to those who have lost patience*[^4]. Whether it be named patience, or endurance, or toleration, it signifies the same thing by several words. But let us fix in our heart not the diversity of sounds, but the unity of the thing itself, and let us have within what we name without. He who knows that he leads a pilgrim life in this world, wherever in the world he is set according to the body, who knows that he has an eternal homeland in the heavens, who trusts that there is a region of the blessed life, which here it is permitted to desire, not permitted to have, burning with so good, so holy, so chaste a desire, lives here patiently. Patience does not seem necessary in prosperous things, but in adverse things. No one patiently bears what delights him. But whatever we bear, whatever we patiently endure, is hard and bitter, and through this, patience is not necessary to happiness, but to unhappiness. Nevertheless, as I had begun to say, whoever burns with desire for eternal life, in whatever land he is in happiness, must needs live patiently, because he bears with difficulty his very pilgrimage, until after love he comes to the desired homeland. The love of desire is one thing, the love of vision another. For both he who desires loves; and he who sees loves; and he who desires loves that he may arrive; and he who sees loves that he may remain. But if the desire of the saints so burns from faith, what will it be in sight? If we so love when we believe what we do not yet see, how shall we love when we shall see?

**3.** Therefore the Apostle says these three things which he especially commends to be built up in the inner man: faith, hope, love, and when he praised these three, at the end he said: *But the greater of these is love*[^5]. *Pursue love*[^6]. What, then, is faith? What hope? What love? And why is love greater? *Faith is*, as it is defined in a certain place of the Scriptures, *the substance of those who hope, the conviction of things that are not seen*. He who hopes, what he hopes for he does not yet have, but by believing he is like one possessing. For faith is, he says, *the substance of those who hope*; the very thing which we shall hold is not yet, but faith itself stands for that thing. For he holds not nothing who holds faith, nor is he empty who is full of faith. Therefore great is the reward of faith because it does not see and believes. For if it saw, what reward would there be? Therefore the Lord, when he had risen from the dead and shown himself to his disciples, not only to be seen with the eyes but also to be handled with the hands[^7], and when he had convinced human senses that it was he who had risen, who a little before hung on the wood, conversing with them for some days, as much as seemed sufficient to him to confirm the gospel and to establish faith in the resurrection, ascended into heaven, that he himself might not be seen, but be held by believing. For if he were always here, and visible to these eyes, there would be no praise of faith. But now it is said to a man: Believe. And he wishes to see. It is answered him: That you may at some time see, meanwhile believe. Faith is the merit: vision is the reward. If you wish to see before you believe, you demand the reward before the work. What you wish to have has a price. You wish to see God. Of so great a good faith is the price. You wish to arrive, and you do not wish to walk? Vision is the possession, faith is the way. He who refuses the labor of the journey, how does he seek the joy of possession?

**4.** But faith does not fail, because hope underlies it. Take away hope, and faith fails. For how, by walking, does one move even his feet, who does not hope he can arrive? But if you take away both, that is, the love both of faith and of hope, what does it profit that he believes, what does it profit that he hopes, if he does not love? Rather, indeed, he cannot even hope for what he does not love. For love kindles hope, hope shines with love. But when we shall have arrived at those things which, believing, we hoped for unseen, what faith there will be praised? Since indeed *faith is the conviction of things that are not seen*. When we shall see, it will no longer be called faith. For you will see, you will not believe. Likewise hope itself. When the thing is present, you no longer hope. *For what one sees, why does he hope for it?*[^8] Behold, when we shall have arrived, faith is ended, hope is ended. What of love? Faith is turned into sight, hope is turned into the reality. Now there will be sight and reality, not faith and hope. What of love? Can it be ended? For if it was already inflamed toward what was not seen, surely when it shall have seen, it will be inflamed more. Well, then, was it said: *But the greater of these is love*[^9], since sight succeeds faith, the reality succeeds hope, no thing succeeds love; it itself grows, it itself is increased, it itself is perfected by that contemplation.

**5.** Hence, desiring the face of God, sighing for the homeland of everlasting happiness, however much we flow around with the happiness of the world, however much we are filled with the abundance and overflow of things, set in that desire, shall we not hold our earthly pilgrimage to be wretched, and by that very wretchedness pray to God, saying always: *Lord, free me*? The poor man says: *Free me*; and you think he says it to be freed from poverty. The rich man says: *Free me*; perhaps he is sick. Rather, he is healthy, and rich, and cries out: *Free me*. Whence, except that the very prayer says: *Free us from evil*[^10]? In whatever goods he is engaged, a Christian must needs cry out: *Free us from evil*. If he cries out: *Free us from evil*, there is something from which he may be freed; if there is something from which he may be freed, he is in evil; if he is in evil, however many goods he has by which he is delighted, he has what he must bear until he fully enjoys God. Toleration, then, is necessary in this world for the poor, the rich, the healthy, the sick, captives, free men, pilgrims, those set in their own homeland; toleration is necessary, because all are on pilgrimage in the world. And until they are freed from this pilgrimage and cling to that truth and unchangeable substance, for which, on pilgrimage, they sighed, they are engaged in temptations and cry out with a true heart to God: *Free us from evil*.

**6.** But wretched men see those who in this world are called happy, and wish to be such, and think that, when they have been such, they will then no longer be in evil. This is a perverse mind, and not Christian, full of greed, not of faith: they think there is nothing for them after this life. I do not say: He is happy in this life who has abounded in all things. Far be it that I should say so, but if he thinks there is nothing after this life, he is never happy. For reason convinces, truth convinces, that no one here can be happy—happy, I say, as wisdom judges, not as greed judges. And behold, when I have set it forth, every man has judged that he can be happy. And I begin to seek him, whether he be found on this earth. Do I say: He is happy who is healthy, who is rich, who is honored, who lives safe with all his own? I do not say this, but: He is happy who is in no want. It is at once answered: Therefore the rich man is happy, because he is in no want. If he is in no want, he does not covet, if he covets he is in want. You attend to means, I question covetousness. How is he in no want, to whom what he has is too little, and he desires to have more? You see that all the things he has heaped up that he may have are wood for the flame, whence it may grow, not whence it may be satisfied. Therefore if he covets the more ardently the more he has, I do not say to you: He is needy, but he is more needy than that beggar. For the covetousness of beggars a few coins satisfy, but the covetousness of a greedy man not the whole world. Now here perhaps you answer: what if he seeks nothing more than what he has? I praise it if I find it, I greatly rejoice, he has fixed a measure to an infinite thing, he has been able to say to his eagerness: Thus far. Great strength, great dominion of the mind it is to apply a measure, to break the greedy appetite, to rein in covetousness, to set bounds to burning lust. A great virtue, I confess, a great virtue. Nevertheless, according to what I was defining, I do not yet say he is happy. For what did I say? Who is happy? He who is in no want. Behold, this man is in no want: and he abounds in all things, and seeks nothing more. Still I ask: Does he not wish to have more? It is answered: He does not wish to have. What he has, does he not fear to lose? It is answered: He fears. How, then, is he in no want, and if now he is in no want of means, he is in want at least of security. And who is there who can make him secure in this world that what he possesses cannot perish, from whom may he receive security about things wavering and slipping with constant nodding? Many rich men have slept, and risen poor. No one, then, gives him this security. And he himself too knows it, and therefore he fears. The mind of this man, then, must needs be increased, must receive ampler strength, must come to a greater firmness, so that, as he set bounds to covetousness, so also he may not fear to lose what he has, and may so strengthen his heart that he can, with all things lost, say: *Naked I came forth from my mother's womb, naked I shall return to the earth. The Lord gave, the Lord has taken away; as it has pleased the Lord, so has it been done, blessed be the name of the Lord*[^11]. Great is this athlete of God, because he wrestled with a great adversary too! Let us make, then, a man so magnanimous that he is neither puffed up by these things which he has, nor ever broken by their loss and ruin, but is such, having as though not having, using this world as though not using it[^12], having a great means, the will itself of his Lord, like the one we mentioned: *As it has pleased the Lord*, he said, *so has it been done, blessed be the name of the Lord*; to be rich: the will of my God; to be poor: the will of my God. See how he fulfilled what all sing but few do: *I will bless the Lord at all times; always his praise in my mouth*[^13]. What is, *at all times*? Both when it is well, and when it is ill: this is at all times, this is always. He is truly rich who is rich within, not within walls, but within thoughts, in conscience not in a strongbox. Such riches not even the shipwrecked can lose; for he can come forth from the waves both naked and full. I think we have found one who is happy? If he is happy who is in no want, whoever is such on earth, why is he not happy? I think we have found even such a one in want; if he is still in want, he is not yet happy. Behold, he covets nothing, behold, he loses things securely, he is not puffed up by prosperity, he is not weakened by adversity. How is he not yet happy? If I find him in want, I do not dare to pronounce him happy. And whence, says someone, will you find even this man in want? I find it plainly. If he believes in God, he is still in want, he is still a beggar of God. For it can happen that such a man says not in prayer: *Free us from evil*[^14], but says: I am in want. Of what is he in want? Of eternal life. This he does not yet have. He is engaged among temptations. Behold Job himself, provoked to love God freely, for to this the devil provoked him, with all things lost, the solace of his children gone, his wife alone remaining, a tempter not a consoler, was struck also with a grave wound from head to foot, that even a poor man's patrimony might be taken from him[^15]. For a poor man's patrimony is health, which the rich man, when he does not have it, whatever he has is bitter. Perhaps you find a poor man to whom a rich man's patrimony is not necessary; you do not find a rich man to whom a poor man's patrimony itself too is not necessary. The patrimony of the poor, that is, health, Job lost, struck with a grave wound from head to foot. Yet he was not led to give folly to God and in his evils to have God displeasing to him: God was always pleasing. The new Eve suggested blasphemy at the prompting of the serpent with whom he was invisibly wrestling: *Say something*, she said, *against God, and die*. And he: *You have spoken like one of the foolish women. If we have received good things from the hand of the Lord, shall we not endure evils?*[^16] She was Eve, but he was not Adam. That one was conquered in paradise, this one conquered on a dunghill. But yet, amid those very things which he was suffering, he says in a certain place: *Is not human life upon the earth a temptation?*[^17] Set, then, in this human life, he was without doubt set in temptation. He wished to be freed from this temptation. He himself too was still in want of the life where there is no temptation. If he was in want, he was not yet happy. And through this, whatever kind of man you set here, describe, depict, you desire what you do not find. No one can be happy on this earth.

**7.** What had I proposed? To speak of patience. If it has not slipped from your love, this I proposed. Now, then, how great a good patience is . . . in this earthly happiness we endure for its sake. Whoever does not have it, fails. Whoever fails on the journey, will not arrive at the desired homeland. You see how true it is that in our patience we possess our souls[^18].

**8.** Since, then, while we live here, in whatever happiness we live, let us live patiently. But all of us, or nearly all, fear to die. Sickness can be, can not be. The loss of dear ones is feared: they can be lost, can also not be lost. Whatever evil you fear on this earth, both can be and can not be. Death cannot not be. It can be deferred, it cannot be taken away, and yet for its deferral all labor, keep watch, fortify themselves, bar their doors, sail, plow. On the earth, in the house, wherever they labor, they labor for this, not that they may not die, but that they may die a little later. Consider, dearest ones, how great a labor is spent by all men that they may die later. If we spend so much labor that we may die later, what must be spent that we may never die? This too is numbered among the things to be borne and to be tolerated and to be had patiently: the very fear of death with which every soul wrestles. For see what the Apostle says: *We groan, being weighed down* on this earth under this burden of corruptible flesh, *in which we do not wish*, he says, *to be unclothed, but to be clothed over, that the mortal may be swallowed up by life*[^19]. We are indeed weighed down, he says, by the burden of the corruptible body, and under this body we groan, and we do not wish to be unclothed. What is, we do not wish to be unclothed? We do not wish to lay aside that very corpse under which we groan. We do not wish to lay it aside. O burden unhappily sweet! *We groan*, he says, *being weighed down*. Therefore lay it aside willingly, if you groan under the burden. *In which we do not wish*, he says, *to be unclothed*. But what? *To be clothed over.* So that you may carry two burdens? No, he says. But why? *That the mortal may be swallowed up by life.* For we wish to arrive at eternal life. We wish to arrive where no one dies, but through death we do not wish it, if it could be done, that while we live we should be snatched there, and our body, while we live, should be turned into that spiritual form into which it is to be turned when we rise. Who would not desire this? Does not every man wish it? But to one wishing it is said: Migrate. Remember what you sang in the Psalm: *I am a sojourner on the earth*[^20]. If you are a sojourner, you remain in another's property. If you remain in another's, when the householder commands, you migrate. And the householder must needs at some time command that you migrate, nor does he fix the day of your stay. For he made no written agreement with you. Since you remain gratis, your migration is his command. And these things are tolerated, and on this account patience is necessary.

**9.** That servant saw this, that his master was going to command him to go out from his stewardship, and he thought ahead, and said to himself: *My master is going to cast me out from the stewardship. What shall I do? I cannot dig, to beg I am ashamed.* Here labor calls him back, here shame, but counsel did not fail him in his anxiety. *I have found*, he said, *what I shall do.* He called together the debtors of his master, he brought forth the bonds: *Say you, how much do you owe?* And he: *A hundred baths of oil. Sit down quickly, make it fifty. Take your bond. And to another: you, how much do you owe? A hundred cors of wheat. Sit, make it quickly eighty. Take your bond*[^21]. This he said: When my master casts me out from the stewardship, they will receive me, and neither will want force me to dig, nor to beg.

**10.** Why did the Lord Jesus Christ set forth this likeness? For that fraudulent servant did not please him, he committed fraud against his master; he took and did not do it of his own. Moreover he even stole, brought loss to his master, that he might prepare for himself after the stewardship a place of rest and security. Why did the Lord set this forth? Not because that servant committed fraud, but because he foresaw for the future, that a Christian who lacks counsel may blush, if even a fraudster's ingenuity is praised. For so he added: *Behold, the sons of this age are more prudent than the sons of light*. They commit frauds that they may take counsel for themselves for the future. For in what life did that steward take counsel for himself? From which he was going to migrate by obeying. He took counsel for a life that was to end; will you not take counsel for the eternal one? Do not, then, love fraud, but, he says, *make for yourselves friends, from the mammon of iniquity make friends*[^22].

**11.** Riches are called mammon by the Hebrew name, whence in Punic too gain is called "mamon." What, then, do we do? What did the Lord command? *Make for yourselves friends from the mammon of iniquity, that they too, when you begin to fail, may receive you into the eternal dwellings*[^23]. It is easy indeed to understand that alms must be given, that they must be bestowed on the needy, because in them Christ receives. This he himself said: *When you did it for one of my least ones, you did it for me*[^24]. Likewise in another place he said: *Whoever gives to one of my disciples a cup of cold water only in the name of a disciple, amen I say to you, he will not lose his reward*[^25]. We understand that alms must be given, and that those with whom we do it should not be chosen too much, because we cannot examine hearts. When you do it with all, then you will come to the few who are worthy. You are hospitable, you prepare your house for pilgrims: let even the unworthy be admitted, lest the worthy be shut out. For you cannot be a judge and examiner of the heart. Although, even if you could: He is evil, he is not good, I add: He is even your enemy. *If your enemy is hungry, feed him*[^26]. If good must be done even to an enemy, how much more to one unknown, because even if he is evil, he is yet not an enemy. We understand these things, since those who do them procure for themselves friends who may receive them into the eternal dwellings when they are shut out from this stewardship. For we are all stewards, and something has been entrusted to us to manage in this life, of which we must render an account to the great head of the household. And of him to whom more has been entrusted, a greater account is demanded. The first reading which was recited terrified all, and especially terrified those who are over peoples, whether they be rich, or kings, or princes, or judges, or bishops, or prelates of churches. Each one must render an account of his stewardship to the head of the household. This stewardship is temporal, the reward of the steward is eternal. But if we so conduct this stewardship that we render a good account of it, we are secure that after lesser things greater are entrusted to us. *Be*, he said, *over five possessions*[^27], said the master to his servant who well rendered an account of the money he had received to dispense. He calls us to greater things, if we have done well. But because it is difficult in a great stewardship not to sin much, therefore alms must not cease, so that, when we are about to render an account, we may see not so much a judge of integrity as a merciful father. For if he begins to examine everything, he will find many things which he may condemn. We ought on this earth to help the wretched, that there may be done in us what is written: *Blessed are the merciful, for God will have mercy on them*[^28]. But in another place: *Judgment without mercy to him who has not done mercy*[^29].

**12.** *Make for yourselves friends, then.* Let each one do it from what he has. Let no one say: I am poor. Let no one say: Let the rich do it. Those who have more, let them do more too from more. Do not even the poor have something from which to do it? Zacchaeus was rich[^30], Peter poor[^31]. The former bought the kingdom of heaven with half his riches, the latter bought as much with one net and one boat. For not because the former bought it did there fail for the latter what he might buy. The kingdom of God is not so for sale that, when one has bought, another has nothing to buy. Behold, the fathers bought, and kept for us what we might buy. Did they buy one thing, we another? It is the very same. It is always being bought, and up to the end of the age it is set forth for sale. Do not fear lest you be shut out by the increase. There is no reason for you to say: That man is going to buy it: for he has as much as I do not have. He who set it forth for sale answers you: Bring what you have; what you too may buy is whole for you. I said that it was whole for Peter on account of the one boat he had. It was whole for that widow who put two mites into the treasury[^32]. She put in two coins and bought the whole. For she put in much who left nothing for herself. And what I said a little before, what is cheaper than a cup of cold water? The kingdom of heaven is worth even so much. Whoever has not had a boat and nets, whoever has not had the riches of Zacchaeus, whoever has not had the two coins which the widow had, has at least a *cup of cold water*[^33]. I think he therefore added *cold*, lest the firewood trouble you. But perhaps for the hour you do not even find a cup of cold water to hold out to the thirsty. You do not find it and you suffer along with the thirsty. God sees what you have within, he does not see the power in the hand, but he sees the will in the heart. You too have bought, be secure. This possession is called peace: *peace on earth to men of good will*[^34].

**13.** But to that which we had passed over, brothers, let us return. What does the mammon of iniquity mean? What did the Lord wish to admonish us? Ought we to commit fraud that we may have whence to give alms? Many do this, but they do not do well. They take much, and give something of it, and think they remit their sins as though by bribing the judge. He to whom you give rejoices, but he from whom you take laments. God has his ears in the middle. *With him there is no respect of persons*[^35]. He hears the groaner against you more than the one giving thanks for you. Let no one, then, persuade himself, because the Lord named the mammon of iniquity, that for the sake of giving alms one must be eager for frauds, robberies, despoilings, and whatever else displeases. What, then, does he say: *Make for yourselves friends from the mammon of iniquity*? I understand nothing else, brothers, except that mammon is gold, that is, riches. For let us now speak Latin, that you may understand. There are true riches, there are false. Iniquity calls the false riches riches; it calls them riches, for the true riches are with God. The true riches the angels have, who are in want of nothing. But we, the riches which we seem to have, we seek as it were medicines of weakness. If we were healthy, that is, in that immortality which we are afterward going to have, we would not seek these riches. These riches iniquity calls riches. Therefore he said: Make for yourselves friends from the riches of iniquity, not from the riches which you gather through iniquity, but from the riches which iniquity calls riches when they are not true riches.

**14.** Hear how these riches, which are not true riches, are called riches by iniquity. The Psalm says in a certain place—a man groans there and wishes to be freed from certain alien sons and says to the Lord: *Free me from the hand of alien sons, whose mouth has spoken vanity and their right hand is a right hand of iniquity*. Those of whom he says, *Their right hand is a right hand of iniquity*, speak of nothing but frauds and prepare themselves for frauds. Is he not going to call or name someone a fraudster? But what is he going to say? *Whose sons are like new plantings established, their daughters arranged and adorned like the likeness of a temple. Their storehouses full, overflowing from this into that. Their oxen fat, their sheep fruitful, multiplying in their goings out. There is no breach of the fence, nor outcry in their streets.* How great a temporal happiness he described! Where, then, is iniquity? Where vanity? Hear what follows: *They have called blessed the people who have these things.* Behold whence iniquity, because they called blessed the people to whom these things abound. Another blessedness they did not see, the one that is true they did not seek. They consumed all their desire on earthly happiness. They were unwilling to lift their heart on high. But this man who groans and wishes to be freed from such people, what does he say? When he had said of the alien iniquitous sons, *They have called blessed the people who have these things*, as though it were said to him: But whom do you call blessed? *Blessed the people whose God is the Lord himself*[^36]. Behold the true riches. But those are the riches of iniquity. Whoever has those riches of iniquity, let him make friends from them before he goes out from this stewardship. For if he makes friends from them, he uses them well. Are they not riches with him? For he has other true riches: he reckons his God as riches. From earth he treads earth. Finally, you love them. The Lord said to a certain rich man loving his possessions much: Do you love what you possess? Migrate them where I tell you. I do not wish you to lose them. But what? *Make for yourselves treasure in heaven, where the thief does not approach nor the moth corrupts*[^37]. By loving your riches, you are going to lose them. Migrate them where you will not lose them. Place them there where you may come.

**15.** *Make for yourselves friends, then*, he says, *from the mammon of iniquity, that they too may receive you into the eternal dwellings*[^38]. Let this sentence be expounded thus. Suppose the mammon of iniquity is money from frauds. Your father acquired it from usury. He made you rich. Let not the usury of your father please you. Do not be the heir of iniquity: be the heir of the money of iniquity. Do not, lest you too imitate your father by lending at usury. But now that great money is in the house! Make from it friends from the mammon of iniquity, not that you should now commit fraud and give from it, but what has already been gathered from fraud. If your father had learned to seize, do you learn to give out.

**16.** But why from a hundred to fifty, why from a hundred to eighty? By the hundred to fifty he wished to signify a half. For Zacchaeus did this. *Half of my goods I give to the poor*[^39]. By the hundred to eighty he wished to signify two tenths. For to give from a hundred and twenty so that eighty remain, this is to give two tenths. For they used to give one tenth. But the Lord said in the Gospel: *Unless your justice abounds more than that of the Pharisees and scribes, you will not enter the kingdom of heaven*[^40]. If, then, the justice of the Pharisees and scribes was to give a tenth, how will your justice abound unless you give at least two tenths? To give something more is to abound.

**17.** You make friends, then, you stretch out hope, you exercise desire, by patience you tolerate present things, whether prosperous or adverse, since by him who seeks happiness on high, even this happiness here is tolerated. For it is tolerated, because, as long as we are on pilgrimage, whatever holds us back from our God is held among evils. And of greater struggle is the breast that fights against happiness lest it be corrupted, than that which fights against unhappiness lest it be broken. Through this patience, then, with the age ended, or our life ended, not far ended, whose end hastens for each one, we shall be secure about the eternal dwellings, because we have made for ourselves friends from the mammon of iniquity.
