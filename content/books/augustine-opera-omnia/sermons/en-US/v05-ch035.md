# Sermon 172

**1. 1.** The blessed Apostle admonishes us that concerning those who sleep—that is, our dearest dead—we should not be saddened, like the rest who have no hope, namely the hope of resurrection and of eternal incorruption. For Scripture's most truthful custom calls them "those who sleep," so that, when we hear "those who sleep," we may by no means despair that they are going to wake. Hence too it is sung in the Psalm, *Surely he who sleeps will not also rise again*[^1]? There is, then, concerning the dead, in those who love, a certain sadness in a way natural. For nature, not opinion, shudders at death. Nor would death befall man, except from the punishment which a fault preceded. Wherefore, if the animals which were so created that each dies in its own time flee death, love life; how much more man, who had been so created that, if he had been willing to live without sin, he would live without end? Hence, then, it is necessary that we be sad when those whom we love forsake us by dying; because, even though we know that they do not leave us to remain forever, but go before us a little to be followed, yet death itself, which nature flees, when it seizes the beloved, saddens in us the very affection of that love. Therefore the Apostle did not admonish us not to be saddened; but, not *like the rest who have no hope*. We are saddened, then, in the deaths of our own by the necessity of losing them, but with the hope of receiving them again. Thence we are anguished, hence we are consoled; thence infirmity afflicts, hence faith refreshes; thence the human condition grieves, hence the divine promise heals.

**2. 2.** Accordingly, the pomps of the funeral, the trains of the obsequies, the sumptuous diligence of burial, the opulent construction of monuments, are some sort of solaces of the living, not aids of the dead. But by the prayers of the holy Church, and the saving sacrifice, and the alms which are distributed for their spirits, there is no doubt that the dead are aided; so that the Lord deals with them more mercifully than their sins deserved. For this, handed down from the fathers, the whole Church observes: that for those who have died in the communion of the body and blood of Christ, when they are commemorated in their place at the very sacrifice, prayer is made, and it is mentioned that it is offered for them too. But when, for the sake of commending them, works of mercy are celebrated, who can doubt that they are supported—those for whom prayers are not vainly presented to God? It is by no means to be doubted that these things profit the dead; but only such as lived before death in such a way that these things could be useful to them after death. For those who went out of their bodies without the faith that works through love[^2], and without its sacraments, in vain are offices of this kind of devotion bestowed on them by their own; for, while they were here, they lacked its pledge, either not receiving the grace of God, or receiving it in vain[^3], and treasuring up for themselves not mercy, but wrath[^4]. Therefore no new merits are acquired for the dead when their own do something good for them, but these consequent things are rendered to their preceding merits. For it was brought about only while they lived here, that these things should aid them somewhat, when they had ceased to live here. And therefore each one, ending this life, can have after it nothing but what he deserved in it.

**2. 3.** Let the devout hearts of the dear, then, be permitted to be saddened over the deaths of their own with a healable grief, and let them pour out consolable tears for the mortal condition; tears which the joy of faith should quickly check, by which the faithful are believed, when they die, to go away from us a little, and to pass over to better things. Let fraternal services console them too, whether those that are shown at funerals, or those that are applied to the grieving, lest there be the just complaint of those who say, *I waited for one to grieve with me, and there was none; and for comforters, and I found none*[^5]. Let there be, according to one's strength, care for burying and for constructing tombs: because these too are reckoned among good works in the holy Scriptures; nor only in the bodies of the patriarchs and other saints, and in the human corpses of any persons lying dead; but even in the body of the Lord himself, those who did these things were proclaimed and praised. Let men fulfill these offices of the last duty toward their own, and these soothings of their human mourning. But those things which aid the spirits of the dead—oblations, prayers, distributions—let them bestow much more observantly, urgently, abundantly for them, who love their own, dead in the flesh, not in the spirit, not only carnally, but also spiritually.

[^2]: See Gal 5:6
[^3]: See 2 Cor 6:1
[^4]: See Rom 2:5
