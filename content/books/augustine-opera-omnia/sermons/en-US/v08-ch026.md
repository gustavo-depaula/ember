# Sermon 293/C: On the Birthday of Saint John the Baptist

**1.** Throughout Christ's Church, spread far and wide, the birthday of John the Baptist, the bridegroom's friend and the Lord's forerunner, is celebrated today. I owe this solemnity a sermon; you owe it attention; we all owe it devotion. For *among those born* *of women no one has arisen greater than John the Baptist*; only the one through whom he was created was placed before him. A marvelous thing happened: he preceded in birth the one without whose help he could not have been born at all. Rightly the one is voice and the other Word, for John said: *I am the voice of one crying in the wilderness*; but of Christ it was said: *In the beginning was the Word.* Something similar happens in our very different speech: the word is born first in the mind, so that the speaker's voice may arise; the voice is uttered by the mouth, so that the word may become known to listeners. Thus Christ remained in the Father, through whom John, like everything, was created; John came forth from his mother, through whom Christ might be known by everyone. Christ was the Word in the beginning before the world; John, at the end, the voice before the Word. The word is uttered after understanding, the voice after silence. Thus Mary believes in giving birth to Christ; Zechariah, about to father John, became mute. Finally, Christ was born of a flourishing young woman, John of declining old age. The word multiplies in the thinker's heart; the voice is spent in the listener's ear. Perhaps this too pertains to *he must increase, but I must decrease*. For every legal and prophetic announcement sent before Christ, like a voice before the word, lasted until John, in whom the final figures ceased. Then Gospel grace and the manifest preaching of the kingdom of heaven, which will have no end, bear fruit and grow throughout the world.

**2.** The births of John and Christ indicated this, as did their sufferings. For John was born when the day begins to decrease; Christ when the day begins to grow. John's decrease signified the cutting off of his head; Christ's increase, his exaltation upon the cross. There is also another, somewhat more hidden understanding, which the Lord opens to those who knock, of what should be understood in John's saying about Christ: *He must increase, but I must decrease.* Whatever belongs to human righteousness, the furthest a human being can advance, had been brought to completion in John, of whom the Truth says: *Among those born of women no one has arisen greater than John the Baptist.* Therefore, no human being could be greater than he; yet he was only human, whereas Christ is God and man. But because in Christian grace this is chiefly commended and learned—that no one should glory in a human being, but *the one who glories should glory in the Lord,* the human being in God, the slave in the Lord—he says: *He* *must increase, but I must decrease.* God in himself neither decreases nor increases. But in human beings, the more and more we advance in true piety, divine grace increases and human power decreases, until God's temple, which is in all Christ's members, is brought to that perfection in which, every principality, authority, and power abolished, *God may be everything in everyone*. John the Evangelist says: *He was the true light, enlightening every person coming into this world*; John the Baptist says: *We have all received from his fullness*. Therefore, when the light, always complete in itself, nevertheless increases in the person enlightened, that person certainly decreases in himself, as what he was without God is abolished. For without God a human being can do nothing except sin. Here human power decreases when divine grace, the destroyer of sins, prevails. The creature's weakness yields to the Creator's strength, and the pride of private love gives way to common charity, as John cries in our misery about Christ's mercy: *He must increase, but I must decrease.*
