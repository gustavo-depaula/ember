# Sermon 308: On the Same Feast

**1. 1.** On account of this passage, which we heard today when the Gospel was being read, I say to Your Charity: you see that this wretched Herod loved John, a holy man and a man of God: but because he had rashly sworn, drunk with joy and with delight at the dancing girl, he promised that he would give whatever that girl, who had pleased by her dancing, should ask. But when she asked for a cruel and wicked thing, he was indeed grieved; for he saw so great a crime being done: but, set between his oath and the girl's request, where he saw the bloody deed, there in turn he feared the guilt of perjury; lest he offend God by perjuring, he offended God by raging. Someone says to me: What then ought Herod to have done? If I say, He ought not to have sworn: who does not see that he ought not to have done this? But I am not consulted about a man, whether he ought to swear; but what he ought to do who has sworn. That is the great deliberation. He swore rashly: who does not know it? Yet he slipped, he swore. Behold, the girl asked for the head of holy John: what ought Herod to have done? Let us give him counsel. If we say, Spare John, do not commit the crime; we counsel perjury. If we say, Do not perjure; we provoke him to fulfill the crime. A bad situation.

**2. 1.** Before you come, then, to this two-headed snare, take rash oaths from your mouth: before you come to this bad habit, I admonish my brothers, I admonish my children: what need is there for you to come to this point, where we cannot find counsel?

**2. 2.** Yet, the Scriptures searched more diligently, one example occurs to me, where I see a godly and holy man fallen into a rash oath, and preferring not to do what he had sworn, rather than to fulfill his oath by the shedding of a man's blood. I recall it, then, to Your Charity. When the ungrateful Saul was persecuting holy David, he went with his men wherever he could, lest he be found by Saul and killed. And on a certain day, from a rich man who was called Nabal, and was shearing his sheep, he asked for support of food, for himself and those who were with him. The merciless man refused to give, and, what is graver, answered insultingly. Holy David swore that he would kill him. For he was armed. And what had been easy, and seemed just to do at the urging of anger, he poured out an incautious oath; and he began to go, to do what he had sworn. Abigail, the wife of Nabal, ran to meet him, and brought him the necessaries he had asked for. She begged him humbly, bent him, and called him back from her husband's blood. He had sworn rashly, but did not fulfill the oath, by a greater godliness.

**3. 2.** Accordingly, dearly beloved, I return again to admonishing you. Behold holy David did not indeed in anger shed a man's blood; but who can deny that he swore falsely? Of two sins he chose the lesser: but that was the lesser in comparison with the greater. For weighed in itself, false swearing is a great evil. First, then, you ought to labor, and to fight against your bad habit, bad, bad, and very bad; and to take swearing from your mouths.

**3. 3.** But if someone provokes you to an oath, thinking perhaps that he can be satisfied in this way, if you swear concerning that matter which he thinks you have committed or done, and perhaps you did not do it; lest an evil suspicion remain in him, if you swear, you do not sin as does the one who provoked you: because the Lord Jesus said: *Let there be in your mouth, Yes, yes; No, no. Anything more than this is from evil*[^1]. He was speaking, however, about swearing, where he wanted us to understand that swearing itself is from evil. If you are provoked by another, it will be from his evil that you swear, not from your own. And this is close to the common evil of the human race, since we cannot see one another's hearts. For if we could see one another's hearts, to whom would we swear? When would an oath be exacted from us, when the very thought would be seen by the eyes of our neighbor?

**4. 4.** Write in your hearts what I say: he who provoked a man to an oath, and knows that he will swear falsely, surpasses the murderer. For the murderer will kill the body, that one the soul; indeed, two souls, both of the one whom he provoked to swear, and his own. You know that what you say is true, and that what he says is false, and you compel him to swear? Behold, he swears, behold, he perjures himself, behold, he perishes: what have you found? Indeed you too have perished, who wanted to glut yourself on his death.

**5. 5.** I will say something which I have never said to Your Charity, among this people, which happened in this church. There was here a certain man, simple, innocent, well-faithful, known to many of you, that is, to the people of Hippo, indeed to all, called Tutuslymeni. Who of you who are citizens does not know Tutuslymeni? From him I heard what I say. Someone—I do not know who—denied him either what he had entrusted, or what was owed him; and he committed himself to the man's good faith. Disturbed, he provoked him to an oath. The other swore, this man lost his case: but while this man lost, the other utterly perished. So this Tutuslymeni, a serious and faithful man, said that on that very night he was presented before a judge, and that with great force and terror he came to a certain lofty and admirable man presiding on high, to whom there obeyed a retinue of others likewise lofty; that, being troubled, he was ordered to be called back, and was questioned in these words: Why did you provoke a man to an oath, whom you knew would swear falsely? He answered: He denied me my property. It was answered him: And was it not better that you should lose the property you were demanding, than that you should destroy that man's soul by a false oath? Thrown down, he was ordered to be beaten. He was beaten so gravely that, on the back of the waking man, the marks of the blows appeared. But it was said to him, after he was corrected: Your innocence is spared; henceforth beware lest you do it. He indeed committed a grave sin, and was corrected: but a much graver sin will he commit, who after this sermon of mine and this admonition and exhortation of mine does anything of the sort. Beware of false swearing, beware of rash swearing. From these two evils you will most safely beware, if you take from yourselves the habit of swearing.

[^1]: Matt 5:37
