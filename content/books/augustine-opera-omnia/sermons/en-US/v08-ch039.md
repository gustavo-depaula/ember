# Sermon 299/E: A Treatise on the Birthday of the Holy Martyrs of Scilli,

Delivered in the Basilica of the New Men

**1.** The strength of the martyrs of Christ, men and women, is Christ. For if men alone stood firm in suffering, courage would be ascribed to the stronger sex. The weaker sex was able to suffer bravely too, because God was able in all. Therefore, whether one be a man, or be a woman, in tribulation one ought to say: *The Lord is my strength*[^1]; and: *I will love you, Lord, my strength*[^2]. Love itself is strength: for one who knows how to love can endure all things for what he loves. And if wanton love has persuaded lovers to bear many things bravely for their trifles and offenses, and those who lie in wait against another's chastity set no peril before their eyes, how much braver ought they to be in the love of God who love him from whom neither living nor dying can they be separated? An unchaste lover indeed loses what he loves, if he is killed for his beloved; but the lover of God, brave and just, not only will not lose what he loved because he dies, but by dying finds what he loved. Finally, the lover of his offense is afraid to confess, the lover of God is afraid to deny. So let us choose, brethren, the love in which we may live innocently and die secure; for once this love is chosen, when it has taken possession of our hearts, to live will be *Christ* for us, *and to die gain*[^3]. By dying we escape what we hate; by dying we reach what we love. So let one who loves this life do so, if he cannot afterward love a lasting one. Whether you love it or not, what you love flees; it flees, nor do you hold what you love. The years come on, your span declines, what remains is shortened; so the years have not been added to lengthen your life, but, if you attend to what remains, they have receded. For if they had been added, they would have made your life longer; but as it is, thirty years, say, remained to you; you live to this end, that they be diminished. Without cause did you add many years on your birthday, which by living long you have made fewer. Attend to the fingers of one counting, not so that those that have passed are reckoned for you, but those that have remained; and you see them come to this, that they are not. For if you are passing your third hour, you do not call back the first; nor do you make tomorrow out of yesterday now fled; after a little tomorrow too will be yesterday. What good is it not to despise these things, which by loving you cannot hold fast? The day you love flees you, the God you long for draws near. Love this, that by loving you may arrive. He is faithful, he stands toward you: come to him. And at this you were sluggish: he came to you, was born for you, died for you.

**2.** Do not then fear the bitter cup of death: for death is bitter, but through this bitterness one passes to great sweetness. This bitterness heals the inward parts of your soul; not if you die, but if you die for the truth. This bitterness is medicinal, not lethal: it heals your inner parts, drink it secure. What the physician did not hesitate to drink, why does the sick man hesitate to drink? He had nothing in himself that the bitterness of that cup might heal; he drank for your sake, lest you think poison was being given you. He drank for your sake, that you might learn to say: *Precious in the sight of the Lord is the death of his saints*[^4]. Confessing Christ, they suffered in various ways: some struck by the sword, some burned by fire, some thrown to beasts, some not even permitted to be buried. All hard, all savage, all horrible: but in the sight of men. Therefore Scripture, when it commended the deaths of the martyrs: *Precious*, it says, *in the sight of the Lord is the death of his saints*; in the sight of him who knows how to judge, and does not know how to err. For fools and unbelievers, seeing present happiness despised by the wise and faithful, thought those wretched who were dying for the name of Christ; for they did not have the eyes of faith, and so they were not able to look ahead to the things that were promised. The promiser and giver came: by warning he strengthened, by giving the Spirit he perfectly healed. For he said: *Do not fear those who kill the body, and after that have nothing more they can do*[^5]. You see fools raging; if they hear this, they have nothing more they can do. For they do many things to corpses: they mangle, burn, scatter, do not allow burial; and, as if boasting, they say: Where is what Christ said, that when a man kills a man, he has nothing more he can do? Behold, I have done so much to the corpse of the dead. He is like in heart to one who does not feel in his flesh. Cruel and stupid one, what have you done? If he feels, you have done it; if he does not feel, you have raged in vain. *I show*, he says, *you whom you should fear: him who, when he has killed, has power to cast into hell*[^6]. What the man who strikes and slays does not have, who strikes as far as the flesh, but with the departing spirit does not follow, because he does not even see it. Who is to be feared? See by whom you are killed, see how you die. He has you in his power after death who had you before death; for no man could do anything against you, unless he permitted it. Do you wonder that he permits it? Hear the prince of martyrs, who, when he was judged by a man—God hidden, man manifest, in which he was despised—when he was judged by a man, the man puffed himself up. *Do you not*, he says, *answer me? do you not know that I have power to kill you and to release you?*[^7] And he, gentle, the Lord of all, the servant of all, serving the sick not by condition but by love, healed even that puffed-up and swollen man. Christ was as it were judged by him, he was healed by Christ; the swollen man terrified, but the physician cut. But when he had puffed himself up as he wished, he received an answer; not that it might be said to him: You have no power over me, but rather, I have power over you. And if the Lord had said this, he would have said the truth, but he would not have offered us an example. He taught even while he suffered, just as he taught while he was tempted. As he taught you what to answer the tempter, when he was tempted, so he taught you what to answer the persecutor, when he was judged. That voice was ours, the head was speaking for the body. So what does he say? *You would have no power over me, unless it had been given you from above*[^8]. He did not say: You have none; but: You would have had none, unless you had received it. He taught the martyr to be subject not to man, but to God; he taught the martyr, when he suffers something from a man, not to fear the man, but him who permits the man to act, who gives the man power. Taught by that instruction, a most brave woman: *Honor*, she says, *to Caesar as to Caesar: but fear to God*[^9]. She rendered to each its own, distributing justly; she answered, neither proud nor weak. She attended to the apostle saying: *Be subject to every human institution for God's sake*[^10]. *Honor*, she says, *to Caesar as to Caesar*. Though he rage, let him be honored; let the order of humility be rendered to him, even if he has not the rule of power. For the highest power has he *in whose hand we are, both we and our words*[^11]. Him therefore, says the Lord, fear, who has the power by which, even when he kills, he can go no further—he can do nothing beyond—but, even when he kills, has it in his power to cast into the hell of fire[^12].

**3.** O unbeliever, you look at present things, you are frightened by present things: sometime think of the future. Tomorrow and tomorrow; sometime there will be a last tomorrow; day presses on day, nor does it snatch away him who made the day. For with him there is a day without yesterday and tomorrow: with him there is a day without rising and setting: with him there is everlasting light, where is the fountain of life, and in whose light we shall see light. There let the heart be, so long as it is necessary for the flesh to be here; there let the heart be: the whole will be there, if the heart is there. The rich man in purple and fine linen ended his delights; the poor man full of sores ended his miseries. The one feared the last day, the other longed for it. It came to both, but did not find both equal; and because it did not find both equal, it did not come to both equally. To die and to die was alike: to end this life and to end this life is the common condition. You have heard the sharing: attend to the separation. For it happened that the poor man died, and was carried by angels into the bosom of Abraham; but the rich man also died, and was buried[^13]. He indeed was perhaps not even buried. You know the rest so well: the one was tormented in hell, the other was refreshed in the bosom of Abraham. Those delights and those miseries passed away; both were ended and changed: the one from delights to punishments, the other from miseries to delights. Indeed those delights and those miseries were brief; but those punishments and those delights followed without end. It was certainly not the riches that were condemned in the rich man, nor poverty praised in the poor man; but impiety condemned in the one, and piety praised in the other. For sometimes men hear these things in the Gospel: and those who have nothing rejoice, at these words the beggar exults. I shall be, he says, in the bosom of Abraham, not that rich man. Let us answer the poor man: You have fewer sores; add to your merits, wish for the tongues of dogs too. You boast that you are poor, I ask whether you are faithful: for unbelieving poverty is both torment here and condemnation there. Let us address the rich man too: When you heard the Gospel about the one who was clothed in purple and fine linen and feasted sumptuously every day, you were afraid; I do not disapprove that you were afraid, but fear more what was disapproved there. He despised the silent poor man before his door; the poor man longed for the crumbs falling from his table[^14]; no covering, no shelter, no kindness was offered him. This was punished in the rich man: cruelty, impiety, arrogance, pride, unbelief: these were punished in the rich man. Whence do you prove this? someone says; surely the riches were punished. If I do not prove it from the very chapter of the Gospel, let no one listen to me. When that rich man was in torments in hell, he longed for a drop of water on his tongue from the finger of that poor man who had longed for the crumbs of his table; and perhaps the one came to the crumbs more easily than the other came to the drop: for the drop was denied him[^15]. Abraham, in whose bosom the poor man was, answered him: *Remember, son, that you received your good things in your life*[^16]. For this I have proposed to show, that impiety and unbelief were condemned in him, not riches and the abundance of present things. *You received*, he says, *your good things in your life*. What is: *your good things?* You thought no others good. What is: *in your life?* You did not believe in another life. So *your good things*, not God's: *in your life*, not Christ's. *You received your good things in your life*: what you believed in is finished, and so you did not receive the better goods; because, when you were among lower things, you would not believe in them.

**4.** Perhaps we accuse this rich man, and interpret the mind of father Abraham according to our own grasp. That something may be said more plainly, that what is wrapped up may be unfolded, that what is shut may be seen, that it may be opened to those who knock. When help or even a little mercy had been denied him, that what is written might be fulfilled, *Judgment without mercy to him who has not done mercy*[^17], he asked that Lazarus be sent to his own brothers, and tell them what was done after this life. It was said that this could not be, but that, if they were willing not to come to the same places of torment, they should hear Moses and the prophets. They have, he says, Moses and the prophets: let them hear them[^18]. And he, who knew himself and his brothers—for unbelieving brothers used to mutter such things among themselves, mocking the divine words, when they had heard something said in the Law or the Prophets that pertained to avoiding eternal punishments and longing for eternal rewards, saying to one another in a mutter: Who has risen from there? who has come from there? who has been able to tell us what is done there? Since I buried my father, I have not heard his voice. Knowing this, that he and his brothers used to talk thus with unbelieving heart and mouth, he asked for this, that what they said was not done might be done, on account of which they despised the divine words. And he: Let someone go from here, he says, and tell them. And father Abraham: They have Moses and the prophets there; let them hear them. And he, mindful of his own conversations: *No, father Abraham*[^19]. As if to say: I know what we used to say. *No, father Abraham*: I know what I say, I know what I ask. The despiser of the poor man, merciful too late, wished mercy to be shown to his brothers, which he had not shown himself. *No*, he says, *no, father Abraham*: they do not believe Moses and the prophets. I know, I was such: *But if someone goes to them from the dead, they will believe*[^20]. And father Abraham: *If they do not believe Moses and the prophets*—for they were Jews, nor would he say, father Abraham, unless a Jew. So father Abraham answered: *If they do not hear Moses and the prophets, neither will they be persuaded if someone rises from the dead*[^21]. It was done, it was fulfilled: they did not hear Moses and the prophets, they despised the Lord rising again. For just as they were despisers of Moses and the prophets, so they would not hear the Lord rising from the dead, against whom they bought false witnesses. *We give you*, they say, *money*—to those guards of the tomb—*and say that while we slept his disciples came and stole him*[^22]. Witnesses asleep, bought, corrupted, denying their own life, dreaming of another's thefts! If you were awake, why did you not seize the thieves? If you were asleep, how did you see what was done?

**5.** We have proved, I think, that in that rich man it was not riches that were accused, but impiety, unbelief, pride, cruelty. Hear a greater proof that riches were not accused. Where was the rich man plunged? Into the hell of torments. Where was the poor man lifted? Into the bosom of Abraham. See the poor man in the bosom of Abraham: Abraham received him, he was received. Abraham himself, who was the faithful patriarch. Add—what I would say—read in the book of *Genesis* the riches of Abraham, gold, silver, herds, household[^23]: Abraham abounded. Why do you accuse the rich man? The rich man received the poor man. Far be it then from us to accuse riches; nor yet do we therefore build up avarice. Let not the rich man say that I spoke on his behalf; let not the rich man say that I wished to be his consoler. For he was afraid at the recounting of the Gospel: when he heard the rich man cast headlong into the punishments of hell, he was afraid. I have given security. Let him not fear riches, but vices; let him not fear abundance, but avarice; let him not fear means, but covetousness. Let him have as Abraham had, and have with faith; let him have, possess, not be possessed. Someone will say to me: And how did Abraham have? Do you wish to know how Abraham had? What was with him? Piety. What? Faith. What? Obedience. What? Inner riches. Do you wish to know? Do you wish to learn from the lesson read? Every man, whatever he thinks he can excusably gather, keeps for his children. So since all men keep their riches for their children, and those who have no children suffer constraint, because they have none to whom they may leave what they have gathered; since therefore it is manifest that all men love their children more than their riches, love those for whom they keep more than what they keep, do you wish to know how Abraham held that inheritance? Read how at God's command he despised his heir. Set before your eyes a rich father: there is the one who keeps, there is what he keeps, there is for whom he keeps. Weigh both, distribute the merits, set love in order. Surely he valued more the one for whom he kept than what he kept. If the Lord Jesus Christ had said to him: *If you wish to be perfect, go, sell all that you have, and give to the poor, and you will have treasure in heaven, and come, follow me*[^24], I believe that, like that rich man in the Gospel, even Abraham would have gone away sad. Would he hear "Let go your riches" sadly, who heard "Offer me your heir" gladly? Offer me your only one, your beloved son; what I gave, give to me. He did not hesitate, did not waver, sadness did not cloud his devotion, neither his who was to offer, nor his who was to be offered; for not even the boy himself trembled under his father's sword. He was led rejoicing by one rejoicing, he was bound, set on the altar, and there was no delay; the father's armed right hand was raised, nowhere trembling, nowhere weak, nor was it laid down except at the bidding of him at whose bidding it had been raised[^25]. Behold how you should have, and have whatever you can; not that you may nourish covetousness, but that you may perfect acts of piety, and await the last day secure; truly rich, rich within; without as you can, within as you are bidden. Do you have? *The Lord gave*. Have you lost? *The Lord has taken away*[^26]. Rejoice, since he who took away did not take himself away. Or does he not suffice you, who makes you? *As it pleased the Lord, so it was done*[^27]. Tell me, what do you fear? When you are evil, and he is good, does good please you, and evil him? It cannot be. Believe that to be good which pleased the Good One. *As it pleased the Lord, so it was done; blessed be the name of the Lord*[^28]. *We know that for those who love God all things work together for good*[^29].
