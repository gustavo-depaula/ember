# Sermon 341: , Augmented.

**1.** This psalm, as is known to all Christians, since it is written there: *They have pierced my hands and my feet, they have numbered all my bones; and they themselves have looked and gazed upon me, they have divided my garments among themselves, and over my garment they have cast lots*[^1]. For it was figured in the person of our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ. But how the person of our Lord Jesus Christ is commended and conveyed to the hearts of the faithful—although I do not think it is hidden from you, yet you must be reminded, because some do not know it, and many have forgotten what they heard, and some wish to be confirmed in what they hold, nor are there lacking those who wish to hear even what they are firm in from us, for our own sakes. But this understanding, which, according to the strength the Lord deigns to supply, I try to convey to your love, avails for understanding many obscure things in the most sacred books, that is, how Christ is spoken of.

2 [1]. For as far as we have been able to observe the sacred pages, he is named in three ways when he is proclaimed, whether through the Law and the Prophets, or through the apostolic letters, or through faith in the deeds that we come to know in the Gospel. One way is according to God and that divinity coequal and coeternal with the Father before the assumption of the flesh. The second way is the one in which, the flesh having been assumed, he is now both the same God who is man and the same man who is God, according to a certain property of his excellence by which he is not made equal to other men, but is the mediator and head of the Church—so he is both read of and understood. The third way is when, in a certain manner, the whole Christ, in the fullness of the Church, that is, head and body, according to the fullness of a certain perfect man, in which perfect man we are members, is both proclaimed to believers and offered to the prudent to be recognized. Yet we cannot recall or explain in so small and short a time all the testimonies of the Scriptures by which all these three kinds may be proved; but we will not leave them unproven, so that, with certain testimonies brought to mind, the rest, which we are not permitted to mention because of the constraints of time, you may now be able to observe and find for yourselves in the Scriptures.

3 [2]. To the first way, then, of presenting our Lord Jesus Christ our Savior, the only Son of God, through whom all things were made, belongs that most noble and most splendid passage in the Gospel according to John: *In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God. The same was in the beginning with God. All things were made through him, and without him was nothing made. What was made, in him is life; and the life was the light of men, and the light shines in the darkness, and the darkness has not comprehended it*[^2]. These words are wonderful and astonishing before they are understood; once understood, they are to be embraced. But that they may be understood is not provided by human resources, but by the inspiration of the very one who deigned to inspire fishermen to say them. For that fisherman said this, the son of Zebedee, who left his father and his boat and his nets and followed God[^3], not abandoning his human father, but choosing God as his father. To him, surely, in letting go his little boat and his nets, the whole world was reckoned as let go. For our Lord Jesus Christ did not consider what the poor who followed him had left, that is, what they had left in their possessions, but what they had left in their desire. For everyone who has little wants to have more, and he who lets go the little he had has let go more of what he was longing to have. And therefore, when that rich man departed from the Lord in sorrow—whom, to receive counsel, he called good teacher, and the one giving counsel he left as though a bad teacher[^4]—the disciples, after that man departed in sorrow, when they heard that by the mercy of God even the rich could enter the kingdom of heaven, of whose salvation they had despaired when they heard that it is easier for a camel to enter through the eye of a needle than a rich man into the kingdom of heaven[^5], put in, saying: *Behold, we have left all and followed you: what then will there be for us?*; and the Lord said: *You shall sit*, he says, *upon twelve thrones, judging the twelve tribes of Israel*[^6]. He also promised to others who leave all that is theirs and follow him a great reward, and a great consolation in the very promise and a joy in the receiving, since whoever for his sake leaves all that he had in this world, *shall receive*, he says, *in this age a hundredfold, but in the age to come eternal life*[^7]. All these things, if they were expounded more carefully, would hold us and turn us away from the matter proposed. Yet let your love now attend to what pertains to the matter undertaken: that the first poor men left all that was theirs and followed God and were made apostles, and that as much is reckoned to them, who left little, as is reckoned to those who left much. And what is truly to be wondered at: then a rich man, hearing from the mouth of the Lord that all things were to be left and God to be followed, departed in sorrow, hearing this from the mouth of the Lord; now, without seeing the Lord in the flesh, men hear this from his Gospel and do what that man did not do, and so there is fulfilled in them: *Blessed are they who do not see and yet believe*[^8].

**4.** {Why, then, did the Lord first choose the ignoble, the poor, the unlearned, the unpolished, when he had before his eyes a great crowd[^9]—fewer indeed in comparison with the many poor, but in its own kind many rich, noble, learned, wise, for whom he afterward also provided (for he did not abandon them: all such kinds came to the faith)? The Apostle expounds the mystery: *He chose the weak things of this world to confound the strong; and he chose the foolish things to confound the wise; and the ignoble things of the world God chose, and the things that are not*, that is, are not reckoned, *as though they were, that the things that are might be brought to nothing*[^10]. For he had come to teach humility, to overthrow pride; God had come humble. In no way would he here seek the lofty, who had come so humble. First, because he chose to be born of that woman who was betrothed to a carpenter[^11]. He did not, then, choose grand origins, lest nobility should grow proud upon this earth. He did not even choose for his birth the greatest city, but was born in Bethlehem of Judea[^12], which is not even judged worthy of the name of a city. For even today the inhabitants of that place call it a village: it is so small and slight and almost nothing, were it not ennobled by the nativity of Christ}. He came, then, not as one who would be noble from the place, but as one who would himself make the place noble; and so too with the rest concerning our Lord Jesus Christ which it would take long to recall. {He chose, then, the weak, the poor, the unlearned, the ignoble: not because he abandoned the strong, the rich, the wise, the noble, but because, if he chose these first, they would seem to themselves to be chosen by the merit of their dignities, by the merit of their riches, by the merit of their birth, and so, puffed up over these things, they would not receive the salvation of humility, without which no one can return to that life from which one would not have fallen except through pride}. By contrast, then, the physician heals disease: the cold by hot remedies, the hot by cold, the dry by moist, the moist by dry. If, then, we see the art of medicine healing the ailing by contraries, it is not to be wondered at if we are healed by the humility of God, we who were sick through human pride. And so the Lord more wholesomely gained an orator from a fisherman than a fisherman from an orator. For the orator Cyprian was a martyr, but the fisherman apostle came first. Christian emperors were made afterward, but first Christ himself was being preached by fishermen. And so truly, God choosing the weak things of the world confounded the strong—confounded them in order to heal, cast them down in order to lift them up—so that what we have come to know in our own times, and what is not to be passed over in silence, was made manifest to us, the faith being made known by the very facts, that *God chose the weak things of the world to confound the strong*[^13]. For in our times an emperor came to the city of Rome: there is the temple of the emperor, there is the tomb of the fisherman. And so that pious and Christian emperor, to beseech salvation from the Lord, did not proceed to the proud temple of the emperor, but to the tomb of the fisherman, where, humble, he might imitate the fisherman himself, so that, being then regarded, he might obtain something from the Lord[^14] which the proud emperor could not deserve.

**5.** Why, then, did I say this? Because we recalled that way of presenting Christ according to his divinity before the assumption of the flesh, a certain way wonderful and astonishing to all who hear, but known to few who understand, when they have knocked in such a way that they are, as it were, dazzled by a flash of that eternal and ineffable light, saying and recalling: *In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God*[^15], and so on—and lest you should seek the understanding of these words from us in vain, we said that you can understand these things by the inspiration of him by whose inspiration it came about that an unlearned fisherman proclaimed them. For that fisherman did not know these things of himself, nor was he so great in talent or learning that he could penetrate with the edge of his mind and transcend the whole of this air and all the ethereal powers, and from there arrive at the natures of the luminaries, virtues, powers, and angels, and the whole most pre-eminent spiritual creation, fallen by no sin but clinging always to the contemplation of unchangeable truth, and could transcend even this and arrive at that *which neither eye has seen nor ear heard, nor has it entered into the heart of man*[^16]. What kind of thing is the Word of the Father, how is he the Word? Was he thought? Did he sound? Surely not. For if he was thought, he took up time; if he sounded, he passed through the beaten air. Not so the Word of God, but the abiding Word, and always the Word, uttered and not taken away—or rather, not even uttered, lest some bodily extension be understood. And how it is to be said, no one speaks with a human mouth: it is piously believed that the Word is begotten; he himself can express and utter himself, the Son of God alone can utter himself; but the one to whom he utters himself can understand him, yet cannot utter him. Whence did the fisherman see this, except because he willed to show himself? From this the fisherman saw it, from which he drank; but from where did he drink it? Let us attend to the banquet of the Lord, in case we may find from where the fisherman drank this. All the disciples were reclining with the Lord; John is written in the Gospel to have been accustomed to recline upon the breast of the Lord[^17]. What wonder, then, if from his breast he drank what he said of his divinity? For the Lord of the banquet, and the Lord of those banqueting, would not allow his disciple to fill his belly from that table and not fill his mind from his breast. So indeed he did: he fed and filled the disciple from his breast. But that one, filled, belched, and that belching itself is the Gospel. You have therefore seen with the eyes of faith, in the Gospel, the fisherman feasting; hear him belching: *In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God. The same was in the beginning with God*[^18]. You used to despise it when you heard "word"—for daily words are heard; now do not despise it, because *the Word was God*. "And how shall I understand God and Word?" Let him who filled the fisherman make you drink. Meanwhile, for now hear him belching, believe the one belching, so that you too, ascending by the step of faith, may be filled by the liveliness of understanding.

**6.** "What, then?" you will say. "Am I now to believe that the Word of God is the Son of God?" The only Son of God, not two words, but one Word, even though there are two words of Scripture—for instance, those two commandments pertaining to the twofold love[^19], or those pertaining to retribution. For the Lord says certain things to the pious set at his right hand, and says other things to the impious set at his left. He does not say to the pious what he says to the impious. As though two words are distributed by the one who narrates, according to our capacity, according to our merits: here they are distributed, but there something &lt;...&gt;, just as, if I could speak a certain likeness from corporeal things—yet one suited to the senses of the weak—if I could speak of how the brightness of fire or of a star or of the moon or of the sun is one and is in one manner. But if it is regarded by different eyes, by one pure and healthy, by another wounded and bleary, to the one it seems a peaceful light, to the other as though raging and angry. For it strikes back the wounded gaze and causes pain, whence to the healthy it was causing joy. Behold, it is peaceful; behold, it is fierce. Is it itself divided, itself dissimilar? No, but according to the merits of those who behold it diversely. Attend, my brothers, and from small things come to know greater ones. So the Word of God is one, but he distributes things worthy according to merits, saying to those: *Come, blessed of my Father, receive the kingdom which was prepared for you from the foundation of the world*[^20], but saying to those: *Go into the eternal fire which was prepared for the devil and his angels*[^21]. What is so diverse as *come into the kingdom* and *go into the eternal fire*? Is the word, then, diverse? No. It is one, but the merits of the hearers are diverse. Hear the prophet in the psalms saying this same thing: *God has spoken once: these two things I have heard*[^22]. A great question: now, if you have understood the things I have said, let it not trouble you. *He has spoken once*, and you have heard *two*? *He has spoken once* according to the one Word; how, then, have you heard *two*? *Because power belongs to you*, he says, *and to you, Lord, mercy*—power, then, to punish, mercy to set free. Hear what follows: *Because you will render*, he says, *to each according to his works*[^23].

**7.** O the one Word, the sweet Word: may he inspire in us his love; and he inspires it through the Holy Spirit. For so is the Trinity: the Father who begot, the Word whom he begot, the Spirit by whom love is inspired; he who begot, and that which he begot, and that by whom it is inspired. That the most sweet and most excellent and ineffable Trinity may be loved, pre-eminent above the whole creation which it began, perfected, and ordered, altogether pre-eminent, it desires the hearts of those who love. But "desires" is rightly said, because it makes us desire. For the Spirit too is said to rejoice[^24], because it makes us rejoice. And of God: *The Lord your God tests you, that he may know whether you love him*[^25]. What is *that he may know*, except that he may make you know? Therefore he feeds the hearts of those who desire, who love purely, who love freely, because they find nothing better to love—or let them find something better, and receive this as their reward; let them ask for this, if they do not find something better. If, then, your Creator did not give himself to you, than whom you find nothing better, you would always have to groan rather than seek something else; if your Creator did not give himself to you, than whom nothing is better—if indeed he allows these words to be said of him (but he does allow it, because we are little ones; he does allow it, because, if we wished to say anything worthy, we should say nothing at all)—if, then, your Creator, as I had begun to say, were unwilling to give himself to you, you would always have to groan rather than seek something else. But now he himself offers himself, and you seek something else; he in a certain way asks to be loved, he who is not loved. O you are wretched, not he! He seeks, then, the hearts of those who sincerely love him, who by pious intent go beyond every mutable creature—and they do go beyond it, if they are humble: lofty places are not surpassed by the lofty. If you wish to surpass every creature and arrive at that other thing which you have heard from the mouth of the belching fisherman, be humble, seek piously. For when you have transcended all mutable things, both corporeal and spiritual, you will come to behold that Trinity, and you will drink from where he drank; and when you have come to it, you will laugh at all the slanderers—and when you have first laughed, perhaps afterward you will weep over those who by vain contentions stir up smoke for themselves, lest they see it.

**8.** This, then, will be enough, brothers, to have conveyed to you. But that you may understand, knock at his door. Let no one say to himself with carnal thinking: "How was the Word with God and how in the womb of the virgin, that he might be born? Did the Word itself, then, come down, so that, when it was in the womb of the virgin Mary, it withdrew from the Father? If it did not withdraw from the Father, how could it be here? Or did half perhaps remain with the Father, and half be in the womb? Or did a great part perhaps remain with the Father, and a little something or a small piece come down to the womb of the virgin?" Do not cut God into pieces; let the dissensions of your thoughts be in a certain way united in him, let him not be torn apart in them; let him gather you, do not you scatter him. "And how," you say, "am I to understand it? I do not know, I cannot. He is both with the Father and in the womb of the virgin. Who is there who can understand this?" But you are hearing of God; you make for yourself a certain body by the habit of carnal thinking. And when you have done this, you must then cut it up. For you do not find the whole everywhere. One part of the earth is here, another outside in the street, not the same, because it is earth, because it is a body: one part is large, another small. So too water is divided: one part is near this shore, another near that, and the part that is there is not the part that is here; although the whole seems to be spread out, as it were, everywhere in its places, yet it does not have the same part everywhere, but one part here, another elsewhere. So too this diffusion of the air: there is one air in this basilica, another in that, since the air is one throughout, but one part is in this, another part in that; the part that is there is not the part that is here, nor that which is here that which is there. So too the parts of the sky: those are different which we behold when we look at the east, and different the parts we behold when we turn ourselves toward the west. It cannot happen that one and the same part is everywhere: even if the whole seems to be everywhere through its parts, yet not the whole of it is everywhere, but one part is in one place, another in another. Do not, then, think of God in such a bodily way. Or perhaps you cannot think except such things? I will give an example, perhaps, by which this may come to the notice of your loving attention.

**9.** You wish to divide the Word of God, and it does not seem credible to you that it should be whole with the Father, whole in the womb of the virgin Mary. I say more: it is whole wherever you wish, but it has not everywhere taken up the person of a man, with whom it would become one God and man. I do not want you to divide: grasp as much as you can. Do you wish to divide the Word of God? Hear the word of man. Surely it seemed to you that the Word of God could not be both in Mary and with the Father, unless distributed as it were piecemeal, so that one part of it would be here, another there. When you hear a word from us, or when you hear words from us—attend to what we are speaking: for you receive a better lesson from this word which is spoken to many of you than from those words which, though of the same nature, you say to one another. For you deal with fewer people, we deal with so many: all hear what we say, and all hear the whole. If I set before you bodily food on which you might feed, you would be going to divide the food among yourselves, and one would take away this part, another that, to eat it. Even if you all fed on the same and one food, yet not all on the same parts, but you would divide among yourselves, according to your measures, piecemeal, what was set before you, one taking away that part, another this; one food would reach all, but not the whole food to all. So it surely would be. But just as that food would be set before your mouth, so now a kind of food of voices and words is set before your ears, and yet the whole reaches all. Or perhaps, when I speak, does one take one syllable for himself, another the other? Or one one word, another a different word? If that were so, I would have to speak as many words as I see people, so that single words at least might reach all. And indeed it easily happens: I say more words than there are people here, and all reach all. Is the word of a man, then, not divided into syllables so that all may hear it, while the Word of God is cut into pieces so as to be everywhere? Do we think, brothers, that these sounding and passing words are in any respect to be compared to that Word which remains unchangeably? Or have I, because I have said these things, compared them? But in whatever way I wished to convey it to you, so that what God displays in corporeal things might help you toward believing the things you do not yet see concerning spiritual things. But let us now pass on to better things. For words sound and pass away. Think of spiritual things, think of justice. When one person thinking of justice is set in these western regions, and another thinking of justice is set in the East, how does it come about that the one thinks of the whole, and the other of the whole? That the one sees the whole, and the other the whole? For whoever sees justice, according to which he does something, himself acts justly. He sees within, he acts without. From where does he see within, if nothing is present to the one seeing? But if it is present, then, because he is himself set in one place, will another's thought not reach that same place? But when you, set here, see with your mind the same thing that one set so far away sees, and the whole shines for you, the whole for him too, see that the things that are divine and incorporeal are everywhere whole, and believe that the Word is whole in the Father, whole in the womb. For you believe this of the Word of God, who is God with God.

**10.** [3]. And hear another presentation, another way of presenting Christ which Scripture proclaims. For it says those things before the flesh was assumed. But how does Scripture proclaim this one? *The Word*, it says, *was made flesh, and dwelt among us*[^26]. For he who had said, *In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God. The same was in the beginning with God. All things were made through him, and without him was nothing made*[^27], would proclaim to us the divinity of the Word in vain if he kept silent about the humanity of the Word. For in order that I may see that, this is done with me here; in order that I may be purified to contemplate that, he himself comes to the aid of my weakness. By taking from human nature human nature itself, he was made man. He came with the beast of the flesh to the one who lay wounded on the road[^28], so that he might shape and nourish our little faith by the mystery of his incarnation, and might make our understanding serene to see that which he never lost through what he took up. For he began to be man; he did not cease to be God. This, then, is the proclamation of our Lord Jesus Christ insofar as he is the mediator, insofar as he is the head of the Church: that God is man and man is God, when John says, *And the Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us*[^29].

11 [4]. Hear now both in that very well-known passage of the apostle Paul: *Who, though he was in the form of God*, he says, *did not count equality with God a thing to be grasped*[^30]. This is: *In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God*[^31]. How did the Apostle say, *He did not count equality with God a thing to be grasped*, if he is not equal to God? But if the Father is God and he is not God, how is he equal? Where, then, that one says, *The Word was God*, there this one says, *He did not count equality with God a thing to be grasped*. And where that one says, *The Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us*, there this one says, *But he emptied himself, taking the form of a servant*[^32]. Pay attention. Through this, then, that he was made man, through this that *the Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us*, through this *he emptied himself, taking the form of a servant*. For what did he *empty*? Not so as to lose his divinity, but so as to put on humanity, appearing to men as what he was not before he was man. So by appearing he emptied himself, that is, keeping the divinity of his majesty and offering the garment of the flesh of humanity. Through this, then, that *he emptied himself, taking the form of a servant*, not taking the form of God—for when speaking of the form of God, he did not say he took it, but, *though he was in the form of God*; but when it came to the form of a servant, *Taking*, he says, *the form of a servant*—through this, then, he is the mediator and head of the Church, through whom we are reconciled to God, through the mystery of his humility and his passion and resurrection and ascension and the judgment to come, so that those two things may be heard, since God has spoken once[^33]. Where are the two heard? Where he renders *to each according to his works*[^34].

12 [5]. Holding this, then, do not be amazed at the questions of men, which spread like a cancer, as the Apostle said[^35]; but guard your ears and the virginity of your mind, as those betrothed by the friend of the bridegroom for a chaste virgin to be presented to the one husband, Christ. Bodily virginity is in few in the Church; virginity of mind ought to be in all. The serpent wishes to corrupt this virginity, of which the same Apostle says: *I betrothed you to one husband, to present you as a chaste virgin to Christ. And I fear lest, as the serpent led Eve astray by his cunning, so also your senses may be corrupted away from the chastity that is in Christ*[^36]. *Your senses*, he says, that is, your minds. For this is the more proper meaning. For the senses are understood also of this body—of seeing, hearing, smelling, tasting, touching. The Apostle feared that our minds would be corrupted, where the virginity of faith is. Now, then, O soul, guard your virginity, to be made fruitful afterward in the embrace of your bridegroom. *Hedge, then*, as it is written, *your ears with thorns*[^37]. The question of the Arians troubled the weak brothers of the Church, but in the Lord's mercy the catholic faith prevailed. For he did not abandon his Church, even though it was troubled for a time, so that supplication might always be made to him by whom it would be confirmed upon the solid rock. And still the serpent murmurs and does not keep silent: by a certain promise of knowledge he seeks to cast out from the paradise of the Church the one whom he does not permit to return to that paradise from which he was first cast out.

13 [6]. Attend, my brothers: what was done in that paradise is done in the Church. Let no one seduce you from this paradise: let it be enough that we fell from there; let us, having had the experience, at least be corrected. He is the serpent who always suggests impiety; he sometimes promises impunity, just as he promised there too: *You shall not die*, he says, because God had said, *You shall die*[^38]. He suggests such things now, so that Christians may live badly: "Will he destroy them all?" He says: "I will condemn; I will pardon those who have changed themselves: let them change their deeds, and I change my threats." He, then, is the one who murmurs and mutters and says: "Behold, it is written, *The Father is greater than I*[^39]; and you call him equal to the Father?" I accept what you say, and I accept both, because I read both. Why do you accept the one and not want the other? For you have read both with me. Behold, *The Father is greater than I*: I accept it, not from you but from the Gospel; and you, accept from the Apostle that he is equal to God[^40]. Join both: let both agree, because he who spoke through John in the Gospel, he himself spoke through Paul in the letter. He cannot be at odds with himself, but you do not want to understand the harmony of the Scriptures, since you yourself love to quarrel. "But," you say, "I prove from the Gospel: *The Father is greater than I*." And I from the Gospel: *I and the Father are one*[^41]. "How is each true?" How does the Apostle teach us? Hear: *I and the Father are one. Who, though he was in the form of God, did not count equality with God a thing to be grasped*. Hear: *The Father is greater than I. But he emptied himself, taking the form of a servant*[^42]. Behold, I have shown why he is greater; you show in what he is equal. For we read both. He is less than the Father insofar as he is the Son of man; equal to the Father insofar as he is the Son of God, because *the Word was God*. The mediator, both God and man: God equal to the Father, man less than the Father. He is, then, both equal and less: equal in the form of God, less in the form of a servant. So tell me, from where is he equal? Is he equal in one part and less in another part? Behold, apart from the assumption of the flesh, show me him as equal and as less. From where you are going to demonstrate it, I wish to see.

14 [7]. Attend to foolish impiety, which is wise according to the flesh, as it is written: *To be wise according to the flesh is death*[^43]. I still set aside, and I do not yet speak of the incarnation of our Lord, the only Son of God, but as though what has already been done were not yet done, I attend with you: *In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God*[^44]. I attend with you: *Who, though he was in the form of God, did not count equality with God a thing to be grasped*[^45]. There show me the lesser. What will you say? Will you divide God by qualities, that is, by certain corporeal or animal affections, in which we perceive one thing to be different from another? I can indeed say so naturally, but whether it is such that you too understand it, God will see. So, as I had begun to say, before the assumption of the flesh, before the Word became flesh and dwelt among us, show me the lesser, show me the equal. Is God one thing and another, so that on one side the Son is less than him, on another side equal? As if we should say: "There are certain bodies," you could say to me: "Equal in length, but less in strength." For very often two such bodies are found, that they are equal in stature of length, but in strength one is less, the other greater. Are we, then, going to think of such bodies: are we going to think of God and his Son in this way? May God turn such thoughts away from the hearts of Christians. He was wholly with the Father, wholly in the flesh, wholly above the angels. But perhaps you will think this, so as to say: "Equal in both strength and length, but they are unlike in color"? Where is there color except in bodies? But there is the light of wisdom. Show me the color of wisdom. Show me the color of justice. If these things have no color, would you say those things of God, if you had the color of shame?

15 [8]. What, then, will you say? Are they equal in power, but the Son less in prudence? God is unjust, if he gave equal power to a lesser prudence. If they are equal in prudence, but the Son is less in power, God is envious, who gave a lesser power to an equal prudence. But in God everything that is said is one and the same. For in God power is not one thing and prudence another and fortitude another and justice or chastity another. Whatever of these you say of God, it is not understood as one thing and another, and nothing is said worthily, because these belong to souls, which that light in a certain way floods and affects according to their qualities. Just as when this visible light rises upon bodies: if it is taken away, there is one color for all bodies—or rather, no color should be said to exist; but when, brought in, it has illumined the bodies, although it is itself of one kind, yet according to the diverse qualities of the bodies it sprinkles them with diverse brightnesses. These affections, then, belong to souls, which are well affected by that light which is not affected, and are formed by that which is not formed.

16 [9]. Yet we say these things of God, brothers, because we find nothing better to say. I call God just, because in human words I find nothing better: for he is beyond justice. It is said in the Scriptures: *The Lord is just, and he has loved justice*[^46]. But there it is also said that God repents[^47], there it is also said that God does not know[^48]. Who would not shudder? Does God not know, does God repent? Yet for this reason Scripture has wholesomely descended even to those words which you too shudder at, lest you should think that those things which you consider great are said worthily. As though you wished to believe that according to human feeling God repents of something, and another who already understands better should rebuke you, explaining to you that, if you find anything of the sort in the Scriptures, it is not said for the reason that God suffers any such thing as you do, with grief of heart, in the condemnation of your counsel or deed, but on account of this, that he changes—other than men suppose—because, if men do anything of the sort, they do it by repenting, if they turn themselves from a purpose, whereas his counsel remains certain and eternal; but because one thing was hoped he would do and he does another, it is said figuratively that he repents. And so, if you ask: "What, then, is said worthily of God?" Perhaps he may answer you, so as to say that he is just. But another, understanding better than this one, may say that even this word is surpassed by his excellence, and that even this is said unworthily of him, although it is said fittingly according to the capacity of men. But when that man wishes to prove from the Scriptures, because it is written, *The Lord is just*, he is rightly answered that it is set down in the same Scriptures that God repents. As you do not take that according to the custom of those who speak, as men are wont to repent, so also you should understand that what is called just does not befit his pre-eminence, although Scripture has set this down well, so that through whatever words the mind may be led step by step to that which cannot be spoken. You call God just indeed, but understand something beyond justice, something other than you are accustomed to think even of a man. But the Scriptures called him just, and for that reason they also called him repenting and unknowing, which you no longer wish to say. As, then, you understand those things which you shudder at as said because of your weakness, so also these things which you esteem highly were said for the sake of some weakness of the stronger. But whoever transcends these and begins to think of God worthily, as much as is granted to a man, will find a silence to be praised with the heart's ineffable voice.

17 [10]. Therefore, brothers, because in God virtue is the same as justice—and whatever you say of him, you say this same thing, while yet you say nothing worthily—you cannot say that the Son is equal to the Father in justice and not equal in virtue, or equal in virtue and not equal in knowledge, because he who is equal in any one thing is equal in every thing, because all the things you say there are the same and are worth the same. It is enough, then, that you cannot say how the Son is equal to the Father, unless you posit certain diversities in the substance of God. And when you have posited them, the truth will cast you out, and you do not approach that sanctuary where he is seen most purely. But since you cannot call him equal on one side and unequal on another, because there are no parts in God, nor can you say he is equal in one quality, less in another, because there are no qualities in God; according to God you cannot call him equal except equal in every way: from where, then, can you call him less, except because he took the form of a servant? And so, brothers, always observe this. If you have received a certain rule in the Scriptures, the light itself will show you all things. Wherever you find the Son named equal to the Father, take it according to a certain essence of divinity; wherever you find him less, take him as less according to the form of a servant which he assumed: according to what is said, *I am who I am*[^49], and according to what is said, *I am the God of Abraham and the God of Isaac and the God of Jacob*[^50]; so you will hold both what is in his nature and what is in his mercy.

**18.** But let it not disturb you that this too was said according to this distinction, which the Lord spoke in the Gospel and which is in the psalm that we have just sung. But that we may expound and convey it with a more manifest testimony: in the Gospel it is said, *To my Father and to your Father, to my God and to your God*[^51]. *To my Father and to your Father*—let it not trouble you. For he is always Father to the Son: never was the Son not begotten, never was he not Father. But he is our Father in another way, through the mercy of adoption. Him he begot, us he adopted. Him he begot *before the morning star*[^52]—which you should not take as one star, but mystically the morning star is what bears light, not because it is light, but because it is illumined by light, so as to bear light. For of that archangel too who did not stand in the truth it was said that he was rising like a morning star[^53], but he did not stand in that light. Therefore every soul that is illumined, so as to shine, is called a morning star; if it turns itself away from the illuminating light, it is darkened. Therefore John the evangelist says of the Lord himself: *He was the true light*. And as though it were asked, "What is the *true light*?": *which enlightens every man*[^54], he says—not, then, that which is enlightened, but *that which enlightens*. But of John the Baptist he says: *He was not that light*[^55]. But what kind of light *was he not*? Such as is that *which enlightens* and is not enlightened. For John was a light that is enlightened, because he had received of his fullness[^56]. Whence the Lord himself says: *And you were willing to exult for an hour in his light*[^57]. And to his disciples he says: *You are the light of the world*[^58]. For they were enlightened so as to be light, but he was *the true light which enlightens every man*; the light that is enlightened was made another thing. Therefore *the true light which enlightens*, this is our Lord Jesus Christ; but the made light that is enlightened, this is John, this is the apostles, this is all holy souls and the most blessed intellectual spirits, who by receiving light become morning stars. Therefore *before the morning star I begot you*[^59], that is, before every creature. It is to be understood before every creature, in which the spiritual and rational creature holds the primacy, which, illumined, bears light. Let us, then, brothers, retain both in our Lord Jesus Christ: both according to that which he is God, he is equal to the Father, and according to that which he is man, he is less than the Father. But, as I had begun to say, let no one take offense that he says, *My Father and your Father*. For he is always the Father of the only-begotten Son, he is always born and born before the morning star, that is, before every creature which by being illumined becomes light. Rightly, then, *my Father and your Father*, because we received from him to be sons of God: *who gave us the power to become sons of God*[^60]. And our adoption, which the Apostle mentions, is well known to your love, of which he says: *Awaiting the adoption, the redemption of our body*[^61], and again: *God sent his Son, made of a woman, made under the law, to redeem those who were under the law, that we might receive the adoption of sons*[^62]. Rightly, then, he first said singularly, *my Father*, then *your Father*. But how *my God and your God*? Now if the rule is known, what do you await from me? *My Father* always, *my God* from when he is man. Hear the psalm that was read: *Upon you was I cast from the womb, from my mother's belly you are my God*[^63]. I think enough has been said about that way too, in which our Lord Jesus Christ our Savior, the head of the Church, made the mediator through whom we are reconciled to God, is presented in the Scriptures as God and man.

19 [11]. The third way is how the whole Christ is proclaimed according to the Church, that is, head and body. For both head and body are one Christ, not because he is not complete without the body, but because he has deigned to be complete with us also, who is also always complete without us, not only in that he is the Word, the only-begotten Son equal to the Father, but also in the very man whom he took up and with whom he is at once God and man. Nevertheless, brothers, how are we his body, if not also one Christ with us? Where shall we find this, that head and body are one Christ, that is, the body with its head, the bride with her bridegroom? He speaks as though singly in Isaiah, and one and the same speaks, and see what he says: *As on a bridegroom he has set a crown upon me, and as a bride he has clothed me with ornament*[^64]. He calls the same one bridegroom and bride: bridegroom according to the head, bride according to the body. Two are seen, and he is one. Otherwise, how are we members of Christ, the Apostle saying most plainly: *But you are the body of Christ and his members*[^65]? If we are members of Christ, we are also all together the body: not only we who are in this place, but also throughout the whole earth; nor only we who are at this time, but—what shall I say?—from righteous Abel down to the end of the age, where men no longer beget nor are begotten, whatever of the righteous has made its passage through this life, whatever is here now (that is, not in a place, but in this life), whatever there will be of those born hereafter, all this is one body of Christ, but individually they are members. If, then, all are the body, and individually members, there is surely a head whose body this is. *And he is*, it says, *the head of the body, the Church, the firstborn, himself holding the primacy*[^66]. And because it says of him also that he is *the head of every principality and power*[^67], this Church, which is now on pilgrimage, is joined to that heavenly Church, where we have the angels as fellow citizens, because we should shamelessly claim that we are going to be equal after the resurrection of bodies, had not the truth promised, saying, *They shall be equal to the angels of God*[^68]; and one Church is made, *the city of the great King*[^69], whose Son too he willed to be, taking flesh from her pilgrim part, her king and her one who makes her fruitful, to call back what had wandered from there. For she is Zion in the figure of a mystery, of which it is written: *Mother Zion, a man will say, and a man was made in her, and the Most High himself founded her*[^70], that is, that very one who *was made man in her*, while he was most humble, *himself founded her, the Most High*, because *all things were made through him, and without him was nothing made*[^71]. A maimed body cannot be called whole without the head, but the head wills to be united with its body. And one Christ is with his body by graciousness, not by necessity. For we need the good of God; God does not need our good. Hear the prophet: *I said to the Lord: You are my God, for you have no need of my goods*[^72].

20 [12]. So, then, Christ is sometimes presented in the Scriptures in such a way that you understand the Word equal to the Father. Sometimes in such a way that you understand the mediator-man, when *the Word was made flesh*, to dwell *among us*[^73], when that Only-begotten, through whom all things were made[^74], *did not count equality with God a thing to be grasped, but emptied himself, taking the form of a servant, becoming obedient unto death on a cross*[^75]. But sometimes in such a way that you understand head and body, with the Apostle himself most plainly expounding what was said of man and wife in Genesis: *And they shall be*, he says, *two in one flesh*[^76]. Attend to him expounding it himself, lest we seem to dare to say anything by our own conjectures. *And they shall be*, he says, *two in one flesh*, and he added: *This is a great mystery*. And lest anyone should still think it was about a husband and wife according to the natural coupling of the two sexes and bodily commingling: *But I*, he says, *say in Christ and in the Church*[^77]. According to this, then, that what was said is taken *in Christ and in the Church*: *They shall be two in one flesh: now no longer two, but one flesh*. As bridegroom and bride, so head and body, because *the head of the woman is the man*[^78]. {Whether, then, I say head and body, or say bridegroom and bride, understand one thing. And therefore the same Apostle, even when he was still Saul, heard: *Saul, Saul, why do you persecute me?*[^79], since the body is joined to the head; and when, now a preacher of Christ, he suffered from others what he as a persecutor had done: *That I may fill up*, he says, *what is lacking of the afflictions of Christ in my flesh*[^80], showing that what he suffered belonged to the afflictions of Christ. This cannot be understood according to the head, which now in heaven suffers nothing of the kind, but according to the body, that is, the Church, which body, with its head, is one Christ.}

21 [13]. Show yourselves, then, a body worthy of such a head, a bride worthy of such a bridegroom. That head cannot have any but a worthy body; nor does that man marry any but a wife worthy of himself. *That he might present to himself*, it says, *a glorious Church, having no spot or wrinkle or anything of the kind*[^81]. {This is the bride of Christ, having no *spot or wrinkle*. Do you not wish to have a spot? Do what is written: *Wash yourselves, be clean, take away your wickedness from your hearts*[^82]. Do you not wish to have a wrinkle? Be stretched out on the cross. For it is not enough only that you be washed, but also that you be stretched out, that you may be without spot or wrinkle. For through the washing sins are taken away; through the stretching-out comes the desire for the age to come, on account of which Christ was crucified. Hear Paul himself, washed: *Not*, he says, *by works of justice which we have done, but according to his mercy he has saved us, through the washing of regeneration*[^83]. Hear the same one stretched out: *The things*, he says, *that are behind forgetting, stretched out toward the things that are ahead, I follow with intent toward the prize of the heavenly calling of God in Christ Jesus*[^84]. Rightly, then, he himself, without the spot of iniquity and without the wrinkle of a double heart, as a good and faithful friend of the bridegroom[^85], betroths to him *a chaste virgin to be presented to the one husband* without spot or wrinkle}. For not without cause is the prophecy of Isaiah recalled beside the road of the fuller[^86].

**22.** All such things, brothers, are in a mystery: whatever things in the Scriptures sound as it were absurd, as it were unnecessary, are closed up. But they are not closed up and empty: God closes them over something full, but he seeks those who knock, that he may open[^87]. Surely, weigh it. For, to say something cheerfully, when boys buy nuts for themselves, lest they be deceived, they weigh them in the hand, and when they have found one heavy, they more securely keep what is still closed. Weigh, then, when you hear certain sentences closed up in such holy Scriptures, so illustrious, known to the whole world, spread throughout the whole earth; from when they began to be proclaimed up to this time, nothing else has been done in human affairs than was foretold there. A great weight of authority, then. According to this weight, weigh the sentences, and let your soul—which perhaps already wished to despise it—say what was said: *And they shall be two in one flesh*[^88]. Already it was saying to itself: "What is this? Was God really going to take care how man and woman are joined together, so as to say, *They shall be two in one flesh*?" Do not throw it away; you are a boy: weigh it. "And how," it says, "am I to weigh it?" Say to yourself: "Truly, any fool would say this, let alone whatever sort of man is called a man of God. Moses, who wrote these things, had at least a fair heart." Add that not without cause have these writings become known to the whole world and are honored throughout the whole world by the religion of believers: "Not without cause would he say, *They shall be two in one flesh*, unless there were there some I-know-not-what which human thought shudders at, but which is in some way closed up. It is not empty." When you have said these things to yourself, you are weighing it; if you have weighed it, you have found that it is heavy; you keep it securely. But perhaps you are so weak a boy that you cannot break it open: just hold it and rejoice, know that you are holding something full; there will be no lack of one to break it and feed you. "And who," it says, "will break it?" There is indeed one to break it—as though we were dealing with a little one—give it to a certain kindly father who said: *I do not say these things to confound you, but I admonish you as my dearest children*[^89]. Behold, there is the Apostle—in a certain way he is surely a father—he breaks open for you this which you carry closed, which you have already weighed and felt to be heavy. He will break it—do not fear—who has both fatherly love toward you, when he says: *Though you have many guardians in Christ, yet not many fathers. For in Christ Jesus through the Gospel I begot you*[^90], and motherly love, when he says: *I became a little one in your midst, as a nurse cherishing her children*[^91]. {Therefore he did not say "mother," because mothers, sometimes either too delicate or not loving their children, after they have given birth, hand them over to others to be nursed. Again, if he had only said, *As a nurse cherishing*, and had not added, *her children*, he would seem to have received them to be nursed as though another had given birth to them. Therefore he called himself both a nurse, since he was feeding them, and her children, because he himself had given birth to them, saying: *My little children, whom I am again in travail with, until Christ be formed in you*[^92]. But he travails, as the Church gives birth, with her womb, not with her seed.} Give, then, to this father or mother—call it what you wish: he is not angry, since he willed to be both in affection, while he is neither in sex—give to be broken what you carry enclosed, so heavy, of such great authority. It is written in the book of Genesis: it is not a slight thing, something lies there enclosed. Does he not seem to have said something who said *mystery*? "For I perceive: it is heavy, but it is still closed." *But I say*, he says, *in Christ and in the Church*[^93]. Behold the food: feed, you who did not despise what was closed. But whoever despises what is closed and throws it away will not come to the food.

**23.** One thing comes into my mind from another, since I mentioned the nut, which indeed we weave most fittingly into this sermon, because it came about that we were dealing with closed mysteries. For not in vain did Jacob put three streaked rods in the water, where the flocks, gathered at their time, would drink, nor did he want them to be of one wood, but of different woods[^94]. For the effect that he was contriving it would have sufficed that they be of one wood, nor was there need for there to be three, but either fewer or more, so long as he only put streaked rods in the waters. What, then, does it mean that he put three rods and of three woods, except that it intimates a mystery, but this is closed up? According to the strength which the Lord deigns to grant us, I will break it and open it to you. Jacob had made an agreement with his father-in-law, whose sheep he was pasturing, that, if anything variegated were born either among the sheep or among the goats, it should be his, that is, should belong to the wages of the shepherd[^95]. He therefore brought this about by means of the variegated rods, which the flocks, when they saw them at conception, by a desire of this kind impressed through the eyes, would give birth to variegated offspring[^96]. In the variegated flocks was signified the variety of the nations. And those flocks were of one kind, and they conceived variegated and gave birth to variegated. For from the one nation of the Jews came forth the first preachers of the Gospel, but, so that many nations might be begotten, it was fitting that they conceive variegated and give birth to variegated. And this is *the portion of Jacob*[^97]. For in Jacob Christ was prefigured. For that lesser people was in Jacob, whence it was said: *And the greater shall serve the lesser*[^98]. You will recall that I have already expounded to your holiness concerning Esau and Jacob, to whom it was also said in that blessing which he received from his father: *All nations shall serve you*[^99]. The variety of the nations, then, belonged to Jacob, but unless there were heralds from the people of the Jews, so that the flocks of one kind might conceive from that drink of the rods, the faithful would not be born through the variety of the nations.

**24.** But from where would the flocks conceive the variety of the nations? From the three rods, of course, this could come about. For the animals were at conception when he variegated the rods, that is, peeled them in places at intervals, and put them in the waters, from which, in drinking, they would draw the desire for the variety which would appear in the colors of those being born[^100]. But this could be done from any number and any kind of rods! Yet the mystery of the future Christian people was not known to the people of the Jews, except to a few holy prophets and a few envious teachers of the law, to whom the Lord says: *Woe to you who hold the keys of the kingdom of heaven, and neither enter yourselves nor permit others to enter*[^101]. These are also signified in that parable where the tenants of the vineyard who did not render the rent said: *This is the heir. Come, let us kill him, and the inheritance will be ours*[^102]—which they would not say unless they had known Christ in some part, whose divinity, however, by which he is equal to the Father, was hidden from them: *For if they had known it, they would never have crucified the Lord of glory*[^103]. But he sent preachers not from their number, who *chose the weak things of the world to confound the strong*[^104], so that it might be said: *Where is the wise man? Where is the scribe? Where is the disputant of this age? Has not God made foolish the wisdom of this world?*[^105] Therefore the mystery that was hidden from them, revealed to the unlearned and the simple[^106], was intimated through the baptism of Christ even to the various nations: for this reason the flocks conceived variegated offspring from the three rods set in the water. For as long as God the Father was being preached, but the incarnation of the Son was hidden, which was still being foretold prophetically and understood by very few, the variety of the nations was not being brought forth. But it came about that those sheep, that is, the first Israelites, through whom would be born the variety of the nations belonging to the lot of Jacob (that is, to the inheritance of Christ), drank from the three rods; of which Israelites the Apostle says: *For I too am an Israelite, of the seed of Abraham, of the tribe of Benjamin*[^107]. For Peter too was an Israelite, and Andrew and John and James and the other apostles and the rest of the first preachers of Christ, of whom the Apostle says that the nations are *their debtors*: *For if*, he says, *the nations have shared in their spiritual things, they ought also to minister to them in carnal things*[^108]. Those Israelites, then, were as it were flocks of one kind, because they were of one nation: they drank, in a certain way, the mystery of the Lord's incarnation, so that through the mystery of the Lord's incarnation they might bring forth various nations in the Gospel, that is, a variety of flocks.

**25.** How, then, is the mystery of the Lord's incarnation shown in the three rods? Of what were those rods? One was *of the nut tree*, another was *of the plane tree*, another *of the storax*[^109]. So it is written. Let us therefore question our faith as to how the faith of the Lord's incarnation stands. For we believe that he was born of the Holy Spirit and the virgin Mary[^110]. His being born belongs to the rod of the nut tree. For just as in the nut one comes through wood to the food, so our Lord Jesus Christ would not bear us, unless we came to his body through the wood of the cross. This is so clear that you anticipated my voice with your understanding; for before I unfolded what I had begun to say, you declared it with your voice. Who led you to this food, that you understood so quickly, except he who hung upon the wood? For unless through the cross of the Lord, you would not be Christians; if you were not Christians, you would not receive this so quickly and so pleasantly. The rod of the plane tree, to what does it belong? We say that Christ was born of the Holy Spirit. I think the rod of the plane tree belongs fittingly to the Holy Spirit. For the rod of the storax that remains is, beyond doubt, to be attributed, on account of its most sweet scent, to the inviolate integrity of the virgin Mary. For from this began the report of the most delightful fragrance and sweet odor of the Lord's nativity, that he was born of a virgin. Concerning the rod of the plane tree there is a more laborious understanding, how it is shown to belong to the Holy Spirit. The Lord will be present, with your prayers helping, and will open to you, through our ministry and through our humble and devoted service to your progress, how we are to take the rod of the plane tree as the Holy Spirit. In the plane tree I seek what I may choose, and I find the plane tree praised for no other reason than that it offers a most broad shade to those resting from the heat. Those who know this kind of tree understand that I speak the truth. For this the plane tree is chosen and sought, on account of the breadth of its shade and the delight of its shadiness, where we rest from the heat. But the virgin Mary, who was going to conceive a son not by the heat of lust, but in a certain refreshment of most faithful chastity and uncorrupted virginity, not panting for male embraces but conceiving by faith—a pregnant virgin, a virgin who would give birth, a virgin who would remain—she therefore received this from the Holy Spirit. The Spirit himself offered her refreshment from the heat of all carnal concupiscence, and therefore he was figured by the rod of the plane tree. I lie, if in the Gospel itself the angel does not speak and say to her: *The Spirit of God will come upon you, and the power of the Most High will overshadow you*[^111].

**26.** Now, then, most beloved, just as the Lord has willed, he offers something not from our merit, but on account of our faith, which he himself grants. As we always say and must never keep silent: let every advance of the word be found in your works. Very wretched is the land which, when it is well rained upon, either bears no fruit or even produces thorns[^112]. Grieve with us over those whom we lament. We are accustomed to say that fasts are to be practiced during these festal days of the pagans for this purpose, that we may pray to God for the pagans themselves. But we so shudder at the unhappy debaucheries of many that we exhort you, brothers, to pray with us for certain Christian brothers, that they may at some time allow themselves to be amended and corrected from this wickedness. For what is this, how great and how lamentable an evil! Such a Christian does not despise such trifling, such empty amusements: what will he despise, what will he suffer for Christ, when some trial of tribulation comes? Whom a little spittle overwhelms, how will a river sweep him away? Therefore, brothers, not in vain have I spoken our grief to your holiness. Let those who are here today, who did not fast yesterday, grieve that they have so lived on the other festal days of the pagans, while we are sorrowful for them, and let them at some time deign both to take away our sorrow and their own wickedness.
