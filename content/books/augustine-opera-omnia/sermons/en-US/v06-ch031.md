# Sermon 210: In Lent

**1. 1.** The solemn time has come, which more than throughout the other spans of the year admonishes us, by prayers and fasts, to humble the soul and chastise the body. But why this is celebrated as the solemnity of the Lord's passion draws near, and why it is celebrated by the mystery of the number forty, since it is wont to move some, deservedly we undertake to bring forth to your Charity what the Lord has deigned to grant should be said on this matter. But those whom we know to seek these things not for quarreling but for knowing, by their faith and piety we are much aided, that we may be able to obtain the things to be said.

**1. 2.** For this is wont to make a question: why the Lord Jesus Christ himself, who, having taken up a human body, appeared made man for men surely for this, that he might offer us an example both of living and of dying and of rising, fasted not before he was baptized, but after he was baptized. For so it is written in the Gospel: *But being baptized, he immediately came up from the water; and behold, the heavens were opened to him, and he saw the Spirit of God descending upon him. And behold, a voice from the heavens, saying: This is my beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased. Then Jesus was led into the desert by the Spirit, to be tempted by the devil. And when he had fasted forty days and forty nights, afterward he hungered*[^1]. But we, together with those who are to be baptized, fast before the day of their baptism, which day, the Paschal one, draws near, after which day, for fifty days, we relax our fasts. This would deservedly move us, if it were not lawful to baptize or be baptized except on the most solemn Paschal day. But since throughout the whole year, as for each one there is either need or will, one is not forbidden from baptism, by the gift of him who gave them the power to become children of God[^2]; but the yearly passion of the Lord may be celebrated only on a certain day of the year, which is called Easter: the sacrament of baptism is without doubt to be distinguished from Easter. For this it is lawful to receive on any day: that it is right to enact on one and a certain day of the year. This is given to renew life: that is commended to the memory of religion. But that to that day a far greater number of those to be baptized flows together, here it is not that a more abundant grace of salvation differs, but that a greater joy of the festival invites.

**2. 3.** What of this too, that even the baptism of John, which Christ then received, is to be distinguished from the baptism of Christ himself, which his faithful receive; nor for this reason is that with which Christ was baptized better than this with which the Christian is baptized, because Christ is better than the Christian; but for this reason rather is this baptism, because it is Christ's, preferred to that? For John baptized Christ, while he confessed himself less than Christ: but Christ baptizes the Christian, who shows himself greater even than John. As, in the circumcision of the flesh — although Christ received it, and no Christian now receives it — better is the sacrament of the resurrection of Christ, by which, to put off the fleshly and old life, the Christian is circumcised, that he may hear the Apostle saying: *As Christ rose from the dead by the glory of the Father, so let us too walk in newness of life*[^3]. As that old Passover itself, which was commanded to be celebrated by the slaying of a lamb[^4], is not, because Christ celebrated this with his disciples[^5], better than our Passover, in which Christ was sacrificed. For it pertained to his offering us an example of humility and devotion, that he deigned, in coming, to take up even those sacraments by which he, who was to come, was foretold: that hence he might show with how great religion it behooves us to take up these sacraments, by which it is announced that he has now come. Not, then, because Christ, after receiving the baptism of John, fasted immediately, is he to be believed to have given a rule of observance, that after receiving the baptism of Christ himself, it is necessary to fast immediately: but plainly by that example he taught that one must fast, when perhaps with a sharper struggle we contend with the tempter. For on this account Christ, who as man deigned to be born, did not refuse as man to be tempted, that the Christian, instructed by his teaching, might not be overcome by the tempter. Whether, then, immediately after baptism, or after any interval of time, when a like battle of temptation is brought upon a man, one must fast: that both the body may fulfill its warfare by chastisement, and the mind may obtain victory by humiliation. In that example of the Lord, then, the cause of his fasting was not the washing in the Jordan, but the temptation of the devil.

**3. 4.** But why we fast before the solemnity of the Lord's passion, and that relaxation of fasts is completed on the fiftieth day, this is the reason. Everyone who rightly fasts either humbles his soul in the groaning of prayer and the chastisement of the body out of faith unfeigned[^6]; or, his intent being suspended from the fleshly enticement by some spiritual delight in truth and wisdom, descends to a certain want, to feel hunger and thirst. Of both kinds of fasting the Lord answered those who asked why his disciples did not fast. For of that first kind, which has the humiliation of the soul: *The sons of the bridegroom cannot*, he says, *mourn, as long as the bridegroom is with them. But the hour will come when the bridegroom will be taken from them, and then they will fast*[^7]. But of that other, which has the feast of the mind, he consequently spoke thus: *No one sews a new patch on an old garment, lest a greater tear be made: nor do they put new wine into old wineskins, lest both the wineskins burst and the wine be spilled; but they put new wine into new wineskins, and both are preserved*[^8]. Accordingly, because the bridegroom is now taken away, we, the sons of that beautiful bridegroom, must surely mourn. For, *beautiful in form beyond the sons of men*, whose *grace was poured out on his lips*[^9], among the hands of his persecutors *he had no form nor comeliness, and his life was taken from the earth*[^10]. And rightly do we mourn, if we burn with longing for him. Blessed are they who were allowed to have him then present before the passion, to question him as they wished, and to hear him as they ought to hear. Those days the fathers longed to see before his coming, and did not see: because they had been ordered in another dispensation, through whom he who was to come might be announced, not by whom he when coming might be heard. For of these he speaks to the disciples, saying: *Many righteous men and prophets wished to see what you see, and did not see; and to hear what you hear, and did not hear*[^11]. But in us that is fulfilled which he likewise says: *Days will come, when you will desire to see one of these days, and you will not be able*[^12].

**4. 5.** Who is not burned by the flame of holy longing? who does not here mourn? who does not labor in his groaning? who does not say: *My tears have become my bread day and night, while it is said to me daily, Where is your God?*[^13] For we believe in him now sitting at the right hand of the Father: but yet, as long as we are in the body, we are pilgrims away from him[^14], nor can we show him to those who doubt or deny and say: *Where is your God?* Deservedly his Apostle longed *to be dissolved and to be with him*: but to *remain in the flesh* he judged not best for himself, but necessary for our sake[^15]. Where *the thoughts of mortals are timid, and our foresights uncertain*; since *the earthly habitation weighs down the mind thinking on many things*[^16]. Hence *human life on earth is a temptation*[^17]: and in this night of the age *a lion goes about seeking whom he may devour*[^18]. Not the Lion of the tribe of Judah, our king[^19]; but the lion the devil, our adversary. But he, expressing in himself alone the figures of the four animals from the Apocalypse of John, was born as man, worked as a lion, was sacrificed as a calf, flew as an eagle[^20]. *He flew upon the wings of the winds, and made darkness his hiding place*[^21]. He set darkness, and night was made, *in which all the beasts of the forest pass through. The whelps of the lions roaring*[^22], the tempters, namely, through whom the devil seeks whom he may devour; not indeed having power, except over those whom they receive: for in that very Psalm it follows thus: *seeking food for themselves from God*[^23]. In so perilous a night of this age, full of temptations, who would not fear, who would not tremble in all his marrow, lest he be judged worthy to be left to be devoured in the jaws of so cruel an enemy? Whence one must fast and pray.

**5. 6.** And when rather, when more urgently, than as the very solemnity of the Lord's passion draws near, by which yearly celebration the memory of that same night is in a certain manner carved afresh for us, lest it be deleted by forgetfulness, lest that roaring devourer find us sleeping not in body but in spirit. For the Lord's passion itself, what else did it especially commend to us in our Head, Christ Jesus, than the temptation of this very life? Whence, as the time of his death now drew near, he said to Peter: *Satan has asked to sift you like wheat: and I have prayed, Peter, for you, that your faith may not fail; go and strengthen your brothers*[^24]. And he plainly strengthened us through his apostleship, through his martyrdom, through his epistles. Where, admonishing too that the night of which I speak is to be feared, by the consolation of prophecy as it were a nocturnal light, he taught us to watch with caution. *We have*, he says, *the more sure prophetic word, to which you do well to attend, as to a lamp shining in a dark place, until the day dawns, and the morning star rises in your hearts*[^25].

**5. 7.** Let our loins, then, be girded, and our lamps burning, and let us be like men awaiting their Lord, when he comes from the wedding[^26]. Nor let us say to one another: *Let us eat and drink; for tomorrow we shall die*[^27]. But all the more, as the day of death is uncertain and the day of life is troublesome, let us fast and pray, for tomorrow we shall die. *A little while*, he says, *and you will not see me; and again a little while, and you will see me*[^28]. This is the hour of which he said: *You will be sorrowful, but the world will rejoice*[^29]: that is, this life full of temptations, in which we are pilgrims away from him[^30]. *But again*, he says, *I will see you, and your heart will rejoice; and your joy no one will take from you*[^31]. In this hope of the most faithful promiser we even now in some way rejoice, until that most abundant joy comes, when we shall be like him, for we shall see him as he is[^32], and no one will take our joy from us. For of this hope we have received the Holy Spirit, so welcome and gratuitous a pledge[^33], who works in our hearts the unutterable groanings of holy desires[^34]. *For we have conceived*, as Isaiah says, *and travailed and brought forth the spirit of salvation*[^35]. And *a woman when she is in travail*, says the Lord, *has sorrow, because her hour has come: but when she has brought forth, there is great joy, because a man is born into the world*[^36]. This will be the joy which no one will take from us; into which, into eternal light, we shall be poured over from this conception of faith. Now therefore let us fast and pray, while it is the day of travail.

**6. 8.** Why the fast was instituted by the number forty. This whole body of Christ, spread throughout the whole world — that is, the whole Church does this, and that unity which says in the Psalm: *From the ends of the earth I cried to you, while my heart was in anguish*[^37]. Whence it now becomes clear to us why Lent was instituted as the solemnity of this humiliation. For she who cries from the ends of the earth, when her heart is in anguish, cries from the four parts of the earth, which Scripture too frequently recalls: East and West, North and South. Through all this that decalogue of the Law, now not only to be feared through the letter, but to be fulfilled through the grace of love, was proclaimed. Whence, ten being multiplied by four, we see forty completed. But still in the labor of temptation, with pardon of sins. For who perfectly fulfills: *You shall not covet*[^38]? Whence one must fast and pray: yet one must not cease from good work. To which labor that reward is rendered in the end, which is named by the name of "denarius"[^39]. But as "ternary" took its name from three, "quaternary" from four; so "denary" took its name from ten: which, joined to the number forty, is rendered as it were a reward for the labor. The figure of the number fifty signifies the time of that joy which no one will take from us[^40]: of which in this life we do not yet have the discharge; but yet after the solemnity of the Lord's passion, from the day of his resurrection, through fifty days, in which we relax the fasts, we celebrate this with the Alleluia resounding in the Lord's praises.

**6. 9.** Now therefore, in the person of Christ, that you may not be circumvented by Satan, I exhort you, most beloved, to make God favorable by daily fasts, more bountiful alms, more fervent prayers. Now is the time when both the married should abstain from their wives, and the wedded from their husbands, that they may give themselves to prayers[^41]: although throughout the whole year too, on certain days, they ought to do this; and the more frequently, surely the better: because he too who immoderately seeks permitted things offends the one who permitted them. For prayer is a spiritual thing, and therefore it is so much the more acceptable, the more it fulfills the effect of its own nature. But it is poured forth so much the more by a spiritual work, the more the mind that pours it forth is suspended from fleshly pleasure.

**7. 9.** Forty days Moses, the administrator of the Law, fasted[^42], forty Elijah, the most excellent of the Prophets[^43], forty the Lord himself, having the testimony of the Law and the Prophets. Whence he showed himself with those two on the mountain[^44]. But we, who cannot keep up so long a fast as to take, for so many days and nights, nothing of food, as they did, let us at least do as much as we can; so that, excepting the days on which, for certain causes, the custom of the Church forbids fasting, we may please the Lord our God by a daily or frequent fast. But surely, as abstinence from food and drink cannot, for so many days, be continuous, so can it not be from intercourse? when we see, in the name of Christ, many of both sexes keeping members entirely free from this matter, dedicated to God. I think it is no great thing that the chastity of spouses could last through the whole Paschal solemnity, which virginity can through the whole of life.

**8. 10.** But now this — although I ought not to have admonished it, since I have, as much as I could, especially commended the time of humbling the soul; yet, on account of the errors of men, who through empty seductions and depraved customs cease not to bring upon us a troublesome care on your behalf, I cannot be silent. There are certain observers of Lent who are delicate rather than religious, seeking out new sweetnesses rather than chastising old desires; who, with copious and precious preparations of various fruits, strive to surpass the varieties and flavors of any relishes whatever: they dread, as unclean, the vessels in which meats have been cooked, and in their own flesh do not dread the luxury of the belly and the gullet: they fast, not that by self-restraint they may lessen their usual gluttony; but that by deferring it they may increase their immoderate greed. For when the time of refreshment comes, they rush to rich tables like beasts to mangers; with more numerous courses they overwhelm their hearts and distend their bellies; with artful and exotic varieties of seasonings they incite the gullet, lest it be restrained even by abundance. Finally, they take in by eating as much as they cannot digest by fasting.

**9. 11.** There are also those who so do not drink wine that, by the pressing of other fruits, they seek out for themselves other liquors, not for the sake of health, but of pleasantness: as though Lent were not an observance of pious humility, but an occasion for new pleasure. For how much more honorable, if the weakness of the stomach cannot bear to drink water, that it be sustained by usual and moderate wine, than that wines be sought which know no vintage, which knew no winepresses; not that a cleaner drink may be chosen, but that a more frugal one may be disapproved? But what is more absurd than, at a time when the flesh is to be more strictly chastised, to procure such great sweetnesses for the flesh, that the very desire of the gullet would not want Lent to pass by? What is more unfitting than, on the days of humility, when the food of the poor is to be imitated by all, so to live, that, if one lived so the whole time, the patrimonies of the rich could scarcely sustain it? Beware of these things, most beloved: think on what is written: *Do not go after your desires*[^45]. If this most wholesome precept must be observed at all times, how much more on these days, when it is so base if our cupidity be loosed to unwonted enticements, that he is deservedly blamed who has not restrained the usual ones?

**10. 12.** Especially, indeed, be mindful of the poor, that what you withdraw from yourselves by living more sparingly, you may lay up in the heavenly treasure[^46]. Let the hungering Christ receive what the fasting Christian receives less. Let the chastisement of the willing be the sustenance of the one who has not. Let the voluntary want of the abundant be the necessary abundance of the needy. Let there also be, in a placable and humble mind, a merciful readiness to forgive. Let him ask pardon who did the injury: let him give pardon who received the injury: that we may not be possessed by Satan, whose triumph is the dissension of Christians. For this too is an alms of great gain, to release a debt to a fellow servant, that it may be released to you by the Lord[^47]. Both good works the heavenly master commended to his disciples, saying: *Forgive, and it will be forgiven you; give, and it will be given you*[^48]. Be mindful of that servant, to whom his lord again charged all the debt he had forgiven, because he himself did not repay to a fellow servant owing him a hundred denarii the mercy which he had received for the ten thousand talents he owed. In this kind of good work there is no excuse, where the will alone is the whole ability. Anyone can say: Lest my stomach ache, I cannot fast. He can also say: I want to give to the poor; but I have not whence; or I have only so much that I fear to be in want, if I should give. Although even in these works men for the most part make false excuses for themselves, because they do not find true ones. Yet who is there who would say: I did not forgive the one asking pardon, because health hindered me, or because there was no hand with which I might offer it? Forgive, that it may be forgiven you. Here there is no work of the flesh, no member even of one's own flesh is taken up into the aid of the soul, that this which is asked may be fulfilled. It is done by the will, it is accomplished by the will. Do it securely, give securely, you will feel no pain in the body, you will have nothing less in the house. But now, brothers, see what an evil it is, not to forgive a repentant brother, when one is commanded to love even an enemy[^49]. Since these things are so, since it is written: *Let not the sun go down upon your anger*[^50]; consider, dearest ones, whether he is to be called a Christian, who at least on these days is unwilling to end enmities which he ought never to have carried on.
