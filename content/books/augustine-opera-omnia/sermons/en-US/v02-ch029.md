# Sermon 41

**1** When the divine Scriptures were read, since we cannot now speak of all their sentences, I noted one little sentence, briefest in the number of words but most ample in the weight of its meaning. From it I chose, with the Lord's help, to serve this great expectation of your Charity, according to the mediocrity of our strength, and to minister to you from the Lord's storehouse whence I too live with you. This, then, is the sentence of which I speak: *Keep faith with your neighbor in his poverty, that you may also enjoy his goods*[^1]. Let us take this first as it seems to sound plainly, as all can understand it, even those who search out none of the more secret things of the divine Scriptures. *Keep faith*, it says, *with your neighbor in his poverty, that you may also enjoy his goods*. "It is true," says the one who has heard it plainly, "when a friend is poor, faith is not to be broken with him; one must abide with him, nor must friendship be changed when his means are changed, but faith must be kept, the will being firm. For my friend, if he was a friend while he was rich, when he is poor, is he not a friend? Was it not he who was my friend, but his gold? But if my friend was the man himself, then both while the gold remained and when the gold departed, he is the one he was. Why, then, should he now not be a friend, who, even if he has lost his treasure, has not lost his soul? If I bought a horse, with its ornaments and saddle removed, I would perhaps not despise it bare. And did my friend please me clothed, and does he displease me stripped? Well, then, the divine Scripture commands, altogether salutarily and most fittingly to the morals of men: *Keep faith with your neighbor in his poverty*."

**2** *That you may also enjoy his goods*[^2]. What then? Shall we approach the latter part of this sentence and have such a thought about our friend that we say: "We must abide with him and keep faith with him in his poverty, that we may also enjoy his goods"? For the one who is now poor will be rich, and—he whose poverty you proudly disdained before—he will not admit you to his riches. Keep faith with him, then, even when he is poor, that you may also enjoy his goods when riches accrue to him, and may rejoice with him in them. Keep faith with him. He is poor, but he has a great possession—faith. You who were preparing to possess land with him, and wished to possess it if he had land for you to possess together, how much more firmly do you possess faith with him? For your friend is perhaps such that some wicked man could take away his possession. Will he be able to take away faith? What is it, then, *that you may also enjoy his goods*? Surely because from poor he may become rich, and you will enjoy the riches of the one whose poverty you did not spurn.

**3** The former part of this sentence, according to this popular understanding, pleases me. But the latter, I confess to you, offends me. For if you abide with a friend in his poverty for this reason, that when he is rich you may enjoy his riches, you do not yet love your friend, but something else in your friend. Faith and hope are two good friends, and greater than these is love. These abide in the divine things: *these three abide, faith, hope, love. But the greatest of them is love*[^3]. *Pursue love*[^4]. I ask, then, a friend about his friend: "Tell me, I beg you, do you keep faith with this man in his poverty?" "Plainly," he says, "for I heard this in the holy Scripture, and committed it to my heart and stored it in my memory. I gladly recall it, more gladly do it. For I heard the holy word: *Keep faith with your neighbor in his poverty*." And I: "Why do you do this? Is it for the sake of what follows: *that you may also enjoy his goods*? What, then, do you expect?" "That, when he is rich," he says, "and goods accrue to him, he may admit me to his goods, because I did not disdain his evils." "Then bear with me as I ask a little more. What if this man, with whom poor you keep faith, will never be rich? What if he is to be poor until death? Does faith perish, because hope was deceived? Because you cannot possess gold with the rich man, will you regret having possessed faith with the poor one?" If he savors the human—rather, if he savors the true—he will be disturbed by this question and will say to me: "You speak the truth. It is a good thing to keep faith with a neighbor. But if faith is kept with a poor neighbor in the hope that we may come to his riches, that we may have a share in them with him, without doubt, when this poor man has died, the hoped-for riches not accruing, we will regret all that good, and what we milked well we pour out badly." You see, then, that this sentence must be searched out more deeply, not as it can be understood commonly, but as it was founded by divine authority, to insinuate, prescribe, command us something great, where our hope is not deceived, lest we regret having kept faith. For to that meaning you will not be able to come thus.

**4** Attend, then, to that poor Lazarus lying before the rich man's gate. This poor man was miserably sick, nor did he even keep his bodily health, which is the patrimony of the poor. He was ulcerous too, the dogs licked his sores. But in that house was a rich man. He was clothed in purple and fine linen, he feasted splendidly every day, and refused to keep faith with the poor man. Rightly the Lord Jesus, the lover and giver of faith, attended more to that very faith in the poor man than to gold and delights in the rich. He attended more to the poor man's possession than to the rich man's elation. For this reason he named that poor man; but the other's name he judged should be passed over in silence. *There was*, he says, *a certain rich man who was clothed in purple and fine linen, and feasted splendidly every day. But there was a certain poor man named Lazarus*[^5]. Does it not seem to you that he recited from a book, where he found the poor man's name written, the rich man's he did not find? For that book was of the living and the just, not of the proud and the unjust. The rich man was named by men, the poor man passed over in silence. On the contrary, the Lord named the poor man, passed over the rich man in silence. That rich man, then, refused to keep faith with the poor man. Both died. *It happened that the needy man died, and was carried by the angels into Abraham's bosom. The rich man died, and was buried*[^6]—for perhaps he was not even buried—*and when, in the underworld*, as we read, *he was in torments, he lifted up his eyes from afar, and saw the poor man in Abraham's bosom*[^7], whom he had despised before his gate. He could not have rest in common with the one with whom he refused to have faith in common. *Father Abraham*, he said, *send Lazarus, that he may dip his finger in water, and drop it on my tongue, because I am tormented in this flame*[^8]. It was answered: *Remember, son, that you received your good things in your life, but Lazarus evil things. Now, then, he rests here, but you are tormented. And besides all this, there is a great chasm between us and you, and no one can pass over from us to you, nor cross over from you to us*[^9]. He saw mercy denied to himself because he had denied it. He saw how true it is: *Judgment without mercy to him who has not done mercy*[^10]. And he who refused in his own time to have mercy on the poor man had mercy, too late, on his brothers. *Send, then*, he said, *Lazarus. I have there five brothers. Let him tell them what is done here, lest they too come into this place of torments*[^11]. And against this it was answered: If they do not want to come into this place of torments, *they have there Moses and the Prophets, let them hear them*[^12]. That man, who used to mock the Prophets, surely together with his brothers—for I believe, rather I do not doubt, that, speaking with those very brothers of his about the Prophets warning of good things, forbidding evil things, terrifying about future torments and promising future rewards, he mocked all these, saying with his brothers: "What life after death? What memory of rottenness? What sense in ashes? All are carried off there, and buried. Who has been heard to return from there?"—recalling these words of his, he therefore wanted Lazarus to return to his brothers, that they might no longer say: "Who has returned from there?" And to this it was aptly and worthily answered. For this man seems to have been a Jew, and therefore to have said, *Father Abraham*. Therefore most fitly and aptly it was answered: *If they do not hear Moses and the Prophets, neither will they be persuaded if someone rises from the dead*[^13]. It was fulfilled in the Jews, that they did not hear Moses and the Prophets, nor believed Christ rising again. Had he not foretold them this before: *If you believed Moses, you would believe me too*[^14]?

**5** That rich man, then, remained without help, his temporal delights finished, in eternal punishments. He did not do just things, he heard fitting words: *Remember, son, that you received your good things in your life*[^15]. This life, then, which you see, is not yours. You received your good things. These things, then, which from afar, sighing, you desire, are not yours. Where are the words of the rich and of those who flatter the rich, when they see someone flowing with temporal delights, abounding on earth, snatching land to have it, and heaping up by dragging to himself the lead with which he is sunk? For a great weight led that rich man down to the underworld, and the heavy burden pressed him to the depths. For he had not heard: *Come to me, you who labor. My yoke is gentle, my burden is light*[^16]. The burden of Christ is wings. With these wings that poor man flew into Abraham's bosom. The rich man, then, refused to hear these things. For he heard the tongues of flatterers. By these tongues he grew deaf against the Prophets, the tongues of those who praise wrongly and say: "You alone are, you alone live." Therefore: *You received your good things in your life*. For you esteemed these your good things. You did not believe in, did not hope for, others. You received those in your life. For you thought that life alone was yours. When you hoped for nothing to come after death, you feared nothing sad. *You received, then, your good things in your life, but Lazarus evil things*. He did not say his own, but evil things—the things men think evil, which men fear, which men avoid as something great. Lazarus received these evil things. His good things he did not then receive. Yet he did not lose them. But just as it was not heard, "his own," so neither was this heard, "in his life." For his life was another, which he hoped for in Abraham's bosom. For here he was dead, here he did not live. He was dead by that death of which the Apostle speaks: *For you have died, and your life is hidden with Christ in God*[^17]. The poor man bore temporal evils. But God deferred his good things, he did not take them away. Why, then, rich man, in the underworld do you, too late, desire what, while you enjoyed your delights, you did not hope for? Are you not the one who despised him lying before your gate? Are you not the one who, in despising the poor man, mocked Moses and the Prophets? You refused, then, to keep faith with your neighbor in his poverty; now you do not enjoy his goods. For you mocked when you heard: *Keep faith with your neighbor in his poverty, that you may also enjoy his goods*. Now you see his goods far off, and you do not possess them with him. For those goods were to come, to come invisibly. When they were not seen, they were to be believed, lest, when they are seen, there remain for you to grieve and not be able to hold them.

**6** Therefore, brothers, as far as it seems to me, this sentence has been refined out. For it must be understood by Christians in a Christian way. Let us not so keep faith with a poor neighbor that we hope for riches to come to him temporally and keep our faith in order to possess them with him. Not so at all, not so. But how, except according to the precept of our Lord: *Make for yourselves friends of the mammon of iniquity, that they too may receive you into the eternal dwellings*[^18]? For here there are poor men not having dwellings into which they may receive you. Make them your friends of the mammon of iniquity—that is, of gains which iniquity calls gains. For there are gains which justice calls gains: these are in the treasures of God. Do not despise the poor, who have nowhere to return, nowhere to enter. They have dwellings, and eternal ones. They have somewhere where you will wish in vain to be received, like that rich man, if you do not now receive them into yours, because *he who receives a just man in the name of a just man will receive a just man's reward; he who receives a prophet in the name of a prophet will receive a prophet's reward; and whoever gives to one of the least of mine a cup of cold water only in the name of a disciple, amen I say to you, he will not lose his reward*[^19]. For he keeps faith with his neighbor in his poverty, and therefore will enjoy his goods.

**7** Your Lord too says to you, your very Lord, the one who, though he was rich, was made poor. He will expound to you this sentence better and more solidly. For perhaps about that poor man whom you received into your house your soul sometimes doubts and hesitates, whether he is a truthful man, or perhaps a deceitful pretender, a hypocrite. The soul wavers in doing mercy, since you cannot look into the heart. If, then, you cannot look in, do it even with the evil man, that you may come also to the good. He who feared lest his seeds fall on the paths, on the thorns, on the stones, slothful to sow in winter, hungered in summer. Yet your Lord says to you, of whom surely you do not doubt if you are a Christian: "I was made poor for your sake, though I was rich." For when *he was in the form of God*—what is richer than that form?—*he did not count it robbery to be equal to God, but emptied himself, taking the form of a servant*—what is richer than the form of God? what is poorer than the form of a servant?—*made in the likeness of men, found in habit as a man, he humbled himself, made obedient unto death, even the death of the cross*[^20]. Add still more. He thirsted on the cross, he received drink, not from one having mercy but from one insulting, and the fountain of life drank vinegar in death. Do not spurn it, do not despise it, do not say: "So my God was made man? So my God was killed, was crucified?" Yes. Plainly, so, he was crucified. His poverty is commended to you. He was far from you. He drew near to you by poverty. *Keep faith with your neighbor in his poverty*. Surely here that sentence nowhere wavers, nowhere grows dim. In place of the name "neighbor," take the name of Christ, and take it humbly. For, humble, you suit the humble one; you grasp the lofty as humble. Take it humbly and understand the neighbor—for *the Lord is near to those who have crushed their heart*[^21]—that you may say in your prayer: *As a neighbor, as our brother, so I was well-pleasing*[^22]. So that one word which the prophet added, naming the neighbor—for prophetic speech had to be covered a little by the veil of a sacred sign, that it might be sought more longingly, found more sweetly—where, then, he named the neighbor, do you name Christ, because that prophet too named Christ thus prophetically, and see that liquid sentence flowing and, as from the fountain of truth, watering your thirst. Keep faith with Christ in his poverty, that you may also enjoy his goods. What is it to keep faith with Christ? In that he was made man for your sake, in that he was born of a Virgin, in that he received insults, that he was scourged, that he was hung on the wood, that he was wounded by the spear, that he was buried—do not spurn these things, let them not seem incredible to you, and so you keep faith with your neighbor. For this is his poverty. But what is it, *that you may also enjoy his goods*? Hear that this is what he willed, hear that for this reason he came to you in poverty, hear the voice of the poor man, of your Lord God for your sake enriching you, see how you may enjoy his goods if you keep faith with him in his poverty: *Father*, he says, *I want that where I am, they too may be with me*[^23].
