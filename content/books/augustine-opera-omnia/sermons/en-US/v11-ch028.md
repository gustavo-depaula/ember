# Sermon 20/B: (Dolbeau 28)

**1.** {I do not suffice to give thanks to the Lord God and to your love before him, for this rejoicing which I see flowing from the fount of love. For this is what refreshes us, brethren, what consoles us: pure and sincere love, which helps me before the Lord who deigned to restore our voice to your ears. Nor wonder that we suffer such things in this body. For it is necessary that we suffer, nor can the will of the Lord be in anything unjust: because both we are sinners and ought to be scourged, and if we were just, we ought to be proved. For he who wishes for himself health of body in which the inhabitant of the body, the mind, makes no progress, wishes something vain. But God does not attend to what the error of the one wishing asks, but to what the mercy of the one freeing bestows.} For the Apostle says that *what we should pray for as we ought, we do not know, but the Spirit himself,* he says, *asks for us with unspeakable groanings*[^1]. For sometimes we wish for ourselves what God knows to be adverse to us; and then he has mercy, if he does not hear us, or rather, in a manner it is more truly said: then he hears, if he seems not to hear.

**2.** For who of us, brethren, can be matched with the merits of the apostle Paul, of whose praises there is no need that we say anything? For he who is daily read is in vain praised by anyone. Because neither did he himself ever wish himself as it were to be praised, when he said of his conversion, that the churches which were in Christ from among the Jews, that is, those who had believed in Christ, heard and marveled that he was converted, he did not say: "They magnified me," but: *In me they magnified the Lord*[^2]. Likewise when he said much of his own efficacy: *Not I,* he says, *but the grace of God with me*[^3]. Who, then, can be matched either with his humility or his piety or his teaching or his labors or his tribulations or his merits or his crown? He, then, twice asked of the Lord that the sting of the flesh be taken from him, and it was not taken away; and yet the Lord says to the soul serving well: *While you are still speaking I will say: Behold, here I am*[^4], that he might grant what it asks. Who of us dares to promise himself what was denied to Paul? What, then, do we say—that God was not present, when the Apostle says: *There was given to me a sting of my flesh, an angel of Satan, to buffet me*—lest he be exalted; *on account of which I asked the Lord three times that he take it from me, and he said to me: My grace suffices you; for strength is perfected in weakness*[^5]? Was he, then, not present? Did he not say: *Behold, here I am,* when he taught why he would not give it?

**3.** God always hears, dearly beloved brethren—know this that you may ask with certainty—God hears, when he does not give what we ask. God hears, and, if we ask something useless not knowing it, by not giving he rather hears, who, by giving to certain ones what is to be punished, rather does not hear. This we say. Sometimes a faithful man asks of God, as it is pious to entreat the Lord, but does not receive specifically what he asks, but receives that on account of which he asks this. Sometimes an impious man, a perverse man, a criminal man asks something, and it is given him, because he was worthy of condemnation, not of being heard. We have, then, the example of the apostle Paul, because he asked, and it was not given him, but it is shown that that was given him on account of which he was asking this. Because, whatever a Christian and faithful man asks, he ought to ask for the sake of the kingdom of heaven, for the sake of eternal life, for the sake of that which God promised and will give after this world, for this reason he ought to ask, who asks anything, for the sake of that perfect health which we shall have even in the resurrection of the body. For then there will be perfect health, when death is swallowed up in victory. Now, then, because we have named health and eternal salvation for the sake of which one ought to ask, whoever asks anything even temporally . . . , let us take daily examples from that physician.

**4.** When a sick man asks of the physician what delights for a time—and he summoned the physician for this reason, that health might be granted him through him. For there was no other cause for summoning the physician, but that he might obtain health. And therefore, if perhaps fruits delight him, if cold things delight him, he chooses to ask the physician rather than his own servant. For he can, to the ruin of his own health, hide it from the physician and ask the servant: the servant indeed obeys his commanding master at the nod of dominion rather than for the remedy of health. But the prudent sick man, who loves and awaits his own health, chooses to ask the physician this very thing which delights him for a time, so that, the physician refusing, he may not receive something for his pleasure, but rather trust him for his health. You see, then, that even when the physician does not give to the one asking something, he does not give for this reason, that he may give. For he does not bestow the immoderate pleasure, that he may bestow useful health. To this one, then, by not giving he gives, and rather gives that on account of which he was summoned, that is, health. If, then, to this one by not giving he gives, to the one importunately extorting by giving he does not give; but very often even to the despaired-of he gives what he asks. But sometimes he so gives that by the stings of pain the sick man may become more corrected and may learn to trust the physician; but sometimes, despairing of his health, as physicians are wont to say: "Now give him whatever he asks: for there is no hope of his health." Therefore, for these three asking, let us find examples in the Scriptures.

**5.** Someone asks, and does not receive what he asks. Let us find the example of the apostle Paul. For the physician told him too why he would not give: *Strength,* he says, *is perfected in weakness.* Be secure: what he does not give you, he wishes to heal you. Leave it to the physician: he knows what to apply, what to withdraw, that he may lead to health, and therefore he does this. The Apostle asks the Lord three times. He was now being heard, but he did not think himself heard, unless the physician were present in such a way as even to say: *Strength is perfected in weakness,* so that he too, secure, might now say: *When I am weak, then I am strong*[^6]. We have, then, this one asking for something useless, I know not what, and not receiving it, that he might receive that on account of which he was asking it, that is, on account of eternal salvation.

**6.** Let us now see whether men receive anything for admonition, that, having suffered some tribulations from it, they may at some time return to medicine, because they are sick. Whence the Lord: *The healthy have no need,* he says, *of a physician, but those who are ill*[^7]. He came, then, to the sick and found men given over to their own concupiscences. For the sake of tribulation, *God handed them over,* says the Apostle, *to the concupiscences of their heart, to do the things that are not fitting*[^8]. They coveted the things that are not fitting, and it was given them to be able to do them; thence they came to greater pains, to those disturbances which all the unjust, all the wicked necessarily suffer: of fear, of desire, of error, of grief, of affliction, of solicitude. Never security, never rest, never a friend. In their own conscience they rest a little, seeking outside another consoler, having within themselves a tormentor. It is necessary that the unjust justly suffer, but, when they suffer, they also live. God attends, who grants these things for the seeking of medicine, that, having suffered the afflictions and entanglements of their own concupiscences, they may learn what they should ask. For to them it was said: *Do you not know that the patience of God leads you to repentance?*[^9] He handed them over to the concupiscences of their heart, and they did what they wished; and yet he spared them, lest he take them from this life, where there is still a place for repentance, and he always invited them to repentance, as he does now too, and never will he cease to do this with the human race even to the last day of judgment.

**7.** Let us, then, find also that one to whom he grants for this reason, that he is now despaired of, and this from the Scriptures. What is more despaired of than the devil? Yet he asks to tempt Job, and it is not denied him. Great mysteries, a great matter indeed, and most worthy of consideration. The Apostle asks that the sting of the flesh be taken from him, and it is not granted; the devil asks to tempt a just man, and it is granted. But that the just man was granted to be tempted by the devil neither harmed the just man nor profited the devil, because both the one was proved and the other tortured.

**8.** Hold this surely, brethren, which we have often said to your holiness, lest the cares of the world take from your hearts what you have heard: when God grants the just to be tempted, that they may be proved, or to be scourged, if he wishes to correct them on account of the sins that remain, if he grants them to be scourged on account of sins, it profits them; but if it is that they may be made manifest, because they were unknown, it profits those to whom they become known, that they may imitate them. For God knows his servants well, but sometimes they are unknown to the rest and cannot be made manifest to them except through some temptations. Sometimes even a man is unknown to himself and altogether does not know what he can do: either he thinks he can do more, and it is shown him that he cannot yet do it, or he despairs and thinks himself unable to endure I know not what, and it is shown him that he can; when he is immoderately exalted, let him be brought down to humility, and when he is abjectly broken, let him be raised from despair.

**9.** Therefore let us understand in that very psalm which we were singing, that {many ask for health, and sometimes it does not profit them. For one is healthy and abuses his health unto sin. It would be better for him to be sick that he might rest, than to be healed unto restlessness. But sometimes from the scourge of tribulation, when there comes about what he did not wish, he is converted to God. For he will be more cautious, will be more chaste, will be more modest, will be more humble, and rightly he sings: *Give us help from tribulation, and vain is the salvation of man*.} He sought help, and whence help? He says to the Lord: "*Give us help from tribulation,* that, troubled, we may be corrected, may be converted to you humbled, may not raise the neck against you. For when you have given us *help from tribulation,* we shall understand that {*vain* is the *salvation* which often the foolish man was wishing for and, when he had received it, uses not for the joy of quiet, but for the occasion of his own restlessness."} Very often a man was about to go forth, angry with unjust indignation, about to harm one who perhaps had done him no evil; suddenly he begins to be sick: what was useful to him? to go forth and do iniquity, or to be sick and entreat for health? For not vain is the salvation of God, but *vain is the salvation of man,* which a man thinks as it were necessary to him as a great thing. Not vain, then, is the salvation of God, but *vain is the salvation of man.* It is a false salvation and is rightly called *of man,* if this alone is thought by man to be it. For there, where *of man* is not added, it is said: *Salvation is the Lord's*[^10]. What is *Salvation is the Lord's?* The Lord gives salvation, who knows what to give, when, to whom to give. As often as men ask for salvation as it were despaired of, the Lord himself gives it. There follows in the psalm: *And upon your people your blessing*[^11], that is, only have mercy on your people, and give them the salvation which you give to those who are not of your people, even that which your people itself does not know. For you know what you give; he does not know what he receives, except when he has received it. For what is it, brethren, that you may know these to be the things you are to receive, *which neither eye has seen nor ear heard, nor has it ascended into the heart of man, what God has prepared for those who love him?*[^12] What do you think he *has prepared?* Eternal salvation surely, which can neither ascend into our heart, which neither the eye can see nor the ear hear; and yet he prepares it for those who love him, and, when we have received it, we shall see what is the true salvation and how vain were the things we thought a great thing.

**10.** For the martyrs, if they had wished for and held as a great thing this salvation, that is, the salvation of man, would not say from the heart: *And the day of man I have not coveted: you know*[^13]. What is said in the psalm? Therefore, wishing for this salvation and holding it as a great thing, they would have lost that eternal one. But now, understanding what it is: *Give us help from tribulation,* they choose rather to be led to eternal salvation, than to find this salvation of man chosen unto ruin and to consent to the persecutors. For at once that persecutor gave salvation. The martyr was bound, was pressed in prison fettered, was even wasting away with his own wounds: had he yielded to the persecutor, he would at once have had salvation; but *vain is the salvation of man.* The persecutors indeed promised salvation, which they at once gave. And what kind did they give? Such as they knew, even before they were in those tribulations, but they stretched themselves to that which *neither eye has seen nor ear heard, nor has it ascended into the heart of man.* What the persecutor promises is seen, and what he promises is uncertain and brief and slight. Even if there were an eternal salvation of the flesh, it would yet be carnal, would be such as the eye has seen and the ear heard, and has ascended into the heart of man. If that greater salvation is not seen, but he who promises is sure to follow and cannot deceive, let us hold ourselves under his discipline, let us not murmur under his scourge, let us willingly suffer the one curing us, and we shall rejoice healthy to God, now knowing what he has given us and saying: *Where, O death, is your strife? Where, O death, is your sting?*[^14]

**11.** {I know, brethren, your eagerness, but it is needful that you too spare our weakness. For we do not wish to deny our voice, such as it is, and our ministry to your holiness, that we may serve the Lord who restored us. Yet the fresher scar must still be spared, perhaps not yet perfectly formed and closed. May the Lord order us in his will, fit us to the salvation of us all and to the service of his holy Church.} Turned to the Lord let us ask: he will regard us and perfect us in the Word his Savior, and grant us to rejoice according to him and to live according to him. May he turn away from us the prudence of the flesh; may he make the enemy subject under our feet, not by our strength, but by his holy name, in which we have been cleansed through Jesus Christ our Lord.
