# Sermon 361: On the Resurrection of the Dead

**1. 1.** We noticed, when the Apostle's Letter was being read, the praiseworthy movement of your faith and love, how you shuddered at men who, thinking this to be the only life, which we have in common with the beasts, and that after death the whole of what belongs to man is ended, and that there is no hope of any other better life, with the itch of evil ears corrupting, say: *Let us eat and drink; for tomorrow we die*[^1]. From here, then, let the beginning of our disputation be taken, and let this be as it were the hinge of our sermon, to which the rest of the things which the Lord shall deign to suggest may be referred.

**2. 2.** For our hope is the resurrection of the dead; our faith is the resurrection of the dead. Our love also is that which the proclamation of things not yet seen inflames, and kindles with desire, by the greatness of which our hearts are made capable of the blessedness which is promised to come, as long as what is not yet seen is believed: therefore our love itself too ought not to be occupied about these temporal and visible things, so that we should hope to have such a thing in the resurrection, as that which now, if we despise it, we live better and are better, namely carnal pleasures and delights. And so, with faith in the resurrection of the dead taken away, all Christian doctrine collapses. But with faith in the resurrection of the dead founded, there is not at once security about the Christian mind, unless that life which is to come is distinguished from this which passes away. And so it must be set forth thus: If the dead will not rise, there is no hope for us of a future life; but if the dead will rise, there will indeed be a future life; but the second question is, what it will be like. The first disputation, then, is whether there will be a resurrection of the dead; but the second disputation is what the life of the saints will be like in the resurrection.

**3. 3.** Those, then, who say that the dead do not rise, are not Christians: but those who think that the dead, when they have risen, will live carnally, are carnal Christians. Whatever disputation, then, there is against the opinion of those who deny the resurrection of the dead, is against those who are outside, of whose number I think no one is here present. Whence our discussion can seem superfluous, if it should linger, that we should try to teach that the dead rise. For by the weight of authority the Christian must be led, who has already believed Christ, who in no way thinks the Apostle lies. It is enough, then, for this one to hear: *If the dead do not rise, vain is our preaching, vain too is your faith*[^2]. *If the dead*, he says, *do not rise, neither did Christ rise*[^3]. But if Christ rose, in whom is the salvation of Christians, then surely it is not impossible for the dead to rise: since he who raised his Son, and he who raised his own flesh, demonstrated in the head an example to the rest of the body, which is the Church. The disputation about the resurrection of the dead could, then, be superfluous, so that we might now take up that one which Christians are wont to have among themselves: when we have risen, what we shall be like, how we shall live, what our occupations will be, whether there will be any or none: if there will be none, we shall live idly, doing nothing; or if we shall do something, what we shall do: then, whether we shall eat and drink, whether there will be unions of males and females, or some simple and incorrupt common life; and if it is so, what that life will be like, what its movement, what the figure of the bodies themselves. These are the disputations of Christians, with faith in the resurrection safe.

**4. 4.** To this disputation, then, as much as it can either be undertaken or brought forth by men for men, such as either we are or you are, I would now and now pass over, did not a certain anxiety about our too carnal and almost pagan brothers compel me to linger a little in that question where it is asked whether the dead rise at all. For I think no one here is now a pagan, but all are Christians. But the pagans and mockers of the resurrection do not cease daily to murmur in the ears of Christians: *Let us eat and drink; for tomorrow we die*[^4]. And what the Apostle said, bringing in his anxiety and subjoining to this sentence: *Evil conversations corrupt good morals*[^5]; fearing these evils and bearing anxiety for the weak, not only with a fatherly but even with a certain motherly love, we will say something even on this matter, as much perhaps as may suffice for Christians, because today a greater devotion toward the Scriptures has brought all these who have gathered. For no solemnity of any feast day has called even theatrical crowds to the church of God. For certain ones are wont to run together not out of piety, but out of solemnity. This consideration made us first speak about the resurrection of the dead; and then, if the Lord gives the means, what the future life of the just will afterward be like.

**5. 5.** *I fear*, says the Apostle, *lest, as the serpent seduced Eve by his cunning, so also your minds be corrupted away from the chastity that is in Christ*[^6]. But the minds of these are corrupted by those conversations: *Let us eat and drink; for tomorrow we die.* Those who love these things, who pursue these things, who think this to be the only life, who hope nothing for the rest, who either do not ask God, or ask him for this, to whom a sermon of diligence is burdensome, let them hear us saying these things with great sadness. They wish to eat and drink; for tomorrow they die. Would that they truly considered that they are going to die tomorrow. For who is so mad and perverse, who so an enemy of his own soul, that, going to die the next day, he would not consider all the things for which he labors to be ended? For so it is written: *In that day all his thoughts shall perish*[^7]. Even if men, on account of those whom they leave here, bear the care of a testament when the day of their death is imminent; how much more ought they to consider something about their soul? Man considers whom he leaves behind, and does he not consider himself who leaves all these things? Behold, your children will have what you leave, you will have nothing, and your whole thought is consumed in that by which sojourners may pass on after you, not in that whither those passing on may arrive. Would, then, that there were thought about death. But when the dead are carried out, death is thought of, and it is said: Alas, the wretched man! such he was, yesterday he was walking; or: seven days ago I saw him, this and that he spoke with me; man is nothing. These things they murmur. But perhaps when the dead man is lamented, when the funeral is cared for, when the obsequies are prepared, when he is carried out, when they go, when he is buried, this discourse thrives: but with the dead man buried, such a thought too is buried. Those deadly cares return, he forgets whom he has escorted, he thinks of succession who is going to depart; he returns to frauds, to robberies, to perjuries, to wine-bibbing, to the infinite pleasures of the body, not, I say, going to perish when they have been exhausted, but perishing when they are being drunk in; and, what is more pernicious, from the buried dead man an argument for burying the heart is taken, and it is said: *Let us eat and drink; for tomorrow we die.*

**6. 6.** They mock too the faith of those who assert that the dead will rise, saying to themselves: Behold, this one is placed in the tomb, let his voice be heard. But his cannot be: I shall hear the voice of my father, of my grandfather, of my great-grandfather. Who has risen from there? Who has reported what is done among the dead? Let us do well for ourselves while we live: but when we are dead, even if our parents, or dear ones, or kinsfolk bring things to our tombs, they will bring them for themselves who live, not for us dead. And these things indeed Scripture too mocked, saying of certain ones not perceiving present goods: *As if*, it says, *you should set feasts around a dead man*[^8]; and it is manifest that this does not pertain to the dead, and that this is the custom of the pagans, not coming from that stock and vein of justice of our fathers the Patriarchs, for whom we read that obsequies were celebrated, but we do not read that funeral feasts were made. This too can be noticed in the morals of the Jews: for they did not retain from there the fruit of virtue, but yet they retained in certain solemnities the oldness of custom. And what certain ones object from the Scriptures: *Break your bread, and pour out your wine over the tombs of the just, and do not give it to the unjust*[^9]; about this indeed there is no need to discourse, but yet I say that the faithful can understand what was said. For how the faithful do these things religiously toward the memorials of their own is known to the faithful; and that these things are not to be displayed to the unjust, that is, to unbelievers, because *the just man lives by faith*[^10], this too is known to the faithful. Let no one, then, seek the wound from the medicine, and try to twist from the Scriptures a cord by which to throw the noose of death upon his own soul. It is manifest how that is to be understood, and open and wholesome is this celebration of Christians.

**7. 7.** Let us see, then, that which, as I had begun to say, is on account of men murmuring in the ears of the weak: *Let us eat and drink; for tomorrow we die*; because they say: No one has risen from there, I have not heard the voice of anyone, from the time my grandfather was placed there, from the time my great-great-grandfather, from the time my father; the voice of no one have I heard. Answer, Christians, if you are Christians: lest perhaps, when you wish to be inebriated among the peoples, it irks you to answer the corrupters. You have what you may answer; but you waver with the desire of pleasures, and you wish to be gorged and buried alive. The greed of drunkenness rises, and as it were a certain wave rushes into the soul, drawn by the breath of the one persuading evil. You suffer, then, a great storm, you are unwilling to answer the corrupter, while you favor the toaster; but the wave of concupiscence is raised too high, and wishes to overwhelm your heart as a ship. O Christian, Christ sleeps in your ship: rouse him; he will command the storms to be all calmed[^11]. For at that time, when the disciples in the ship were tossed with Christ sleeping, they signified that Christians are tossed when the Christian faith is sleeping. For you see what the Apostle says: *That Christ dwell*, he says, *through faith in your hearts*[^12]. For according to the presence of his beauty and divinity he is always with the Father; according to bodily presence he is now above the heavens at the right hand of the Father; but according to the presence of faith he is in all Christians. And therefore, then, you are tossed, because Christ sleeps: that is, therefore you do not overcome those concupiscences, which are stirred up by the breaths of those persuading evil, because faith sleeps. What is, faith sleeps? It is lulled to sleep. What is, it is lulled to sleep? You have forgotten it. What, then, is to rouse Christ? To rouse faith, to remember what you have believed. Therefore recall your faith, rouse Christ: your very faith will command the waves, by which you are disturbed, and the winds of those persuading perversely: at once they will depart, at once all will grow quiet; because even if the evil persuader does not cease to speak, he now does not move the ship, does not stir up a wave, does not sink the vehicle by which you are carried.

**7. 8.** What, then, do you do, rousing Christ? What had that evil discourser said to you? What had that corrupter said, corrupting good morals by evil conversations? what had he said? Surely he had said this: No one has returned from there, I have not heard my father, nor my grandfather; let someone return from there, let him say what is done there.

**8. 8.** You, with Christ now roused in your ship, recalling your faith, answer him, and say: Fool, if your father rose again, you would believe; the Lord of all rose again, and you do not believe? For from where did he so wish to die and rise again, except that we might all believe in one, lest we be deceived by many? And what would your father do, if he rose again and spoke, going to die again? Attend with how great power he rose again, who now does not die, and death will no longer have dominion over him[^13]. He showed himself to his disciples and faithful ones: the solidity of his body was handled, since it was too little for certain ones to see what they remembered, unless they also touched what they saw. Faith was confirmed, not only in the hearts, but even in the eyes of men. He who demonstrated these things ascended into heaven, sent the Holy Spirit to his disciples, the Gospel was preached. If we lie in these things, ask the whole world. Many things which were promised, have been done; many things which were hoped for, have been fulfilled; in the Christian faith the whole world thrives. Even those who have not yet believed in Christ do not dare to detract from the resurrection of Christ. Testimony in heaven, testimony on earth; testimony from the angels, testimony from the dead. What has remained, that does not cry out? And you say: *Let us eat and drink; for tomorrow we die!*

**8. 9.** But you have been made sad about your buried dear one, because you did not at once hear his voice. He was living, he has died; he was eating, he no longer eats; he was perceiving, he no longer perceives; he does not share in the joys and gladness of the living.

**9. 9.** Would you lament the seed, when you plowed? If, then, there were anyone so unskilled in things, that, when the seed is carried out into the field, and put into the earth, and the broken clod buried; if, then, there were anyone ignorant even of things almost about to come, who would lament the wheat, because he remembered the summer, and considered within himself, saying: This grain, which has just now been buried, with how great labor reaped, brought, threshed, cleansed, was being kept in the granary, we used to see its beauty, and rejoiced; now it has been taken from our eyes; I see the plowed earth, but the grain neither in the granary nor here do I behold: he would lament the grain as it were dead and buried mournfully, would weep abundantly, attending to the clods and the earth, but not seeing the harvest: how would he be mocked by any unlearned men, but in that matter not unlearned; unskilled indeed in other things, but skilled in that thing which that man, unbecomingly unskilled, lamented? And what would those who knew say to him, if perhaps he was lamenting for this reason, that he knew none of these things? Do not be sad; this which we have buried surely is now not in the granary, is not in our hands: we will come to this field, and it will delight you to see the appearance of the crop, where now you lament the bareness of the plowing. He who knew what would come of the grain would even rejoice in the very plowing: but that incredulous one or rather foolish, and, to say it more truly, inexperienced, would lament indeed perhaps at first, but believing the experienced one would depart consoled, and would hope for the future harvest along with the experienced one.

**10. 10.** But harvests are wont to be seen each year: but of the human race one last harvest will arise at the end of the age. It cannot now be shown to the eyes: but from one principal grain a proof has been given. The Lord himself says: *If the grain so remains, and is not put to death, it will remain alone*[^14], speaking of his own putting-to-death, because manifold will be the resurrection of those believing in him. An example was given from one grain, but such an example as all would believe who wished to be grains. Although every creature too speaks the resurrection, if we are not deaf: whence we ought to conjecture what God is going to do once with the human race at the end, since we see so many similar things daily. The resurrection of Christians will be once, the sleep of living beings and the daily waking is a thing of every day. To sleep is like death: to wake is like resurrection. From what is done daily, believe what is going to be once. The moon every month is born, grows, is perfected, lessens, is consumed, is renewed. What in the moon over months, this in the resurrection once in all time. As that which is of those sleeping daily, so that which is of the moon over single months. From where do the leaves go away from the trees, from where do they return? into what secret places do they depart, from what secret places do they come? It is winter, surely now the trees, like withered ones, in springtime grow green. Has this now been done for the first time, or was it so in the past year too? Indeed, in the past year too it was so. It was intercepted by autumn in winter, it returns through spring in summer. Therefore the year returns in time, and men, made to the image of God, when they have died will perish?

**11. 11.** But someone, looking less diligently at the changes and restorations of things, can say to me: Those leaves rotted, new ones are born. But one considering well sees that even those which rot pass into the strength of the earth. For from where is the earth made fat, except from the rottenness of earthly things? Those who cultivate the field attend to these things; and let those who do not cultivate it, because they always live in the city, learn at least from the gardens neighboring the city with what zeal the contemptible refuse of the city is kept, by whom even it is bought at a price, at what cost it is carried. Surely now contemptible, emptied of all usefulness, it could be thought by the inexperienced. And who deigns to look at dung? What man shudders to look at, he takes care to keep. That, then, which seemed now consumed and cast away, returns into the fatness of the earth, the fatness into the sap, the sap into the root; and what passes from the earth into the root, by invisible passages migrates into the trunk, is distributed through the branches, from the branches into the buds, from the bud into the fruits and leaves. Behold, what you shuddered at in the rottenness of dung, you marvel at in the fruitfulness and greenness of the tree.

**12. 12.** Do not now object to me what you are wont to object: The body of the buried dead man does not remain whole: for if it remained, I would believe the resurrection. Then do the Egyptians alone believe the resurrection, because they diligently care for the corpses of the dead? For they have the custom of drying bodies, and making them as it were of bronze: they call them gabbaras. Therefore, according to these people, who are ignorant of the secret recesses of nature, where all things are safe for the Creator, even when they are withdrawn from mortal senses, do the Egyptians alone well believe the resurrection of their dead, but the hope of other Christians is in a narrow place? For often, when tombs are opened or laid bare either by age or by some not sacrilegious necessity, bodies have been found to have rotted, and men, sighing, have groaned, who are wont to be delighted with bodily beauty, and have said in their hearts: Will this ash at some time have that appearance of beauty, will it be restored to life, restored to the light? When will this be? When may I hope for anything living from this ash? You who say this, see in the tomb either ash: turn back over your age, if you are, for instance, thirty, fifty, or more years old: in the tomb is either the ash of the dead man, you fifty years ago, what were you? where were you? The bodies of all of us, who now speak or hear, will after a few years be ash, a few years ago they were not even ash. He, then, who was able to prepare what was not, will he fail to repair what was?

**13. 13.** Let the murmurs of those who speak evil cease, then, and of those corrupting good morals by evil conversations. Fix your feet on the way, fix them: not so that you abandon the way, not so that you remain on the way, but as it was said: *So run, that you may grasp it*[^15]. Let Christ always thrive in the heart, who wished to show in the head what the other members may hope for. On earth indeed we labor, our head in heaven now neither dies, nor fails, nor suffers anything: yet it suffered for us. Because *he was handed over for our offenses, and rose for our justification*[^16]. This we know by faith: but those to whom he showed himself learned it through the eyes. Yet we are not rejected, because he rose again, and we could not see him with carnal eyes. We have for ourselves the testimony of the Lord himself, which he said to the doubting disciple seeking by touch what he might believe. For when he had cried out, convinced by the handling of the scars, and said: *My Lord, and my God*: he in answer: *Because you have seen me*, he said, *and have believed; blessed are those who do not see and believe*[^17]. Wake up, then, to your blessedness, let no one persuading evil shake out of your hearts what Christ has fixed in.

**13. 14.** Nor let that be said to me. For this is said by all, who now even unwilling have yielded to the authority of Christ. For almost all the pagans, even those who are unwilling or defer to lay hold of Christ with devotion, do not dare to reproach him: although they dare to reproach Christians, they do not dare to reproach Christ; they yield to the head, and still insult the body. But let the body, hearing the insults of those who now yield to the head, not think itself cut off from the head, but resting upon it. For if we were cut off, we ought to fear the voices of those insulting: but that we are not cut off, that very one attests, who said to Paul, still Saul persecuting the Church: *Saul, Saul, why do you persecute me?*[^18] He had now passed through the hands of the impiety of the Jews, had now penetrated the lower regions, had now risen from the tomb, had now ascended into heaven, had now enriched and strengthened the hearts of believers by the gift of the Holy Spirit, sitting at the right hand of the Father interceding for us: not going to be handed over to death again, but going to free us from death, what could he suffer from the raging Saul? From where did that hand touch him, although that one was, as it is written, *breathing slaughter*[^19]? Upon Christians laboring on the ground he could make an assault: but upon Christ, when and how? Yet he cries out for the rest of the members, and does not say: Why do you persecute my own? For if he said: Why do you persecute my own? we would believe them servants. But servants do not cling to a master so much as Christians to Christ. That joining is different: a different order of members, a different unity of love. The head speaks for the members, and he does not even say this: Why do you persecute my members? but he says: *Why do you persecute me?* He was not touching the head, but he was touching what is joined to the head.

**14. 14.** We have already often said it, but because the likeness is apt, and conveys the matter well, it must be repeated. He who treads on you in a crowd presses your foot, but does nothing to the tongue. What does it mean, then, that the tongue cries: You tread on me? It is a pressure of the foot, no injury of the tongue, but one joining. *And if one member suffers, all the members suffer with it; and if one member is glorified, all the members rejoice with it*[^20]. If, then, your tongue speaks for your foot, does Christ in heaven not speak for Christians? Your tongue, then, does not so speak for the foot as to say: You tread on my foot; but: You tread on me, though it itself was not touched. Recognize him as your head, when for you from heaven he speaks, and says: *Saul, Saul, why do you persecute me?* Therefore, brothers, why did we say this? Lest perhaps those creep up on you of whom the Apostle says: *Evil conversations corrupt good morals*; because they say: *Let us eat and drink; for tomorrow we die*; so as to say to you (for they do not dare to reproach Christ, they tremble at the authority of his majesty founded throughout the whole world; but, as it is written: *The sinner shall see, and shall be angry, he shall gnash with his teeth and waste away*[^21]: gnash he can, and waste away he can; but blaspheme Christ he does not dare); lest, then, perhaps they so speak and say: It was permitted only to Christ. For sometimes they say it even from the heart, but sometimes they say it from fear: but do you attend both to what they dare to say, and to what they do not dare.

**15. 15.** They are going to say, then: You tell me that Christ rose again, and from there you hope for the resurrection of the dead; but it was permitted to Christ to rise from the dead. And he now begins to praise Christ, not to give him honor, but to bring you to despair. The cunning destructiveness of the serpent, that by praising Christ he may turn you away from Christ, he deceitfully proclaims him whom he does not dare to disparage. He exaggerates his majesty, to make it singular, lest you hope for any such thing as was shown in him rising again. And he appears as it were more religious toward Christ, when he says: Behold one who dares to compare himself to Christ, so that, because Christ rose again, he thinks he too will rise again. Do not be disturbed by the perverse praise of your Emperor; the hostile snares disturb you, but the humility and humanity of Christ console you. He proclaims how greatly Christ is raised above you: but Christ says how greatly he descended to you. Answer him, then: rouse that faith; there is a storm, there are waves, the ship labors, Christ sleeps[^22]; rouse that faith, do not forget what you have believed. At once you will answer, when in you the gospel faith has begun to keep watch. You will not be helpless in answering: for it will not be you who speak; Christ remaining in you will seize his instrument, your tongue, as his sword, using your heart and your voice as a possessor dwelling within, he will resist the adversary, will make you secure: only do you rouse the sleeper, that is, recall your forgotten faith.

**16. 16.** For now what shall I say, from which you may answer such people? I will say nothing new, but that which you have believed. Rouse, then, your faith, and answer the one saying: Christ alone could, we cannot: answer and say: Christ could for this reason, because he is God; he surely, because he is God. If because he is God, because he is almighty; if because he is almighty, why shall I despair of what he will be able to do even in me, what he demonstrated in himself for my sake? Then I ask from where Christ rose again. He will answer: From the dead. I ask why he died. For can God die? Could that divinity, the Word equal to the Father, the art of the almighty artificer, through whom all things were made, the unchangeable wisdom, remaining in itself, renewing all things[^23], reaching from end to end mightily and disposing all things sweetly[^24], could it die? No, he says. And yet Christ died. From where did he die? Namely from this, that *he did not count equality with God a thing to be grasped, but emptied himself, taking the form of a servant.* But before, what does he say? *Who, though he was in the form of God*[^25]. Had he received the form of God, or was he in it naturally? The Apostle distinguishes. When he spoke of the form of God, he said *was*: when he named the form of a servant, he said *taking*. Christ, then, was something, received something, that with him there might be one what he had received. In the form of God he was equal to God, as that evangelist fisherman says: *The Word was in the beginning, and the Word with God, and the Word was God*[^26]; this is: *Though he was in the form of God, he did not count equality with God a thing to be grasped.* For what is not naturally present, but is usurped unlawfully, is a thing grasped. The angel usurped equality with God, and fell, and was made the devil: man usurped equality with God, and fell, and was made mortal. But this one, who was born equal, because not born in time, but the eternal Son to the eternal Father, always born, through whom all things were made, was in the form of God. But that he might be the mediator between God and men, between the just one and the unjust, between the immortal one and mortals, he assumed something from the unjust and mortal, keeping something with the just and immortal. For keeping justice with the just and immortal one, assuming mortality from the unjust and mortal, made a reconciler in the middle, casting down the wall of our sins; whence it is sung to him by his people: *And in my God I shall pass over the wall*[^27]; rendering to God what sins had alienated, redeeming by his own blood what the devil possessed; he died for us, and rose for us. He bore our sins, not clinging to them, but bearing them: just as Jacob bore the skins of the kids, that he might seem hairy to his father blessing him[^28]. The evil Esau had his own hairs, but the good Jacob bore another's. For sins cling to mortal men. But they did not cling to him who had said: *I have power to lay down my soul, and I have power to take it up again*[^29].

**17. 16.** Death, then, in our Lord was a sign of another's sins, not the punishment of his own. But in all men mortality is the punishment of sin: for it is drawn from the origin of sin, whence we all come; from the fall of that man, not from the descent of this one. For it is one thing to fall, another to descend. One fell wickedly, the other descended mercifully. *For as in Adam all die, so in Christ all will be made alive*[^30]. Bearing, then, another's sins: *What I did not seize*, he says, *then I was paying back*[^31]; that is, not having sin I was dying. *Behold*, he says, *the prince of this world will come, and in me he will find nothing.* What is: *he will find nothing in me*? He will not find in me the merit of death. For the merit of death is sin. Why, then, will you die? It follows, and he says. *But that all may know that I do the will of my Father, rise, let us go hence*[^32]. And he rises, going to the passion. Why? Because he was doing the will of his Father, not because he owed anything to the prince of sinners, in whom there was no sin. Therefore our Lord Jesus Christ brought divinity with him, but assumed mortality from us. This he received in the womb of the virgin Mary, joining himself, the Word of God, to human nature, as a bridegroom to a bride in the virginal chamber, that he himself as a bridegroom might come forth from his chamber[^33].

**17. 17.** Return, then, to that which I was saying. Mortality came into all men from sin: but in the Lord it was from mercy, and yet it was true; because such flesh was true, and was truly mortal, having the likeness of the flesh of sin; not the likeness of flesh, but the likeness of the flesh of sin[^34]: for it was true flesh, but not the flesh of sin. For he had not, as I said, received that mortality by the merit of sin, who *emptied himself, taking the form of a servant, and was made obedient unto death*[^35]. What, then, was he, and what did he have? He was divinity, having mortality. But from where he died, from there he rose again.

**18. 17.** Now look at those who say: Christ alone could rise, but not you. But answer and say: Christ rose in that which he had received from us. Take away the form of a servant, there would be nothing in which he might rise; because there would be nothing in which he might die. Why, then, do you wish from the praise of my Lord to destroy in me the faith which my Lord has built up? For from that, that he received the form of a servant, he died. But according to this he rose, according to which he died. In no way, then, would I despair of the resurrection of the servant, since in the form of a servant the Lord rose again. Or if they attribute to the power of the man that Christ rose from the dead—for this too they are wont to say, that he was a man so just that he could even rise from the dead—so that I may for the moment speak according to them, and say nothing of the divinity of our Lord; that man, so just that he merited even to rise from the dead, could in no way deceive us, when he promised the resurrection to us too.

**19. 18.** All the things, then, which have been said, brothers, avail for this, that you may be instructed, if any say that the dead do not rise. But they were said, if you remember, as much as God deigned to suggest as necessary, and testimonies were said both from the nature of things and from daily examples; and from the very omnipotence of God to whom nothing is difficult, who, if he could make what was not, much more can repair what was; and from the very Lord and Savior Jesus Christ, who, it is agreed, rose again, and his resurrection was made only in the form of a servant, because death too could not come about, from which there would be need to rise, except through the form of a servant. Whence, since we are servants, we ought to hope in our form that which he deigned to demonstrate beforehand in the form of a servant. Let the tongues of those saying be silenced, then: *Let us eat and drink; for tomorrow we die.* Plainly do you too answer, and say: Let us fast and pray; for tomorrow we shall die.

**20. 19.** It remains for us to say what the life of the just will be like in the resurrection. But because you see that the moderate time has now today been taken up, ruminate on what we have rendered: but pray that we may at some time render what we owe. Hold above all why we have spoken, especially on account of these festal days, my brothers, which the pagans celebrate. Attend to yourselves: this world passes away, recall the Gospel where the Lord foretells that the last day will be thus, as in the days of Noah. *They were eating and drinking, buying and selling, marrying, being given in marriage, until Noah entered the ark; the flood came, and destroyed all*[^36]. You have the Lord most plainly forewarning, and in another place saying: *Let not your hearts be weighed down in carousing and drunkenness*[^37]. *Let your loins be girded, and your lamps burning*[^38]; *and be like servants awaiting their master, when he comes from the wedding*[^39]. Let us await him going to come, let him not find us torpid. It is base for a married woman not to desire her husband: how much more base for the Church not to desire Christ? The husband comes to carnal embraces, and is received with the great vows of a chaste wife: the bridegroom of the Church is going to come to hand over eternal embraces, to make us his everlasting co-heirs, and we so live that we not only do not desire his coming, but even fear it! How true is it that that day will so come, as in the days of Noah? How many will it find thus, even those who are called Christians? Therefore the ark is built for so many years, that those who do not believe may wake up[^40]. For a hundred years it was built, and they did not wake up to say: Not without cause does the man of God build an ark, except because destruction threatens the human race; and let them appease the wrath of God, turning to the morals which please God, as the Ninevites did.

**21. 19.** For they brought forth the fruit of penance, and propitiated the wrath of God.

**21. 20.** Jonah announced not mercy, but wrath to come: for he did not say: In three days Nineveh will be overthrown; but if you do penance in these three days, God will spare you: he did not say this. He threatened and foretold the overthrow alone: and yet they, not despairing of God's mercy, turned themselves to repenting; and God spared them[^41]. But what shall we say? that the prophet lied? If you understand it carnally, he seems to have spoken falsely; if you understand it spiritually, what the prophet said came to pass. For Nineveh was overthrown. Attend to what Nineveh was, and see that it was overthrown. What was Nineveh? They were eating and drinking, buying, selling, planting, building, given to perjuries, lies, drunkennesses, crimes, corruptions: this was Nineveh. Attend now to Nineveh: they lament, grieve, are saddened, in sackcloth and ashes, in fasts and prayers. Where is that Nineveh? Surely it was overthrown, because it is not set in those former deeds.

**22. 21.** Therefore, brothers, even now the ark is built, and those hundred years are these times: this whole stretch of time was signified by that number of years. If, then, those deserved to perish who, while Noah was building the ark, disregarded it; what do those deserve, who, while Christ builds the Church, disregard salvation? There is as much difference between Noah and Christ as between a servant and a Lord; rather indeed, as much as between God and man. For a servant and a Lord can also be called two men. And yet, because, with a man building the ark, men did not believe, an example to be guarded against was given of them to those who came after. Christ, God on our account man, builds the Church; he placed himself as the foundation of that ark: daily imperishable wood, faithful men renouncing this world, enter into the framework of the ark; and still it is said: *Let us eat and drink; for tomorrow we die*? Do you, then, as I said, brothers, say against them: Let us fast and pray; for tomorrow we shall die. For those say: *Let us eat and drink; for tomorrow we die*, who do not hope for the resurrection: but we who now both believe and announce the resurrection, with the prophets speaking, and Christ and the apostles preaching, who hope that after this death we shall live, let us not fail, nor weigh down our hearts with carousing and drunkenness; but, anxiously awaiting, with loins girded and lamps burning, the coming of our Lord, let us fast and pray, not for the reason that tomorrow we shall die, but that we may die secure. What remains, then, brothers, demand of us in the name of the Lord at another time. Turned to the Lord, etc.
